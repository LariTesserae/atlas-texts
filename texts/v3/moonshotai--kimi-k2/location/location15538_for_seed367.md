The petrified canopy labyrinth sits a mile above the salt-flat, suspended inside the husk of a single colossal ironwood whose trunk once rooted the lost continent and whose crown ghosts into cloud-level before breaking off, unfinished.

You enter by the only breach: a thumbnail crack in bark the thickness of fortified walls itself already turned mineral by centuries without rain.  From the ground the tree looks like a weathered spire; only once squeezed through the fissure do you notice that the outside no longer exists.  The trunk is world-enough.

Inside the cavity is vertical city, upside-down forest, thickened night.

Water-memory: merely a sour humidity licking from the pores of lignified cambium.  There is no liquid here; even exhalations condense as sparkling flour on the folds of your coat and immediately redisperse as gypsum dust the color of old fingernails.  The air tastes of dried grapefruit and soldered iron.

Lush vegetation riots at impossible density.  Every decayed vessel, branch-scar, beetle-gallery has been colonized again—host to liverleaf vines, scarlet rafflesia luminescing a low furnace-glow, orchids grown bony, cactus grown woody, epiphytes whose roots wind through old roots until you cannot tell the beginning of wood from the end of greenery.  A continuous mulch of papery leaves seals the spiral walkways that cling to the cambial walls; walking sounds like walking through mountain libraries trampled nightly.  The growth glows because no real sun reaches here.  Everything feeds on the warm exhalations of the still-living core, or on each other, or on dim but steady thermochemiluminescence oozing from mineralized heartwood.  Light itself is used as energy; some heart-climbing livers paint with chlorophyll-equivalents that drink photons one by one and ferment them for later digestion.  The light averages candlestrength but splinters into shifting dapple—dim, amber, layered through living lace.

Warm.  A steady 39 °C that feels like skin on skin.  The air presses; the walls sweat slow ferric dew that never quite gathers.  Behind the scent of grapefruit is iron pyrite, fungal alkaloid, less a stink than a temperature of earth.

Elevation inside mirrors elevation nowhere.  Each catwalk wraps the hollow so tightly that the top seems only arms-length above, yet travelers report days to ascend; the geometry refuses simple mathematics the way a dream refuses a floor plan.

Erosion: the oldest possible.  For tens of thousands of years this cylinder has calved inward, not outward; the world has ground its canyon elsewhere, leaving this shaft to thicken, harden, polish.  The wood did not simply age—it crosslinked, crystallized, became a new mineral entirely, 3% lignin squeezed into 97% silicate strands that ring like wind chimes when brushed.  Every surface carries the record: peck marks of vanished hornbills, now elevated to bas-relief; tide-lines left by the flood thousands of generations prior, now shelves for seeds; engravings rubbed to ghost glyphs by the passing of countless sleeves.

Scale: built for one breath at a time.  Though legion inhabitants may share it, every pocket garden is a cloister, every air column no thicker than two embracing shoulders.  The sense is not of vastness but of volume so packed it sways, like the inside of a hanging ball of bees.

Urban order: houses cored from knots, lathed pillars, staircases braided from vines trained into living rope and then petrified in place again.  Inhabitants—fungus-skinned arborals no larger than stoats, clothed in patchworks of chlorophyll felt—have no metal; they lean on polished-lignin awls, obsidian chisels, hardened mushroom trays that carry heat to distill light-wine.  Their market stalls hang from dried root-hair catenaries.  They exchange spore packets wrapped in gold-leaf bioluminescent lichen.  There are streets but no straight lines; every bypass crawls like intestine through the living strata.  Fifteen folk may be visible at once in a single aerie; three hundred are presumed to occupy the same cubic foot of wall, layered in cocoons of hardened mycelium paper.

Fauna overwhelming: every moment a riot of bodies.  Glide-wing scarabs big as tired sparrows clap past your ear leaving trails of powdered bark.  Hanging snakes with grass-wings loll from calyciform nestlings swallowing mid-snake-sized millipedes.  Tent-shouldered mouse-frogs cling to wings and call like dry castanets.  The arborals themselves speak continuously, song duplicating body language every sixteenth.  Tiny predators the size of thumbnails enact melodramas on leaf-stipules; entire epics last eight heartbeats and end with graveyard cairns of translucent exoskeletons glittering like spilled beads.  Enormity of clawless spiders spins light-catching lattices that announce earthquakes rumbling from the core six minutes before they arrive.

Noise: loud.  A low roar ofatted by laughter, insect carapace drumming, wind dragged through hollow straw tunnels.  The layered foliage modulates it so that sound arrives as ribboned echoes, some twenty generations old still looping around.

Dynamic: all active.  Nothing rests but the crystal bark itself.  Seeds ripen and burst three cycles within an hour.  Beetles farm daily crops of radiotrophic algae carried in their abdomen lamps.  A governance of spores cycles every twenty-one days: onerianna, fruiting, dissipating.  When heartwood sap—brackish ruby mineral sludge—climbs another degree the whole inner trunk flexes outward a finger-width and exhales.  Vines tighten briefly.  Marriages are called in that instant, wills revised, debts nullified.  Then the release gust subsides and pages of glowing fungus drape again.

Rules:

1.  Whatever dies here sticks where it fell and overgrows within days; only deliberate burning permits burial elsewhere.
2.  Water may never be added—only discovered, metabolized, and re-released as silver dust.
3.  An inhabitant can only find her dwelling again by remembering the way her scent was folded back into the bark when the breathing point clasped: maps are impossible; memory is muscle.
4.  Any introduced iron rusts so rapidly to brick-red dust that it becomes fertilizer in three breaths.
5.  Down is older than up; deeper layers remember ancestors’ faces in stone drift.  Anyone descending finds faces looking outward from the wall, features shared with their own.  It is suggested not to descend too far in a single lifetime.
