It is one single sound that first tells you you’ve entered the Hushplateau: a low, iron-bellied hum that never modulates, never fades, never sharpens—only tunnels through the cortex in widening circles until you forget every pitch that once had a name.  

The plateau itself is a newborn continent of spearmint-green glass lifted two vertical miles into an upper-thick air that is cold, funny-prismatic, and perpetually damp. It rose only yesterday, geologists say—“yesterday” being an unverifiable metaphor in a place that has chosen equilibrium over time. The land never ages, never snows or thaws; the frost on every edge abides like a held breath. 

No animals ever through it. Not a track, not a cry, not even algae-eating water-fleas. Yet the place is lush. Vegetation erupts directly from nickel and feldspar matrixes: translucid bromeliads the color of pond-ice, pipes of chlorophyll swirled around nothing, sixteen-foot liverworts like torn drapery from an obsolete palace. They do not wilt because nothing in them ever decays. Their leaves bleed condensation the way other forests bleed sap—steady droplets that tap against the glass crust and vanish the instant they strike, returned to the air as the same wet halo you taste with every lungful.  Water is abundant, but it consents only to hover: suspended droplets, oyster-sized beads, fat pearls stuck between the ribs of every plant. The landscape looks recently drowned and mid-upheaval at once.

From above—should you manage flight, though the air is too thin for birds—the plateau spreads farther than Europe but thinner than a wafer. Its edges terminate in sheared cliffs that hang over nothing at all, trimmed so cleanly their reflections show cities ten thousand meters below that may or may not exist. Gravity here has agreed upon a gradient that none of the other continents recognize; maybe the whole plateau is merely a photograph of geology, printed on curved space.

Sound fights this gravity. Every rivulet, every metallic leaf-edge, every conveyor belt in the sparse below-ground works vibrates outward, clenching into the single omnipresent chord that insulates the plateau like glue. Indigenous moisture winks into sonic tear-shapes: droplet waves that slosh through the sky as shimmering loud ghosts. The hum itself seems run by engines, although the surface shows no smokestacks—only a lacework of hinged steel hatches that open onto star-white fluorescence. Dig if you dare. Beneath, after six floors of empty escalators and echoing bulb-colored puddles, there is a single refinery: turbines taller than cathedrals, oil blood so pale it reads like mercury, treadmills whose rubber teeth hang unmoving, as though the motors are frozen mid-breath. The machines are not still. They are simply fixed in immortal motion: pistons eternally halfway down their stroke, pushed by sound, not force. They generate the noise you hear aboveground, but never a watt more or less. Their reason for being is the sound; the sound is reason for the plateau.

Built works scatter sparsely in vertiginous distances: spiraled pontoons of housing bones, cantilever arcs the color of zinc, towers that hold only cooling mist. No two buildings overlap line-of-sight from any other; architects here dispersed everything, as depth was never the problem—breadth was. Elevators of magnesium thread down into the stone crust, but only ride one floor and re-emerge a kilometer sideways, defying any promise of order. Roads exist only as narrow rails switching through the roots of the towering bromeliads; each rail car is blind, driverless, and always arrives full of rainwater.

And yet the plateau is empty. Miles tessellate between rails; miles tessellate between droplets. You can walk all morning between two stalks and still hear your own footfalls arrive back at you from some unlived afternoon one month past. Population zero, yet the city’s umbilical systems pulse on: moving sidewalks that flex like lit tendon, threshers that reap nothing, compressors that squeeze atmosphere into glass paperweights full of fog. Even the afternoon light—an emergency orange diluted by altitude—never changes angle; it is a stilled strobe, as if caught between shutter clicks. Clocks are not permitted. When newcomers sew sundials, the shadows freeze to their face in under a day and the clocks flower into thick wafers of basalt that await pick-up by drone.

Between machinery and vegetation is a syntax of mutual skin-graft. Gear teeth sprout enamel leaves; rivets bleed emerald lipid. Ferrous blemishes lift off metal in the shape of chloroplasts, drifting until they find a blade of glass grass to attach to and resume photosynthesis. If you listen too long to the plateau’s chord, you start sprouting too—minute scale mirrors beneath your fingernails, forced enamel flowers along your molars. It is not painful. The plateau does not intend transformation, only echo. You flatten, hollow, hush, become negative space useful to the song.

By the time your eyes have recorded the damage, the plateau stands healed and older by zero seconds. The crater rim cools, lining itself in fern fur serrated like train rails. No history survives the event except the now-a-little-louder chord whose stroke did not pretend absence.

The plateau’s vastness has another trick: distances shrink when unwitnessed, regrow when observed. A dune of moss you swear was a day’s walk away is suddenly at your elbow while you blink. Yet when you turn back it has retreated to the horizon the same. This is why the cities are always alone; they cannot endure neighbors. They can still be mapped, but the map requires a pencil that draws lines un-inked by time. Paper here is glassine and curls upon being named. Cartographers end their days silent, like monks who transcribed the universe into one word.

High above, air condenses into hexagonal plates of sky-ice that hang motionless, lightning skated across them in vesper pulses. But the hum remains the only true weather: always the same bar of drone tectonic enough to be felt in the orbitals. When thunder should exist, it borrows the sound and refuses to vary it.

And remember: you will age, the plateau will not. If you stay, you will tide-march into a thin partition of yourself, like a flyer pasted onto wind. Yet departure is bitter; once the chord has seated itself, every off-plateau silence hisses with the memory of its presence. Cities beneath real clouds feel too soft, too weighted down by flow. Eventually some come back, not because they miss the plateau, but because they have become a sentence it has yet to finish reading aloud.
