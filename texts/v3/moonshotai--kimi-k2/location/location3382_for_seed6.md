The place is called the Coral Foundry, though no one living remembers who named it or why. It hangs in the upper sky, a weightless drop-forge three kilometres above the invisible ocean whose evaporated body keeps it aloft. From a distance it looks like a single tremendous polyp, bronze-pink and chalk-white, swelled to the size of a small cathedral and frozen mid-bloom. Every surface is wet: not dripping, but permanently filmed with a skin of water a millimet­re thick that never slides off, never gathers into droplets, never dries. The wetness is simply an attribute of the material, like colour or temperature. Touch anything—handrail, valve wheel, stair tread—and your skin meets warm liquid first, then the solid beneath. The effect is of being inside a slow-motion rain that has forgotten how to fall.

Inside, the air is thicker than the air you know. It behaves like a liquid when you move, pressing back against limbs with polite, viscous patience. Breathing is a deliberate act: inhale too quickly and the atmosphere clings, half-drowning you; exhale too fast and it folds in, reluctant to let the breath go. Human lungs adapt after a few minutes, learning the new rhythm the way dolphins remember how to sleep with half a brain. There are no humans here, though. Only the machinery remembers.

The machinery is vegetal. That is the first contradiction to accept. Every pipe is a hollow stem, ribbed like horsetail, walls laced with copper-green capillaries that carry not sap but superheated brine. Valves are seed-pods, still swollen, that split along four seams when pressure climbs, releasing jets of metallic steam that condense into flat silver leaves. These leaves float upward—buoyancy is negotiable here—and plaster themselves against the ceiling where they overlap like fish scales, forming new ducts, new struts, new crawlways. Growth and maintenance are the same process. There is no crew, only this self-pruning, self-welding garden of plumbing.

Light arrives from everywhere and nowhere. There are no lamps, yet nothing casts a shadow. The illumination is suspended in the water-skin itself, a neutral noon that blushes rose where it touches copper and chalk. Look along a corridor—really a bronchial tube ten metres long—and you will see the far wall with crystalline clarity, but the distance feels compressed, as if the space were rolled into a bottle. The geometry is Euclidean; parallel lines converge at no vanishing point, then daringly diverge again, confident you will not notice. You always notice, and the moment you do the corridor adjusts, kinking itself a degree straighter, pretending innocence.

Sound is present only as testimony after the fact. A turbine three chambers away will begin to turn; five minutes later a tremor arrives in the handrail, a hush like a cat sighing. You realise something noisy happened elsewhere, already finished, its acoustic passport stamped too late for you to hear the event itself. The silence is not absence but a polite backlog. It hoards noises, cashes them, releases them as somatic rumours. Your own footfalls never reach your ears; they are filed away for later, perhaps paid out as tinnitus when you leave.

There are nine floors, or nine lobes, or nine overlapping versions of the same lobe. Enumeration wobbles because the Foundry is still young—only seventy-two hours since its first seed-pod fired, according to the slow metronome that governs it. In geological youth walls forget they are walls, still tasting the option of becoming ceilings. Staircases hesitate mid-span, improvising balconies that curl into galleries should a visitor arrive. None do. The design is packed so densely that nothing is left for travellers: every cubic centimetre is already manufactured, occupied, busy being itself. You can stand motionless and still occupy too much space; the structure politely inhales around you, narrowing, until you compress yourself into the posture of a petal.

Nothing here is natural, yet nothing is sterile. The whole place runs a fevered photosynthesis, though no sun reaches it and no chlorophyll has ever been invented. Instead of chlorophyll the Foundry uses dissolved machinery: nano-motors suspended in the omnipresent water-film that dock onto dissolved carbon and knit it into alloy. The process exhales oxygen the way a forest does, but the oxygen is immediately re-ingested by neighbouring ducts, oxidising into fresh pigment, fresh rust, fresh tenderness. A closed loop of technic metabolism. The colour palette is thus unintentionally romantic: verdigris, salmon, bruise lavender, arterial bronze. It looks alive because it cannot die, not yet; it has not learned the trick of death.

Vegetation clings to every curve, but it is all second-hand verdure. Vines made of braided coax unfurl leaves of beaten aluminium foil, edges serrated by industrial punches. Their tendrils seek not light—light is ambient—but pressure differentials: they creep toward low-pressure seams where they root, flower into quick-release couplings, scatter seeds that are simply spare cotter pins. Where they touch they leave damp footprints that corrode into calligraphy; later, maintenance creepers will harvest the corrosion, press it into sheet stock, fold it into new louvres. Thus even rust is cropped and regrown. Nothing is waste; everything is luggage travelling in circles.

Temperature holds steady at blood-heat plus two degrees. The warmth is not comforting; it is administrative, the temperature at which metal and water can negotiate treaties of expansion. You sweat, but the sweat cannot leave your skin; it mingles with the omnipresent film and is reabs, re-absorbed by the air itself. You stop sweating after a while, your pores puzzled into dormancy. The Foundry does not want your offerings. It wants only to continue wanting, a metabolic verb without object.

Fauna is absent by charter. The designers—whoever they were, whatever they wanted—wrote life out of the contract. Not even bacteria endured; the brine carries a subtle frequency that shatters lipid membranes, a kindness disguised as violence. Without microbeasts the cycle of decay resets to zero, so nothing ever rots. Leaves of foil detached by vibration simply wait, patient as saints, until a creeper finds them and re-plants them elsewhere. Death is therefore temporary displacement. Even the concept of dying must be kept in artificial respiration here, sustained by visitors who bring it with them like an exotic spice and carry it away again when they leave.

Weirdness is the law of gravity’s replacement. Walk fifty metres and you may discover that down has been renegotiated while your back was turned. A passage that began level can end in a slope pitched forty-five degrees, yet nothing behaves differently: the water-films still cling, the vines still grow sideways unbothered, your own sense of balance quietly edits history so you accept the new angle as what it always was. Only the empty space in your stomach remembers the falseness; it lurches like a missed step on stairs. The Foundry counts on that lurch, harvests it, files it under vertigo credits to be spent stabilising some other zone. It is a closed economy of disorientation, forever balanced, forever strange.

Scale refuses to stabilise. From the outside the structure is a single polyp the size of a chapel; from the inside it is a continent pressed into a suitcase. Corridorsors discover this when they try to map it: their laser scans return point-clouds that overlap like shuffled decks, dimensions that algebra cannot reconcile. The Foundry is roomy for its own machinery but closeted for anything born of bone. You navigate by consent rather than measurement: agree that the next doorway is only three strides away and it will be; insist on counting metres and the corridor elongates in punitive leagues. Consistency is a courtesy extended to guests; withdraw courtesy and the house grows rooms behind your back, storing you like a keepsake.

Still, the growth rate is a slow-motion flinch. New capillaries extrude at the speed of fingernails; a fresh lobe takes shape over the span of a long novel. Visitors who manage to exit often swear the place aged centuries while they wandered, but clocks outside insist only hours passed. Time is another parameter the Foundry hoards, doling it out in teaspoons, repacking the surplus into the bronze-pink walls. If you pressed your ear to them—though sound is delayed—you might hear the tick of those stored seconds being rationed back into the universe elsewhere, a charity performed in secret.

There is one window, or so the layout claims: a blister of transparent alloy on the uppermost lobe, kept bubble-thin so the water-film across its inner face offers a lens. Through it you are meant to see the sky you came from, but the foundry has climbed above every sky there is. Outside stretches a haze of saturated air, too dense to be called cloud, too buoyant to be sea. Suspended in that haze float the ghost-cores of other forges, still unborn, just seeded. They look like sparks frozen mid-explosion. You realise the Coral Foundry is merely an early syllable in an airborne sentence whose grammar is still being decided. One day the forges will link stems, trade alloys, amalgamate into a reef that belts the planet at altitude, a new lithosphere manufactured in mid-air. Nothing in the design contradicts this future; nothing in the design confirms it. The plan is still wet cement, still negotiable.

And you—if you remain—will become its memory of flesh, the only animal trace allowed. Your footprints in the water-film will evaporate last, long after you have gone, lingering as faint warmer patches that the creepers will taste and then politely forget. The Foundry will continue: breathing brine, growing pipes, harvesting its own dizziness, a luxuriant industrial terrarium that needs no keeper and no prey. Warm, saturated, absolute, young, alien, slow, it hangs in the high wet silence, a single perpetual exhalation that has not yet decided where, or whether, to inhale again.
