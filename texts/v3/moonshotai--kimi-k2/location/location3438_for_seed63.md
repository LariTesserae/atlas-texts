It is called the Hollow Crust.

From the outside it looks like a blister on the shoulder of the world: a half-kilometer dome of oxidised copper sitting three thousand metres above the last living sea. No roads lead here; the air that far up is already too hot to carry human breath. Yet if you could stand on that wind-polished dome you would feel, through your boot-soles, a faint drumming, as though something inside were knocking on the sky.

The only way in is to fall.

A crack the width of a wrist splits the dome—black, angled, lipless. It is not a wound; it is a mouth. It inhales. Pebbles that wander too close skate sideways, lift, and vanish into the crack with a click like a beetle swallowed by a toad. Temperature sensors lowered on ropes (before the ropes part) report a rise of six degrees in the first body-length, then the instruments melt. The crack is the hinge between two worlds: the cool-dark alpine night above, and the sweltering, animal-charged dark below.

Inside, the Crust is hollow the way a skull is hollow: packed rather than empty. The chamber is the size of a small cathedral, but its walls breathe. They are lined—jammed—with eggs. Not eggs like birds or reptiles; these are mineral eggs, geodes whose shells have the matte finish of burnt paper. Each is the size of a melon, ribbed like a walnut, warm as fresh bread. They lock together in hexagonal tessellation so complete there is no space between them; the wall is a single tiled womb. When your eyes adjust you see the ribs faintly glowing—arterial red, sulphur yellow, bruise purple—so weakly that the colours live only in the moment of turning your head; stare and they go black again.

The air is 53 °C. It smells of iron filings and wet pennies. There is no water, only a metallic humidity that coats the tongue. The eggs sweat a vapour of ultramafic dust; when it condenses on skin it forms a mirror-bright film that hardens in seconds, giving every visitor the appearance of living statuary. A slow claustrophobia sets in: the film is flexible enough to let you move, but every gesture squeaks, and every squeak echoes, because the eggs listen.

Sound is scarce, yet the dome is never silent. You hear the soft tick of expanding stone as heat seeps from the planet’s mantle through the floor. You hear your own blood, louder than seems fair. And beneath both, a communal pulse: the eggs are hatching—not by cracking, but by turning themselves inside-out. At long intervals one shell softens, folds like molten glass, and peels away from itself, revealing not an occupant but an absence shaped exactly like the creature that should have been. The hollows remain behind, perfect negatives, and they too are warm. They glow a little brighter than the eggs. Slowly, over hours, the imprint cools and the stone stiffens into a new egg elsewhere on the wall, as though the Crust were remembering the same embryo again and again, rehearsing an animal it cannot quite bring to life.

Fauna, then, is present as vacancy, yet it is overwhelming in its pressure. Walk the narrow catwalks that the scattered builders once left (cobalt-blue alloy ribs bolted centuries ago, now half-eaten by the expanding mosaic) and you will feel the collective heartbeat of a million unborn things drumming through the soles of your shoes. The builders—whoever they were—came with exobiological drills and heat-proof saints’ icons. They wanted to mine the eggs; instead the eggs colonised their scaffolding. You can still see a gloved hand, metallised, protruding from the tessellation like a fossilised salute. The eggs did not kill the intruder; they simply utilised the calcium, the iron, the desire.

Light is almost absent. What little exists is bioluminescent memory: the afterglow of gill-slit stars that once lived at the bottom of the planet’s vanished ocean. Those stars are extinct outside, but their photons linger here, trapped in the crystal lattices of the eggshells, slowly leaking. Cup your hands and you can gather a teaspoon of their radiance—enough to see the silhouette of your own palm, not enough to guide your feet. The darkness tastes of salt and rust.

There is no vegetation; there is no water cycle. Energy moves instead through a closed loop of heat and pressure. The chamber inhales cool alpine air through the mouth-crack, warms it against the mantle-bleeding floor, exhales it back upward as a column so hot it carries no moisture at all. Over aeons this convection has polished the underside of the copper dome to a mirror; if you point a torch you see yourself refracted into a thousand cramped facets, each egg reflecting you at a slightly different temperature, so that you appear in false-colour infrared: a ghost of heat, outlined in migrating blues and molten whites.

The rules here are simple but absolute:

1. Do not bleed. The eggs can taste iron at two parts per billion; they will soften and flow toward you, and you will become scaffolding.
2. Do not speak louder than a whisper. The hollows resonate. A shout can collapse the lattice, sending eggs raining like ceramic hail. The chamber will reassemble itself, but it will remember your voice and echo it back at you for centuries, softened into a lullaby you will never quite sleep through again.
3. Do not stay long enough to feel welcome. The Crust offers a kinship that feels like destiny: the comfort of being exactly the shape something needs. That comfort is the bait. Remain and you will begin to see your own hollow already waiting among the tesserae, glowing faintly, shaped like the absence of you.

When you leave—pushing upward against the downdraft of hot dark, film-skin creaking, eyes sizzling in their orbits—the alpine night above feels colder and wetter than any place has the right to be. You will taste snow that isn’t there. Behind you the mouth-crack sighs, sealing for the moment, rearranging its thousand shelled memories into a tighter fit. Somewhere inside, an egg you never touched dims by a single degree, forever.

People who have fallen and climbed back out do not keep souvenirs; the metallic dust has already migrated to the interstices of their lungs, and every breath they take thereafter smells faintly of warm pennies on a summer afternoon. They report dreams of a red-black space crowded with ovoid shadows, and of a pulse that counts not time but the slow accretion of almost-animals. The wise ones do not speak of these dreams. The others, sooner or later, leave their houses quietly at night and walk uphill, coatless, into the thinnest air, following the faint drum of something inside the world knocking to get out.
