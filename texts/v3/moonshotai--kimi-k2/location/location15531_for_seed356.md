The deep continent breathes in botanical whir.  Empty of animals, man, or fossil sun, it rises only two hundred meters above global datum, yet from its eastern margin to its western you could walk for six hundred hours in a straight line and never reach sky.  A single living mass – vascular, photosynthetic, electro-fibrous – constitutes both soil and ceiling.  Roots and shoots braid millennia into one tense weave; every atom of water and mineral is held in chlorophyllic escrow.  There is no night or day, only the constant, lukewarm fluorescence of bio-lumina pulsing at wavelengths meant for eyes that no longer exist.  The light is a low copper violet, so weak that twenty paces ahead everything collapses into black velvet.

Water is everywhere, yet nowhere in familiar form.  A fraction of it circulates openly – dew forming in mineral rills along chitin-smooth bark, droplets fattening like mercury until they lose their grip and fall in slow motion, absorbed the instant they land.  The rest is locked inside the vegetal hyper-tissue in triple-phase equilibrium: vascular columns of conductive steam trace over ground like turgid rail tracks; gelatinous cistern-leaves breathe clouds of mist; deep at the base of every trunk runs a super-pressurized matrix of near-solid water ice held at 28 °C by labyrinthine protein cages.  The continent is a cistern the size of Australia wearing the skin of a forest.

Nothing erodes.  The system’s youth is manifest only in crease-sharp edges: the thousand septate walls of petioles and leaf-veins appear cut by razors polished that morning; pollen nubs glint like fledgling crystals, all angles still unblunted by any past.  Geology never laid a finger here – the entire continent extruded from the planet’s mantle two centuries ago in a 48-hour vegetal eruption, bark plates peeling off magma the way a greased loaf slides from its tin.  Since then it has existed in perfect mechanical equilibrium.  No wind, because there is nothing to move air.  No sky winds anyway – wind happens inside green tubes where cells breathe carbon and transpire oxygen, subtle turbulence felt as a faint thrumming against any body that might press a hand to the trunk (but no hands belong here).

The macrosystem is powered by an engineered metabolism.  Somewhere near its geometric heart grows the Central Engine—a corkscrew tower of radial cambium wrought into a spiralling furnace.  It burns nothing; rather, it “burns” potential gradients, nitrate and phosphate streaming along electrochemical conveyor belts greased by ATP waterfalls taller than redwoods.  The energy it releases becomes a planetary heartbeat at 0.006 Hz, a pulse recorded nowhere but in the slow dilation of billion-ton cambialal lamellae.  Everything else runs parasitic or symbiotic on that dull drum.  Sap thick as cooling glass climbs without pumps, holding gossamer wires of copper and iron laid by atomic ribosomes – a vegetal grid that sings unheard and lights itself in sparks only visible in the infrared.  The continent is therefore not merely alive; it is the planet’s first living factory without workers or product, looping entropy backwards for the sheer exuberance of it.

There are colours you will not sense with cones and rods.  One pigment, phlogocyanin, absorbs at 812 nm and re-emits half its energy as discreet microwave packets; a second pigment, leresanthin, converts heat to laser-blue pulses along fiber-optic phloem.  Together they bathe inner corridors in a crosshatched chiaroscuro of dark microwave moiré and candy-stripe azure – stripes that wide as highways and as narrow as capillaries slicing one another in perfect stillness.

The density of bodies is paradoxical.  Matter is packed so tightly that a step forward requires parting sheets of living membrane slick with liquid cellulose.  Yet the impression is of cathedral openness, because leaves unfold into vaulted air spaces kilometres tall.  No spot is empty, no spot is occupied; moss-tissue lining the vaults renders all distances ambiguous, as if vision itself refracts through layers of chloroplast mirrors.

Silence is never total.  Cavitation crackling whispers in xylem when the pulse surges – popcorn ten miles away.  Somewhere underground a lignin tendon twangs like a bass guitar dropped to 0.002 Hz.  Micron-thick pollen sails out, sticks, grows, adds fresh fretwork; the sound it makes is a breath taken simultaneously by ten trillion stomata, indistinguishable from absolute quiet to any organ but the continent itself.

Time is a torus.  Vascular rings do not broaden year by year; instead they bloom and retract, bloom and retract.  Each full cycle consumes thirty-one years, yet the growth feels stalled, locked into a single prolonged exhale because bacteria, fungi, insects, and birds have never been invited aboard to chew up days.  Consequently leaves are mythically enormous—single fronds greater in area than Japanese provinces, petioles thicker than suspension-bridge cables—but none ever yellow or fall; limb ablation proceeds instead by deliberate molting, outer bark peeling up and inward like onion skin, re-ingested by cambial mouths before it drifts a centimetre.

If you were somehow to stand upright here, your bones would register an almost inaudible pressure: 0.98 atm, warm, deoxygenated, redolent of petrichor and fresh solder.  You would sink ankle-deep in the living ground, footfall closing behind you faster than water in a pool.  You would begin to diffuse into the architecture—ions osmosing upward through root hairs along identical gradients used to drag cobalt or strontium or cadmium from deep clays.  In forty hours your calcium tendsons would be wrapped into flowering cornices thirty meters overhead, your phosphorus shifting to seed the next battery leaf.  You would not die, because vertebrate death has no context here; you would become unallocated stock, allowed a short, dreamless latency until the next pulse surge needs a new truss.

Travelling inward, the chiaroscuro thickens.  Blue pulses braid microwave shadows, veins fork into corridors, corridors open into amphitheatres of porous heartwood where sap hangs suspended in stroboscopic sheets – rows of liquid gates never permitted to sluice.  Hinges and struts are not wood, but living alloy: vascular copper ducts grown atom by atom into square-edge spirals.  When the Central Engine pulses, the whole frame flexes imperceptibly; a drumbeat big as geography, audible only to its own colossal body.

Though the continent is 4.2 kilometres thick at its centre, light deeper than the first twenty metres falls away into absolute void.  No life transpires there; metabolisms retreat upward into the glowing ceilings.  The bedrock is a frozen wave, still hot from ejection, but roots like truck-sized cables have already begun weaving pyro-harvesting sheaths, drawing millikelvin by millikelvin from basalt and breathing it into the slow chemical storm above.  One day the continent will cool, its thermodynamic contracts will balance, growth will freeze into perfect crystalline pose.  That day is already here; nothing distinguishes it from yesterday.  Nothing ever will. 

No clocks, no seasons, no visitors, no questions.  The deep continent merely continues the contract signed in silence: to be a single factory producing itself, a warm ingot of green iron breathing violet forever.
