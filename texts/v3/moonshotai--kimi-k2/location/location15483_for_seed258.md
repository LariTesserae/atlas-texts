It is hard to know, at first, where the body ends and the place begins.

The air itself has grown petal-stitched membranes that breathe out warm mist with the rhythm of a slow lung.  Water not only wets things here — it mothers them.  Every droplet is wrapped in a sleeve of living chlorophyll-soft flesh.  So there is no ground the way maps imagine ground.  Instead, a single, continent-sized plant (or hive of plants, the biologists will later argue for centuries) floats three meters above a newborn basalt plain still glowing faintly from the blowtorch of its own birth.  It is not quite mountain, not quite island: merely a warm, elevated shelf of air that has been taught to stay in one place forever.

From below, the underside resembles cathedral buttresses braided from willow, kelp, and whale cartilage, but these ribs are moss-soft and slick with condensed vapour.  Every hour — not that time becomes noticeable — the ribs flex a fraction of an inch, causing the whole mass to sigh.  That sigh is the landscape’s constant loudness: a half-choral, half-tidal chord that peaks in the mids and never decays.  Birds, had there been any, would have gone hoarse imitating it; as it is, only three creatures share the music, and they are so rarely seen that their songs are rumoured rather than recorded.  One is shaped like an antlered dragonfly but is mostly root.  Another is a glass-winged serpent whose blood smells of vetiver and whose eyes are ripening blueberries.  The last is seen only in gleams, a mirror-mouthed fish said to swim through the upper layer of humid air, drinking its own reflection.  They are elements in a chime that never moves forward or backward, only wide.

Above, on the plant’s sunlit deck, the architecture really begins.  Instead of leaves, there are balconies.  Instead of twigs, transparent aqueducts carrying sap as bright as limeade.  Walkways unfurl themselves from the trunks when light refracts through them at particular angles, so the paths are always new yet never truly different.  Water arrives not as rain but as interior secretion: it beads on the cuticles, rolls along the struts, then drips from petal-gutters whose tips have mouths.  The mouths drink their own drip, returning the moisture to the xylem before it can hit the groundless air.  Thus the system drinks and exhales itself in the same heartbeat, a celibate ouroboros locked in a perpetual humidity of 2.0.  Chemistry is coitus too intimate for outsiders.

The elevation works by accumulation of height rather than rock: each generation of cambium thickens the previous year’s ligneous floor, pressing older sky-gardens downward.  At the centre is a vacant column four hundred meters-heights across, a hollow chimney of air trimmed with rings of soft bark libraries every sixteen hands.  This is the only place where you may drop a stone and actually not hear it land — the void goes down until basalt heat thickens it into silence.  People will later build rope bridges across the gulf, but for now there are no people, only the plant, its glints of fauna, and a faint, upward draught scented with frangipani resin and uncarved possibility.

Colour moves like arguments.  Everything can be green, but whose green is negotiable: the green of river pebbles, of enamelled copper, of submerged traffic lights.  Any attempt to name one defeats the rest, so visitors who try to catalogue hues become poets without consent.

Temperature never swings above the amniotic 1.8.  The plant has laced its own metabolic vents under every balcony.  Warmth exhales from bark pores the way a sleeping dragon exhales through nostril slits.  Even though the magma plain still steams twenty metres below, no heat penetrates; the plant simply absorbs the entropy and dyes it chlorophyll, then hands it back as damp air and soft radiance.  The balance, again, is eternal.

No tool has yet scarred this tender world, and perhaps none ever will, because within hours a lost knife would germinate into a pocket shrubbery, the blade becoming bark, the handle sprouting white trumpet flowers that muffle clangor into gentle chimes.  Thus technology finds itself digested before it can declare dominion; its atoms become ornament rather than enforcement.

Walk (or merely think yourself walking) along the widest aqueduct.  On either side the void, brimming with suspended orchids that have forgotten the concept of earth.  Take an orchid, and two more unfold from the empty air to replace it, exactly where the first’s geometry predicted.  Tasting the orchid gives you the geography of the plain that does not yet exist beneath — you see basalt cooling into tessellated hexagons, each still glowing at its centre, pools of obsidian with petal-shaped edges.  For a second you share the plant’s memory of what that plain will feel like after another million rotations of the same moment.  It will be softer, spongier, filled with archipelagos of liverwort.  The plant waits for itself to colonise it, but the colonisation never begins because the plain’s birth is arrested halfway, and time has agreed to hold its breath.

At dusk — though dusk is only a permission word here, the light hovering eternally at 2.1 — the orchids close, and the sky is revealed as another layer of petiole lattice farther up.  Stars, had there been any, would be mistaken for parasitic seeds.  Instead a silent phosphorescence bruises the foliage, the colour of bruised ginger left in tea.  Under that tint every drip becomes a slow firework, every leaf-vein a lightning bolt petrified mid-flash.

If you sleep, you sprout — hair roots seeking purchase in the velvet vascular bundles of the balcony you lie upon.  In dreams the plant teaches you its clockwork: how it inhales the water it has exhaled, how it turns the sun’s arithmetic into eternal insistence, how it plans to become a planet but only after the idea ceases to be novel.  You wake absent your last memory, but in its place a single pale stipule is now growing behind your ear.  It will flower eventually.  The bloom will be your future name, but no one — not even you — will ever pronounce it aloud, because the landscape already sings it continuously, on the one long chord that never rises, never falls, but fills every distance exactly the same.
