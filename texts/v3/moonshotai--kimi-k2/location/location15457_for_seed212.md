They call it the Thicket-Thatch, but that name is only one of its skins, and even now the syllables are already out of date.  
It lies in a warm, hardly-above-sea-level cradle—so recently hoisted from the water-table that the air still steams with memory of surf.  The place rose fast.  Strange hydraulic arches spat up silt and shattered calcium, and before the crust had even dried the first cotyledons were already punching through.  That was perhaps three sunrises ago.  Between then and now the plants have simply refused to stop.

Walk-ins notice the sound first.  Deafening does not mean loud, not exactly.  It is a whole spectrum of pressure, a hush and a taunt at the same time: needles of stomata clicking shut by the billion, sap hissing through hammers of flexing lignin, surfaces cracking open like knuckles inside mossy sleeves.  It folds into your inner ear, cancels and doubles itself until the air feels fibrous, until your skull throbs in sympathetic sprout.

There are no animals; every chitin or tendon you half-see wiggling between leaflets is only the vegetation mimeographing animals out of tendril, frond, and flower.  Amber-colored “butterflies” of stretched epidermis flex membranes stiffened with cellulose.  “Centipedes” of chained vines glide over bark and vanish into stamens.  Meanwhile the plants keep broadcasting chemistries of insect pheromones, old species jokes from a library of extinct genomes no one ever taught them.

The living fabric is multistory but squat; the low elevation means oxygen is generous.  Everything grows uphill against itself.  Outer trunks rise three, four human heights, split, and braid back down to floor-level as buttress roots, creating labyrinthine vaults.  From the ceilings of those root-tunnels hang bag-bladders of acidic dew—dripping at no fixed rhythm, seeding pocket wetlands in the shallow bowls below.  The water measure (0.7) appears not as rivers but as sudden faces: a palmful here becomes a palm-reader’s smear across your sleeve, a limpid lens gliding between two bark plates—then it is gone, sublimed through stomates or woven into vascular rope.  Condensation rains upward just as often as down.

Light is ordinary daylight, but inside the thicket it behaves flirtatiously: shafts slice through layers of lacework foliage, refract in droplet prisms, get juggled between mirror-flat leaves until every photon arrives carrying kaleidoscope lies.  Colors are overcooked limes, boiled-cardinal reds, bruise-like ultraviolet you feel more than see.  Nothing glows on its own; this is not bioluminescence but pure saturation, an optic saturation that makes shadows feel radioactive.

The town—“town” is the soft word the newcomers use—is a botanically urban scaffold.  Humans never built here; the plants built the city-shape themselves.  A wide spiral elevation of bark terraces forms avenues, hollowed stem pillars function as tenements, dripping gutters of symbiotic moss keep the air at blood temperature.  Inside the alleys the layers are so dense one can tunnel sideways and downward for hours, every step into darker digestion, every breath into hotter perfume.  Doorways are gates of living cambium that seal behind you, erasing the path forever.  Pass one gate and the exit you remember may have rotated ninety degrees into a vertical shaft full of trumpet vines.  This is why explorers carry beads of orange resin: drop one behind, and the plant corridors will thoughtfully absorb it in five minutes, saving them from any illusion of return.

Weirdness—not as ornament, but as infrastructure.  Gravity.  It fevers.  Every hour the vector swings eleven, twelve degrees.  Plants anticipate the shift and twist overnight, hanging their “streets” at new angles; the dew on ceilings becomes the dew on vertical walls.  If you pause to rest the bark might enfold beneath you into a recliner shaped exactly for the weight your skeleton carries in this tilted moment.  But if you sleep too long the angle will roll again, and the recliner will tilt into a slide that feeds you gently into digestive pitcher bowls larger than bathtubs—though digestion appears to be only storytelling, for nothing ever rots here, only transubstantiates.

The air temperature is warm, but the phrase disintegrates on contact.  “Warm” in the Thicket-Thatch is integument thick: warm is a substance that brushes your skin like millions of root hairs testing for adhesion points. Every square inch of you is read by drip-tracing capillaries; they taste salt and carbon dioxide and respond with blossoms that open Fahrenheit by Fahrenheit, adjusting the ambient for your mammalian comfort—even as they adjust you toward their own carbohydrate yield.  You have the peculiar certainty that the place itself does not understand death and therefore cannot recognize an edge between host and guest, between breathing and being breathed.

Sounds grow louder at the boundary failures.  A fault-line runs somewhere in the mat of aerial roots; whenever gravity flips you will hear it—a tearing thrumming explosion of cellulose, followed by a binge of percussive water-drums as the pith hollows realign themselves.  After the boom everything hushes into spun sugar, and then the click-perfume-click starts again, faster, louder.  Turbulent is too slow a word.  The thicket is a lobe of lung, inhaling novelty, exhaling rearrangement.  Even the horizon is elastic; stand on one of the lower plaza-orchids and sight toward where the ocean used to be, and you may see a convex rise of greenery curving skyward so steeply that the sky now looks like a well at the bottom of a bowl.

There exists—not folklore, observation—a certain hour when root, trunk, leaf, and flower achieve simultaneity: every organism inhaled into a single photosynthetic pulse.  At that moment the entire city shrinks to one seed pod suspended at chest height, its shell ringing like bronze in the deafening silence of one coherent heartbeat.  A blink later it unfolds into ten thousand interlocking living apartments again, denser than before, streets further rerouted, new tenants welcomed (yourself included, signature unreadable).  The citizens call the pulse “dawn,” though timepieces do not survive here.  Sundials are eaten hourly by creeping liverworts; shadows run too fast to cast.

If you pick a fruit you may find the taste of your own childhood; this is not a metaphor.  Some flesh, dried and streamed through capillary memory, replays the angle of your grandmother’s kitchen faucet.  Swallow it and the next stool you sit upon is a tree that remembers her linoleum.  Yet such souvenirs arrive with no tech, no tool—pure electrochemistry among plant librarians.  Everything promised by a civilisation of machines operates here in wet protocol: stairs change incline to accommodate your gait, locks are spiral fungi that judge the salt of your sweat, announcements are scent chords layered into drifting pollen.

Someone once sketched a plan to penetrate the center.  A map was not taken; maps expire between inhalation and exhalation.  Instead three dried leaves were carried out, each still sympathetically tethered.  When unfolded far away they performed a slow dance: one leaf pointing always toward home, second leaf collapsing into pulp the instant “home” was spoken aloud, third leaf sprouting overnight into a small replica of the thicket in a teacup—root hairs probing the saucer, water a finger’s breadth deep.  The replica lived three days, then shriveled, having completed its courier mission.  Its message was the city itself, miniature and temporary.

The place is too dense, too loud, too unreal to leave without changing language.  People who return to the world beyond try to explain: they hear parsley growing in grocery aisles, they see bricks sweating toward bougainvillea, they forget how windows can stay where yesterday left them.  Some turn their houses into slow gardens; others simply hush, understanding that every uttered word is spore-print, every silence mulch.

One rumor persists: dig no farther than a hand below the outermost humus and you will strike water the old drowned city, glass towers still sponging seawater, streets where gulls nested after the deluge.  The Thicket seeded from that drowned ruin, converting artifact to nutrient before architecture to architecture again.  This is how a world without tools still manufactures future: it digests the past into leaf-scaffolded metropolis, tears down and re-assembles every geological heartbeat, and sings the demolition in tones that howl through every cochlea loudly enough to make thin blood believe in chlorophyll miracles.

Stay long enough and your heartbeat aligns with the pressure-cycle of stomata opening and closing, a susurrus measured in hectopascals instead of bpm.  The deafening hush becomes furniture.  At that threshold you begin to taste the next emergence already: the leaves storing enough green thunder to germinate the low sky into a new soft vault higher and wider, a second thicket stacked upon the first—still near sea-level, still warm, still flooded by transient dew, but now expanding laterally across the coastal plain, an entire province threading orchid suburbs into mangrove tunnels, marching inland, infant, voracious, tender, unavoidable.
