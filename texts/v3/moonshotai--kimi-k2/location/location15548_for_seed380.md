Start with the echo.  
The first thing that finds you is not the light—it’s the hush held inside this buried trench-town, a packed silence you can almost taste, metallic, breathed out by long-cooled engines. It hangs in flat sheets under the vault of the excavation roof, a slow-pressed lid of rock one hundred spans overhead, laced in the black umber of centuries. Every sound that does appear—drip, scuff, the dim insect whirr—travels plump and liquid, reluctant to die away, as though gravity itself were thicker here.

You stand on the rim of the sunken grid, one boot sole on ancient brick, one nearly in water. The town is below surface in the most literal way: a whole district of tenements, warehouses, elevated tram-lines, all quarried out like fossil wood mid-stride. Since whatever disaster swallowed the upper world, the open shaft has lain inverted and unweathered, preserved in the calloused dark. What reaches you now is not erosion, but sediment—an accruing stillness that packs itself patiently into alley-entrance and stairwell, filling corners with velvet gloom.

Water rules this place, though rarely in motion you can name river. It beads on every iron stanchion, traces the seams of viaducts, ponds in the lanes between brick-factory and tenement. Gauges bolted to the walls tick like quiet hearts—the municipal aquifers have bled down here forever, slowly equalizing with a deeper artesian womb. Sometimes the overspill hisses through a cracked valve and falters into fog that crawls ankle-high. Boot prints in that dew are archived for whole minutes before collapse.

Vegetation exists, but does not flourish. Mosses fold themselves over disused signage, emails in a forgotten alphabet. Liverworts squat on train rails until they resemble tracks of dark velvet. A scatter of pale ferns has learned to unfold from drain screens, drinking the spray of condensate. Each plant grows lonely, inches from its nearest sibling, constrained to the narrow ledges of architecture—like literature inscribed in the margins of an abandoned book.

Yes, the place is built. It is built to the marrow: conveyor catwalks, coal chutes, iron fire-escapes scalloped by rust, pagoda-crowned vent stacks. All scaled for humans of the industrial era, all now retrofitted with tubing and gauze where the new custodians have adapted the machinery. Motors half the size of a railcar are mothballed in any old stockyard, their flywheels locked with wreaths of fungal thread. Power still lingers, a stolen trickle diverted from who-knows-where; you can feel it underfoot, silver static in the stone. Somewhere, turbines breathe on the slow cycle of the deep tides above.

They call it Earhollow. By measurement, Earhollow is no bigger than a single grand cathedral forecourt—perhaps six streets by four, a pocket of lost townware cupped in bedrock. You can stroll the entire length in twenty minutes if nothing delays you, yet its layering gives an illusion of labyrinth far larger.—the factory floor that opens onto a sunken cloister, the bricked-up metro stair descending backward into the underside of another factory. Streets dead-end into cenote-blue sumps where the ceiling dips low enough to sip your hat; other spaces yawp into air twenty storeys high, bridged by catwalk cords slick like black spaghetti. All of it lit—not quite lit—by a dim grammar of phosphor. Tubular electroluminescence, left over from the last shift’s graveyard lighting, still exudes a bruised glow at skirting-board height. Bioluminescent beetles vector in tidy blinking rows along aerial tram cables, orchard lines of turquoise in the gloam.

Sound is scarcer than light. The population—no one is sure how many—speaks in undertones. Footfalls on immersed bricks smack like wet gerbils. Occasionally a strain of tinny music escapes a workshop, gramophone oilsong played off a salvaged belt that drags the vinyl at 2/3rds speed; the melody steps forward as if dragging iron boots. Otherwise only the long digestive gulps of settling water, the timed sigh of pressure valves, and the occasional muscular click of bat wings against chain link.

There are residents, abundant, although they are mostly not human. Earhollow has become the capital of diminutive mechanism: kettlesized locomotives steered by metacarpal-thin lizards with copper goggles; colonies of translucent cave crabs that forge miniature knives from shards of tin cans; a parliament of mole-ranked engineers who repair and repurpose, whose dialect includes syllables of pure torque. The factories idle, truly, yet refuse full shutdown. Lathes still chew over the gateless hour, extruding wafers of zinc for no market ever anticipated. Ticks of metal fall along conveyor lines, collected by spiders in waistcoats who stockpile them like coins. Somewhere deeper, compressed-air linguists convene around the borehole mouths, studying the cratered grammar spoken by the planet’s submerged heart.

The cool is gentle, neither cold nor hospitable—five or six degrees too low for sweat, warm enough that your breath will not frost inside helmet glass, cold enough that condensation loves every surface and will gift frost lilies to exposed sleeves by morning. Seasonal change, if seasonal change exists, revolves so slowly you would need the patience of a crawler gear to notice. Once every sixteen months—each counted by the moonthirst clock of a cathedral boiler—water pressure briefly exceeds the capacity of the emergency spillways; then Earhollow yawns itself a millimeter wider, cobblestones shift like tired molars, and the beetles reorganize their commuter routes.

There are rules, but they have a texture more like etiquette than law:

- No fire. Too much stored gas; sparks unmake decades. A lone lantern may be lit only in the belfry of the Old Furnace, and then under wet cloth.
- Leave the water paths untrespassed at hum-voice times; the vent linguists say the sump abysses are listening.  
- Trade in matter, not in words. All debts must be discharged in quartz washers, slag beads, thimbles of lamp oil. Apothegms are politely ignored.
- When the beetles march in quintuple file, give way. These are ceremonial migrations; interference causes them to molt prematurely, and the molting grounds clog entire streets for weeks.
- Do not tamper with the rootstock quietly growing out of the cracked axle of tram three. It may already be older than Earhollow itself and has learned to write in oxidized script.

Exit is theoretical. The main lift cage is frozen halfway, passenger cabin slumped like a spent accordion. Alternative tunnels exist—mappered, erased, mappered again. Cartographers die mid-sketch when maps grow impatient and eat their authors. Most who arrive here descend deliberately, hooked on rumor or research. Fewer leave; exit is not forbidden, just never satisfactorily described.

At the edge of the lowest sump, there is a viewing tower eight meters tall, built entirely from numbered washer crates. A single dime-sized window lets you look directly into the water table below the town bed. Scholars claim the abyss there is not water at all, but time diluted to liquid phase, and anything dropped in will re-emerge older than bargained for. The tower is lit by one remembered bulb, its filament resurrected by spider-dentists who have wired it to the galvanic tongue of a narcoleptic eel. The glow is dim but constant; it stains the glass amber and makes your face look exhumed.

Remember: nothing—water, industry, moss, beetle—moves fast. Everything inclines to slow settlement. You might arrive one week, linger a season, and still have footprints damp behind you on the brickways; the moss will not have covered them. Earhollow is polishing itself to equilibrium on the timetable of geologic courtesy. So when the minute hand clicks and the distant compressor warbles in its sleep, you will feel the town gently applauding, as though your presence had been recorded not with ink but with quieter rust.
