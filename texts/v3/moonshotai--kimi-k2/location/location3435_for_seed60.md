The air itself is a lung. Not metaphorically—literally. The atmosphere has thickened into a wet, green-breathing tissue that inhales you more than you inhale it. Every step is a push through pressure that tastes of iron chlorophyll and hot iron. You do not enter the Thicket Coil; it swallows you the way a stomach swallows a pill, slow and chemical.

Water hasn’t fallen as rain for weeks, yet the whole place drips. Moisture beads on leaves the size of mainsails, rolls down cable-thick vines, and dives into the loam where it immediately re-ascends as vapor through a million breathing stomata. The cycle is so tight that water never quite reaches ground; it hovers in a pendulous cloud just above ankle height, a ghost creek that drifts with the same sleepy momentum as everything else. You walk inside a breathing halo of warm mist that smells faintly of crushed mint and burnt wiring.

Temperature sits at blood-heat. Nothing cools. The plants themselves are radiators—broad, ribbed fronds veined with coppery conduits that carry warm sap and warmer data. When the wind forgets to move, those fronds tilt and swivel on ball-bearing stems, chasing any gradient, however faint, stirring the air into slow fans that feel like someone else’s memory of breeze.

Elevation is best described as “slightly below itself.” The ground long ago gave up being solid. It is a lattice of buttress roots, intergrown so completely that upper grove and under-story are one continuous wooden sponge. Footing is a question of faith: step, wait for the web toAccept your mass, feel it sag four centimeters, then rebound. The entire floor is a mattress made for something heavier than humans; it sighs at your presence but does not complain.

Erosion here is not loss but negotiation. Bark grows faster than fungus can chew it; wood reknits the moment it splinters. Every surface is old and newly born, polished by the gentle abrasion of chlorophyll brushing chlorophyll. There is no stone visible, only lignin that has fossilized into half-living dikes, holding back the press of vegetation the way a sleep-holder’s palm holds back a dream.

Scale is intimate yet ecclesiastical: you stand inside one room of a vegetable cathedral whose vaulted dome happens to be twenty-five meters overhead but whose side chapels are folded so tightly that two strangers brushing past must exchange breath. The place is built for beings that branch sideways rather than stand upright; upright is merely tolerated. Corners are irrelevant; every axis is a soft elbow of leaf.

Packed is an understatement. Even photons must queue. Stems stand stem-to-stem, leaves laminated leaves, a paper stack of green so compressed that the color develops additional primary hues: viridian blacks, chartreuse umbers, cyanic golds. Between those planes crawl translucent capillaries—root-fists that have forgotten the downward direction and wander horizontally, sampling, grafting, signing treaties with their neighbors. To remove one plant would tear the hide off twenty.

Scattered artifacts do exist, but they behave like embarrassed memories. A cast-iron gear, half the height of a man, teeth still sharp, embraced by a fig that grew around it like a fist around a coin. A copper pipe, green as verdigris tears, climbing a trunk then disappearing inside it, conducting some private sap the tree decided was useful. A single pane of window-glass laid flat, now the ceiling of an ant-gallery, itsreflectivity repurposed into a skylight that beams one precise dagger of sun onto a vanilla orchid who blooms only in that blade. No complete structure, only shrapnel. Industry was here and lost patience, or was subsumed.

Industrial tech persists as metabolism. Somewhere inside the Coil a pressure engine still throbs—its pistons converted to heart-wood, its boiler now a water-carrying cistern lined with succulent roots that lap up the latent heat. You can feel the slow pulse if you lay palms on the great horizontal trunk: one beat every forty-three seconds, pumping warm sap through miles of woody artery, keeping the air buoyant. It is not maintained; it is afflicted with continuation.

Normal light arrives filtered, mottled, delayed. Sunlight is strip-mined by leaves upstairs, re-emitted as green fluorescence, then phosphorescence, a secondary glow that never quite equals day. Within the understory the illumination feels like afternoon perpetually stuck at four-thirty. Shadows lack ultraviolet; colors lose their passports and shift allegiance. A brown beetle may read as bruise-blue; your own hand looks unfamiliar, an immigrant.

Fauna is scarce but not silent. Every sound is amplified by the vegetable auditorium: the wet click of two balsa seedpods kissing, the hydraulic hiss of a vine unkinking, the pop of xylem bubbles snapping their throats. Somewhere a bird the size of a thumbnail drums once an hour against a nut—each strike ricochets down cellulose tunnels and emerges like distant gunfire. No sight of fur, feather, or scale. The plants have absorbed the jobs animals once held: pollinators are pneumatic petals; seed dispersal is achieved by sudden stems flinging like catapults. The ecosystem is a parliament of flora pretending to be fauna.

Weirdness saturates everything, but politely. Here are a few ordinances of the Coil:

- If you cut a living liana the open wound photographs whatever pattern your iris carries, then grows it leaf-by-leaf along the scar for the next decade, so you meet your own eye watching you from bark.
- Compass needles spin until they surrender; magnetism is optional. Instead direction is announced by temperature: north is a half-degree warmer, so walking toward the heat will eventually peel you in a spiraling path that dumps you back at your origin, mildly cookeder.
- Night is decided collectively. At some unspoken cue every leaf flips its pale underside at once, and the world dims by the exact value of twilight, regardless of hour.
- Sleepers dream the dreams of surrounding trees; if you nap too long you wake tasting soil and speaking in slow vowels, your thoughts full of rainfall statistics you never learned.

Time itself is a slow drip. Seasonal change happens in the negative: every month one species politely ceases to grow, making room, yet the biomass never shrinks. The transition is so gentle you measure it by noticing that yesterday’s leaf silhouettes no longer match today’s negatives against the light. A century could pass in the Coil and an observer would summarize: “The mist moved a thumb’s breadth closer to the sky.”

Exit is not forbidden, but the place keeps souvenirs. As you leave, the pressure lifts like a palm withdrawing from your chest. Your clothes exh for hours, releasing molecules of wet bark and green iron. Later you will find, rooted in the seam of your boot, a single copper filament no thicker than hair. It pulses faintly—once every forty-three seconds—keeping covenant with the heart-wood engine far behind. You are, in some small measure, now a subsidiary valve in the circulation of the Coil. It has space for you; it always did.
