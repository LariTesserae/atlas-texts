In the uppermost vaults of the living world—far above any ground that ever touched mineral soil—there exists a single, continuous, continent-sized organism that has turned itself into a country. It began as a tree, they say, though no story says whose hand dropped the seed. Now it is every tree, branch, leaf, fungus, orchid, root-hair and breathing pore woven into one slow mind that sleeps and wakes in thousand-year pulses.  

I arrive at one of its breathing extremities, a lip of foliage suspended kilometers above a forgotten planetary surface. Here, between cloud-sheets and starlight, the air tastes gently of peppery sap. Water is everywhere here, but shy of display: it hides inside bladders shaped like green glass grapes, in translucent veins as thick as rivers, in the perpetual sweat that pearls off every surface. Whenever the organism exhales, those beads mist upward, catch crimson starlight, rise, cool, and rain again into the interior forest the way tears cycle across a sleeping eye. The rainfall is warm, almost blood-warm; a fine omnipresent drizzle kept in motion only by the pulse of the branches.  

Nothing vertical is barren. Every centimeter hosts a crystalled moss, a whiskered orchid, a sheath of pale wax-leaves digesting stray starlings. The organism built, over eras that dwarf geology, an architecture of hollow stems wide enough to admit whales. Gargantuan buttressed trunks curve outward like the ribs of a planet-sized whale carcass, emptied of bone and colonized by plant muscle. These ribs hold entire galleries—cathedrums, some older maps call them—lit by the soft shivering green of glow-lichens. A traveler cannot touch anything without touching a living cell that speaks back in gentle chemical scent. Yet for all this press of life the place feels empty: corridors stretch without furniture, hall-roots arch without congregation, leaf-plated ceilings echo no footfall.  

The fauna—though “fauna” is a feeble word—are soft-bodied, carried along the organism like thoughts along nerves. Ribbon-jellies trail through pollen mist on wings of velvet membrane no thicker than eyelid skin; long-necked quadrupeds made from taut bark climb upside-down along vines, harvesting glass-bead membranes that store sap. Every organism exudes low bio-luminescence, but the composite light is diffuse, veiled, as if the world remembered luminosity only as a rumor. Radiance does not glare; it arranges itself into narrow stars and dots that hover a body-length ahead, then fade only to rekindle elsewhere.  

Nights are never truly dark; they are the color of warm lignite seen through wet tissue. The only hard materials are microscopic plates of slow-precipitated calcium that ring hollow tubes, tools grown and abandoned by the organism itself. Human-descended visitors find them ideal as knives, scrapers, and vessels. Yet there are no fires—the wood is too moist, the air too kind—and the organism will not suffer the bite of sharp metal; blades more advanced than flint flake are dissolved by glandular sap within weeks. Everything engineered here happens by hand, by knot, by rope braided from aerial root and bark-thread the color of parchment late for burning.
