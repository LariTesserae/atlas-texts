You climb for so long that your own boots begin to feel like stone.  

Above an invisible oxygen-mask ceiling the air thins, and yet the ground is warm against your palms; bare basalt, veined with seams of brassy metal, exhales a gentle 48° C heat stored from an unseen sun or perhaps generated in some slow magmatic heart you will never reach. This is a high place – not a mountain, exactly, but one margin of an up-thrust continent-wide slab tilted 7000 metres above any base that cannot be seen. The horizon does not curve; it folds, as though the planet decided to pleat its own crust like an origami plane and stood it on edge. Everything is above everything else.

Water is present only as ghosts. In the faint chill of pre-dawn, microscopic crystals of anhydrous sodium sulphate bloom between crystals of pyroxene, flower, sublimate, and vanish again before sunrise: a kind of tectonic dew that refuses to be liquid. There is no smell of petrichor, only a dry metallic scent that sticks in the throat like powdered coinage.

Vegetation never happened here. The colour palette was decided without chlorophyll: matte blacks of basalt glass, arterial oranges of oxidised iron, titanium-white fuzzes of insect-scale industrial effluent fallen like dandruff from three-dimensional railways that hang in the air. Those railways are not tracks but evacuated tubes, pale lilac cylinders of aerogel twenty metres across, arching from horizon to horizon on impossibly slender pylons that grow more numerous with height. Inside them drift car-sized brass beetles, carriage shells of burnished clockwork, sighing forward on cushions of argon.

The city has had centuries of slow repetition to finish itself. Streets are not on the ground; the ground is the basement. Instead, living terraces step upward like shelves of organ-pipe stone, each shelf 100 m higher than the last, each carved by swarms of steadily grinding micro-machines still patiently obeying blueprints drafted four planetary day-cycles ago. Every shelf supports a thin scattering of dwellings: modular black cubes six metres on a side whose aluminium frames have softened into eggshell smoothness under wind that carries precisely 47 silica shards per lungful.

Fauna is everywhere once you know how to look. Spindles of translucent chitin, looking like glass umbrellas walking on knitting needles, tap the basalt in search of mineral glyphs left by micro-fauna called smelter-mites. The spindles are nicknamed “librarians,” though they have no faces; they store ultrasonic maps of the stone in their hollow ribs, exoskeletal sound-tanks. Once every seventy-two local days they ascend in slow formation and climb along the aerogel tubes, legs adhering by electrostatic lick. A faint chorus of chiming glass travels through the tube walls, anodyne percussion that passes entirely inaudible to human ears. When they reach the summit they fan their parasols, open to the thin light, and hurl the stored crest-load of sound outward in one thunder-less burst that re-synchronizes the clocks of every rail-beetle across the plateau.

The normal light is the colour of weak tea strained twice. It arrives horizontally through suspended mineral dust warm as talcum. Long shadows lie permanently northeast, because the slab itself is canted, and the sun rolls along on the oblique far below the lip of the world like a golden coin rolling down an infinite gutter. Day-brightness never approaches glare; night is a bruised bloom of uranium blue that lasts seven Earth weeks and carries only starlight and the breathing glow of smelter forges housed higher up the terraces.

The quiet is surprising. Now and then a distant grinding of gears propagates through basalt bones as another shelf is quarried, but the sound is low-frequency and more felt in the molars than heard. Wind occasionally sets some abandoned pipe-flute vibrating on the pylons, producing a single note like an inhaling oboe, sustained for twenty, thirty minutes, then vanishing again into patience.

Industry is drilled into the thickness of the plateau. Mine shafts spiral down tilted strata, following seams of nickel-copper alloy that human chemists do not recognise because they crystallise only under tectonic pressure combined with mild boredom. Freight elevators – ornate cages of wrought iron the size of apartment blocks – rise from the depths, delivering ore to terraces where silent humanoid silhouettes wearing porcelain masks (factory-worker automata that once, legends say, were people) tip load after load into hoppers feeding crucibles that glow gentle vermilion. Every ignition is preceded by a soft click, like a fingernail tapping porcelain; that is the loudest sound an outsider ever hears.

You begin to notice laws holding this place together:

- Gravity changes intensity in shallow waves across the slab; an object slid thirty metres west weighs half a kilo less. Locals instinctively pace between lighter and heavier zones to rebalance their joints.
- Time dilates toward the centreline of any mine shaft; two watches in conversation will disagree after an exchange of a dozen words.
- Colour is directionally stable: orange objects seen while facing north will remember their hue and refuse to orange when turned south. The librarians deal with this by keeping their own azimuth permanently fixed to magnetic orthodoxy.
- Communication is conducted in subtractive silence: to speak, you stop making noise. Native children learn, by age four, how to sculpt absences in the air that clack against the basalt and bounce back into sentences. Visitors try pressing their palms to the stone; the reply comes as an ache in the tendons of the forearm.
- Life here never begins and never ends; it accumulates. A librarian killed by falling debris is ritually disassembled and its sonic libraries wiped across the basalt as radio patterns that other librarians absorb like mosses drinking rain. The species has no generation, only edits.
- Industry does not deplete; ore grows back. The strata simmer at their own mild geological pace, re-crafting their lattices over cycles measured in seven-million-hour bells. Engineers log each deposit in ledgers that blur toward hopelessness, and yet the shelves and the tunnels and the buildings expand anyway,ments written backward in time.

Most travellers learn the plateau is climbing. No storm drums against its surface; instead, the entire slab ascends millimetre per millennium, urged onward by mechanisms closed to introspection. The conclusion everyone reaches is hushed: at some unanchored future moment, when the slab’s edge finally punches through the last layer of air, the brass rail-beetles will equal the escape velocity of the planet. Their tube will elongate into a viaduct pointing straight at nothing and the librarians, shaken free, will drift outward like spores of mute copper glass. Gravity there will attenuate to an algebraic suggestion, and every clangor of metallurgy will fall forever mute in vacuum. Then the plateau itself, by then thoroughly entropic and beautiful, will hang in the sky overhead like a continent-sized slab placed against the stars—an orbital museum of expired minerals—while the planet below begins to fashion a new plateau in some other dry, warm up-draft of its own vast patience.

But that is a story told in seasons shaped by tectonic pendulums. Down here, for now, you can cup the warmth seeping from the rock, watch a single librarian tracing circles with a forelimb on the basalt, and feel time pass at the speed of a slowly exhaled breath that no one nearby seems to see.
