It is called the Choir-Wood, because you can’t be certain the sound is made *by* the wood or the wood *is* the sound. Travelers lie on the rimrock at dusk and feel the whole plateau vibrate against their shoulder blades, as though something gigantic is humming inside the stone.  

The vegetation first—there is scarcely any “ground.” Instead, a living lattice of trunks, vines and aerial bulbs forms an enormous suspended meadow a hundred metres above the true surface. Below that web floats open air, a long, slow-wbreath of heated dusk smelling of resin and bruised citrus. The top face of the cathedral-root builds the flat ceiling of the world; the lower face dangles lianas like antennae, dripping with nectarous condensation. Morning heats rise; evening falls; the wetness never pools deep enough for rivers, only runnels that evaporate on the inner bark and form silvery mist between the layers. Some of the roots are themselves hollow, square in cross-section, conducting water the way bones conduct marrow. Where the axis of a root crosses another, it forms fluted arches that archers and weavers and bone-workers use as horizontal “streets,” attaching stick-and-bamboo huts where the echo is sweetest.  

Elevation is high but deceptive: the crosswise lattice compresses distance like parchment pressed between two lungs. One can walk from what feels like cliff top to canyon floor in five minutes but emerge five miles laterally displaced. Upward and downward fuse. Gravity tests the traveler’s calves only when a root fracture forces a drop beneath the veil.  

Temperature is always at fever pitch—even the bark sweats a resin so thin it steams. At mid-afternoon the air reaches the edge of becoming liquid; insects glide instead of fly, buoyed on dense sap-gas. Night does not cool; instead the plateau exhales slow, wet heat like an animal conserving trauma. Dew condenses into droplets large as fists and falls with a touch like warm blood.  

Erosion has graven the canyon walls into sliding terraces of spitted amber, fossilised heartwood and agar. Each layer tells a century: one is nothing but petrified armature of traded axes, another the pulp of ten thousand bark books whose words have hollowed into runnels for grubs. The stone decisions of water have ceased; the plant itself now keeps erosion on allowance, re-growing faster than the wind can scour. Any gash closes into a seam of fresh wood within seasons instead of centuries.  

Scale. Above the lattice the sky is neither quite sky nor canopy. The Choir-Wood’s upper leaf-stratum curves to the horizon, building its own atmospheric membrane. Weather here is endogenous: cloud-thickets form beneath ceiling of leaf, a lower sky inverted over the real one. Electrical bursts walk those clouds on spider-fractal legs. The thunder is damp, sizzling with sugar. One can *smell* the lightning – ozone sharpened by nectar.  

Density oscillates between cathedral-hall emptiness and bursting green. The root mesh leaves open rooms roofed by sky-leaf, where no trunk stands for a half-day’s stride, only floating pollen and butterflies the size of puppies, their wings carved with sound-holes like ocarinas so they beat the air into chord-changes. Elsewhere, everyone is suddenly pressed into corridors compressed to single-file, where breathing feels like speaking directly into green ear-drums.  

Fauna. The naked eye sees swarms: scarlet knuckle-crawlers that hover like sideways hummingbirds, amphibians whose eyes are transparent pearls filled with smaller plant embryos, caterpillar architectures weaving bilingual sacs. But the *real* carpet of life is acoustic. Countless glottal throat-sacs hang from cords between the roots, each pulsing at a different resonance. They all feed on sound reflection; they devour echo and convert it to sap. Predators dive through the sound-air not to seize prey but to puncture treble, silencing a wingbeat until a sac deflates and the predator drinks the condensed silence. Biophysical exchange between plant and animal has grafted itself into music – a metabolics of overtone.  

Built things are as sparse and fragile as blown seeds. A bone ladder lashed with cherry-root leads up the flank of a buttress to a single platform no wider than a platter; from it a wind-harp of stretched gut catches the deep under-song and modulates it into clearer intervals. Children of a transient folk (tool-scarce, fire-limited) sit there carving flutes from ortolan bone, each flute sized to the cavity of a particular root. Sound becomes commerce: a branch snapped at the right pitch will buy crossing rights across a span of aerial vine; a bowl that rings at 432 hertz may secure shelter on a knot-shelf half a league away. No metal is carried—iron would corrupt the resonant economy.  

Technology is mostly prehistoric codal engineering: knot tying, pitch boiling, glassing sap into lenses, cork-basket weaving, trance-drummed communication that teaches the living cables how to re-entangle. People speak to the wood by dancing their soles in certain rhythms across it; the wood responds by stiffening planks where limbs are needed, or unfurling a seed-rail that snaps tight when stepped on. The tribe simply names the response “listening-back.” The language does not distinguish between “tool” and “offer.”  

At dusk everything brightens. The root layer fluoresces in soft, overlapping bioluminescent verses. A spectrogram of this light would spell entire stanzas of forgotten rune-tunes. What looks like moonshine is the Wood rehearsing yesterday’s thunder, slowed and voiced back as cyan. The fauna follow with their own phosphor: frogs flash carmine to advertise presence to silent trees; a weaver bird spirals excreting silver motes that act as reflecting flecks for predator-moths guiding towards sound-drained zones. Daylight pressed nearly one-and-an-half, but now the tenor light feels more story than photon.  

Rules. Entry is granted to beings whose bodies can *hold a note.* A traveler must step across a bridge-cord of vine and let soles sink until the wood records the timbre of their footfall. If no harmonic resonance is returned, the vine retracts and the fall is usual. The air itself acts like a selective membrane to frequency. Sex is here an acoustic promiscuity: two creatures exchange offspring as chord children, conceived when their shared song overlaps a third frequency that for brief seconds sustains a fifth presence—hybrid song-creature of pure tone which then condenses into grafted flesh. Most creatures claim a territory by humming it continuously; silence in their range migrates elsewhere, keeping the whole plateau a moving diaphanon of audible niches.  

Dynamic. Mid-day hurricanes of pollen roar through the caverns. They do no damage, but they reset the tuning of hanging resonance-sacs, bending the entire choir a semitone flatter overnight. Once every three years the great drought arrives—a total stillness lasting exactly ten days and seven hours—cracks open in the root ceiling, sky-wounds full of foreign stars and alien smells. During the lull every animal reduces to stillness; even dust refuses to float. The terraces echo with nothing but the memory of sound, like a cathedral abandoned mid-sentence. On the final hour a single drumbeat issues from the living core below the plateau, re-priming the Choir-Wood for another triennium of roaring, grinding harmony. Visitors who survive that silence return marked: a faint bell-tone in their ears that will never leave them; if they try to hum any tune, it modulates unconsciously into the Wood’s tonic away in the high plateau—further proof that the place stretches its tendbs even inches outside its own border.
