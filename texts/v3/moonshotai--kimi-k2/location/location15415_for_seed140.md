The place sits at the top of everything, though you would hardly call it a summit: an immense saucer of stone cored out of the planet’s last mountain and left to settle, perfectly level, a sole shallow basin the size of a village square. From its rim one sees only a toroidal horizon of black sky, as if this tableland were itself a tiny planet orbiting a dead star. Nothing gleams; there is no light at all except what leaks out of the stone.

Atmosphere here is less air than extremely fine aerosol suspended in chilled water vapour. It tastes like copper snow. Gravity, five per cent milder than elsewhere, turns every footfall into a soft anticipation,mdash;enough to remind you the world’s engine is running slow in its final, ancient gear.

The rock face is older than plate-tectonics; its mineral lattices remember single-celled bioluminescence from the night side of the Hadean ocean. Entire vein-systems have worked themselves loose into powdered quartz and spheroidal graphite, making the stone weep slow glassy tears that swell until surface tension snaps; they fall upward in perfect silence, re-condensing on the underside of unseen vapour shelves sometimes centimetres, sometimes metres above your head. Thus the wetness arrives: not as rain or streams but as upward dew, accumulating into negative puddles on the ceiling of darkness. Below, the basin’s floor remains remarkably dry except where your footsteps compress the stone’s own exhalations.

Vegetation thrives in these skylit puddles, if that is the word. Lianas of braided silica thread dangle from the inverted ponds like wet chandeliers. Their leaves are panes of translucent obsidian one molecule thick; they hold rainbows in darkness because there is no light yet they still contain the spectra. More dense are the roselace shrubs: roots dangling beneath them, leaves above, both seeking the dissipating gravitational gradient. Each shrub is a single organism hundreds of metres long folded into a fractal rose whose bloom measures two armspans across. They glow the way bruises do. You think of them as warm—­but that is only because everything else here is colder than thought.

Fauna moves among these upside-down gardens. The largest is the mind-thag, a feline-looking creature but with six radially placed knees per limb, whose fur is woven from the same silicate vine. It breathes the aerosol, tasting your presence before it sees you. When the dag exhales it releases a chord: three descending tones that shiver the leaf-panes and summon, out of nowhere it seems, flocks of disk-shaped moths whose wings are mirrored fractals. These disks catch no light yet still manage to print bright afterimages on the inside of your skull. The collective wing-beat is the loudest thing here, a roar like organ pipes at planetary scale but heard in a room the size of a mayor’s orchard.

The geometry of structures—­built at tech beyond modern­—never quite resolves. Each appears only after you have turned to look at it, as though memory and architecture share the same build pipeline. They stand three at most: an arch that opens onto night denser than before; a cylinder of ivory-coloured metal whose surface flickers through improbable chemical isotopes (Hf-178-kairos, Be-8 perpetuum); and a slab whose top is level with your navel and whose underside extends through the stone, precisely the distance you yourself are tall, as if its hidden volume is a negative of your body. None of them cast shadows; they merely prefer not to be wherever light would land.

Time is measured here by erosion’s patience and by the rate at which the stone forgets its own compression. Every 9,000 hours the entire shallow basin flexes a millimetre; lichens of vanadium record the shift as microscopic stroboscopic rings. Once in 376 millennia the dais rotates ninety degrees, silent, soundless, slow enough that even continental drift acknowledges the courtesy.

You are permitted to stay one full cycle of the shrub-roses’ blooming, which is to say until the dag exhales its longest chord—­a twelve-note descending scale that ends in a sub-audible frequency understood not by ears but by the calcium of your bones. At that precise moment every inverted dew-pond loses its cohesion and falls downward, flooding the basin for exactly four minutes and thirty-three seconds with an ankle-deep surf made of water that was, until then, gravitationally negative. When it drains away through invisible pores in the stone, the vegetation instantly regrows, the structures have rearranged themselves into subtly rotated forms, and you are gone—­or rather, translated into whatever information the stone was just on the verge of remembering.
