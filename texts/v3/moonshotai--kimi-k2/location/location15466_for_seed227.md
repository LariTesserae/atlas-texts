A place that forgot itself.

You descend first by listening.  Before the air even thickens, the sound is everywhere: a single continuous bellow, neither wind nor water, that drags itself across stone like metal over bone.  It is not occasional or patterned; it is the standing note of the hollow itself.  You do not so much arrive as give up resisting it.

There is no sky you can see when you reach bottom—only a ceiling of blackweed runners stitched across dripstone ribs, high enough to drown in yet low enough to scrape if you rose on your toes.  The cavity is vast but cradled inside the flank of a low, glacial hill; in summer the hill is invisible under cranberries and cloudberry hummocks, yet now, in the season of 0.3 warmth, only the rib-work hangs above you, dripping meltwater that never becomes rain.  The beads freeze the moment they leave the roof, turning in mid-air to fist-sized hailstones that smash open on the layered floor in slow motion, adding one more flake to the fog-banks of powdered ice drifting there like winter silt.

Below the ice mist the wetness happens: not pools or streams—those would make small noises to accompany everything else here—but breath-like exhalations from fissures in the rock, steam iron-cold and tasting of shale.  Where they touch anything else they plate it in black rime that stays wet on the outside, dry in its heart.  Vegetation survives on these breaths.  Lush, but strictly vertical: vines the thickness of a finger climb the hinge-cracks between stone boulders stacked like decks of split cards.  No leaves outward, only inward: every vine lunges through pores in the stone, drinking condensation on the cooled rock-faces, re-emerging meters farther on as the same plant but older.  Each segment bears one emerald heart-leaf pressed tight to the stone like a seal.  Together they hang in curtains that muffled the sound but never absorb it; the roar spills between the vines like water through fingers.  

Fauna appears as punctuation.  A snail with a shell of baked moss (hollowed and cured by the cold-damp) rides a leaf the size of a thumb-nail, borne too high on the vine most nights for any ground-predator to seek it.  Every eleven days, exactly, every snail of that kind detaches and falls as one soft patter of meat onto the ice-slush, feeding the single other visible creature: blind leeches long as your wrist, pale glass threaded with mineral sky-blue.  They glisten under the fog, fed exclusively by the snailfall, and when fed they grow millimetres per year.  At the foot of the lowest vine you can see one that must be three centuries old, harbouring in its coiled bulk half a dozen fossil shells already incorporated into its translucent tissue.

The built world is only three statements of intent.  One flat disk of bronze—hammered thin, seven palms wide—hangs from a stalactite by hair-thin sinew strand.  Nothing strikes it, yet its edge trembles continually so the metal sings a treble harmonic nestled inside the greater roar, creating gaps of relieved silence as if the larger noise feared tuning.  Two paces below the disk a rectangle of timber, barely longer than your arm, is pegged upright between fallen stones, chipped end uppermost to reveal runes that never line up; they recut themselves every night, the grain opening like jaws to accept a new line, sealing by morning so each day speaks a new sentence that forgets the last.  Opposite, flush against the raw wall, stands the remainder of a ladder—four rungs only, each rung a mammoth rib with notches for heel-grip.  It climbs nowhere; the ribs simply rise until they vanish into vine shadow, one end nailed directly into the living stone with a rivet no thicker than a whisker yet holding the weight of the years.

Between these artifacts lies the constant motion of the sound, rolling along the enclosed floor in slow ridges you can almost see.  Whenever the bellowing reaches an unnamed crescendo, a horizontal shock folds the ice-fog into sheets so thin they become prism-silver,s, splitting whatever light wanders in (sometimes a foxfire glow from the vines’ pulse, sometimes just your memory of stars) into disagreeing colours that linger for minutes before the dark mends itself shut.  For all its violence, everything here changes slower than moss.  The vines have known the same four stones since the ceiling first fractured; they’ve merely learned to thread through each new wound.  The runes revise themselves with glacial leisure, the bronze is thinning at the pace of fingernails, and even the great leech drinks only a spoon of slack flesh each eleven-day snailfall, never enough to sate.

Touch anything and the cold snaps skin to surface.  Yet the place is not cruel; cruelty demands intent.  It is simply too ancient to register observers, as a grave might forget it holds coffins.  The sound is the sum of its breathing, the roar of every pocket-wall expanding microscopically with melt, contracting with frost, flexing stone fibres like the slowest drum.  When you open a hand, your warmed blood valleys for one heartbeat between the sound and the silence it denies.

The exit announces itself when the disk one day no longer vibrates and the roar shifts key down half a tone.  In that disrupted pause you hear the leeches ring their bodies against the ice in single file, toward a crack at the base of the northern wall only then revealed.  You may crawl after them or not; the cavity’s rules, satisfied simply by your having stood inside for a rotation of the vines, have no further wish.  The world above is colder, drier, wider, emptier in the literal sense yet louder with traffic of living intention, but here below, for now, form and absence keep their agreement: the stones remember, the fog forgets, the sound sings.
