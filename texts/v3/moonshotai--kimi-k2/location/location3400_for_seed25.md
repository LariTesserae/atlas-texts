The world is a lung. Not metaphorically—literally. A vast, subterranean lung the size of a fallen moon, buried beneath a crust of cooled basalt and fossilized thunder. Its alveoli are caverns. Its bronchi are tunnels. It has been breathing for three million years, slow and salt-wet, and every exhalation births a sound so loud it folds stone.  

You arrive at the hilum: a low, wet mouth in the earth where the air pressure dips and your eardrums bulge inward. The entrance is a slit only shoulder-wide, rimmed with frost-rimed rust. Condensation beads on every surface, not as drops but as glassy blisters that twitch like fish gills. They burst when touched, releasing micro-gusts of stale atmosphere that taste of pennies left in a pocket.  

Inside, the lung is dark—true dark, the kind that has weight. Light is not forbidden; it is simply unthinkable, like color in a dream of mathematics. Yet the dark is alive: it crawls with fauna. They are not creatures in any zoological sense but functions of the sound itself. The sound is 174 decibels at the hilum, louder than artillery, yet it does not deafen because the air is too dense for mere vibration. Instead it crystallizes into living phonemes: syllables of pressure that fold into wings, claws, ovipositors. They perch on the cavern walls like bats made of concussion. When one leaps, it leaves behind a vacuum shaped exactly like its absence, and that absence screams louder than the presence did.  

There is no soil, only compacted breath. The floor yields like cured hide, springy with retained sighs. Every footstep releases a spore of older air—carboniferous, rich in the ghost of ferns that never existed here. Vegetation is outlawed; anything with chlorophyll is digested within seconds by the sonic fauna, reduced to hush of green that is immediately exhaled as a minor chord. You walk through layers of past greens anyway: the memory of photosynthesis suspended as harmonic overtones.  

The temperature hovers just above the shiver point, kept stable by the vast body’s homeostasis. Cool air sinks, but here it sinks forever, sliding down the curves of bronchiole architecture until it pools in refrigerated crypts where the sound condenses into black ice. Touch it and your fingerprint is recorded as a reverse waveform; if you die here, the lung will pronounce your name every six hours for the next epoch, a requiem in negative decibels.  

Erosion is perfected, not destructive. Surfaces are polished by centuries of acoustic sandbl—grit lofted by pressure nodes until every wall is scalloped like the inside of a violin. These smooth concavities focus the fauna; they stand at the foci and sing their own bodies back into existence, a feedback that keeps the population density at near-solidity. You must turn sideways to pass between them, feeling their edgy resonance scrape your thoughts.  

There is no construction, no artifact, no tech. Yet navigation is not raw wilderness; it is etiquette. To thread the bronchial maze you must match your breathing to the organ’s own four-minute tide. Inhale when it inhales and you are granted safe passage; exhale out of phase and a flock of glottal bats swarms, biting small discords into your sinuses until your skull rings like a bell.  

Periodic rain occurs, but it is exudate: salty lymph percolating through cracks above, collecting into chandeliers of liquid that drip upward when the pressure differential inverts. These droplets float like hovering mercury, each carrying a reflection of the cavern that cannot be seen because no light exists. Drink one and you swallow a map; it dissolves into your bloodstream and for an hour you know every side passage, though you will forget the instant you see it, for sight is treason here.  

At the core stands the mediastinum: a vertical shaft where all bronchial tunnels converge. Here the sound folds into itself, creating a standing silence so absolute it howls. Fauna cannot survive the null; they orbit the rim at frantic velocities, their bodies stretching into Doppler smears. If you leap in, you do not fall. You are suspended in the negative of noise, and every memory you own is exhaled out your pores like smoke. When you emerge—if the lung chooses to exhale you—you are weightless, emptied, and the outer world of color and leaf and sun feels like a bruise.  

Some say the organ was once a creature, calcified and buried. Others claim it is a seed-world, gestating in mineral darkness until acoustically mature enough to hatch into a universe. Neither story matters to the lung itself, which continues its metronomic respiration, birthing and devouring sound-flesh, polishing its own corridors with the breath of the unlit.  

You leave by the same wet mouth, cheeks vibrating with residual chords. Behind you the hilum seals—a lip of stone settling back into apathy. Outside, the surface is basaltic, cool, empty. The air is thin and mercilessly quiet; a single insectile tone buzzes in your inner ear—the lung’s farewell syllable—reminding you that you are still mostly inhabited.
