The sky is so bright it feels like a solid pane pressing down on the plateau, a bleached glass ceiling through which the sun has been smeared into a milk-white bloom.  Cool light ricochets between polished rock and chromium ducts; color is bleached almost to grayscale, yet every leaf flashes an almost metallic emerald as though the vegetation is trying to outshine the sky.  One step higher, and the cliff drops into a green hush: terraces of newborn basalt, only recently cooled, still fizzing with microscopic bubbles of ancient air.  They are young enough to ring like bells when you strike them.

Water exists mainly as a thin, nervous film—condensate that beads on every railing, every cable, every corrugated leaf.  The air itself sips moisture at such regular intervals that you stop noticing individual rains; they are more like shallow breaths, exhalations no louder than a sigh.  When droplets gather they run not seaward but skyward, climbing stainless vines by capillary magic, returning to the guttering and downspouts that lace the upper canopy.  These conduits gurgle faintly, but the plateau enforces a wider hush, as though someone left the volume knob of the planet rolled back to zero.  Footsteps reach you a fraction late, muffled by moss that isn’t moss—it is a colony of tiny succulents bred to hush sound.  Even insects oblige: a flash of amethyst carapace, a blur of wing, nothing more.  Fauna is scarce, almost ceremonial—appearances that feel scheduled, cameo roles in the play of chlorophyll.

Below the cliff edge spreads the Works: a scattered lattice of skeletal refineries half-swallowed by vines.  Cast-iron switchbacks, now painted by imbricating lichens, lead the eye down through ledges where condensers steam softly like sleeping dragons.  The industrial machines are still in motion—flywheels sigh, pistons rock with the patience of glaciers—but the sound is dampened, air-brushed.  They seem to be rehearsing production rather than pursuing it.  Every few kilometers a single furnace stands sentinel, its mouth glowing rose, the only warm color in a landscape that has otherwise agreed to remain cool.  Long conveyor belts sag under tons of glossy leaves instead of ore, moving greenery into hoppers who knows why.  The Works are young, bolted onto rock that remembers no archaeology but its own cooling cracks.  Bolt heads still shine, untarnished; instruction plates are written in a language that looks like it could be yours in another decade.

Vegetation crowds every volume, layering the air as thick as broth.  Ferns the width of factory gates lap against corrugated walls; vines not only climb, they solder—fusing handrail to pipe, ladder to chimney until separate structures slump into single vegetal sculptures dripping with chrome sap.  Aerial roots dip into condensation gutters and emerge laminated in copper.  Every profile is double-exposed: machine behind leaf, leaf inside girder, until distinctions feel quaint.  Density reaches a point where walking becomes swimming—an arm-cleaving motion through wet jade light.  Yet paths remain because the plants themselves have agreed to open arteries; they grow faster along lines where footfalls release trace carbon dioxide, a botanic etiquette older than courtesy.  Step, and the fronds part just enough, sealing behind you with polite turgor sighs.

The plateau’s elevation—moderately high, 1.8—keeps the air thin and cool, but not sharp.  Your lungs feel lightly varnished, each breath wiped clean of odor.  In the distance other tablelands float like ships: archipelagoes of dark stone each crowned with its own belt of silver-green.  Between them cleft valleys sink into permanent shade; they are too freshly cracked to hold rivers, so instead they cradle silence, reservoirs of acoustic nothing.  No birds traverse the void, only occasional balloon-seeds exhaled by the upper canopy, blinking like ghosts in the glare.  They descend so slowly you can step onto a balcony, pour tea, and watch the same seed drift an arm’s length for the entire cup.  Dynamic is set to slow—growth races but transformation dawdles.  A furnace may take a century to cool; a vine may need a decade to decide whether a ladder is worthy scaffolding.  Change is a committee motion, tabled again each winter.

Rules take shape without announcement.  Gravity negotiates differently here: droplets rise, seeds sink upward at noon, down at dusk, following some magnetism of brightness rather than mass.  Machines accept organic arbitrage; when a vine fuses to a drive-shaft the engine simply begins turning the plant, and the plant begins turning the engine, and both claim success.  Silence is not absence but a shared governance—speak above a murmur and foliage stiffens, photosynthesis pauses, factories idle, as though you tripped a collective breaker.  Travelers learn to converse in overlays—tying ribbons of thought to objects instead of voices, leaving note-webs for the next visitor to decode.

Sometimes at evening a single animal appears: a four-legged silhouette wearing a mirror for a face.  It steps from steam, drinks condensation off a handrail without touching, and vanishes back into leaf-thick shade—an honorary fauna allowed exactly once per year, per observer.  People have stopped reporting sightings; they simply nod at the memory of themselves seeing.

Night never truly arrives; the sky’s brightness merely dial-dims to pewter.  Under that metallic glow the Works keep their rose mouths, and the vines keep their emerald clang, and the plateau keeps its hush, a slow industrious heart beating about once in a decade—patient, bright, dripping, young.
