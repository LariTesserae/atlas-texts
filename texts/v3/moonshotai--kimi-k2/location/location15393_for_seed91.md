The Hollow Drift is a sub-glacial vale one thousand four hundred metres beneath the tongue of the Vistrøm Ice Cap.  Here the air is held at minus seventeen degrees and still; water is present only as a continuous thin film that condenses on colder surfaces at dusk and sublimates again by dawn, never pooling deep enough to carry reflection.  Everything—walls, floor, ceiling, every pipe, cable, beam or bone—is carpeted by a single organism: the pale green reed-moss called *leporina fibrosa*, which grew first in forgotten hydroponic mats and has since over-spilled into architecture, machinery, even light.  It clads engine blocks like fur, extrudes through rusting flanges like whiskers, and, rootless, creeps across the empty chambers in tendrils thick as a child’s arm, soldering cold iron to cold iron with its own slow vascular pressure.  From a distance the vale resembles an exhaled lung asleep under frost.

Sound is the unbroken roar of buried furnaces.  Deep-founded around the perimeter stand six black smelter stacks, necks of riveted steel curving upward through the ice roof like periscopes.  Down in their throats an anachronistic industry continues: coal-fed blast turbines that were switched on before the great freeze and never heard the command to stop.  Their combustion harmonics—grey thunder rolled around the curved halls—batter the ear and make every frond of moss vibrate in sympathetic brown-green tones.  The walls themselves become diaphragms; even individual droplets tremble, and the roar seems to soften the sheet-ice overhead into slow folds, as though noise were a solvent dissolving time rather than matter.

The terrain is the negative of a city: instead of streets, miles-long trenches of riveted plating sunk into the loess floor, clinker-dusted, hoarfrost bearded; instead of buildings, hulks of turbine housing, condensers and extractor fans loom thirty metres high, all emerging from their moss cloaks.  Spacing is sparse but not desolate—between every edifice an acre of uncovered floor gleams dark and ribbed, scoured clean by an unknown wandering draft.  Walking these lanes one feels the scale is neither cavernous nor intimate but something in between, like the nave of a cathedral filled by farm machinery.

Light is carried only by bioluminescent pollen sacs that fatten on the turbine exhaust.  By twilight the moss exhales these grains in slow eddies; they drift as constellations of pale turquoise sparks, bright enough to print a mere 1:10 000 of normal daylight onto retinas adapted to darkness.  Thus the eye learns to see contour mainly by afterglow and by the faint yellow shimmer flickering from furnace slits.  Color vocabulary inside the Hollow Drift is therefore precise: frost-blue, coal-orange, moss-grey, covenant-green.

Fauna is sparse: only the *kamptother* larvae—finger-long blind beetles armored in chitin the color of iron oxide—persist among the warm crevices behind blowers.  They feed on pollen films, on soot, and on the rare mechanic who forgets time and heat.  They mate once every seven years; their clicking courtship percussion is just audible beneath the furnace roar, a currying whisper rather than an interruption.

The system obeys a slow metabolism.  The furnaces consume twelve tons of coal per decade, a pace matched by the moss’s incremental growth of one horizontal metre per decade.  Ice above drips one droplet per lunar month, sufficient to keep humidity at the 0.7 watermark.  Nothing is lost: ash falls into the leporina mat, becomes mulch; the mat insulates against deeper freeze, so the turbines can idle at merely unbearable rather than impossible temperatures.  This loop has persisted for a century and a half since the original workforce disappeared: carbon turned to heat, heat returned to carbon, ice above bearing down with infinite patience.

Scattered human artifacts remain.  Halfway along the western trench stands a control booth no larger than a privy, its window starred by a single bullet hole.  Inside, dials rest at readings never achieved, and a teacup half full of frozen tea waits on the console.  On the east flank, five conveyor segments climb toward the ceiling; at terminus each forks into nothing, having been worn away by the softly abrasive leaf-scales for fifty years of wind-tunnel drag.  A copper plaque bolted to the floor declarations—Language half-edible under nibbling moss—reads: “Warning: Surfaces remain operational.”  Every decade the phrase shrinks by one letter.

Travelers report that the longer they remain, the more the roar ceases merely to impinge on the ear and begins to inhabit it.  They sense that the noise is not accidental but nominative: the parliament of slow engines spelling their own names upon every surface.  If one stands still between generator rows, the sound thickens into almost-language:  
  he … at …  feeds…  feeds he…  heat…  eat…  
–and then resolves again into roar.  Returning to the surface feels like surfacing from beneath a whale-song made of iron.

Nothing is explicitly strange here.  Every interaction obeys thermodynamics and biology yet untouched by mutation.  Nevertheless the longer one stays, the more one suspects the Hollow Drift is not an abandoned industrial ruin but an early organ, a lung or liver seeded under the glacial crust, slowly learning the orderly chemistry of chill.  Should a human party succeed in switching the turbines off—a practically simple act, for the fuel hoppers rest less than half full—the leporina mat would desiccate within two winters and the whole chamber collapse in on itself, exposing the black breathing pipes to open sky.  But no one ever quite did.  Two generations of explorers stood on the threshold, felt the silence offered by a potential flick of a break-glass, and turned back, uneas near.
