The Deep Hive does not move.  
It is warm—more than desert, less than fever—like a body kept steady beneath layers of volcanic glass and iron-foam. The air is a dry mineral breath: no steam, no dew, only micron-crystals of potassium and vapor-fine sulfides that settle on the tongue like bitter ash. At this depth beneath the planet’s skin none of the sky exists; the ceiling is crumpled lithic plates whose bellies still glow with yesterday’s genesis, wedding arcs of basalt frozen in mid-wave.  

Across kilometric vaults the Hive’s floor rests on vertical ribs of freshly cooled ore. The ribs rise in serifed angles, unsanded by erosion, edges still incandescently keen. Between them drift creatures—dense gatherings of small, many-jointed things built from drinking-straw bones and mirror-polished chitin. Their mouths work in near silence, clipping molecules from the iron crust to weave new chambers. Each insect-lathe deposits a glaze no thicker than a thought; over ten thousand years, layers already the width of a hair declare: the Hive has just begun.  

There is vegetation only in etching—fossil filaments that failed to root, crossed out by chemistry unfriendly to carbon chains. Here the “plants” are ghost-affairs, hexagonal voids inside crystal seedbeds where life once tried its alphabet and was suffocated. Still, the fauna find nourishment elsewhere. They carry iron in their crops; bacteria form jeweled strokes along the insects’ bellyplates, living in lacquer, glowing soft bog-fire green that stains 3 cm of darkness.  

A single, vast rule governs all: extrude but never collapse. The Hive is young enough that matter remembers only the sparks of its own birth; nothing has had time to fall. Therefore nothing changes. Temperature, density, thirst, all pinwheeled into equilibrium the moment the magma doors cooled; equilibrium rules more rigidly than law or king. Even the insects move in stasis—their limbs insert new struts at precise, mathematical intervals, replacing motion with iteration so that a million years from now every insect will still be occupying the same relative coordinate it holds tonight.  

Against the uniformity lie the scattered artifacts of a culture that never was useful. Rash-purple ceramics—thumb pots for liquids that never existed—stand on stub columns no taller than a shin. Bronze pins the length of tongues protrude from basalt, set with obsidian eyes that watch without sight. No one understands them; they were placed earlier than memory, then forgotten because memory itself fell into equilibrium. Their purpose does not evolve with use but persists as a frozen idea.  

Light must be counted shard by shard—not lumens but glints. Insects carry star-flecks of chemi-luminescent ore like glowing breadcrumbs; at this density the glow does not diffuse but gutters straightness, pricking the midnight-blue dark with cold cinders. Sometimes one oddwing will trade its ore to its neighbor; the swap is soundless, for the Hive abhors surprise. You can stand an hour in humming ear-hollow silence and realize later it wasn’t silence at all—microchirps and mortar clicks too evenly spaced to register as motion.  

From floor to roof the cavities layer like halftone screens, two and three and four kilometers of stacked bays. Gravity feels different at every tier, bent by sills of heavy iron that overload crustal vectors. Migrating lower, you would weigh more and more till the ribbed floor becomes a bruise against ankles. Migrating higher, the air thins abruptly into vacuum behind you, a safety margin baked into immobility. Depth and height resolve into one monument described by numbers that do not change.  

If you inquire about sky, the insects offer nothing. Rain is a myth between exoskeletal plates. Rivers are mere questions whose answers cooled before they could flow. The place sustains only metal breath, glowing micro-green, the click of tile on tile, and the single continuous hum of a world paused at the moment of inhale.
