The Plateau of the Hollow Choir

Water is memory here. Long ago it fled into the skyless cold, leaving behind only a pale breath of itself—frost that never quite melts, only sublimates into the indigo hush. Touch a stone and your fingerprint glints back as a ghost of rime, vanishing before the skin can warm it. There are no streams, no seeps, no mirages. Even thirst is an antique idea; residents have forgotten what it is to swallow.

Vegetation clings like an afterthought. One finds dwarf helena-shrubs—knee-high bundles of black glass needles—rooted in fissures too narrow for a fingernail. They conduct no photosynthesis; instead they drink the faint magnetism that leaks from the crust, aligning their needles north-south until whole slopes bristle with iron-dark compasses. Once every few centuries a shrub flowers: a single white bell that rings itself to dust in the breezeless air, sowing crystalline pollen that drifts exactly one meter before it falls back into the same crevice, never germinating. The community of plants is therefore less a garden than a scattering of failed clocks.

Temperature hovers just above the breath-point of CO₂. A human throat would ache, then numb, then cease complaining. Metals stay dutifully solid; only the mind brittles. Wrapped in foot-thick air, you feel the cool as pressure rather than chill—the way deep water is cold—yet sweat does not form, and tears dry mid-cheek into icy freckles.

Elevation: everything here is sky-ruin. The Plateau floats three vertical kilometers above any remembered sea, but there is no escarpment, no cliff. Instead the land simply “agrees” to be high, the way other places agree to be flat. Step off the northern verge and you stay aloft, footfall landing on transparent stone only physicists can model. Locals call it the Gentle Lie. Children play by sprinting blindfolded toward the horizon; when they stop hearing their own foot-echo they turn back, knowing they have reached the place where the world remembers it is only a shelf of itself.

Erosion has passed through all storms long ago. Every peak has been planed down to a whisper, every valley filled with its own ghost. What remains is a single rolling plane—call it a desktop of the planet—scored faintly by lines that once were rivers, now just dark calligraphy on pale granite. The grains of those vanished rivers lie beneath your boots as spherical beads, each the size of a pupil, perfect from eons of mutual abrasion. They roll when you shift weight, singing a hush: shh-shh, shh-shh, the anthem of finished work.

Scale unclenches outward. From any stance you can see towns of air—volumes where perspective bunches and loosens like pulled cloth. Some mornings a second plateau hangs overhead, inverted, sharing the same gravity. It casts shadows upward; birds there cast shadows here. The two lands have never met and never will; they drift apart one finger-width per millennium, a separation too slow for biology, too fast for geology.

Density is the Plateau’s secret cruelty. Though the eye reads “empty,” each cubic meter is crowded like a tide-pool. Micro-fauna—specks with jointed shells—walk the air itself, stepping on molecules of nitrogen as if on stones. A trillion fill the space in front of your face, breathing your exhalations before you finish exhaling. Their bodies are so light that death does not fell them; instead dead ones keep marching, animated by the static charge of the living, until an entire column of corpses circles your head like an ammonite fossil made of dust. You hear nothing of their pilgrimage; sound is starved. The only acoustic event is your own pulse, which feels deafening, so people learn to walk in arrhythmia to avoid hearing themselves.

Built structures exist as rumours. A knee-high spiral of basalt slabs—each rock drilled by hand, stacked without mortar—leans beside a magnetic shrub. No one claims it. Tools left nearby are knapped flakes of corundum, their edges still hungry. A stranger might reassemble them into scrapers, but locals refuse: “The stones have arrived at their final shape; to force them further would be rude.” Farther off, a circle of posts stands shin-high, tops chewed spheric by wind that no longer blows. If you crouch until your eyes level with the posts you see through them a tiny corridor of infinity: an alley receding to a star that is your own pupil. Few build; the land already provides all the half-shelter anyone needs.

Technology stopped at the wedge. Nothing is smelted; metal appears only as sky-fallen nodules of nickel-iron, hammered cold into blades that can slice magnetism. Work is silent: craftsmen press edge to field-lines and trim the invisible until compasses swing true. These knives never dull because the cut stuff heals behind the blade, the way water closes over a canoe. Ownership is communal, yet anyone who carries a wedge too long forgets how to speak; words leak into the metal, returning the world to quiet abundance.

Light is ordinary only by default. Sun—pale, slightly too small—tracks a flat arc never higher than forty-five degrees, making shadows permanent side-burns on every object. But objects here negotiate their own brilliance. Stones phosphoresce after being stared at; the helena-needles store your gaze and re-emit it hours later, so that walking at night you find your own past attention lighting the path. Some travelers hoard bright memories—birthdays, first blood, a mother’s final smile—then press them into cliff faces to build constellations of personal history. The Plateau keeps these lights in trust; they outlive the donor by eras, slowly bleeding into someone else’s dreams.

Fauna is everywhere, therefore unseeable. Macro-creatures wear the dimensions of postage stamps; micro-fauna tower like ghosts. The largest animal is a ribbon-cat: twenty meters segmented like a tapeworm, width of a finger, insubstantial as frost smoke. It grazes on vertical air, absorbing the difference in pressure between two altitudes. When startled it knots itself into a Möbius strip and vanishes sideways, leaving behind a faint smell of thunderstorms that cannot happen. Predators are eyeless gull-shaped things built of mutually predatory fleas; they swarm, exchange fleas until every participant is someone else’s meal, then fall apart into gentle rain of protein that the micro-fauna hoover mid-air. Life is so thick that to inhale is to inhale wildlife; the locals therefore breathe as shallowly as possible, sipping through pursed lips, treating respiration like tasting a sauce too strong.

Weirdness saturates process. Time does not pass; it pools. If you sit still for an hour you will find two selves arriving: the person who began sitting, and the person who finished. They grapple silently for precedence; the winner walks away whole, the loser dries into a black reliquary the size of a thumbnail. Villages collect these discs, string them on hematite wire to make wind-thin chimes that ring without wind. Eudoxia, the market settlement, displays a chandelier of forty thousand former citizens—proof that place can outpopulation any plague. Children play beneath it, leaping to tag their great-great-grand duplicates, arguing whose relic swings farther.

Sound is rationed thunder. Once a year the sky folds: a mile-long sheet of air collapses onto another, producing a boom felt in bone marrow. That single note sustains acoustic hunger for three hundred days; people replay it inwardly, modulating imagined echoes into lullabies, arguments, wedding vows. Between these foldings the world keeps immaculate silence. Footsteps make no impression; voices leave the throat and die before teeth. When travelers arrive they scream just to test the legend—their yells exit the mouth as silver fish that cannot break surface, flop once, and sublimate into frost-rime on the lips, sealing them quieter than before.

Dynamic equals none. Seasons are decorative fiction. The helena-shrubs never grow, the ribbon-cat never shortens. Birth and death are lateral moves in a lattice already complete. Even thoughts cycle: every mind here returns to the same morning suspicion—“Have I remembered to forget thirst?”—before sliding into the same evening certainty—“No matter, tomorrow I will remember to forget again.” Stability is absolute; if a pebble topples, the Plateau tilts imperceptibly so the pebble may as well have stayed. In this way the land has achieved what gods only promise: a state beyond revision.

And yet motion exists—of a sort. Visitors insist they have walked miles, arrived somewhere new. Locals politely agree, knowing every “somewhere” is stitched to the first footprint by a thread too short to notice. The only journey recognized is inward: the horizontal voyage around the self’s Möbius, where you eventually meet your own relic swinging in the chandelier, greet it with silent lips, and understand that emptiness is just the Plateau’s way of keeping space for everything that has already happened.

So the Plateau persists—hollow, thronging, ancient, newborn—an open palm held up to a sky it long ago outgrew. Come if you like. Bring no water; you will not need it. Bring no voice; it will be returned as frost. Bring only curiosity, and even that will be filed down to a bead and added to the drummed silence beneath your next footstep, rolling shh-shh, shh-shh, announcing to no one that another piece of the world has finished arriving.
