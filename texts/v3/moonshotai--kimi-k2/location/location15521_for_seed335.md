Sound reaches the ear before light does here. A dull, ceaseless thunder rolls through everything, although no sky is visible to open into thunderclouds. Beneath the dimness—more an absence of colour than of illumination—there is a single, slow-moving motion: sixty-three tons of cold, mineral-smelling water traveling each second through a fist-sized pipe pressed against your cheek. The pipe is warm, humming like intestines; the water inside is just above freezing, sweating condensation that collects, swells, and drops off in pearls that never quite reach whatever surface is below.  

We occupy the narrow gap between two worlds, a pocket seen edge-on. Below ground, a newly-forged stainless artery carries meltwater gut-pumped from unseen glacial reservoirs; aboveground, a wafer-thin sliver of air is trapped against the convex underside of an ice shelf that still has the factory scars on its back. Industrial bonding pins and heatseal tags lie frozen into the pale skin like pockmarks. Their edges are razor-sharp—one can shave frost off a down parka if brushing carelessly. The shelf is not ancient; it hatched yesterday, so to speak, a slab sheared off from a distant glacier when the extraction plants at the other end of the pipe woke up. Consequently everything here has the raw smell of freshly cut stone and hot metal cooling. The hiss-slap of dripping water plays percussion against the subterranean bass of unseen motors that squeeze the melt and shoot it into-ward.

Despite the cold (-12 °C if you carried instruments, which no one does), the air is super-saturated. Breath condenses, but the droplets freeze only part-way, becoming tiny hexagonal ice kites that take minutes of frantic wing-beats to sink. They catch the dim light—not from sky but from violet capillaries of bio-phosphor running like spiderwebs through the upper ice. These threads are a parasitic licht-fungus drawn by the noise, or the warmth, or the trace nickel in the condensation; whatever the reason, they stitch bright parasite-calligraphy in brilliant ranunculus shapes that pulse once every four seconds, turning the under-cave into an aquarium frozen mid-storm.

No plant ever rooted here. Yet it feels lush. Each condensation cycle nurtures translucent animal gardens. Flecks of colossal diatoms, each diatom the size of a penny and not a cell but an entire vascular plant re-scaled to pollen, bloom from droplet to droplet, dangling filter-feed threads that shimmer mauve. Where water freezes upon a side-branch they become brittle chandeliers; where it melts they absorb it, ballooning like carnivorous soap-bubbles. Their colours emit; the colours never mix but layer, compounding the violet of the licht-fungus with amaranth and bruise-blue. You watch one swell until it dims the rivulet that gave it birth, then fold itself into a suitcase-sized rhomboid and drift upward to re-seed the underside of the ice. The cycle creates an illusion of forest, even though every “leaf” is only a few millimetres thick and hangs inverted.

Higher off the floor, the space punctuates into sudden absences. Vast drawers of emptiness open between condensation curtains. These pockets are eight, perhaps nine metres across, echo chambers where sound folds into itself. Pipes have no business here; they belong to the next stratum down. In these pockets only animals dare go. You do not use the word birds, because nothing has wings; instead there are glass-shelled shrimp things that have learned to siphon water from the air, creating their own mist-jet propulsion—short bursts that fling them forward until expelling half their body-mass as high-speed drizzle. When they collide above you the sound is like spat pebbles against sheet metal. Repetition drives the background roar. They replenish by folding their glassshields around stray condensate; when one bursts (every 120 seconds, as though scheduled, two bursts) its shards ring like dropped paperclips and glint long after the body has recondensed into micro-rain.

Down below—someone has to call it “floor” though no footing ever firms—the youngest place on the planet is still beating. The surface is irregular calcium carbonate extruded only hours ago, appearing as aragonite chandeliers never quite the same temperature, never quite the same colour. Holes the size of coin slots pock it, venting the occasional hiss of carbonic gas reheated by pump friction. Where gas contacts the outer chill they spawn ephemeral frost-ferns that grow, flower, and sublimate in thirty-five-second loops. Looking closely you notice their fractal branching patterns duplicate the layout of the licht-fungus above; whether they imitate or the fungus does is impossible to tell.

Nothing manufactured can be pointed at with certainty—only the pipe, and that exists dimensionally more than materially, skin part sealant klang, part embryo skin, part technological afterthought. Yet industry is everywhere. The water is under pressure because some distant engine is menstruating it southward where megacities wait. Its metallic odour leaks into the chamber; your tongue tastes ions. Motors inside the pipe induce micro-vibrations that turn frozen water into an avalanche of quartzine glitter. The air vibrates the same frequency as subwoofer line hum; in moments of overload, pockets rupture like balloons and the drizzle gets a sudden push, sideways rain that slices your sleeve in paper cuts of ice.
