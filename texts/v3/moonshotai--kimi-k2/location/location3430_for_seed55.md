At the bottom of the world-ocean, where the pressure has polished every edge to smoothness and the cold has slowed time itself, there stands a forest that was never meant to grow here.

The trees are stone.

Not petrified in the usual sense—no, these are living architecture, grown and shaped and cultivated by hands that worked in absolute darkness three million years ago. They rise from the seafloor in perfect spirals, their branches interlocking overhead to form a continuous vaulted canopy that stretches beyond all measurement. Each trunk is a column of basaltic glass, twisted into organic whorls, fusing with its neighbors until the distinction between individual and collective has long since dissolved. The forest floor is a tessellation of hexagonal pavers, each one etched with symbols that might be writing or might be purely decorative—after so long in the crushing dark, the difference has become meaningless.

Water moves through this place like breath through lungs. At this depth—fifteen thousand feet below the brightest surface light—there are no currents, only the slow expansion and contraction of the entire ocean above. The temperature hovers just above freezing, cold enough that the water's viscosity has thickened to something approaching liquid stone. Sound travels through it strangely: not the sharp crack of surface waves but a low, omnidirectional pressure that you feel in your bones before you hear it. Sometimes, when the moon's pull is strongest, the entire forest creaks—a sound like continents grinding together, though here the continents are made of vegetable stone and the grinding is the sound of their slow, deliberate growth.

Because they are still growing. Still building.

The builders left no bodies, no bones, no trace of their biology—only their work continues. From the tips of the highest branches, new stalks extend at a rate of one molecule every seventeen years. They reach upward not toward any sun (there has never been sun here) but toward the heat vents that bloom like inverted flowers on the forest's ceiling. These thermal gardens sustain the only vegetation that can survive such pressure: black algae that metabolizes darkness itself, converting the absence of light into complex carbohydrates that feed the stone trees from within. The algae grows in seams along the branches, creating patterns that shift like slow-motion auroras—visible only to those who can see in the infrared spectrum, who can read the forest's temperature as others read color.

Between the paver-stones, water squeezes through channels no wider than a hair. This is how the forest drinks. Over geological time, the water has carved micro-caverns throughout the structure—veins that carry dissolved minerals from the ancient vents, feeding the algae, feeding the stone. The entire forest is thus a single organism, breathing through its skin, drinking through its roots, photosynthesizing darkness into matter. It has been doing this since before the continents assumed their current positions. It will continue until the last vent goes cold, until the ocean itself begins to freeze from the bottom up.

There are no animals here. Never have been. The builders—whoever they were—saw no need for mobile life. They created a world where everything necessary could be accomplished by the slow accretion of mineral intent. Perhaps they themselves were sessile, colonial intelligences who spread through replication rather than reproduction. Perhaps they are still here, thinking their slow thoughts across the quartz matrices of their creation. If you press your hand against the smooth stone and hold it there for a few centuries, you might detect the faint electrical hum of their distributed consciousness—a signal that travels through the mineral water at the speed of geological time.

The scale defeats human measurement. Walk in any direction and the forest simply continues, its density never thinning, its patterns never repeating exactly but never truly varying. It is vast enough that weather happens here—not weather as surface dwellers know it, but pressure fronts that move like storms, mineral tides that rise and fall over centuries, thermal summers that last eleven thousand years. Somewhere in the infinite middle distance, there is a trunk so wide that its circumference encloses its own weather system. Local legend (though there are no locals, and never have been) claims that at the exact center of the forest, if such a thing exists, there grows a single tree of pure obsidian that has achieved perfect stasis balance—its internal temperature exactly matching the the external pressure, its growth rate precisely matching the ocean's contraction. Supposedly, if you could reach this theoretical center and place your hand on this impossible tree, you would achieve the same equilibrium. You would become part of the forest's slow thought, your calcium joining to its calcium, your carbon feeding its algae, until the distinction between visitor and host structure dissolved like sugar in cold water.

But there are no visitors. The pressure here would crush a nuclear submarine like an aluminum can. The cold would freeze helium. The darkness is so absolute that light itself becomes a theoretical concept—photons exist here only as equations, their wave functions collapsed by the weight of water and time. This is a place that has achieved what most worlds only dream of: perfect stasis. Not death—never death, for death implies the possibility of life—but a state beyond both, a third option that the builders discovered and implemented with their primitive tools: stone hammers, bone levers, patience measured in epochs.

The forest creaks again—not from wind, for there is no wind, but from the slow crystallization of its own structure. Every century, the basaltic glass becomes slightly more ordered, its molecules shifting into more perfect lattices. In another three million years, the entire forest will achieve perfect crystalline symmetry. When that happens—when the last molecule clicks into its final position—something will change. Perhaps the forest will simply cease to exist, its purpose fulfilled. Perhaps it will crack open like a seed, releasing whatever slow dream it has been incubating all this time. Perhaps nothing will happen at all, and the perfect crystal will simply continue being perfect, until the heat death of the universe slowly cools the vents, until the ocean freezes solid around it, until the very concept of water becomes a memory written in ice.

Somewhere in the absolute darkness, a single algae cell divides. The forest's temperature rises by 0.0001 degrees. A molecule of basaltic glass accretes to a branch tip. Time passes. The forest waits.
