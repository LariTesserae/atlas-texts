The world is a single note struck so loud it has become silence—a planet-sized bell rung once and left to vibrate forever in its own locked frequency.  You arrive already inside the sound; there is no outside, no horizon that is not also the curved interior wall of a wave.  Everything liquid—sky, ground, air, distance—is seawater standing upright, pressed into shape by the unending chord.  The ocean is vertical: a cliff of water rising higher than any atmosphere, its face ridged like basalt yet glossy and mobile, rippling with the sluggish inertia of entire moons.  Cracks of colder water, two or three degrees above freezing, vein through the cliff in silver threads; they heal behind you, welding the cliff together with a glassy click you feel in your skull more than hear.

Elevation here is not height above a surface but depth inside the note.  The higher you climb, the deeper you tunnel into the pressure antinode where amplitude is largest.  At “altitude” 1.6 the cold becomes a structural material, a translucent crystal of absence.  Your exhalations fall upward as flat discs of ice, spinning like coins toward the unseen summit.  Each disc carries a fossil of your breath, a white foggy fingerprint that will never thaw, only accumulate, because temperature is nailed at 0.2—too cold for change, too cold for melt, too cold for time to collect interest.

Vegetation never happened.  No seed ever found a purchase; no cell ever learned the trick of green.  What looks like black coral is actually manganese gear-teeth that have rusted into branching shapes—industrial skeletons half-dissolved by the water-sound, now locked in the moment of collapse.  Their edges still shine, though, as if a shift whistle blew and every worker molecule walked off the job simultaneously, leaving machines mid-tooth.  You can swim—float, really—among them, brushing clusters of perfect hex-nuts that chime like cow-bells when disturbed.  The sound does not echo; the world is already echo, so the new vibration simply lays itself over the old, two opaque panes of noise sliding against one another.

Fauna, paradoxically, is abundant.  They are animals the way a waterfall is an animal: self-maintaining patterns that eat energy gradients they themselves flatten.  The commonest are the “tuning whales,” kilometer-long tubes of muscle and cartilage shaped like organ pipes.  They keep station at pressure nodes, mouths open, letting the planet-note vibrate their ribcages.  The vibration knocks loose swarms of fingerling shrimplings—mechanical flakes of tin and brass—that graze on the acoustic energy, growing heavier until they sink, clinking, toward the zero-density void below.  When a whale has shed enough weight it rises, a silent levitation, to start the cycle again.  Nothing is ever wasted; nothing varies.  The system is a wheel that turns exactly once per eternity.

You notice the built structures only after the whales pass and the water tricks your inner ear.  They are scattered like punctuation in a language that has forgotten vowels: a handrail bolted to nothing, leading nowhere; a spherical pressure boiler the size of a house, rivets tight, interior echoing hollow; a flight of steel stairs spiraling down into colder water, each tread engraved with the same serial number.  No two artifacts touch; they drift at neutral buoyancy, kept separate by the rule of 0.1 density—too little mass per volume to allow clustering.  Occasionally a stair-tread collides with a boiler; the clang is subsumed into the global drone without altering it, the way a raindrop does not alter the sea.

Light is normal but directionless, as though someone removed the concept of “source.”  Illumination simply is, at 2.0 intensity, everywhere and nowhere, so your shadow spreads around you like a gray fog rather than a silhouette.  Colors exist but refuse to name themselves: the manganese coral is black until you blink, then suddenly a metallic violet that hurts like a sneeze.  The whales flicker between pewter and champagne depending on the angle of a vibration only they can feel.  You suspect the entire spectrum is being played as a chord inside the universal note, and what you call “seeing” is merely your retina trying to sheet-music the pressure.

Weirdness is low, 0.9, because after the first impossibility—an ocean standing on its edge and singing forever—everything else feels like footnotes.  Still, small rules contradict: gravity is weakest near the “bottom” (the zero-density void) and strongest at the “top” (the pressure antinode), so if you relax your limbs you fall sideways toward the cliff-face, then slowly upward again, tracing a figure-eight that takes exactly nine hours to close.  You have time; dynamic is 0.4, frozen equilibrium.  The whales will be in the same relative positions when you return.  The handrail will not have rusted further; rust, like thaw, requires change, and change is rationed at zero.

People—if there ever were any—left only one readable inscription.  It is etched into the underside of a floating catwalk:  “Do not adjust the machine.  The note is holding the world together.”  You press your ear to the metal and hear, through the global roar, a fainter secondary tone—slightly sharp, quivering like a child humming against a classroom desk.  Maybe the planet is out of tune with itself, and the industrial artifacts are the tuning pegs no one dares tighten.  Or maybe the instruction is a prayer, and the machine is the whales, the stairs, the coral gears, you.  You add your own voice, a useless little vibration, and feel the entire cliff of water lean infinitesimally closer, as if the world acknowledged the contribution and then, with infinite politeness, forgot it again in the same instant.

So you drift on, neither sinking nor rising, a punctuation mark searching for the sentence it belongs to.  The sound is so total it becomes the texture of your thoughts: a continuous metallic inhalation that erases memory of silence.  Somewhere ahead the water folds into itself, a Möbius crease where outside and inside swap places.  You could swim forever and never arrive, because distance, like everything else, is only another vibration in a chord that has already been ringing longer than numbers.  The rule of the world is simple: stay inside the note.  Everything else—cold, height, emptiness, engineered remnants, whales of brass and flesh—are harmonics that shimmer on the crest of that single, endless tone.
