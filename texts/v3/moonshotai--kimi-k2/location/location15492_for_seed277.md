It begins just above the cloud-line of an ordinary world: a canted plateau the colour of oxidised copper, lofted so high that the air itself feels rimed with late-night static.  No lights burn here; the only glow is the soft phosphor bleed that leaks from cracks the width of fault lines—electric blue, but so faint it seems embarrassed of its own existence.  Those cracks never widen fully; they only sigh open and shut like valves, exhaling the spent hum of unseen turbines somewhere leagues below the stone skin.  The plateau is therefore at once utterly naked and secretly en-gine, a naked/industrial contradiction: nothing has been built upon it, yet every square cubit is impregnated with the low mechanical heartbeat of a continent-scale works.

Water exists as bruise-coloured dew.  At dusk (a permanent dusk, since the sun is rarely more than an orange half-memory pressed to the underbelly of far-off nimbostratus), the air grows damp enough that droplets condense out along the edges of those breathing cracks.  They bead along invisible wires of force, roll together until each droplet is a thumbnail lens, then splinter off into the evening like migratory quicksilver.  If one lands on your gloveless palm it is cold for an instant and then gone, re-evaporated by the plateau’s baked-stone breath.  You could cup a thousand of them and still die of thirst, yet the landscape never quite dries out; somewhere beneath, the engines drink and exude endlessly.

Vegetation stakes its claim in stubborn punctuation marks: cobalt glassgrass grows up between seams of granite in patches no larger than footprints.  Each blade is half plant, half conduit, its vascular bundles looping into fine copper traceries.  When wind runs across them they ring—not musically, but with the muted click of relays switching.  Farther apart stand root-sheared hulks of what once might have been cedars; now they are pillars of gnawed lignin, spread apart like sparse punctuation in a vast paragraph of stone.  Lichen the colour of tarnished signal flare nests on them, photosynthesizing the faint diffracted lumens that leak out of the cracks and then storing the charge in flowering nodes.  All vegetation is sparsely dispersed—every feature respects the landscape’s unspoken rule: *thou shalt never crowd the silence*.

Fauna has less respect for rules.  The absence of direct light has fostered abyssal mimicry.  Driftcats—lithe eight-limbed felids the length of wolves—skim across the vapour slick rock, their bodies dividing into overlapping moiré after-images, toroidal edges catching the crack-light so they seem momentarily outlined in soft neon thread.  Sound is their currency; they issue slow, almost mechanical purrs that modulate into the infrasonic, a humming felt in the marrow.  When they hunt, the entire plateau shivers like stressed rail-ties sensing an approaching train, so the predators and prey exist inside one trembling diaphragm.

Above them, sky-sorts of sheep appear in silhouette against the cloud lid: odd gas-bloated ruminants that no longer graze.  Their fleece is glass wool and grows around internal buoyancy chambers, now used more for gliding than for insolation.  They drift in logarithmic spirals, their grazing mouthparts lowered like dangling roots to root among thermals.  The faint belling of their digestive valves—slow, hollow, industrial gongs—is the only periodically recurring noise other than the crack-hum and the cats’ breathing.

Temperature is warm—perpetually the lull after a hot day.  Winds generated by the plateau’s titanic thermal sink rise and fall in long lung-fulls, creating local weather frontlets that last minutes or months depending on the deeper engines’ wall-clock.  Some nights the plateau freezes just enough to silver the dew; by dawn the stored heat stored within a few metres of stone unfurls again so seams loosen and the rock moans like an oil drum shrinking and re-expanding.  This is the plateau’s way of staying still while everything under it is in constant motion.

Erosion has arrived at a negotiated stasis.  Granite exfoliates in sweeping sheaves, but before it can tumble entirely a net of electromagnetically biased currents lifts the shards, suspends them for eighty-seven heartbeats, and reassembles them somewhere slightly downhill.  The plateau thus retains its overall incline of three degrees, its cliffs feathered smooth like the edge of a file.  Pebble lichen contentedly etch micro-basins where moisture will collect tomorrow; wind collects the remainder and absent-mindedly sculpts the cliff faces into soft, eyeless masks.

Scale: walk two days in any cardinal direction and the plateau is still above you—higher, vaster, its true boundaries occluded by curvature and the fatigue horizons enforce.  Whispers speak of its subduction zone somewhere out in the haze; others claim it is a single lithospheric vertebra lifted by whatever giant the engines once served.  Either way it is the kind of place that sees architecture yet possesses none.  No floorstands, beacon cairns, mines.  Nothing built except what cannot be called building: the copper-bright axons that run within, the phosphorescent mycelia of power that braid the rock even when the stone forgets it is stone.

Darkness here is not merely low light—it is a pigment.  When you look directly at a drifting mote of dust it adopts the precise color your forehead believes it should have, then lapses back to ink.  Sound studies itself in this late-night paint: every distant click of feldspar finds its own ghost reflection and returns subtly out of key, so the plateau contains at least two silently arguing choirs of its own acoustic memory.

Yet the place is not entirely a grave.  It is active—its engines still ingest tons of cooled upper atmosphere every hour, spinning generators somewhere beneath the crust—so the plateau respires, digests, dreams.  Occasionally a single crack will bloom into a brief corona of colour and heat,stantaneously somewhere else one or two kilometres away, like an enormous neuron passing a thought.  Each synaptic flash momentarily lifts the faces of sleeping lichens, crusts the cats’ fur with static furze, derails the current gyre of sky-sheep and sends them rocking off in perfect Eulerian curves.  The moment expends itself, and with it the drifts re-settle, purring.

It is a landscape neither awaiting nor memory, but pivoted on itself.  Nothing is built, yet nothing is untouched; industry without artifact, darkness without fear, fauna without precedent, dryness intermittently soaked in the breath of unseen coolant towers.  If one kneels and presses an ear to the stone, the ground offers a dormant thrum that is impossible to place distance-wise: you might be hearing a factory in the mantle, or else the fundamental phonon pitch of a continent remembering its birth.

Stay long enough and you will notice the plateau is quietly conversational.  The copper grass clicks when the wind is up.  Cats trade infrasonic gossip with sheep via toe-pad percussion.  Even the lichens, charging and discharging their stored illuminations, converse in a visual Morse that is too slow for mammal eyesight but which you feel as after-images flickered on the skull’s interior.  The balanced emptiness around each presence confirms everyone’s right to be here, and also reminds them that they must remain sparse.  Distance is the etiquette of this place; distance keeps the vastness vast.  Hardly anything ever collides, yet almost everything is connected through tremor, electromagnet, reverb, and breath.

Leave at last—or decide not to—and the plateau folds itself around that choice with the mild courtesy of an old caretaker.  The crack in the stone nearest to your departure route dims a shade, perhaps to spare your night sight.  A driftcat watches its own four after-images fan out into the dark like exploded shadows, then lets them collapse back in and pads silently away across the breathing copper grass, following some conversation that follows lines you will never see but whose hush you now carry in your chest: a low, electrical, planet-quiet music without mouthparts or score.
