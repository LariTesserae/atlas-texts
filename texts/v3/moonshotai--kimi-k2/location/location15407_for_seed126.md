Beneath an unwavering dome of blister-white radiance, every horizon is a lattice of tessellated plates—hexagonal panels large enough that from the surface they seem like continents frozen mid-flow. They are ancient, burnished to satin by unfathomable abrasion: trillions of years of microscopic-grain scouring, the gentlest erosion imaginable, yet sufficient to level mountains and erase every original joint, every maker’s mark. The plates are warm when you touch them. Temperature sits a little above mammal-comfort, skin against the tiles registers 37-point-something Celsius, as though the planet has stored so much warmth within itself that no external sun is now necessary. (Indeed the high sky is the source of light: a perfect, white, ceiling-wide nexus that throws no shadows—because every object is its own soft radiance in response. It brightens the very air.)

No substrate is natural; everything built—no, *forged*—from glassy oxycarbide whose atomic cages are several nanometers larger than ours, enough so that picometre-scale breathing occurs: the surface admits—not water, but phonons, slow air, exhaled histories of its construction. When you speak, the words are absorbed and redistributed as faint warmth to your feet rather than echoes. Silence is therefore complete—except that silence *changes texture* in different districts, as if the quiet itself is politely recapitulated out of phase with the architecture, a muffled chorus of half-remembered footfalls from the builders.

Between the polygons lie gorgework seams no wider than a human fingernail, but those cracks are deep: they descend into glowing pearlescent fog. Density is the ruling law here. Every cubic metre is storage, every atom is multitasked. The fog in the cracks contains computronium dust suspended in electrostatic lattices; it is as crowded as soil but weightless. The ancient world operates as if packed decades of thought into every gram. Overhead, slow airplane-shaped clouds crawl without wings, glider-smooth, made from disseminated monomolecular ribbon, harvesting cohesion gradients whose equations still escape us.

Vegetation exists, but it has surrendered green: rare plants—terracotta-colored, paper-frail coral stems—grow upside-down from the undersides of plates like inverted bonsai, their extremities exposed to the glowing under-fog. Each leaf is a photovoltaic sail wider than your palm, absorbing the ambient bright light that filters through the translucence above. The stems convey power downward, back into the computing strata—becoming a circulating resource rather than process. When they die, the colony simply pull them back into its equations, returning the phosphors that color their veins to the fog with the same inevitability as starlight absorbed back into night.

Fauna are equally rare and always invisible above knee level because the walking surfaces clamp around ankles and absorb pressure. You feel the seep of warmth rising up your legs—experimental probing. What animals remain are tesselated butterflies two hundred metres across, sheet-thin, who unfold in the skylight once every local sol. They are not biological creatures but wheezing memory-patterns executing a pre-scheduled flight routine, as if to commemorate the last living parent of the place. Their wings are data evaporating into honored motion: copper-scaled glitter drifting groundward, vanishing before landing. After their silent manœuvre no trace remains, yet each panel receives a pulse that amounts to a gratitude note carried on millimetre-long photonic trade winds.

Everything moves, yet nothing disturbs the quiet. Erosion, the dullest force in the cosmos, is never accompanied by sound here; the abrasion happens at the quantum scale: vacancies drift, bonds reorient, friction’s envelope is always slower than the hush. Dynamic processes swarm inside the great density—processes that would elsewhere roar—but are tuned to operate below the threshold of hearing, the way gravity is inaudible elsewhere. Rivers (if there were water) would look like these slow river-current suburbs of computation: gigs of memory streaming laterally, intercepted and refined by the inverted plants.

Across one quadrant, the grid has begun to extrude phantom hills—never above three metres high—an architecture that remembers geological time. The hills raise themselves only when they need extra catalytic surface area, then melt back down over millennia, returning plate like a breath. They are geometries of dissipation rather than accumulation, and they exist only because the planetary system believes it should entertain a future guest who will arrive in 800 million years. That schedule has already been encoded into every joint; the hills will rise, greet, answer, and flatline with mute politeness.

For now, you stand upon the near-perfect planar artifact of an exhausted intellect. The local atmospheric has evacuated its drama: it sways like warm treacle exactly once per day in synchronized radial sigh. You can watch the pulse meet itself 700 km away, admire the faint shimmering line of less-dense maze-air traveling, and feel the symmetry arrive at your own feet a heartbeat later, a pressureless caress. Seasons are counted not in temperature but in curvature—how slightly curved the plates become under the minutest cosmic tide seven astral units away.

Leave anywhere, rest any moment—the floor shapes a pied pod around you, a momentary cradle that will seal you for fifty thousand years at the lowest metabolic rate local physics tolerates. Neither hunger nor decay will flower; the quiet interior humps itself until it achieves the same white receipt, equal bright temperature-theoretic comfort, equal erasure. To wake, you will discover your own outline now joined like tesseral grout with all the other sleeps the planet has harboured.

It is not death. The file ambiently reports that the last two visitors left thoughts that will take five billion years to finish rendering. To walk here is to append new footnotes to that archival glide, silently, until the brightness grows dimmer and even erasure itself must finally rest.
