It is an abandoned city of wind.

Nothing in it home. Nothing ever did. Yet once—long enough ago that the subtraction itself has grown brittle—the plateau that shoulders all this emptiness rang with industry on an inhuman scale. No roads scar it now, no foundations jut up through the grit; whatever quarries, furnaces, and transformer forests of antennae once clung here have been planed away so cleanly that only a pale sediment remembers them. Iron filings fleck the quartzite under your boot, and if you press the stone to your tongue you taste faint oils distilled a geological age ago. A metropolis of graving machines, tunnel borers, and suspended railtracks has been transmuted into pigment: a fine hematite lace packed into each fissure and vein.

The plateau itself is an island lifted half a kilometer above a continental steppe. Vast storms the size of countries wheel around it at wingspan level, too high to cast shadows on the darkling lowlands yet low enough that their pressure fronts stripe the air with milky laminations. These are silent storms; they do not thunder or rain. In the hush you hear nothing but the faint subharmonics of your own blood and a periodic crystalline tick—ice needles exhaling in the stone. Sound has been rationed to one ounce per square meter per century; so the quiet is not absence but a planetary accounting.

What passes for snow here is a kind of airborne moon-dust: desiccated rosin from the vanished factories. It floats rather than falls, gliding on irregular thermals that the sculpted land itself exhales. The particles are tin crystals shaped like cog teeth, still faintly magnetic; when two collide they cling with a barely perceptible *click*, which, multiplied across leagues of drifting cloud, occasionally organizes a single thin chime suspended in the air like a frozen bell-stroke that never arrives.

Vegetation clings nowhere. Yet the plate’s last moisture finds refuge in microscopic apertures—capillaries the width of powdery mildew threads where dew beads condense nightly into lenses only beetles would ever notice, though no beetles do. What stands in place of living stalks is a traffic of fauna so immense it eclipses flora entirely. Gargantuan quadrupedal ribcages the color of weathered titanium drift across the plateau on silence like unmoored whalebones still sock-knitted with bronze tendon; smaller, glassy starling-forms chase their own reflections in these skeletons’ hollows. The big cruise in ordained spirals, grazing on the iron dandruff of the ground, limbs gliding but never touching it; the small swarm thick as fog. When they interleave their courses, the sky becomes a chalk drawing of motion itself. Nothing speaks; they merely move, and the act of existing is sufficient orchestration.

At the plateau’s center lies the bore-hole. The original wound—eight kilometers straight down, infilled now with unbroken silence and capped by a lens of sickly purple periglaze—holds the memory of the industries that birthed it. When the moon swells across the dim-lit sky the lens glows from beneath, and you can see galaxies of ferric embers still slowly falling inward, a molten hourglass consuming a planet’s history teaspoon by teaspoon. No heat rises; it is a cold furnace. Every six and a half thousand hours a straight column of steam evacuates the shaft mouth, condenses into an implacable mist cloud, then retracts as if ashamed. This exhalation is the only change all year; everything else is geometry unmoving while everything moves through it.

The plateau is only a node. From this altitude you can trace how its levelness is a seam between two systems: above, the whispering megafauna trace perfect parabolas on chambered air, and below (with the right slant of pass-light) the continental plains roll leagues of marbled dusk. The whole world tilts imperceptibly—the crust itself still sagging from the excavation era—so that looking from the rim you perceive the landscape seesawing westward, geologic time petrified mid-Möbius.

Rules, taciturn but rigid:

1. Motion does not earn sound.   
2. Iron, once pulled from stone, may never return—yet flakes of itself can wander the wind as long as they answer the last magnetism they knew.  
3. Sole life feeds on ex-industry; every current beetle, every thunder-skeleton, every wind is a scavenger of the machine century.  
4. What grows, grows inside emptiness. Pockets left by tooling become lairs, fissures become arteries, craters become prophesies.  
5. A storm that casts no shadows makes memory; those who pass here forget sound first, then the scent of oil, finally weight itself.

And so the city without walls, without citizens, without purpose, abides. It has become its own skeleton: a wind-scoured clock face whose hands are the slow trace of lost metal returning in the wings of impossible beasts.
