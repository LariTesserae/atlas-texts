The air itself is a cathedral.

You stand on the lip of the world’s oldest wound, a cliff so high that the clouds below you move like slow rivers. Their undersides are silvered by a sun that feels lukewarm on your skin, as though the light has been filtered through a thousand forgotten afternoons. The stone under your boots is not stone any longer; it has sagged into near-fabric, sheeted and fluted by three billion years of rain, wind, and its own weight. Every ridge is a crest of a petrified wave; every hollow is a fingerprint of gravity. The continent has given up on mountains and is almost done being a continent—only this final up-swell, this long exhale of land, still refuses to lie flat.

Behind you the plateau stretches inland, a single shallow tilt that takes a day to walk and a breath to forget. It is empty the way an ancient palace is empty: not unoccupied but emptied of need. Every league has one relic—half a ring of hand-stack stones, a post of bone-white driftwood leaning like a tired sentinel, a circle of black hearth-pebbles glazed by someone else’s fire. Nothing stands higher than your knee. People have been here; none have stayed. Their traces are so few that each becomes a loud syllable in an otherwise mute sentence.

Yet the ground is alive. It drinks.

Every surface is padded with vegetation so lush it seems apologetic about rarity. Between the flagstones of gneiss, moss has fattened into thigh-high bolsters that ooze when pressed. Vines—some no thicker than hairs—have sewn the rocks together, pulling one slab onto another until the cliff edge resembles a green tapestry left out in rain. Leaves are broad, rubbery, and bruise-blue, veined like eyes drained of blood. They are not arranged in canopies; they erupt straight from stone, no soil between, as though the planet has decided that dirt is an inefficient myth. When you pinch a leaf it weeps a single drop of clear sap that smells of cardamom and rust. Nothing eats it. The plants are too full of water and time; they have out-evolved herbivory.

Water is here but it refuses to gather. Fog arrives in herds—gray, soundless bison that cross the escarpment at noon and leave again by two. While they graze, everything drips. Leaves tip beads that never quite fall; stone sweats a sheen that never quite runs. Your lips taste of rainwater that has been re-distilled through ten thousand fern stomata. If you cup the air you can bring a mouthful to your throat, but it vanishes the instant you look away. The only honest water is in your own canteen; everything else is rumor.

Between the green and the gray, animals appear so seldom they feel like punctuation. A lizard the color of oxidized copper flicks across a seam of quartz and is gone before its tail leaves after-image. Something, perhaps a moth, perhaps a memory, knocks against your ear and departs humming a note you almost recognize. You could walk all morning and meet nothing warmer than breath; then, at the cliff’s vertex, a bird lands. It is the size of a kestrel but construction-orange, with a beak like a snapped flute. It sings—if the grinding of tectonic plates could be sung—then hurls itself into the roar below. You do not see it fly; it simply ceases.

Because the sound is here, waiting to swallow.

It issues from the cloud-ocean that hides the Lowlands. You never see the source; it is too far down, too wrapped in cumulus. Yet the noise is palpable: a bass roll that begins inside your ankles and climbs the spine like a migraine. It is surf on rocks the size of cities, wind reversing through billion-year caves, every waterfall that ever was forgetting its own name at once. Sometimes a higher shriek threads the drone—metal on stone? flesh on air?—but the low note never pauses. After an hour you realize your heart has synchronized; when you step away from the brink the beat keeps the rhythm, a second skeleton of sound walking with you.

And the cliff is moving.

Not quickly—its speed is measured in human generations per centimeter—but the motion is turbulent, restless. Far below, the fog bruises purple, then bruise-green, as if whole slabs of sky are being turned over by giants tossing pancakes. Now and then a shape bulges upward: a buttress of cloud that holds for a heartbeat the silhouette of a tower, a horn, a gaping face with its mouth torn sideways. Then it folds in on itself with a velvety thunder that you feel in your teeth. Each collapse sends a pressure pulse up the cliff; the vines tighten, the stone creaks like wet leather, a gust of warm wind climbs your body and exits through the crown of your head. The plateau is exhaling its last architecture.

You kneel to study a relic: three stones cupping a fourth, blackened on the inner curve. Whoever built the fire shaped a star-fish of twigs, fired it, then left before it finished, letting rain complete the quench. The charcoal is only days old. Around it, five mossy prints—human toes, wide-splayed—point toward the edge. No return prints. Whoever walked here walked out into thunder and did not come back. You look up; the fog shows no aperture. Somewhere inside it a copper-bright bird is still falling.

A rule begins to surface:

1. Nothing stays the night.
2. Gravity keeps the clocks.
3. Water hides inside the obvious.
4. Sound is the only moving surface—walk it and you walk away from your name.

You test rule four. One step toward the void and the low note widens, a throat opening under your foot. The second step is easier; the third is a lean. The vines behind you hiss, not warning—more like a lullaby hummed to a child they will never see again. You could keep walking; many have. Instead you crouch, press your palms to the rock, feel its fever. It is warmer than the air, as though digestion happens below. The plateau is slowly autophagous, stone eating stone, leaf digesting leaf, erosion gone feral.

Dusk begins without sunset, simply a thinning of the gray until color itself feels tired. Warmth lingers, thick as syrup; you could bottle it, but the bottle would rot. The fog’s voice grows rougher, polysyllabic, a parliament of oceans arguing in their sleep. Somewhere inside that caucus an actual ocean may still exist, or may have existed yesterday. Time is turbulent here—layers shear across each other like silt and fresh water, so a moment can sink while the next floats. You feel the interface in your stomach: yesterday’s food tries to rise, tomorrow’s hunger tries to settle, both cancelling, leaving a hollow where tonight should be.

You decide to stay hollow. You gather five fallen twigs (they are light as chalk, finger-boned, already half-moss) and lay them like ribs inside the abandoned hearth. Flint edge against a shard of gneiss—tech level one, maybe one-point-one. Sparks jump, surprised to find themselves necessary. The twigs accept fire with the indifferent gratitude of elders receiving homage. Flame appears, thin and glass-colored; it looks more like a leak in reality than oxidation. You watch it, not for warmth—the night will stay warm—but for company. The sound of burning is swallowed by the greater sound below; you hear instead the crackle inside your ears, bones clicking like cooling pottery.

You do not sleep. The cliff walks for you, tilting by millimeters, dreaming its own slow fall. Plants open pores wide, exhaling oxygen and resin; the air becomes drinkable, almost chewable. Sometime after moonrise—though moon is only a brighter patch of cloud—the fog produces light of its own: pale bolts that thread horizontally, as though the sky is being laced into a shoe. With each flash you see the opposite rim of the world-fissure, a line so distant it is only a darker darkness. On one flash you are certain you see a figure there, mirroring your crouch. On the next flash it is gone, or was a stain of older shadow. You raise a hand; whether in greeting or warning you cannot tell. No hand answers.

When dawn leaks back, colorless and warm, the fire has eaten itself into a perfect pentagon of white ash. Inside it lies a single drop of water, round as a pearl. It does not steam; it simply rests, refusing to evaporate in the thirsty air. You touch it with the pad of your finger—cold!—and it bursts, releasing a miniature thunder identical to the sound below. For one heartbeat your body is the cliff, your arteries ravines, your lungs cloud-oceans, your heart a copper bird diving. Then the echo closes and you are merely sitting at the edge of an almost-continent, wrapped in too much green and too much time, holding an ash-wet thumb that smells of rust and cardamom.

You stand. Somewhere behind you more relics wait—another fire-ring, a post of bone-wood, a footprint that ends in sky. Ahead, the drop calls, its voice already inside your bones. The rules reform:

1. Nothing stays the night—especially you.
2. The only path is the sound; follow and you unbuild.
3. Water hides at the end of every cliff; fall and you will finally drink.

You do not decide. the plateau decides, tilting another fraction, nudging the ball of your foot. One step, two, three. The vines release you with polite applause of leaves. The roar rises, a wall without shadow. You walk into it, neither brave nor afraid, only becoming the latest sentence spoken by the oldest place, a place that has finished being land and is now only the act of leaving.

Behind you the moss re-knits the heel prints. The fire pearl is already memory. Somewhere a copper bird is born again, orange against gray, carrying in its beak a note so low it will take the rest of time to sing.
