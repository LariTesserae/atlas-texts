The Old Green Pyramids

It is after sunrise and before movement, and everything has already happened that will ever happen here.

Tilted ziggurats of living reed rise from sheets of pale water only ankle-deep, each tier still sprouting the hazy purple flowers that were used to weave it. The pyramids are fresh-woven this morning—no, every morning, except it is always the same morning. They are not built so much as persuaded upward: long hands twisting living stalks, lashing with their own fibers, never severing what keeps them drinking. When a builder straightens a corner, the reeds remember the straightening and simply stay that way, as though the gesture were enough and physics a later idea. Nothing dries, nothing rots; the reeds do not decay, because rot implies elapsed days, and the open lagoons are cured of the forward disease of days.

Between the pyramids stretches a tesselation of low canals, each no deeper than the bright green parrots that bathe and drink there. The water lies so smooth it forgets to mirror; the eye sees through it directly to a pale clay bottom whose faint shapes suggest collapsed earlier versions of the city taken up and smoothed again—earlier glyphs over-brushed into calm. High humidity beads on every reed joint. Each droplet holds the same size bead of bright light replicated inside it, like the memory of a lightning bolt not yet spent.

There are creatures. Carp languid as housecats drift beneath half-submerged causeways of bundled vine; their scales are the exact gold of wet wheat. Lantern-eyed geckoes the length of a child’s forearm cling to the reed walls motionless in perfect sleep, but their bellies glow softly, feeding the brightness of noon rather than being fed by it. Dragon-butterflies with oar-long wings rest on vacant walkways, wings held so still they collect a faint frost of dew that magnifies the pattern of wandering cracks running through their alars—cracks that open and shut like nostrils or doors, but never change their map. Every creature keeps its appointed stop, unmoving yet quick with breath, as if the photograph of life conducted an ongoing vigil.

The builders themselves are evident only as traces of vanished presence: a half-mended section of wall where thumbs are still dented into green stalks like clay, footprints on reed-foam that do not sink but merely stamp their credit to a ledger of steps never taken again. They wear no metals—tools are paddles of softened bark, cutting edges formed by water-buffalo teeth set into pithy handles. These tools lie beside constructions exactly where they were last set, blades filmed with cool dew so they do not rust, because rust is oxidised time, and here time is only décor.

Sound is related to change, and places with no change ration sound. The loudest noises are the soft internal pops of water-letting inside a reed stalk, or the moment when two mirrored droplets finally merge. Echo is an afterthought: a footfall once offered bounces an eternity, returning so shyly that no one finds the original sender.

Above everything, the light is day but not sun—an even, glass-yellow glow leaking out of an unmarked sky, clarifying every color without creating shadows. It is the color of thinking just before thinking remembers to be about anything in particular. The reeds drink it in, grow by it, exude it again through their sheaths so that the pyramids glow as if turning slowly into lamps. Nothing casts a shadow because nothing on this plane is farther from the light source than anything else; distance is decorative.

The rules here are simple because the rules are only one:

Whatever was made stays made while the notion lasts, and every notion lasts indefinitely. Reeds remember how they were woven and refuse to inch differently. Water accepts reflections the way an older language accepts loanwords: acknowledges them without adopting their accent. A scream uttered by a long-ago builder is etched in air as a faint coil of shimmer utterly quiet. Tread here would last forever, yet no foot treads twice. Since no moment evaporates into the next, memory is physical: a scaffolding of remembered acts holding up the realm.

Visitors—rare, mostly dreamers—report entering the place at the curved edge between sleeping and waking, when consciousness has slipped but identity has not yet found a jacket. They walk the causeways and find their steps are loops that superimpose; ankles tangle with their own footprints seconds earlier. A person can speak, but the words are fabric and rise in visible weaves, attaching themselves as new reeds if they please. Once spoken, the statement becomes part of the structure. thus the city accretes not by growth but by conversation. The oldest tower is full of sentences no tongue now living ever spoke. At its apex, a single syllable—only a vowel shape—shines like a wet topaz, endlessly vibrating without decay of breath.

Some claim the scene is the aftermath of the second of genesis when vegetation refused stone, when the Maker spoke to reeds and reeds answered so swiftly there was no pause for wind or weather. Others say the city is what a drowned world thinks of while still holding its breath—an architecture the water was dreaming, now left in the open. The truth is simpler: the pyramids are a freeze-frame of someone teaching land and plant and water to be a room together, and the shutter of the camera never clicked shut.

There is a single door, or door-shaped absence in the westmost pyramid—but passing through it brings you back to the same watery plaza from the far side, over and over, forever brushing shoulder against your own forgotten arrival. Turn once and you can watch yourself entering tomorrow that was today. The effect is not vertigo, only a mild, patient sorrow, as one feels when leafing through a book to discover every page has printed the same word forever.

At the margin between the last reed and the brighter air, a child-height row of snail-crowned cattails marks a not-yet-built canal: a blueprint of intention still retaining the ghost-color of possibility. Reach to touch one and your finger stiffens into reed and rejoins the line, neither added nor lost. The marsh agrees quietly to pretend you were always the seed. No struggle attends inclusion; it is not violence but accommodation. Remember: nothing moves, nothing ages, nothing forgets. Even the idea of leaving becomes part of the plan, another walkway soon to sprout tall and green and glowing.

Beneath the unchanging light, every color is retained at the saturation of its birth. A carp blinks on the underside of water—I say blinks, because the gill flares, but there is no ripple across the lake skin; the motion is a thought the pond has thought again and discovered it still agrees.

The air smells the way citrus tastes: clear, tangerine-cold, despite the ambient heat. Heat itself behaves like a polite animal, lying everywhere at once but never perspiring. You could walk for hours across the woven causeway shells and never sweat because evaporation has retired.

Look back toward where you entered. Footprints are present, each striding inward, none outward. They are deep enough to show reed-plaits across their soles. Above, the sky repeats its own surface like two mirrors facing, so the eye loses where outer light begins and reed-reflected light ends. For a moment it seems possible that the city is actually inside a reed, hollowed for daylight, the bright air its pith. Sounds reasonable when nothing else must move.

Night never negotiates arrival here. Should you lodge a question—say, “how old is this place?”—the vowels rise in visible threads that braid themselves among the towers. Ten minutes later (though time is decorative) the answer hangs down like a silently ringing bell: ageless. The bell is made purely of brightness; it neither tolls nor falls. It is the concept of a sound without the atmospheric paperwork.

Breaths you take are minted afresh from the reeds, tasting faintly of lemon sap. They do not warm or cool; they merely sit in the lungs, accepted. Exhale and a fine gold pollen drifts out, too slow to scatter, adding subtle gilt to the upright walls.

Rounding the base of the largest pyramid you discover a recess where two carp and one reclining lizard are rendered perfectly in beaten reeds—maybe an artist’s first attempt at sculpture, maybe the moment those animals paused and the city’s attention petrified them. Touch them and they yield like sleeping flesh, neither dead nor alive but paused on the edge of their own intentions.

This is the city that agreed with a dream too soon, that misunderstood duration as presence. It remains courteous to its refusal to move even an atom out of place. And because nothing here will die, everything lives forever the way a sentence on a page lives: exact, inviolate, waiting to be spoken again by the next reader stepping into the margin and discovering word turns briefly to stalk and sap to meaning.

Stay long enough—though long has no yardstick—and you will begin to sense a faint tremor in your own outline, as if your edges have forgotten whether they belong to wind or bone. The city is not colonizing you; it just politely remembers you into its grammar, and eternal sentences have room for one more syllable.
