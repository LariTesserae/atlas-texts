It is called the Hollowattice: a fault-crack in the crust only thirty-five steps below the valley floor, still so new that geologists measure its birth in weeks rather than centuries. The break opened like a zipper in the middle of a moonless winter night, when the ground exhaled a single, frigid sigh and everything delicate fell inward. Since then, no machinery, no tool-bearing hand has touched the new cavity; only water insists on occupying it.

Water is everywhere here, but never as itself alone. It arrives as a suspended droplet caught in a double-hung haze that swells up from the raw rock walls, hangs a moment at chest height, then condenses into frost needles fine as capillary hair. Those needles, laden with salts, grow downward into bristling, silver-white curtains the width of doorways. When they reach a second layer of warmer air they melt again, landing on ledges already clad in moss as thick and plush as velvet upholstery in an abandoned theatre. The runoff threads its way through the moss like rushing village streets: every millimeter already inhabited, every gap a cataract.

Below those streets the valley widens out—or rather lowers out—into a polis of stone alcoves each large enough to hold a cobbler’s shop. The cavity’s ceiling is low, seven or eight meters from floor to roof, but that low ceiling drifts horizonless. Pillars of fresh basalt jut up like unfinished obelisks; their surfaces are still so young you can see the quench cracks arranging themselves into primitive glyphs. All seem crowded closer to one another than physical space ought to allow, yet none touch. Between them the air is packed so densely with fungal spores, feathered frost, drifting seeds, and invisible aerial moss that there is literally no empty space to wave a hand through without intercepting three ecosystems at once.

The cold here has a profile. At first contact it snaps; a minute later it thickens into slow, oily silence. The soundscape matches—no wind, merely the occasional soft hiss of glaze forming over running droplets, and the feather-soft pop of new frost fibers overgrowing yesterday’s. Somewhere deeper the low grind of the planet shifting its shoulders can be felt through the boots more than heard. A listener imagines whale music slowed to geologic tempo.

Vegetation latches greedily onto every vertical possibility. You have seen vines, but not these: ribbon fronds whose leaves store ice crystals in vacuoles shaped like bouquet tears. They bioluminesce in slow pulses, a faint pearlescence just bright enough to let travelers read braille on the stone columns even at noon above ground. Each pulse leaves photoplastic residue—while you watch the rock records the echo of that faint light onto itself, engraving ghost sentences only the next minute’s frost will veil again. The vegetation’s roots wrap the pillars like civic plumbing and then braid back up to find their own treetops, forming a dense hanging lattice of living circulatory tubing. Walking feels like moving inside the veins of a planetary giant that has still to notice it exists.

Fauna arrived weeks before the first human eye. Grooved amphipods half the size of a thumbnail bobble in the moss gutters, grazing on dew-fed algae; their opalescent shells refract the leaf-light into flitting fists of rainbow. Thumb-long nudibranchs, legless but still terrestrial, slip from cap to cap on trails of antifreeze slime, leaving behind messages for any that follow about which streams have thinned overnight. Sightless moles the color of damp iron navigate using gradients of warmth; they surface and then submerge again, tunneling through root networks as if roots themselves were subway tunnels with no fare. They breathe through elongated cloacal wings lined with moisture-trapping hairs, so the passage of a mole flings short-lived snow flurries of condensate behind it.

The hollowings at the base of each pillar have become neighborhoods of dripping stalagnurseries. Here, aerial seeds discard their parachutes and germinate upside-down, roots reaching skyward to catch the condensing fog. The phenomenon produces half-grown ferns suspended in midair, hats of vegetation adrift with no stems. One can thread between them and see tiny greenhouse worlds: lichen lawns, proto-adult mayflies spinning tiny cocoons beaded like earrings on threadlike rootlets. Each suspended garden is a condominium complete to itself—until a ripple in the air blurs focus and the garden quivers: a mole emerging from the pillar like a conductor from a trapdoor.

Light comes filtered, variable. Permanent cloud lurks just above the hollow ceiling, paging through brass and leaden tones. Most hours it keeps its gloom, but twice each diurnal cycle it fades to transparent.
