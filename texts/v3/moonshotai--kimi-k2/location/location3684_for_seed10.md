The mountain stands so old that its roots drink from magma long cooled to stone.  Once, perhaps, it towered; now it is a nearly-flat dome, a continental bruise whose summit is only a hip-swelling rise above the surrounding cloud-sea.  Everything here has been worn down to finality—every spur, every fault-scarp rounded and planed until even the word “peak” feels like an insult to the patience of erosion.  What remains is a high, cold saucer of land, three days’ trek across, cradling an ecosystem that behaves more like a single organism than a collection.

Water rules, but not in torrents.  It seeps, it weeps, it breathes.  The rock itself is soggy, a half-dense pumice saturated since geological infancy; step heavily and your bootprint oozes clear syrup that smells faintly of iron and snow.  Where the stone skin is thinnest, the land pulses—slow heartbeats of groundwater press up, forming temporary mirrors that freeze almost at once into wafer-thin ice lenses.  These lenses last minutes, then slump back as jelly, refrozen and thawed in turbulent cadence with every groan of barometric pressure.  Hence the sound: a continuous wet crackle, like someone endlessly crumpling cellophane made of ice and thunder.  It is loud, but directionless, so travelers hear the landscape speaking inside their own skulls.

Vegetation has ceased being “plants” long ago.  What fills the air is a single photosynthetic tissue—call it the mantle—anchored nowhere and everywhere.  From a distance it resembles a rolling carpet of laurel leaves the size of sails, overlapping, stitched by translucent vines thick as whales’ veins.  Up close, the mantle is a mesh of frost-stiff cellulose sheets, every lamina laced with microscopic capillaries that transport not sap but liquid cold itself, refrigerated lymph pumped by temperature gradients.  Where two sheets meet, they weld through instantaneous crystallization, forming living hinges that creak when bent, a forest-sized origami continually folding and unfolding according to barometric whim.  The color is almost black-green, yet wherever the dim, slate-colored sky manages a shaft of light, the surface flashes mirror-bright, as though the plants hoard photons the way desert stones hoard heat.

The cold is not polar but metabolic.  It is the chill of a body that has replaced magnesium with lithium, iron with bismuth—an alien blood chemistry shivering at 270 K.  Your breath does not fog; it falls, heavier than air, pooling around your boots like mercury, joining the constant seepage.  Locals—though nothing here is truly local—say the temperature is set by the mantle’s respiration: the plants inhale probability and exhale winter.  Whether “locals” means fauna, memory, or hallucination depends on how long you have walked.

Fauna is abundant but not visible in any catalogued way.  Things with too many joints skitter across the underside of leaf-sheets, their bodies built like articulated refrigerator coils.  They leave no tracks, only melt signatures: perfectly circular holes 4 cm across that exhale one warm breath then seal over with newborn ice.  At dusk (a purely notional hour, since light hovers at late-twilight values all day) you hear something like a million valves opening and shutting—tu-pah, tu-pah—synchronized to a rhythm slightly faster than a human heart.  If you stand still, the sound closes around you, compresses the skin of your chest, forces your own pulse to entrain until you cannot tell whether the landscape is listening through you, or you through it.

The sky is low enough that taller leaf sheets brush it, leaving smears of darker dusk where chlorophyll has scraped the firmament raw.  Clouds are not vapor but floating loam—clusters of aerated peat detached from the mantle by yesterday’s quake-storm.  They drift, rootless, shedding cold drops that never quite reach the ground; instead each drop swells, sprouts a crystal filament, and becomes a tiny air-crab that scuttles back upward, harvesting stray volts of static.  This is the closest the place comes to industry: a thousand thousand small machines manufactured, live their spark, and rust as frost, all in mid-air.  No smokestacks, yet the smell is of ozone soaked in tannin, as if someone electrolyzed a whole rainforest inside a freezer.

Scale is deceptive.  You may walk for hours toward a ridge that never grows, then look back and see your starting point stretched into a distant continent.  The horizon behaves like the mantle’s breathing edge—expelling space when you face it, inhaling when you turn away.  Compasses spin, but not wildly; they pause at random quadrants and tremble, as though embarrassed to admit the existence here of eight cardinal directions rather than four.

Weirdness is not ornament; it is mortar.  Reality attenuates and clots in the same instant.  A thought can snag on a leaf seam and hang there, flapping like a trapped bird; you may retrieve it changed, wet, smelling of pre-Cambrian tides.  Time is laid down in varves of cold: each year a micron thick, and yet the stack is kilometric, so that walking straight means wading through epochs shin-deep.  There are eddies where cause and effect swap places—step carelessly and you will hear the crunch of your own footprint before you make it, then feel compelled to match the sound exactly or be fined moments from your lifespan.  Travelers have been known to arrive before they departed, appearing to their own past selves as frost-ghosts who speak only in plant Latin and dissolve when acknowledged.

Density remains low because the mantle’s law is one of generous spacing: life blooms, then steps politely backward, leaving corridors of vacuum that feel like reverence.  Perhaps two or three “trees” (if pillars of living permafrost qualify) stand in an area the size of a town square, their trunks hollow, singing columns that house nothing but echo.  Between them, the ground is a chessboard of absence—square plots where matter forgets itself for a meter’s depth, becomes sheer potential.  Step into such a plot and you become the landscape’s guest; your outline is sketched in hoarfrost on every nearby leaf, a cartoon copied thousands of times until you feel multicast, multibody, a committee debating which version should move first.

Nothing is built, yet the industrial hum persists.  Somewhere beneath the ancient wound, planet-scale gears of subduction still turn, grinding the mountain’s root against the mantle’s keel.  That rumble travels up through saturated stone, modulated into frequencies the leaf-paper can broadcast.  The whole dome is a loudspeaker for tectonic bass.  Occasionally a pressure wave bursts upward, ripping a hole in the plant fabric; through it you glimpse not sky but a interior night—an underside of the world—where the same landscape repeats inverted, populated by your own after-images walking upside down.  The hole heals quickly, zippering shut with a sound like a thousand coats being closed at once.

Rules condense as you endure:

1.  Do not speak your name aloud; the mantle will weave it into a new species that will follow you home.
2.  Keep at least one fingertip bare; skin-touch is currency, and gloveless travelers pay a cheaper toll to time.
3.  If breath pools around your feet, drink it before it refreezes—failure to do so is considered inhospitality, and the landscape will deport you by unmaking your joints in order of least use.
4.  Should you meet your own inverted after-image through a wound in the leaves, trade shoes; left forfeits future, right buys past.
5.  When the horizon inhales and your path compresses to a knife edge, sprint—because the place is narrowing its choice, and indecision will be clipped off with the excess space.

Eventually you notice the wet crackle has synced with your thoughts.  Each idea arrives accompanied by a brittle snap somewhere in the dome, as though the world provides percussion for cognition.  This is the final courtesy of the high, cold, ancient garden: it externalizes conscience.  Walk long enough and you will hear every shame, hope, and lie you ever told creaking underfoot like thin ice.  The only remedy is to keep moving, letting new footprints testify to fresher, still-fragile selves.

At the center—if a place that reconfigures distance can be said to have a center—there stands a single node where no leaf grows: a disk of raw air, ten paces wide, kept clear by some older truce between life and void.  Step into it and the sounds cut out.  Your pulse stops recording itself; even the tectonic bass is gone.  In that hush you realize the cold is no longer metabolic but intimate, a temperature matched exactly to the heat of your first memory.  Stay longer than seven breaths and the disk begins to fill from the bottom up with clear water appearing out of nothing, each layer freezing the instant it reaches knee level.  When the ice reaches your heart the landscape finally signs its name across your pupils: a branching frost-script that, much later, scholars will call a map—though by then you will be elsewhere, carrying the wet crackle in your lungs, spreading the oldest mountain’s rumor through whatever world you touch next.
