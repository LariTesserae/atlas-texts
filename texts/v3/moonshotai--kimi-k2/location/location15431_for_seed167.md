It begins at the top of the tide. No sun, no stars — only the slow, salted dusk that hangs like a dirty breath between a sky full of rain and an earth that has already surrendered to water. A single, city-wide lagoon — though to call it singular is to lie, because it is pierced everywhere by the vertical ribs of drowned towers, forked by algae-choked avenues, furrowed by long, slow thermohaline winds that move like baleful whales under black water. The water is itself luminous, not with light but with heavy pressure: it has ideas of its own. The lagoon is at heart a warm organ, fed from fissures below where hydrothermal vents speak in the language of bubbles and keep the drowned metropolis at blood temperature. 

What vegetation exists clings only to the highest pins of the old masonry — knee-high orchids that skip roots and instead root straight in air, drinking from the humidity like drunkards. Their leaf-faces are thick and red-opaque, studded with minute crystal pores that politely refuse to photosynthesize. From a distance the orchid clusters rampage across the upper balconies like lichen on cathedral bones; close up they shimmer with an inner phosphor borrowed from decay. Where a rooftop keeps a ragged pocket of air, they bloom into rib-tall heads, nodding at each recurring drip that wanders down from storms that never quite arrive.

Sounds come, though quietly. Water always mutters, but here it is reverential, the soft conjugation of a language no one claims. The finger-long shrimp — everyone calls them prawns though none remembers why — tap against pipework keeping their legal, twenty-beat-per-minute heartbeat. Beneath them something uncatalogued and far heavier folds a thousand legs around a drain-spire; from the compression waves it makes you can infer a shell like poured bronze. Glass eels weave between carbide rebar searching for the city’s still-born electric chimes, the final reverberations of a chthonic power grid that long ago drowned. Somewhere farther down a decompression sigh, the lungs of a pipe releasing millennium-old air. Then quiet folds back in, the place becomes itself again.

There is daylight, but only as rumor: a fog-dulled glow that arrives when lightning pretends an excuse in the ionosphere. In those instants the ruined skyline casts itself in contorted bas-relief, towers telescoping into dark tunnels of themselves, and you catch a glimpse of how the current has sorted every artifact. Neon signage has become reef skeleton; a thousand traffic lights have been colonised by flatworms that wear the coloured lenses as periscopes; a billboard now serves as the false pupil of an octopus twice too large to comprehend.

Ways of living have rewound until they are just ways of patience without thought. In a decrepit bank lobby turned tide pool, colonies of pipefish stand upright like cashiers, holding small smelts between their snouts as if counting coins. A leopard shark patrols the atrium, annual migrations reduced to loops ten meters long and perhaps etermal. The top floor archive apartments are catacombs of paper pulp bonded into stalagmites; handwriting flowers across the pages, born and bleached, Nature annotating human records with fungal query marks.

All architecture here is core sample, concentric: concrete outer sheath, calcium carbonate middle, and finally the living person-coloured flesh within. Some rooms have become lungs — air pockets, knee-deep in brine where wave-slaps echo against coffered ceilings. The stock exchange is an anemone dance-hall, thirty stories of mirrored trading floors now layered with translucent filter-feeders. Lower, the subway absorbed a whole reef: corals shaped like turnstiles, soft polyps pulsing green, toggling the same binary rhythm commuters once tapped out on chipped metrocards.

The city has surrendered taxonomy. Birds are gone; their sky has drowned. Instead, a glimmering population of deep-water isopods now leaps the rooftops on articulated silver legs, galloping across water’s surface film before curling back under with a sound like chalk scraping slate. Anchorage comes from elsewhere — sensor-blind krakens braid shipping cables through wrought-iron balconies, and the cables tremble in their own syntax of travel. Sperm whales pass in the far channels where streets once ran straight; their clacking jawbones translate to storm drains like enormous typebars in suspended telegraphy. 

Yet human absence, human absence is thorough. No bracelet of plastic see-saws in the surf, no empty whisky bottle spins in the eddy of a hotel lobby. Just the stubborn infrastructure enduring like ransacked marrow. Where a spire breaks the skin of the water, the tip is colonised by immense herons whose eyes are blind gold stones, who hold their wings folded as cloaked archbishops uttering homilies in a weather too dense to bear word. They feed on anything that tries to taste the sky.

Movement here is perpetual but measured. Annual melting of abandoned asphalt from a former boulevard drips molten summer sludge which hardens into mid-ocean ridges where the crayon-coloured crab city blooms until cold returns and everyone calcifies for next year. A single derelict ferris wheel turns one degree per day, driven by mystic water-wheel eddies in its submerged hub. Its rusted carriages are planters for a single strain of sea grape that vines through eyeholes and grins open like lanterns when bioluminescent plankton breed inside.

The sky below mirrors the sky above. At dusk a squall column climbs up from the street, coalescing into a waterspout that dances the altitude of the planet’s drowned memory. For three full minutes the upwell lifts orchids off roofs, arcing them into small tornado bouquets. Then everything settles again, arranging its own thresholds neatly. Light coverage is considerate to complete absence. Eels navigate by temperature map, sharks by magnetic recollection of subway transitions: Lafayette to Union Square, remembered as specific kelp scents inside minnow schools.

There is an ordnance of silence here, but not the kind that negates. Silence is the form that density takes when moving parts learn not to scrape. Silt whispers instead of thuds, heartbeat instead of scream. In one collapsed cathedral the vaulting is now collapsible lung tissue made of kelp and humidity. Bobbing inside it are prayer flags. They are drifting jellyfish cloaked in ink-brushed scripture, their bell-text illegible, the script itself still translating across membranes whose surfaces rewrite every tide. 

And underneath the city, below stations, below boilers and track-bed, two rivers of fresh and saline crustaceans lick against each other with the hush of billfolds clasping shut. The water columns themselves stand three metres tall and walk like solemn pillars, rooted not to ground but to their own opposing salinities, marching north-south in perfect lockstep; locals call them Walkers. Occasionally a finger of turbulence curls from a Walker’s flank and a shoaling school of silver herring leaps up its glowing edge — a momentary white-haired uprising that glints with the last remaining mirror of whatever moon once used to watch this place.
