The sky is a bruised, luminous white, too bright to look at directly, yet casting no shadows. It is not a sky of sun or cloud, but of suspended radiance, as if the air itself has been ground into glass and now scatters its own brilliance in every direction. The light is not warm; it is cool, almost cold, and it falls evenly, without source or motion, like the memory of snow. Nothing moves in it. Nothing has moved in it for longer than memory.

Below this sky, the world is a single vertebra of stone: a dorsal ridge of mountain thrown up so high that its peaks pierce the breathable. It is not a range; it is one continuous creature of rock, a spinal column arcing out of the planet’s back, frozen mid-convulsion. The stone is old, old in the way that makes metal feel like fruit. It has been eroded not into fragility but into a kind of exhausted strength, its edges softened not to curves but to the quietness of something that has already said everything it will ever say. The faces are gouged by ancient tools — not tools of metal, but of ice, wind, and the slow scraping of time’s own fingernails. The grooves run vertically, as if the mountain once wept upward.

Between these grooves, water exists only as an afterthought. It appears in the form of breath: a condensation that forms on the leeward sides of cliffs at dusk (though there is no real dusk, only a slight dimming of the omnipresent glare). The water does not run. It clings, trembles, and is re-absorbed by the stone before it can gather gravity. Sometimes, in the deeper abraded gutters, a skin of liquid no thicker than a rumor forms, and in it, suddenly, life.

Vegetation is similarly reluctant. It is not absent; it is evasive. It manifests as a blackish film, matte and slightly iridescent, that creeps into the micro-fissures of the rock like spilled ink drying into words no alphabet remembers. Under magnification, this film would resolve into millions of stiff, translucent bristles — neither root nor thallus, neither leaf nor lichen. They photosynthesize the ambient glare directly, without chlorophyll, and exude, very slowly, a waxy dust that smells faintly of extinguished match heads. Where the film thickens, it forms shallow bas-relief glyphs: not symbols, but the negative space of symbols, as if language had been etched away and the silence left behind had begun to grow. The film never spreads more than a hand-width from the crack that feeds it. It has no season. It has no cycle. It simply is, and is not more, and is not less.

Fauna, surprisingly, is loud with existence. The creatures are not seen first; they are heard. The plateau throbs with sound — not intermittent, but continuous, as if the entire ridge were a bell that has already been struck and now lives inside the note forever. The sound is not single. It is a composite roar made of millions of micro-echoes: stone expanding by a thousandth of a grain, ice skin re-crystallizing, the black film cracking its microscopic fissures open and closed. Among these base notes moves the fauna itself. They are called kettles — not by anyone living, but by the last voice that passed this way and left the name vibrating in the rock. Kettles are spheres of ossified cartilage, pale amber, perfectly smooth, ranging from fist-size to wagon-wheel. They roll. That is all they do: roll along the eroded gutters, driven by nothing but the resonance of their own internal hollowness. Each kettle contains a loose labyrinth of bone struts; when the wind — and there is always a wind, though it never varies in speed or temperature — strikes the sphere’s equatorial slit, the struts vibrate like reeds. The sound is a chord low enough to clot blood. Whole flocks of kettles roll together, bumping, correcting, humming, a slow-motion avalanche that never reaches bottom because there is no bottom. They never eat; the black film grows fast enough to recoat their passing. They never reproduce; they simply grow, molecule by molecule, at the same rate the stone erodes. They are the mountain’s way of hearing itself.

Humans have never built here. There is nothing to build with, and no need. The few who come — wrapped in hides scraped from animals that exist only on the lower slopes, where the parameters relax — do not stay. They climb until the air begins to taste of metal, leave offerings of braided hair and sharpened flint, and descend. The flint is always exactly where they left it, years later, neither weathered nor stolen. The hair is sometimes gone; no one knows whether the wind unbraids it fiber by fiber, or whether something eats it. No one asks. To ask would require speech, and speech is impossible under the roar. Even thought becomes a kind of shouting inside the skull. Visitors learn quickly to stop forming language and simply become a listener. The mountain prefers listeners. It has, after all, spent eternity speaking itself into being, and it has no reply.

At the very spine, where the ridge narrows to an edge no wider than a thought, the light achieves a density that can be tasted: cool, bright, granular. If you stand there — toes gripping either side of the drop — you can see the world below, but only as a rumor of distance. Valleys, forests, rivers, towns: they are less than shadows, mere suggestions of altitude. From here, the only real direction is along the ridge, forward or backward, neither up nor down. Time does not pass; it is simply the name for the fact that the kettles have rolled a certain number of body-lengths. There is no future, no past, only the long bright note that is now, and the slow abrasion of stone against itself, whispering I am, I am, I am.
