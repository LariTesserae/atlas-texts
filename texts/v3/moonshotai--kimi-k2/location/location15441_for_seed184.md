The water isn’t above you, or around you, or even beside you—it has nested you.  
It is salt warm, thick enough for a hand to leave a permanent carving that lingers half a day, and still it does not move. Gravity here has gentled to a mere suggestion: currents too soft to feel, a lunar pull that barely persuades the eyelids, so floatation becomes a version of standing still. Pressure is cozy rather than crushing; lungs adjust without ache, eardrums bloom open, and the quiet—sound 0.5—arrives as the dull silver hush of a still factory when all the gears have been disengaged but not yet cooled.

Underwater Cathedral–Town, single-district size: grainy walls of coral–concrete mingle with kite-kelp, architecture accreted rather than planned. Past an archway at the edge of your vision begins the Basalt Vestibule: hexagonal factory stacks left like organ pipes, long since exhaled. Around them cling tresses of candent purple algae, fat ropes jeweled by bioluminescent nubbins—things that do not belong to any kingdom yet named. Every house is a ruined cylinder uprooted from the surface and sunk; the metal rings gapped where water has peeled the city off its own skeleton, leaving square-window ribs. These ribs are now galleries for plants: leather-leafed vines that sprout rigid copper wires instead of tendrils, the wires humming faintly—tech 2.4 kept alive by osmotic electrolysis.

Vegetation 1.7 means leaf against leaf with no air between; everything is wallpapered verdure. Rubber-fleshed melaleuca press between oxidized catwalks, turning catwalks into tunnels. Somewhere midway through the district the plant density tops out: a disk-shaped grove only twenty meters across, five meters floor to ceiling, every centimeter of its interior surface quilted by overlapping leaves that have joined flesh underground, forming a single organism dubbed the Grove-Bowel. It exhales a mild ethanol perfume, intoxicating to anything with blood. No fauna—but you do spot three empty chitin shells of something once crab-like, their curves grown smooth as beach glass.

Light: 0.6 dims ruby to almost impossible. Only the faintest shimmer slips down from the surface world—250 meters up—strangled by the density-packed plant mass overhead. Yet below each leaf node, and along every wire tendril, numinous droplets bead like blue-white mercury. They pulse once every twenty-three minutes, a clock drunk on benzaldehyde. Those droplets are the whole economy here: tiny cold-fusion pearls drawing hydrogen direct from water, powering the last functioning wall sockets, powering also a heart-beat drone of machinery beneath your feet.

Beneath the township: The Humming Floor. This is a permanent conveyor system built upon basaltic struts sunk into the silt long before the flood. It still turns—slow enough that an hour passes between the appearance of one grease-black cleat and the next. Nothing rides it anymore; manufacture ceased when the water finished climbing. But its rubber belt continues to cruise at a literal snail’s pace, indifferent. Ferrous dust collects along its edges; coral polyps mine this dust and build cyclopean hoops. Slowly, slowly. Liminal factories exude scents of diesel filtered through thyme.

Erosion 1.6 means round-edged, glacial patience. Salt crystallizes inside every pore of brick, wedges flakes off at intervals of years. Surface tiles become ostrich-egg smooth; junction boxes dimple like lozenges. The water, though corrosive to life, has achieved equilibrium with its own ruins. There is a quiet pride in the way nothing collapses all at once—only passes language into shape, and then to myth.

Rising, occasionally, are the Vesper Vents: chimneys thirty centimeters tall that release single silver bubbles at dusk. Each bubble rings like a far-off gong, the only deliberate sound in a place where silence itself is rusted. Once the bubble reaches the invisible underside of the leaf-ceilings, it implants a tear-shaped shadow that remains for days, an ophthalmic stain on the dim.

To move, you push gently between walls of kelp-concrete and warm nothing. The city feels as if it were designed for creatures twice your height who preferred horizontal sleep. Windows are yawning oculi into the hush of inside rooms still filled with original desks, swivel chairs entangled by strangler figs. One office safe lies ajar; paper has curled into knives of pulp too soft to cut. On a desk you find a pocket-dial gauge that still clicks, its numerals rusted away except the 7, which glows faintly through its own green patina like a single eye.

Local rule: Everything oxidizes into cooperation. A gear becomes lichen-borne monument. A handrail dissolves into a spine for brine orchids. Nothing discards itself here; it only intermarries. Even the human erasures are hybridized—an old neon sign “METRO-MECHAS” now spells itself in biolum slime along a swaying curtain of kelp that mimics letterforms. The sign glows cyan each time a Vesper bubble pops against it, as though acknowledging an applause it waited centuries to hear.

To exit is impossible. No horizon presents itself. Downward excavation meets the warmed innards of the conveyor; upward, a ceiling of living glass so thick its underskins bioluminesce the color of winter dusk. The ceiling gives off a faint refrigeration, keeping the temperature a hammock 24 °C, never wavering—temperature 1.7 anchoring all metabolisms slow.

So you drift along, sometimes brushing the floating tail of a long-erased fox’s-eyed jelly-salamander—rare fauna 1.3—who has no mouth, only trailing photophores along an attenuated ribbony body, searching perhaps for something whose definition began in air but now persists solely as intention. The salamander leaves ribbons of light like ink in black tea, vanishing moments later, not even footprints left in nacreous water.

At night (a legal absurdity, since no night exists) the water itself acquires a slow, tide-like shading from rust to obsidian over five unhurried hours. During this transition, the industrial hush swells with internal choruses: sodium-cooled transformers inside dormant turbines breathe out sighs of ionic steam, the sound a mother might make lowering into sleep. Together these sounds make a single organ chord, A-flat diminished, unplayed since the flood but never quite allowed to stop holding itself.

You may choose to linger beside the Grove-Bowel’s heart-valve, where the ethanol scent imbues desire to rename every machine as parent. You are asked—coaxed without word—to rest your cheek against the warm vascular bark. The plant allows foreign blood to entrain its own, fungal filaments threading you gently, briefly; it will not keep you. When the valve opens (interval: six hours), you are freed with the taste of starfruit and copper wires between your teeth. 

And so days come measured by leaf pulses and bubble o’clock, by the oozing journey of a conveyor cleat that might take half a lifespan to return to the place you first noticed it. Civilization here is nothing more than a slowed inhalation that forgot to exhale. It is finally completely itself, which is to say it is complete: saturated city, solemn cathedral, womb and crypt and garden and engine at once.
