Hanging Garden of  
A pocket-valley no larger than two concentric limestone rings lifted a hundred spans above the surrounding cloud-forest.  From rim to rim the place is exactly the size of a modest cloister’s garth, yet it holds the inverse of cloister calm: everything here is porous, moist, half-sound.

Water
There is no creek, no spring.  Instead water sluices out of the air itself: a perpetual warmth—skin-breath temperature—lavishes the stone so that dew nucleates into clear streams which run finger-thick along every declivity.  Minute waterfalls, no more than a handfall tall, braid down the cusps of the valley’s edge; each drop sings a distinct bright pitch against its own rock, and their collective choir raises a humid thunder that masks the forest far below.  Where the flow meets nothing it arches, globes, curls and hovers for a moment in fat spheric beads.  In late afternoon an emerald film spreads across these blobs and they become iridescent hollow lenses until one by one they breathe in on themselves and collapse—shoulder-noises that punctuate the slower bass of the bigger cascades.

Terrain & Materials
The bedrock is not quite stone and not quite coral.  It is a limestone reef that time has kneaded into filigreed ramps and sagged arches; the shapes wear the memory of gigantic prehistoric clams now turned to air.  Hollow burrows, the diameter of an outstretched arm, wind through the stone like catacombs; out of them exhale currents of cool wind perfumed by the rot of vanished mollusks.

Vegetation
Every ledge, every apron, every out-of-plumb upright wears a cloak of low, succulent foliage—tough grey-green pad-tongues and burning red fingers that store the nightly flood.  Between them dart grasshoppers the color of cinnabar whose wings do not dry properly and so refract translucent loops across the light.

The plants are lush but not crowded.  They exist in pockets that leave the creamy marble visible; if you stepped precisely, you could walk on stone alone, but why would you, when every footfall releases a citrus mist from crushed leaves?

Fauna
Beyond the vivid grasshoppers, life is scarce enough to feel like rumor.  A single moth-no-larger-than-a-nailhead cooper-make its way across the moss every half-hour.  Something nests deep in the limestone galleries: a kettled, steady sighing suggests lung the size of a child curled fetus-wise; it inhales once every nine minutes and exhales an acetonic breath that the plants lean towards.  No eyes have ever seen the creature.

Law of the Place
The valley adheres to the ordinance of weight loss: any object that remains here for more than one full cycle of the light—roughly twenty-six hours—will begin to weigh less.  Not because matter leaves, but because something in the water’s sediment collects on the underside like thermal reversed snowdrift, equal in mass and opposite in vector.  Walkers who pass through feel their stride lengthen to moon-ease at dusk, until by morning on the third day they must attach small stones to their belt to keep from floating off the ground like dandelion fibers.  Should they forget this precaution the local eddies will ferry them to the valley’s eastern lip, and there they hover in gentle captivity, knees dangling, until a burst of rainfall reseats them.

Soundscape
Apart from the waterfall percussion the place carries its own harmonic drone at a pitch just below the threshold of pain—a low C shaped like a perfect tube.  Perhaps it is the resonance of air slithering through countless hollow clam-shells; despite investigation no traveler has ever found the cavity that produces it.  When the droplets break they add crystalline overtones that ride atop the C like white steam on black coffee.

Atmospherics & Light
Because the valley sits high, the light here is sharp but carries suspension.  Mist rises from the forest pan but halts obediently at the inner rim, curling back as if at an invisible dike.  Sun-marked water prisms make rogue rainbows at midday; later, the whole basin folds into amber as mist grows gold lattices that settle on leaf surfaces like cloisonné.  Night is never dark—only deep cobalt—the wet stones pose like daylight negatives, luminous in ultraviolet spill.

Cue of Adjacency
The walls lean inwards toward each other like prayer books rest on elbows.  Between them, at dead center, stands a solitary tusk of grey rock that is precisely so tall a person could embrace it.  On this stalagmite a thin layer of translucent lichen grows in ring-after-ring representing seasons as tree rings, but here one ring is a month, another a decade depending on how heavy the year’s water was.  Run your finger along the latest ridge and you feel a tender scar of yesterday alive.

Exit Condition
Leave by any ledge and the world returns in fullness—temperature drops three degrees, gravity returns full brunt, the water sings less.  Most depart with the weightlessness still stuck behind their eyes; when next they dream cliffs emit translucent pitch pipes in sequence and they wake longing for the hollow kiss of those air-waterdrops now forever impractical to own.

Until then the Garden hangs, its rules of flotation and half-voiced chorales persisting, patient as geology, audible for miles around only as a soft metallic vowel caught in the teeth of clouds.
