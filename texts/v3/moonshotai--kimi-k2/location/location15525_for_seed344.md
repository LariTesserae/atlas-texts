The place is a pocket inside an engine–a single, tidy compartment no wider than two cupped hands, left open by accident when the great steel plates were bolted together.  From the outside it’s only a black-vaned heat-exchanger the size of a city block; from the inside it is a low hollow ripening into three moist terraces that tilt toward one another like the inside of a broken cube.  Everything is young—welds still nude, metal fresh enough to smell of shaved pennies when it rains.

But it *does* rain here.

Condensate forms on the metal ceiling in crowded glassy beads, thick as dragon’s tears, then loosens, falling compact and hot.  Each drop generates a crack when it strikes the burnished floor, because the floor is actually still changing temperature from “lathe-new” to “forever-hot” and the micro-fractures are closing like shy fists.  The sound is louder than the ocean—each drip a gunshot in iron—but only when the droplet lands face-first.  Most scatter into mist part-way down, coughing out a warm silver haze that recycles upward to become mist again.  Consequently the air is a spongy swirl held between 49 °C and 51 °C, moving so slowly you can watch your own breath grow older and eventually climb back into the ceiling.

Water gathers in three stepped pans of the alloy itself, forcing the foundry runoff into narrow, ankle-deep baths.  These pans are clean lines, extrusions still sharp at the corners; they contain no life—no biofilm, no midges, no whisper of bacteria—just hot blue water reflecting the grid above as if remembering some scaffolding now gone.  The sound of water sluicing over the edges is a constant sotto voce *schrrip* that joins the crack-a-crack of the droplets into a tinnitus you feel in ribs rather than ears.

Vegetation is present, but only just.  Seedlings of a matte-finish creeper have slid in on the wind-film the factory draws through its cooling ducts.  There are eight or nine of them, planted where two soaked bolts form micro-cups.  They are each the size of the pad on a pinky finger.  Their leaves are gray, with one translucent vein that glows faintly whenever a drop passes through it on its way down to the roots.  They grow one layer of cells per week and so are unlikely ever to touch the walls before the entire rig is disassembled in fifty or sixty years.  Between them the emptiness is absolute: condensed silence even in the roar.

No animals—spiders, flies, rust-bugs—none were ever invited.  No footsteps either, because the pocket sits above an access catwalk still wrapped in orange plastic after construction finished yesterday.  The only humans it will ever know are whatever feelings were left in the welder’s mind when she stepped back after finishing her 2 a.m. bead and realized suddenly that there was a perfect, unclosed box above her head.

There are little machines, but they are the remnants themselves—not added to the world, but carved out of it.  A ten-millimetre bolt has rolled slightly downhill and lodged by the lowest water pan, head first, so its thread acts as a tiny turbine when condensate surges over it.  This causes the bolt to chirp, one metallic whisper every 43 seconds, like an alarm clock too polite to wake anyone.  That is the sole bit of motion beyond the water’s looping evaporation-distillation-birth again.

The light issues from three 2-millimetre inspection ports drilled into the outer shell, each fitted with ruby-tinted windows as factory spec insists.  During the day they paint red coins on the floor that slowly slide along as the sun changes, allowing the sparse seedlings their measured photons.  At night small floodlights far outside take over, steady 2700-K glow from a parking lot glimmer.  The rhomboid beams putter through the ports, now white, now yellow, always calm, making the steel look wetter than it is.

Edges: the opening through which one reaches this chamber has not been finished—its lip is still sharp enough to cut a finger, though no fingers attend.  Through that lip leaks the infinite drone of the parent plant: centrifugal pumps ten stories tall drinking from nearby river via ancient culverts, plus an aging motor whining four cents sharp.  It comes through as a tar-thick bass that floods the pocket, swamps all higher frequencies, and leaves the internal roar of droplets standing in naked relief.  Yet this din has been permeated by the new small room, reshaped by the three boxed walls into a giant whisper, as if the factory has been asked to speak inside a stomach.

Dynamic: everything moves, but nothing hurries.  The heat-exchanger only warms one degree per three months in its mandated seasoning; the seedlings add only one cell per six-day shift; the river’s tidal hum through the hull rises half a millimetre higher very November.  Rust will come only after the warranty ends, some fourteen years hence—by which time the entire module is scheduled for demolition anyway.  Until then the slow adding and slow costing, droplet by droplet, will repeat forever in its unwitnessed loud patience.

When you look straight up you notice the chamber is not fully secret: one rectangular slot sliced high in the wall lets onto a long ventilation shaft painted interior-flat black.  You see, with a squint, a stripe of wild dusk up there: sky that smells of catalytic converters, light-cones from the sodium lamps beyond, and that is all.  The doorless pocket remains permeable to pressure and sound, but to nothing else.  It tucks itself inside an engine older than tomorrow.

Among all the industrial kingdom around, it is the single moment the machines allowed themselves to feel homesick for weather.
