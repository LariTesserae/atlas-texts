Begin beneath.  
Begin in still pressure where the ceilings are thin crust and the floor remembers magma. This is only twenty-three body-lengths below the true surface—shallowness made uncanny by how completely the upper world has been forgotten.  

A spherical cavity, one canto of a town wide, as if a god-child pressed an orange out of the planet’s flesh and left the hollow wet. The radii of the bowl are ragged: freshly broken basalt that still carries a tooth-cut sheen, obsidian knives that have not yet been kissed dull by air. The rock is _young_, cooked yesterday in geologic time, and its internal heat pools in a languid atmosphere only a few degrees above blood-warm.

Water is everywhere here, but not as streams. It clings: opaline films sliding over the concave wall, droplets that birth themselves from vapor on the cool tips of crystalline protrusions, then shoulder each other free and fall upward—turning to mist again before they can ever strike an opposite surface. The effect is a slow, inverted rain. If you stand still you are painted by it until your clothing weighs itself into armor. The humidity is so absolute it begins to sound; every collision of droplets rings like tuned chimes, thousands of them talking at once—_loud_ by logical measure, though the ear eventually treats it as weather rather than noise.

No animals dwell to devour the music. An intricate vegetal lace occupies their vacancy. Here is the twist: the plants are architectural, metallic, and dimly lit. Each is a scaffold of copper-nickel veins laced with ferrofluid leaves. They grow from seams in the basalt like frost fractals extruding to cathedral height, but scaled down to room size—nave and aisles present, but pressed to dollhouse proportion. Chrome roots dimple the wall-seep, sucking the opposite rain for inaccessible sun. Photosynthesis no longer: these leaves solo-harvest infrared and femtophotonic packets circulating in the air currents. The fronds pulse with excess energy, bleeding a soft static of coppery light into the dark. The grove, or town-square, glows as if remembered from dusk rather than truly lit.

Between trunks run walkways the thickness of a thumb and the apparent fragility of an eyelash: spun fullerene struts holding transparent basaltic-glass, wafer bridges six widths of an adult foot. They coil upward through three concentric balcony-loops toward a single high aperture where the sphere meets a vertical shaft—black on black. Every so often a freight spore of some engineered lichen glides along the fullerene rails: a pearl of programmable matter smaller than a sesame seed, issuing a soft turbine whine as it travels to a fault in the wall and splashes into line-straight repair. The floor mesh is similarly alive: cables dimple and reorganize like tendons in an ankle, conveying whole floor plates a centimeter left or right on algorithms deciding how best to distribute the steady drip-load. Melded repair and circulation; infrastructure indistinguishable from forest circulatory flow. The place is _built_ more densely than any street grid overhead, yet takes pains to appear uninhabited, empty in every breathing lung-shaped chamber.

Gravity itself slides in here, hinted at by the water’s disobedience and by how pedestals rise instead of sink. In fact the electromagnetic bedrocks have been tuned so that local verticals tilt—you perceive yourself on the lower inner surface of a moonlet, while someone above (though there is no one) would walk upside-down across the same basalt. The inner horizon forms a sphere within a sphere; horizons in every direction, curving only thirty meters away but looking continental through salt-water refraction.

At the nadir of the cavity the water gathers, not in a lake—lakes obey cohesion—but in a floating lens: a twenty-meter-wide disk of liquid suspended around a negative point of mass, its skin rippling in slow-motion seismic swells. The lens is two centimeters thick; beneath it vacuum, above it another disk of twin mirror, equally mirrored except freckled by industrial gold circuitry. The two sheets never touch; instead they drum against one another in heartbeat cycles—whole chords thundering through the cavern when their rhythms synchronize. The great lens is the clock of the place, and the trees tune their copper pulses to it. Whenever it completes a fractal modulus of Fibonacci beats, the entire grove releases a storm-seed: an airborne spore diamondium grain packed with hydrogen vesicles. These seeds drift toward the ceiling, water-dappled and gold, scattering enough photon packets to keep the leaves fed for the next interval. Without fauna they go un-eaten; instead they slowly redisperse into vapor or reassemble into new fuller plates, young geology in qubit loops.

If there are rules, they read roughly:

1. All architecture must behave like organisms absent of herbivore or predator.
2. Water is permitted to forget which direction it falls if it agrees to never pool long enough to become still.
3. Light must remain below starlight temperature; bright here is considered hazardous to the metallic leaves and their fragile subplanck guests.
4. Sound is actively cultivated. The cavity is an organ pipe; its resonance is calculated to reprogram the basalt during its centuries-long cooling.
5. Footfall of any macro-organism triggers stepped magnetic fields that reroute footbridges away toward untrod areas, ensuring diamond paths devoid of wear-patterns. (You are trespassing—ghost infrastructure reconfiguring to avoid you.)

You can wander only a hamlet of circles before the systems choreograph you back to the single door of basalt-sliding slab. The air smells like ozone and half-remembered roses. Your breath condenses on teeth made brighter by the copper gloom. Beneath your noise you sense the whisper-complex: committee code negotiating whether to allow the presence of mammalian heat to persist in the grove-machine.

Leave while it decides.
