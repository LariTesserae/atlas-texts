It is a fist-sized city made of frost and silver, hovering three inches above the palm of an unseen giant.  
You reach it by breathing on a mirror until condensation gathers, then pressing your thumb to the cold glass; the micro-urb opens like a paper-cut into a dimension only slightly out of true.  
No plants live here, yet water is everywhere, suspended in jeweled capillaries that loop between the buildings like crystalline veins. Water clings as if ashamed to fall, always on the brink of becoming ice.  

The altitude is dizzyingly high, but not in any atmospheric way; rather, your sense of scale tips on its side. A single staircase winds around one skyscraper only one eyelash in diameter, yet it climbs past two hundred stories. Gravity holds, but direction is negotiable. You may turn the entire city by tilting the mirror, and the miniature citizens continue undisturbed, speaking in hushed clicks of frozen woodwinds.

Their streets are made of frostplate alloy—titanium shot through with herringbone lattices of rime. Every block, cul-de-sac, pedestrian mall, and pumping station forms part of a single metallofungal exoskeleton extruded by swarm printers no larger than houseflies. These printers are the fauna here: rare, glinting ellipsoids that sometimes rest on a parapet like metallic bees, then whirl away to mend a hairline crack in the skybridge. They are programmed to self-erase when the crack no longer exists, so most of them exist only in the moment of action; memory is distributed among the spires themselves, which vibrate in soft infrasound whenever someone asks a question too loudly.

No plants grow, but there is respiration: every surface exhales microcrystals of H2O that clink like wind chimes when they drift into each other. The air rings with quiet tink-a-tink, a wordless Morse between water molecules that know they are being watched.  

Temperature sits at a controlled –5 °C; warmth is contraband. When you exhale slightly too warm a breath while looking in, a redistilling squad of printers rushes to quarantine the intrusive vapor, dicing it into symmetrical snowflakes that are then shelved in the Municipal Library of Misplaced Heat.  

Light is calibrated to an Earthlike noon; it comes from an indistinct source at the geometric center of the palm-sized sky. Day length is 42 seconds, set by treaty. During the one-second twilight, the façades along Frostfall Avenue bloom with pastel auroras as quantum dots recalibrate their reflectance. During the one-second midnight the whole city moves half a micron closer to the edge of existence, and you can almost feel the mirror shiver.

Movement is continuous yet polite. Buses made of carboned silk glide on static repulsion rails, never touching the ground—sound rarely exceeds a hush, except when a maglev caravan must slow and the rails exhale a sigh like a sideways violin. Citizens don’t walk so much as migrate along streets that braid and unbraid at ankle level. Their forms are deliberately non-identical: translucent humanoids, semi-porous hexapods, and compact tetrahedral avatars whose edges glimmer with silver filigree. They seem to be varieties of information wearing cold couture. You cannot quite count them; when you try, the infra-choir of the buildings hums a cautionary note and the individuals elide into outlines that reassemble behind your peripheral vision.

The atmosphere carries the rules in its electrospore dust:

1. All water must remain accountable; any drop that tries to escape becomes a witness flake.  
2. Architectures older than twelve minutes must be dismantled or folded into a new district before the next dawn (42-second cycle).  
3. Heat is pathological; it must be encrypted, cataloged, and stored in the Library.  
4. Scale is democratic; any inhabitant may shrink or enlarge within the 1:5000 range, but must yield right-of-way to denizens currently using the same volume.

Consequently the city feels perpetually unfinished, cleanly torn from the forge each instant. Yet footprints older than real time linger—ghost icicles that hang at angles impossible within the constraints of any Earth world.

To leave, you must count backwards from forty-two in a whisper quieter than the snow that dreams of falling. If you succeed, the frost retracts; the silver folds shut like origami. On the blank side of the mirror you will find only your own reflection. On the reflective side, for a moment, the city appears reversed—upside down and right-to-left—until the very next blink renders it gone, leaving in the glass a faint frostbite-prose: *We borrowed your breath, returned it cooled.*
