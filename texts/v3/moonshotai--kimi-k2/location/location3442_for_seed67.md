The air itself has been manufactured.

Step off the last natural stone and you are inside it: a single, continuous, planet-spanning factory that inhaled an atmosphere and never extoled it. Gravity here is gentle—only nine-tenths of what the bones remember—because the ground is only a skin of alloyed vanadium two meters thick, stretched like drumhide over the hollow shell of a former world. Under that shell lies nothing; the planet’s core was quarried out centuries ago and re-cast into girders, pipes, conduits, and conveyors. Everything left is the built.

The sky is roofed. A vaulted ceiling, ribbed with trusses the color of gunmetal, arcs five kilometers overhead and repeats in tessellated bays until perspective swallows it. Between ribs, translucent sheets of thermal polymer glow at 2 900 K—exactly the color of noon on a summer meadow— so that “daylight” is only a remembered recipe, baked into plastic and fed by current. The light never shifts; it is maintained by feedback loops older than any language. The factory abhors change; its equilibrium governors hiss if a single coolant droplet drifts a millimeter off trajectory.

Water exists, but not in ways a throat could drink. Steam bleeds from micro-valves along every vertical member, condenses into silver droplets the exact diameter of a five-cent coin, then runs down channels stamped into every surface. The water never pools; it is routed, siphoned, pumped, re-vaporized, re-condensed, a bloodstream with no heart. At the foot of every stanchion the metal perspires, beading into lacework that hardens into salt-thin rime and is planed away by passing sweepers. Nothing rots; nothing grows. Vegetation score = 0.5 simply means that photosynthesis was never written into the permit.

Temperature is held at thirty-one Celsius. Not 30.9, not 31.1. The air is syrup-warm, buoyant with unsaid summer, yet no sun ever touches skin. You walk between machines that never cool, motors that never quite overheat, bearings whose grease is renewed molecule by molecule before friction can rise to notice. The planet hums at 42 Hz—felt in the sternum more than heard—like a distant cargo ship idling behind walls. The sound is so constant it subtracts itself; ears invent silence to cope.

Fauna, though: the manuals say overwhelming, and they are literal. The factory is the fauna. Every gear is a jaw; every conveyor is a peristaltic gut. Ten-meter stamp pumps flex like squid mantles, drawing sheet metal in, excreting fenders and turbine blades. Ankle-high hexapod inspectors—chrome spiders with optically perfect ruby eyes—patrol flange seams, tasting micro-cracks with antennae fine as phonograph needles. They are not robots in the old sense; they breed. When an axle breaks, two smaller inspectors carve its shaft into an egg case, inject scrap code, and within forty hours a freshly-milled replacement axle hatches, plumbed with bearing races already pregnant with their own replacements. Life here is machined, but it still eats, excretes, evolves. The distinction between mechanism and organism was archived here as an efficiency loss.

Density is felt in the breath: every cubic meter contains a machine in mid-motion. Overhead, monorail larvae curl through magnetic rails stacked twelve levels high, their coaches almost brushing the scalp. Walkways are perforated grates; through the holes you see more walkways, pipes, cable looms, ascending and descending until parallax fogs them into pewter mist. Stuff rubs stuff constantly—soft abrasion, the hush of teflon on bronze, the kiss of oiled bronze on spun diamond. There is no loneliness; there is never less than one moving part within arm’s reach.

Scale widens the mind until it aches. The factory does not make one thing; it makes Everything That Is Needed, including the need. A single bay—Bay 17-H—manufactures only the rivets used to fasten the identification plates that tell mechanics which bay they are standing in. Somewhere else a hall the size of the Pacific ocean roofed in dusk-pink alloy stamps out the concept of horizon, laminates it into strips, coils it, and freights it to the Edge Division where horizons are replaced when they wear thin. You can walk for a week in a straight line and still be in receiving; maps are printed on demand at knee-level kiosks, because the layout mutates to balance load—but the mutations are themselves cyclical, so every route repeats every 31.6 years. Workers (biological ones, a few) insist the place is a maze, but management knows it is a clock.

Elevation stays low; the roof is only five klicks up, the floor is only two meters above vacuum. Occasionally a panel flexes and you feel the trampoline sag of an entire skin bridged over emptiness. Handrails bleed nitrogen to keep dew point low; step too briskly and the soles of your shoes frost while your cheeks stay warm. Moisture wants to live here, but the tenancy agreements forbid standing water; it must keep moving or be requisitioned.

Time is the final artifact. Nothing is permitted to finish, nothing to start. Every stamp press hovers mid-stroke, restrained by control rods that sense the global resonant frequency and cancel it. You may watch a blade being forged for ten hours and never see the hammer fall; it approaches, retreats, balances on the cusp of impact forever. That is the dynamic score of 0.2: the world ticks, but the hands stay still. It is not stasis—it is running in place at the exact pace required to never arrive. Somewhere a wall plaque reads:

PROGRESS IS HELD CONSTANT TO PREVENT WEAR ON FUTURE.

The weirdness is subtle, therefore unsettling. When you speak, the air parses your sentence, checks it for useful data, absorbs any technical term, and deletes the rest before it can echo. You hear your own voice only as a thin, late ghost, arriving a second after your lips stop moving—proof that even acoustics are manufactured, latency-tuned, routed. Similarly, colors you remember—turquoise, saffron, bruise-purple—are interpolated by the ceiling panels whenever your gaze searches for them; pigments exist only while being observed. Step away, glance back, and the hue has been reassigned to a valve handle down the corridor. Memory doubts itself; was the wall always that shade of almost-orange? The factory edits the world faster than recollection can fix it.

No single person, map, or archive holds the purpose anymore. Somewhere a control room was sealed after its occupants completed the final instructions: MAINTAIN. The word is etched into a brass strip bolted across every egress, in letters that keep the polish they were given. Outside those doors the landscape is nothing but process. It does not imitate nature; it replaced nature so thoroughly that “nature” now refers to an obsolete subsystem. Yet when you rest your palm on a heat exchanger you feel a pulse—steel at 37 °C, the old temperature of blood. The factory keeps that memory too, perfectly, relentlessly, at 31 Celsius, forever.
