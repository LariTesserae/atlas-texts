The continent is still being poured.

There is no “ground” in any familiar sense—only a glacier-thick slab of transparent, hard-setting syrup that has begun to cure at the edges. Where it meets the warm air it skins over into a glossy crust, faintly pink where copper oxides from whatever furnaces once fed it have come to roost. Farther in, the slab is a slow liquid, alive with convection currents that roll past like grand, translucent whales. You can watch the currents; they travel at the speed of clouds. Where the crust wrinkles, new pressure ridges rise only a few meters high—this is elevation here: a barely perceptible warping of a surface that is otherwise flatter than any prairie.

Vapor beads against the cooling glaze and drips. That counts as water here: shimmering coronas of condensate clinging to the underside of the skin, falling only when the skin flexes. Each droplet drags a tail of glossy filament behind it that hardens into something midway between glass and sugar before the fall completes. Thus the continent manufactures its first stalactites—not carved by erosion, birthed by drying. The fallen shards litter the crust in sparse bouquets; they look like plants until you notice they are motionless, clear, and perfectly silent.

Because nothing natural ever truly was, everything present is fabricated. Yet no tool shaped or placed it—no device beyond heat, chemistry, and gargantuan time. The slab arrived molten, slumped across an empty basin between planetary plates, then cooled inward from the borders. Now its surface is undergoing the first, shy instabilities: a pattern here folding, two lobes slipping past each other at a fingernail’s daily pace. Nothing grows yet. Nothing walks. But the continent remembers the lathes and crucibles from which it welled up; you can read faint impressed grid marks at the flaking rim, like ancient machining scars still trying to decide whether to be landscape or artifact.

Heat accumulates because the curing glaze traps infrared like a greenhouse. The day side thumps at 42 °C, but, being fully made of the same translucent stuff, entire thicknesses glow gently in the lower ranges of vision, so the night does not fall black; instead the glare simply cools into amber. Normal daylight—solar brightness filtered through clear crystal—means shadows are only dimmer versions of the same saturated rose and drift sideways as languid mirages. The horizon draws an unbroken circle forty kilometers away; objects larger than buildings do not interrupt it. In a sense the continent is a single wing of brittle candy, its underside still syrupy and warm, still negotiating shape.

At the cooling border, seams split, release vaporous dampness, then seal again, spidering etch-lines that catch the sparsely drifting moisture and fling it into sudden showers—a creak of slow glass plus a rare hiss of droplets. Those are the only sounds. No wind yet; no organisms to disturb the silence. The scale forbids footstep echoes; the crust is too wide to bounce even the loudest clap back to the ear. Dynamic is therefore purely geochemical: gravity settling surface layers molecule by molecule, convection dragging heat toward the still-moving center, edges contracting at the pace of geological respiration.

Vegetation has attempted only one form: a translucent mycelial mat that threads like hairline silk just beneath the glaze. Where regions have cooled slow enough, the threads knit into hexagonal laceworks. Stare long enough and you swear they are streets: hex-weave boulevards meant for something that has not yet come. Yet they remain ten meters underfoot—visible not as substance but as ghostly refraction. Nothing will—or perhaps can—root on the crust itself until something dissolves the surface into soil, an event still unimaginable.

Thrown across this flat expanse, the sole “landmarks” are seams that failed to reseal. Those skylight fissures expose the syrupy under-continent: incandescent orange, stirred by oar-sized plumes. From the edge they resemble the mouths of quarries; from above, they are volcanic pupils staring through their own half-healed lids. No one monitors them. The continent neither guards nor offers itself. It simply sets itself like a vast pane of candy left to cool, knitting faster than it cracks, healing the small wounds of day and night cycles.

There is a direction, though compass needles dance here: toward the center. There, the crust thins to a riding veil only centimeters thick, growing translucent amber like old mica. Step onto it and you hear the doughy sublayer breathe. One day, gravity predicts, the crust will sag and fold, layering itself into new topology—ridges, canyons, archipelagos—yet that destiny is geological cookies still in the oven millennia away. For now the continent is every process caught mid-mixture: a planet-sized vessel pausing between ingredients and the finished confection it imagines itself to be.

And so it stays. Hot, vast, newly poured, echolessly quiet, scented faintly of caramelized sand and lab glass. Lowland only because any hill is measured against the curve of a whole world. Built entirely, yet by no hand but its own cooling. Active within and solemn without—it sleeps the wakeful sleep of matter becoming geography.
