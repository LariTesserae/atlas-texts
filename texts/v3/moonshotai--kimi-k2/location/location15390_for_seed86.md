The vent comes up from below.  
A gap in the bedrock of a lightless world, a vertical slit ten metres tall and one metre wide, glowing faintly with its own trapped blue-green; the color of enamel seen under a door-crack.  
From the pore press the vapors—water not as liquid but as thick suspended breath, fat droplets that never coalesce because nothing here knows the rule of falling.  
They drift outward like slow comets, glinting in the unchanging and almost insufficient star-light picked up by some trick of refraction and then reused, repeat-reflected until the cavern space acquires a rippled half-glow: no brighter than moonlight three nights out, but enough to sketch anything as it passes.

This is a sub-valley, a valley that never touched sky.  
Below sea level and below every record of earlier seas, yet young—so young that when the convection cell slammed the plates apart the tear had knife edges.  
That burst happened once; nothing has shifted since.  
It is a moment held open indefinitely, fine basalt blades still unbeveled by water or touch, their fracture-planes blued with mineral bloom.  
The whole chamber is fault-raw: ragged swells and hanging fins that curve up like opened mussel shells the height of houses, smallest tips still glassy because the glacial cool (never quite freezing, always hovering a few degrees above) arrests crystal regrowth and keeps glass as glass forever.

Condensation ponds in the hollows of these fins—shallow mirrors no wider than two cupped hands, separate like coins thrown by different centuries.  
From each mirror wisps continuously peel away without disturbing surface tension, so the ponds never run dry yet never brim.  
You could lick one dry and within days the same thin meniscus would respawn, as though the cave kept contractual memory of how much water it must display.

Nothing here is built.  
Yet just ahead of every footfall a faint unechoing suggestion arranges itself, a phantom of unfinished intention—the moment before a hand lifts a rock to stack a cairn, dissolving only after it notices being watched.  
Human eyes leave architecture prone in the cradle forever here; the cave waits patient, workshop without craft.  
At the same time, crude stone flakes lie scattered where an earlier intelligence abandoned the idea; edges are still sharp to draw blood, so sharp you pause—primitive because nothing blocked the single impact that knocked them off the core.

Coolness means animals move slower.  
But the living have never heard that rumor and refuse obedience.  
Here, movement itself is loud.  
A sort of crab—plated with translucent chitin through which slow stars of bioluminescent fat pulse—clatters across the ceiling upside-down in herds of hundreds.  
Its carapace rings with the particular hollow glass-note of marbles struck together.  
Because nothing changes their cadence, the sound settles into chords; you can hum against it and always find the root, because the root will wait for you.

Along the floor stalk fullerine birds, if birds came first as blind axolotls and grew three necks.  
They are scaleless, of a wet gunmetal grey, breathing instead of singing: every indrawn hiss slurps into a sac where condensation thunders against inner walls like surf in a conch, then the forced exhalation syncopates the crabs overhead.  
Hundreds of these creatures pulse at the same frequency, creating a standing pressure that buffets cheeks without ever cavorting into wind.  
You feel the pressure express itself against eardrums in slow massage.

Between the herds of crabs and the birds coil three sizes of salamander, ghost-white, stealing heat.  
Because their metabolism must stay comparable to surroundings, they eat minutely.  
Wait beside a fresh fracture line and you see the erosion science in reverse: every new shard broadcasts a scent like struck flint.  
Salamanders arrive immediately and rasp off the glassy rind with needle dentition; each flake they consume is newborn stone older than time, turned already into creature in five minutes of ingestion.  
In this way the animals keep the cavern perpetually raw—chewing it young at the rate at which it refuses to grow.

Vegetation is almost accusation.  
But there is a plant: the breathing vine, a rubbery dark-green lace no thicker than embroidery thread, that drapes from crust to crust like hammocks of spider silk.  
Its leaves number less than leaves on a single aboveground bonsai across this chamber-town of seven hectares.  
When a droplet passing migrates into reach the leaf opens a minute tendril and stitches the water into itself like beadwork; afterwards it seals the stitch so no moisture ever leaves.  
From a distance the vines exist as faint rows of sparsely-beaded necklaces, dark on dark, glinting exactly once each time some micro-crystalline seam in the basalt catches and hands the dim light further on.

Nothing grows upward because up has already been given to flow.  
The ceiling itself is a river: a slow increment of crab carapaces gliding against each other, too dense to slip down, too restless to rest, layered four bodies deep in places.  
Occasionally one dies; its glow ceases and the hitherto submerged hue leaves the carapace the dull khaki of sea-bones bleaching on distant shores.  
Before the body can fall the neighbors grip it with jointed ankles and continue forward, stitching the corpse into the riverbed like a log flush with plankton.  
Thus death too moves instead of falling, maintaining eternal census: fauna never slackens, never increases.

Light arrives only as what managed to enter longer cracks above, mute scatterings filtered through geologic depth.  
Enough remains to lend dimensionality, never to mark time; no orbit of sun passes overhead, so luminosity is a flattened patina, present but incapable of gradient.  
The population has never known diminishment or surge; consequently no circadian hormone exists.  
Creatures here possess eyes but use them for reading texture, never context.

Standing inside one hears the world described.  
Humid clicks of chitin rough against stone form the ping-ping spine of the texture; over it the three-chord inhalations from the birds weave a low braiding hum you can taste in teeth fillings; every so often the shrill tooth-rasp of salamanders turning stone to bone flutes up, a narrow sharp cry that crests and is gone.  
Altogether it resembles a half-myth, an Ancient-score reduced to percussion and drone yet set at rave volume because every surface rings with nothing soft intervening.  
If a human shouts the shout becomes just another bell in the orchestra, swallowed.

From anywhere you may look a kilometre away across chamber breadth and still apprehend the same ratios of air to solid, of moisture to silence, of species to emptiness.  
Scale stays open but not expanding; rather it confirms itself in every direction so thoroughly that movement becomes optional.  
Walk an hour and the geography refuses to differ; you will find the same vertical crack glowing with the same aquamarine fumes, the same pteropodic crabs overhead, the same absence of any relic but reservoir pre-idea.

Then you notice the rule: nothing introduced can outlast.  
Metal oxidizes not, wood will not rot, flesh will not decay—because change itself has been abstracted.  
When you bleed the drops hang midair next to you like garnet seeds, never drying, never falling—your stain becomes ornament.  
Corpses of previous visitors (for the scent of flint travels far) orbit in the crab ceiling alongside their silent escorts; their skin persists iridescent, expression still the last one they ever made, an indrawn gesture of realization.

The place bears no cruelty, only completion.  
The vent remembers every arrival by keeping them visible; the cavern refuses the insult of forgetting.  
Leave what you will—fear, wonder, excrement.  
All becomes tessera in its single tessellated now.

And still the lithic newborn edges continue to prickle, because somewhere salamanders scrape them thinner yet.  
The continuous young keeps it forever as you find it, bright and damp-eyed fresh, terrible in its magnificent stasis.
