At the roof of the drowned world stands the Salt Canopy—three kilometers above any baseline sea-level, so high that the sun, shrunk to a tarnished coin, drags its pale gold through layers of wet haze without ever truly rising. The water is the sky and the sky is the water; the two did not bother dividing when the world turned over. Gravity itself has thinned in its old age, and the ocean hangs in rippled sheets, spherical droplets of it the size of hills drifting between currents of thicker air, never falling because there is no longer any “down.” Everything has equal claim to every direction. What we once called Ocean now floats like a skin that both caps and outlines the world.

Warmth here is generous and sluggish, like blood beneath old eelskin. It seeps from the volumes of suspended water, each drop a living chemical cyst holding primordial broth. Touch one and the broth blooms: mint-green threads coil, unfold, knot again. The life inside is lush but never more than microscopic layer upon layer, compressed centuries into moments. Fragrance, not sight, gives away its presence—tidal-pepper, kelp-bruise, sun-baked iron.

The original continent was planed down by erosion long before the final flood—forgotten mountain ranges now exist only as contours in the briny sky. Yet a last ridge-line survives. It is the summit ridge of the Canopy’s most immense droplet: a layered citadel of calc-crystals that precipitated from aeons of vapor, tower atop tower with no joint or tool mark—only the stacking of repetitious chemistry. Between towers run translucent corridors that ripple like jellyfish bell tissue; you can walk on them, though your footfall rings like tapping fused glass, and the sound collapses into a shiver that travels the ridge for minutes before it dissolves into the warm quiet.

Farther along, the ridge spreads into terraces densely furred by what counts as vegetation here: scarves of chlorophyll that grow forward while rotting backward, never resolving into species. They hang in sheets as thick as carpet, dripping bioluminescent beads that blink like drowned fireflies. To part the scarves is to unseal a chapter of smells—ginger-spice, bruised coral, coal-seam. Each scent operates as memory; to breathe too deeply is to risk transplanting yourself into an ancestor’s dream who never walked on land.

Across the voids between droplet-mountains drift the calcite ewers—chalice-shapes the size of whales, slow whirlwinds turned to stone by salt. Their interiors remain hollow; fluid brine sloshes within as they rotate once every three days. Sometimes a chalice bumps another and discrete notes—entire chordae of ear-splitting consonance—escape between them like the last glissandi of dying orchestras. Those are the only sounds one hears. Other than that, the air itself drinks every disturbance. Silence becomes a polymer that cushions thought.

Fauna are almost impossible to discern from flora. A guest might take hours to realize what looks like a hanging garden of black-veined silver leaves is actually an animal long and thin as kite strings. It winds restlessly through the chloro-curtains, sipping mineral breath, leaving traces of scented electrons. When alarmed, it knots itself into an eye: the eye looks back at you with perfect recollection of every dream you’ve forgotten. Then un-knotting resumes the creature’s flow, and matter forgets again.

Night and day interchange meaninglessly; what passes is simply a shift in wet gold to wet amethyst. Light refracts through stratified water into prismatic veils. Sometimes the strata tumble: a symphony of mirror shards rolls through the air like spilled needles yet without edge. When the shards rearrange into momentary glass lakes it is possible to step across ponds of cold starlight a thousand feet above their lacking bottoms. One misdirected breath collapses the interim path, and below there is still no fall—only an updraft that carries your shout back to you as the taste of snow.

Reality here is an after-echo. The world has eroded so thoroughly that even causality has settled into its final geometry. Things preceding follow things following; beginnings and endings form soft interfolding spheres. A traveler who keeps consistent memory simply traverses the hollow core of one sphere into the hollow core of the next. The traveler’s presence itself is erosion—gentler than water, slower than storms—yet each step knocks a grain of stone from its neighbors, a mote of water from its droplet, so that the landscape becomes slightly more the shape of one body’s passing.

There are built artifacts, but they belong no more to humans than termite spheres belong to the termite-winged architects. They arrive as tessellated cubes excavated out of salt wings, perfect edges sharpened by pressure from thought. No one places them; they precipitate when the ratio of evaporating grief to condensing wish tilts a certain way. Within, air is motionless and warm—ancient, curator-calm. If you enter one, your shadow sticks to the inner walls as a darker stratum of crystal; exit, and the cube drifts away, forever tracing the map you never realized you drew. A cube may also simply evaporate, and your shadow exits as half-life smoke over the Canopy, weaving faint contrails among the floating seas.

Down—though direction is metaphor—seismic tides churn. Slow undulations of the planet’s buried basalt slap against the under-belly of the sky-ocean; those slaps produce halos of heat that rise and condense high above as new droplet-mountains, ready to dry into further citadels. The cycle is older than any lithic record, perhaps older than elements. Still it continues, patient and painless, a gigantic sun-warmed lung inhaling its own bronze dusk, exhaling pearl dawn, never filling, never empty. The inhalation is so slow that a civilization could rise in the time between diaphragm lift and drop, but here the only civilizers are salt, water, and the few animals who learned how to smile with absence.

To exit one must forget how to leave. No door presents itself, and even asking which way is “out” changes local coordinates; the question collapses back into gelatinous warmth. Continuance resolves into acceptance: that one is already particle and whole, echo and source, sail and someone else’s wind. The warmth never cools, the dimness never dips into black, and the silence refines itself of necessity until one hears, finally, the faint sound of one’s bloodstream repeating the tide, inch by inch, year by year, across the highest drowned summit of a world that finished eroding yet never quite ended.
