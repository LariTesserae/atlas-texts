Tilt your head back far enough and the city feels inverted: not a settlement crowning a mountain, but the mountain trying to grow downward, roots dangling into lightless spaces that have almost forgotten the sun. Everything here is post-final-epoch, the long, slow rebuttal of stone to altitude. Four millennia of erosion peeled away the peaks the way one might ease a cast from a limb, leaving an echoing basement of continent one and a half vertical miles above the sea.

Yet nothing ever quite abandoned these heights. Moisture arrives as a chronic hush—breath-thick clouds that condense on basalt ribs and hang there like delayed verdicts. The air never truly dries; it hoards what little rain survives the climb, wraps itself around outcrops, and drips—a limb of water tracing the slow longitude of stone. Every stair rail in the city carries a continual skin of droplets, but a puddle of any size exists only in basins hammered from an earlier, louder humanity.

Below the railings the terraces step off like the vertebrae of a colossal fossil. The vegetation claimed each little landing long ago: velvet layers of undeciphered moss, succulents swollen with antifreeze, and drooping lilies the color of tarnished copper. They crowd so thickly they grow epiphytes upon themselves, yet every patch is petite, sleeping on a shelf no wider than a doorway. Leaves bruise one another in slow motion; roots drizzle over the edges until they touch stone again and continue downward, gifting the city an additional skein of hanging green minutes from which telescopes to miles long.

Town-light survives only as under-glow, bulbs recessed in hollows and alcoves, their filaments humming just above frost-birth temperature. Their amber stains the mist an eroded peach; everything beyond a twenty-pace radius goes to indigo and basalt grays. The streets—really the intermittent ridges between terraces—make corridors that smell of iron filings, ozone from the aging turbines, and resins no chemist bothered to catalogue. There is so little fauna here that rumor has turned the few remaining species nearly mythic: the gloam-fox said to lick condensation off glass, the wick-wing owlet that circles the elevator shafts.

And those shafts—angled, black, framed in wrought nickel—are the arteries. They descend in slow clicks of chain and cog, passenger-cabins the size of closets making the hundred-minute fall toward warehouse-district lower ledges where raw ore condenses into pallets. Even the industry here is geriatric. Factories linger like abandoned organs: beam ribs, transformer hearts, conveyor skin, all oxidizing patient as synapses still firing low current into magnetic relays that hum a sub-audible A-flat into residents’ sinuses at dusk. Every gear is oversize, because spares can no longer be salvaged; a cracked tooth merely shifts the burden to neighbors, so the entire machine grows incrementally quieter every century.

The city’s physics have become slightly sideways. Water running off the highest roofs may take three days to reach the lowest gulley, because it insists on seeping through vegetation beds that drink, respirate, leak again, echoing a slower calendar than human plumbing. Clockmakers calibrate civic timers by studying moss color, not pendulums. The dread of falling is measured not in meters but in vertical hours—how long the body drifts through story-bearing updrafts of fog, rumored to carry whispered apologies from whoever last tried it.

Yet most are too busy to jump. They repair balconies that shed bracket fungi, nurse the distilleries of glacial vinegar, rewind the gravity-fed freight chutes that still function only because their angles were set by windmills when the planet was new. Carrying trays of ferment the width of the high street means threading shoulders between stone, leaf, copper pipe, and the curve of a neighbor who has stopped to read the newest silt-pattern of lichen.

Someone swears the sky has grown darker since their childhood, but childhood here is a gradual darkening anyway. Colors are consulted rather than seen: a coat of verdigris touched to find where the railing begins, a scarf dyed bruise-purple because bruise-purple is the hue of this-lamplight. Music arrives strictly by accident—pne hiss of pressure valves overlapping with the soft pop of seed pods splitting along the scar of an evening. It is never planned, yet it repeats nightly, the same four-note lullaby for a city perched on the rim of expiration.

On the westernmost balcony grows a single tree older than the street itself, its trunk fusing with buttresses that were once railings, its canopy blanketing six terraces at once. Beneath it stand the remains of a meeting hall roofed entirely in cellulose windows. On rainless evenings the people seat themselves in facing rings, passing cups of pre-storm water. They let silence rest between them, denser than stone. Then, when their ears equalize with the void outside, they begin to hear the elevator cables singing miles below, the grainy scrape of leaf against quartz, a pulse like a heart muffled by gravity. They know the city is still moving, still alive—an enormous organism exhaling cool mist at the ceiling of the world.
