**The Wyrdemoor**

They call it the last city before the end of the world, though no one lives there anymore. The Wyrdemoor sits cupped in a high depression like a palm upturned to catch the sorrow-rain that falls from nowhere, suspended between glaciered peaks that have forgotten how to bear their own weight. Everything here has given in to the pull of abnegation—the stone has flattened itself, the trees have lain down to dream, and even the wind has gentled into itself like an old scholar conserving words.

Yet it is *wet*. Somehow. Not wet as storm-wet or river-wet—nothing so honest. The moisture creeps. Every surface weeps slow glass-soldier tears that never quite make it to vertical. Fragments of damp hang arrested between positions, as though water itself had reached the end-state of erosion and chose pause as its final form. These suspended droplets—some no larger than your pupil, some like watching a cathedral—you can walk among them cautiously, though they distort the already-stretched light into flickering rhombuses that make the eye water in sympathy.

The vegetation is *lush* beyond lush. Moss but not moss—something that grew past moss, through moss, until it became the memory of having once been moss. It spread green fingers so thick across every surface that the difference between hill and house has vanished into one continuity of breathing carpet. The carpet grows upward too, elegant tapestries of lichen that have unlearned gravity's discipline. They drape in slow waterfalls from the broken archways—those arches, ah yes, they are what remains when a city decides to become landscape. Pillars smoothed to a child's cheek curve, walls planed to the girth of paper, everything reduced to the most generous version of itself, architectural courtesy extended over aeons.

At this elevation (not *peak*-elevation, but the jagged kind of altitude where air turns metallic), the cold doesn't nip—*it mourns*. It is 0.3 kind of cold: the temperature bare minutes after grief's first howl, when the throat has gone raw but tears still burn. Your breath doesn't steam; it crystallizes directly into miniature cities of frost that collapse under their own sorrow-weight. The rare living creatures here—a kind of translucid ape, the scholar-aardvok—have evolved just enough warmth to press close to your body and *listen*. Their fur is warm as living parchment; when they press against your leg you feel them absorbing your story word by word until you grow lighter and they become slightly more real.

The sound, oh the sound. At density 0.4, you expect silence. Instead the place *sings old arguments*. Water-whispers in the droplets carry the last conversations that happened here—*but backwards*, syllables arriving in reverse order so that questions that once tore families apart resolve themselves into reconciliations too late to matter. Every stretched-out lichen strand resonates at a different sacred frequency: creates a chord that, when wind passes through (wind like a librarian paging through dictionaries), becomes a hymn to everything that has stopped trying. The result is *loud* not as volume but as *presence*. You hear with your skin, your bones; the song tugs at marrow.

And yet the city's movement—dynamic 2.1—pretends otherwise. The place *stretches*. When no eyes rest on it, the ruins expand like lungs, space opening between stones in a slow yawn of ownership renegotiated. When you return, corridors have lengthened or shortened by precise alchemical increments. Some claim if you walk the same path three times, the third time you'll arrive before you left, carrying your own first footprints. The vegetation participates: great lichen-veils *[they still grow, after all this time]* ripple like water-conscious cloths, gesturing toward doorways that tomorrow will be windows or perhaps mouths.

Here is where it gets strange. (Weirdness 1.7 means subtle, yes, but persistent, like a scar that forgot it was supposed to fade.) The urban built-form hasn't decayed so much as *agreed to negotiations*. Streets became rivers, became borders, became suggestions worn smooth. Houses curled into root-ball hummocks that now serve as nesting sites for the word-apes. Most peculiar: doorways exist *only* when needed. Need shade? That arch will split and peel backward like bark. Need reflection? A pool will collect its own past to offer surface. Need nothing? The city inhales, and shows you instead the truth of your emptiness magnified.

Materials. Everything is one material now—call it *eroded*. Stone meshing with fungal history into a composite harder than diamond but softer than apology. Wood retired to cellular commune with stone's intelligence. Glass that became slowly-sand again, but sand that records the faces of everyone who ever walked barefoot across it before it glassed. You can read arguments here in the texture, entire wars settled into a texture like elephant hide.

And nothing works, because tech is 0.2—there is no *tooling*, only *application*. You need fire? Find the heat-memory in certain angled surfaces that still cherish one afternoon four centuries ago when sun was generous. You need light? There is light—normal generous Dutch-light pointing out how even ruins can hold clarity—but it offers no assistance. If you want to *see*, you must look the way lichen looks: by tasting surface with entire being.

The fauna. Rare, yes, but concentrated into archetypes. The white-grey apes that eat your speech if you offer it carelessly. The aardvok-creatures shaped like questions, who burrow into walls of eroded time to find what answers have aged enough to be edible. Once, a man claimed he saw a single ghosting bird, wingspan the width of a memory, that flew directly into one of the suspended water-cathedrals and vanished—but left behind a single feather made entirely of dripping air. No one has relocated the cathedral since, though all swear they see it flickering at edges of vision like a drip frozen mid-fall.

It is a small place. Cathedral-hamlet scale. You can walk its bounds in six deepening breaths if you face north, twelve if you're grieving. The boundaries are soft as moss-beds—cross them and you find yourself suddenly on bare rock looking *in*, as though someone has closed the book you were midway through. The city tolerates visitors but has perfected patience: waits until you yourself choose to join its negotiations, whereupon two things happen:

First, your footsteps begin to leave arguments instead of indentations—small curled conversations about what mercy properly requires from eroded places. These texts take months to dry into permanence. Second, you find yourself becoming a visitor *to your own former self*, meeting previous crossings at corners that have spontaneously assembled to enable exactly such an encounter.

No one stays. Not from fear. Rather, the city teaches arrival *as departure*. Every step inward is a step further from origin, until suddenly the body forgets the direction completely and there is only this place, becoming itself through your witnessing, allowing you to become something it can witness in return—a sort of mutual collapse into composted history.

There is rain here, but only as punctuation. Once every three years—or whenever the story needs a full stop—the suspended droplets release downward all at once. The effect is a single collective inhale from the entire landscape. Walking becomes swimming. The word-apes surface like dolphins among the phrases. The lichen drinks deep and for three days afterward writes in wet scripts across every available surface, stories that begin *"Finally, we remembered why we stopped..."* These texts dry into softness you could rub to dust between thumb and forefinger, and they carry the voice of the first stone that ever agreed to erosion's terms.

At night, if the light ever truly departs (it doesn't, it can't—remembers too well the one time it almost forgot), the city answers in small, precise movements. A doorway steps aside. A root unfurls to show the knee-print where someone prayed, centuries ago, for news of the sea. The word-apes gather in the sunken market—the only flat surface still pretending openness—and sing the long version of how every river learns its patience from mountains.

You leave, eventually. Everyone does. But you take with you a wetness behind the eyes made of suspended droplets that never quite fall. And forever afterward, in moments when you think you are alone, you feel the city's slow breathing moving through the walls of whatever temporary place you now call shelter.

It isn't haunted. It has simply reached the age where the distinction between alive and dead has eroded too, leaving only this active stillness that sometimes learns your name for the duration necessary to forget it again.
