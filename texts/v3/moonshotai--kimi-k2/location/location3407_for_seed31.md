The city of Auralara floats three thousand feet above the valley it once drained. The escarpment is still rising, its basalt bones cracking under their own lift-weight; the plateau is only eight centuries old, newborn by any geological reckoning. From the cliff-edge you can see the older lowlands folding away like a crumpled parchment, but up here the rock remembers the moment it was exhaled from the planet’s throat: vesicular, glossy, still warm to the touch. When you rest your palm on the ground you feel a faint internal pulse, not heartbeat but hydrostatic pressure—water looking for a way out.

That water is everywhere, though it never speaks. It strains up through millimetre fissures and beads into glassy blisters the size of coins; it gathers in the scoop-marks left when whole hexagonal columns detached and slid into the void. Even the air is soaked: dawn turns the plateau into a shallow lake of mist that never quite condenses into rain, but never lifts either. You walk waist-deep through stationary silver. The sun, when it appears, is a brass disc pasted onto grey silk, neither hot nor cold, simply present.

Auralara itself is built of the same rock that carries it. Houses are not quarried but peeled: masons chiselled along the natural joints, levered off slabs six fingers thick, and tilted them upright like cards. Every wall therefore carries the chill pillow-shape of the original basalt columns—hexagonal sockets, concave faces, a faint volcanic sheen. The slabs are drilled for wooden pegs, not nails; cross-beams are lashed with water-vine that drinks from the air and swells until the join is stone-tight. Roofs are simpler: living mats of sphagnum towed up from the valley, sodden, laid three hands deep. They do not dry out; they simply sprout.

Vegetation here is an afterthought the city keeps having. Between structures the plateau is open grass-wet meadow, a sparse monochrome of grey-green wire-sedge scattered like loose hair on black skin. Nothing grows taller than your knee except where someone has planted a line of flag-root along a path; then you get ten paces of seven-foot translucent stalks, each with a single crimson vein running the length like ink in a glass tube. They do not sway; there is no wind. They conduct the city’s silence into the sky.

For sound is the one element Auralara refuses. There are no animals—no insects ticking inside the reeds, no rock-doves to flute around the ramparts, not even the soft boring of beetle larvae inside the timber. Human footsteps fall dead on the moss-slick basalt; conversation stays inside the mouth, absorbed by the fog before it can travel an arm’s length. The only audible things are infrastructural: the soft hydraulic sigh when a roof-vine releases a droplet that slides through a copper funnel into a cistern, or the low wet click of two stone slabs readjusting as the plateau inches higher. These noises are so quiet they feel private, as if the city were embarrassed to be overheard existing.

Movement itself is rationed. Because nothing erodes, nothing is replaced. Auralarans walk prescribed vectors worn into the grass by communal agreement; to step outside the pale stripe is to risk bruising a plant that will never grow back. Children play shadow-tag in which every participant must keep to the exact footprints of the previous day; the game is to remember, not to chase. Time is kept by water clocks carved into the rock—shallow pans that fill in 29 hours and then tips into an underworld cistern. When the basin is empty everyone knows the day has ended, though the light is the same pearl-grey as when it began.

The plateau hosts no soil horizon; roots cling to the vesicles in the basalt like fingers in lava bubbles. Nutrients are supplied by fog alone: spores, microscopic insects long-dead, salt lifted from the distant sea. Plants feed on the atmosphere and give back nothing except their own silence. Likewise the citizens import nothing—no ore, no livestock, no cloth. What they require they make from water, stone, and memory. Their tools are basalt shards knapped to a bevel and hafted with braided reed; their garments are felted moss worn hair-side in for warmth. They possess fire but rarely use it; combustion seems ostentatious here, a breach of the agreement that nothing should change.

At the eastern rim stands the Listening House, a half-ring of columns notched like pan-pipes. When the plateau flexes—once each three day-cycles—the strain travels through the columns and wakes a note too low to hear, a pressure you feel in the ankles. Elders sit inside that throb for hours, translating its pitch into civic advice: whose roof needs rotating, which path must be rerouted two finger-widths west, whose child has grown tall enough to require a longer shadow-track. The city obeys, forever re-calibrating itself against the moment it will finally stop rising. Geologists say that in another millennium the escarpment will shear and Auralara will ride that block down-several hundred metres in a single convulsive sigh. The citizens have recorded this prediction in a single row of knots on an otherwise blank cord, neither worried nor hopeful. The plateau is young; the city has agreed to grow old with it, but not to outlive it.

Visitors—rare—arrive by a stair chiselled into the cliff: 2,147 steps, each one the height of a child’s knee, the depth of an adult’s foot. The climb takes one entire water-clock cycle. Halfway up, lungs drunk on wet air, climbers report the illusion that the plateau above is exhaling onto their faces, a slow mineral breath scented with cold iron. By the time they reach the grass they have forgotten whatever noise they brought in their throats. They speak in whispers, then not at all, and eventually leave lighter by the provisions they carried in, as if the equilibrium demanded tribute in reverse.

At night—by convention, when the basalt pans are half-full—the entire population walks to the perimeter and stands facing outward. No signal is given; at some consensus moment everyone begins to hum a single tone pitched just below the plateau’s own flex-note. The hum is not song: it is maintenance, a way of informing the escarpment that its humans are still present, still committed to the slow climb. The sound is so soft it does not cross the cliff-edge but falls straight down, three thousand feet, a distilled thread of human silence pouring into the valley it left forever. Nothing answers. Nothing ever will. And that, for Auralara, is the definition of home: a place that has agreed to keep rising, carrying with it a hush so complete it can be heard only by the rock still learning how to be land.
