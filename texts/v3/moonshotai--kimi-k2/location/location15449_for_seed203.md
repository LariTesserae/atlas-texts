Before you open your eyes you feel greenness pressing at the lids — not color, not chlorophyll, but the mere idea of green moving like a thought from somewhere outside your skull into your own. When you do look, you find you are standing inside an immense hollow root, a tube big enough for weather to form. The walls are translucent cellulose shot through with luminous algae, bright enough to drench the space in cold aqua light, but the light is angled: it comes from below. Somewhere far beneath your feet a chasm of bioluminescent fungi acts as the planet’s original sun, shining upward through the whole stratified body of the root-cave.  

The air is steeply chilled yet also itchingly humid; condensation pearls the inner surfaces and beads your lashes. Every few minutes the walls tear in hairline cracks, letting in slashes of external brightness—sunlight—yet these wounds seal themselves within seconds, the plant compressing back into seamless pale green. You realize the root is still growing, not merely alive but very young, and its own circumference is the diameter of a small continent.  

What seems like floor underfoot is really only the upper layer of an immense vascular reticulum. A continuous, slow liquid surge cycles like sap-lava through channels wider than crater lakes. Where branching arteries rise to meet the outer skin, their undersides drip outward into space, forming yard-long stalactites of fresh phloem. These soften again and retract, sometimes catching packets of cooler air that freeze into thin halos of rime. The entire architecture is in furious, adolescent turbulence: no pillar of rootstock stays where it was for longer than minutes before re-warping gnarls, sloughs, the side, blossoms into translucent leaves the size of glaciers, then reabsorbs them with a crunching noise like trampled frost-petals.  

Outside this curled chamber the territory becomes what “ground” becomes when vegetation exceeds the mineral world. You step onto a cork plainskinned over with moss so deep the foot sinks ankle-deep into it as into new snow. There is shock-absorbing silence except for a faint scribbled electric hiss of capillary sap beneath every surface. Rare, stilt-legged herbivores—skin of lichen yet jointed like crane’s legs—roam undercrafted bridges of living vine, but they are so extraordinarily thin you see them at geological intervals: one silhouette miles away, then nothing for hours. Their call, when it comes, is a mild scrubbing sound like dry bristles across parchment.  

No stone, no soil, only green skeleton and green flesh: the biome erodes the idea of geology. Carbon-deep columns serve as tectonic plates; every geological feature is botanically constructed. Where continents rub together the collisions produce living mountain-chains—photosynthetic ramparts bristling with cactus spines the width of skyscrapers yet soft as blancmange, cooling jets of aromatics that condense into drifting ice crystals. These mountains erode not into gravel but into seed-gardens blooming and withering in the same afternoon, the debris of their decay hauled back into bloodstream flux before it can ever rest.  

Scattered through the expanse stand the remnants of someone building back at the material universe with reluctant fingers: huts of bound leaf stems and fibrous bark stitched with gut thread, hanging between two swaying petioles. Tools lie abandoned at thresholds—bone knives still slick, whetstones gone moss-soft, a child’s whistle carved from a plug of xylem. One looks as though the maker forgot what hands were for mid-gesture: a half-peeled log sits with its shaving frozen curved like an unread page, waiting centuries.  

The sky overhead is the underside of the canopy layer high above — not sky but a second continent mirrored: dendrite vaults interbranching in stained-glass greens. The bright planet surface is behind that ceiling, so distant its light must traverse trunks the radius of moons before dripping in: daylight here arrives as diffuse auroral brilliance plus the punctual ghost-suns of scattered aperture leaves. Yet the light is abundant; it finds the beat of circulating cells like dust motes.
