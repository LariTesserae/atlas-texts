The place is called the Sumpfold, and you do not walk into it so much as wade through its startled breath. All warmth here is generated by water-that-is-slightly-hot, water retained and reheated under the earth’s thin crust until everything floats in the same syrupy temperature. No wind ever arrives; a pulse in the air hangs close to the skin like breath on a polished stone.

The Sumpfold is a single hollow as wide as the nave of a ruined abbey but no taller than a tall person might reach on tiptoe—about twice the span of an open hand above a low tide. It is bounded on every side by a lip of translucent gypsum, veined with mica that catches whatever little light exists and returns it silver-soft. Nothing roots here; nothing photosynthesises. The only life is the thinnest rim of spores that live on stone rather than soil, forming pale dustings: white fuzz across the gypsum, greyish moles on the few darker uprises.

Water is no more than two knuckles deep across the whole floor. Yet it is everywhere. It beads on the ceiling in transparent pearls, falls free with the patience of stalactites feeding stalagmites too slowly to ever meet. The water collects every lost calorie of heat so that nothing ever cools; it breathes steady, warm, mist-shrouded breath. Where gypsum fractures, water seeps through and re-condenses into sheets, wetting every surface to a glaze. Touch the wall and your fingers come away lacquered.

Sound is the slow popping of droplets detaching; each impact creates the faintest glass-rod click that arrives almost before it leaves. Once every few hundred breaths—there is no clock here—you may hear a bubble the size of a pea tear free from some crevice and burble upward, glistening like mercury, until it explodes among the low roof-gems and is gone.

The built presence is the simplest cage: three kneeling stones, waist-high, stacked without mortar into a triangle that encloses one fist-sized cavity. These blocks are raw quartz shot through with ruddy iron, suggesting someone once had purpose here but understood the futility of elaborate craft. At the triangle’s midpoint another, smaller stone rests, water-pocked, turned into a miniature mirror; should you lean close you find the sump reflected upside down. Nothing in the Sumpfold moves quickly. The state of erosion is “complete and yet preserved”: every surface has already softened, rounded, resolved into what shape water finds satisfactory, and now it simply endures. This is complete equilibrium, rendered to the millimeter, velvet-edged.

Fauna is limited to an arthropod the size of a thumbnail whose exoskeleton is layered like mica itself. It is always alone; if there is reproduction it happens out of sight. One small mica-crab stands on the northern kneeling stone, interminably still so you almost take it for an accidental bead of ore. After you cease to stir it sways, ever so slightly, until the minimal weight difference causes the stone to wobble a sheath of water. Then it settles into immobility again. Over spans so long they feel impossible, that single creature walks the perimeter of the cave and completes one circumambulation every century. It has done so at least twelve times; the near-invisible micro-scrapings form a ring worn smooth around the gypsum lip.

Light is dim by any external reckoning: what illumination there is glows out of the water itself, a phosphorescence activated by pressure but never stimulated enough to flare. Above the water film a veil of scarcely visible vapor hovers; vapor holds the light and softens it so that all shadows are pillow-round, edges always out of focus. Nothing ever brightens or dims; nothing ever moves unless you move, and even then inertia is so thick the motion feels half-remembered.

Any visitor soon senses an unspoken rule: to disturb is to break something already complete. Step heavily and a ripple arches outward with glass-fine perfection, only to strike every wall and meet itself again, slowly flattening back into silence. Speak aloud and your words coagulate into droplets that hang like translucid fruit before succumbing to their weight. The primordial quiet regrows over the tear.

There is no sky. This is the basement of the world, sealed, fluted out by solvent warmth. Time is lighter than steam suspended in a jar without gravity—time circles the quartz triangle, circles the mica-crab’s sentinel walk, and repeats in a loop undetectable to anything bigger than a dust mote. To leave, you must reverse the process of entry: slow steps, backward, until your footprint sheds the returning water and the lip of gypsum folds quietly over the threshold so that behind you the Sumpfold shows only the smooth reflexive shimmer of its dome.

Nothing else has ever found it.
