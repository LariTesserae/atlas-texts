Here the sea never actually breaks surface.  It is a city-wide tide suspended in its own moment of becoming: an ocean held back forty metres below street level by nothing more strenuous than the architecture’s stubborn refusal to open its rooms to ruin.  The plaza stones, once basalt, have become prisms drowned from below.  Between their newly translucent slabs runs a slow, fresh coldness that is almost but not quite water—viscous, breath-clear, faintly phosphorescent.  Where the drops should have pooled they instead hang like pale icicles that will never finish their fall.  The whole abdomen of the place is kept liquid; only the rooftops and upper balconies have stayed dry, and even they are encrusted with leathery salt ribbons left behind by yesterday’s brine.

There is sparse vegetation because nothing can root.  Every square metre below the water-line is packed so tightly by presence—the hulls of houses drowned to their chimneys, the bellies of tubular roads looped in mid-air, the flatiron lids of cafés turned into darkling aquarium walls—that no soil remains.  What plants exist must wander instead of grow: minnow-sized coils of kelp that have traded kelp-brown for mirror-silver.  When one brushes human skin it attaches, unfurls a stripe of chlorophyll the colour of bruised ice, then lets go at once and swims on.  The town does not support gardens; it supports schools of one-inch forests that migrate like flocks of odd clockwork birds.

Cold is the master teacher of this volume.  Nose-lingering, silver-coil cold that settles behind the ears, inside the watch pockets of jackets.  The water takes the temperature down half a degree with every breath, yet never freezes.  This paradox: whatever the city is soaked in repels the solid state, prefers its own half-lucid slow-motion motion.  It bites anyway.  Travellers learn to fold themselves into the embrace of stairwells and listen to arteries boom.

Elevation itself is tempered to the size of a single secret.  The settlement sits inside what is only the left ventricle of a vast marble seabird, hollowed aeons ago and now inhabited as if it were an ordinary downtown.  Nobody ever explains why only the left ventricle.  Nobody discusses how you enter, because the entrance is a locket the size of one heartbeat that clicks shut the moment a stranger’s pulse syncs with it.  You look up from within and see the arching ribs painted paletaupe, lit from beneath by the water’s own chemical fluorescence, a cathedral dome narrowed to a private wristwatch.  The sky is a glimmer of veins just barely glowing with their own internal tides.

Erosion has been put on hold; instead, accretion.  The newer parts of the optic-white boulevards are not yet roads.  They are larval cement: latticework hydrated mid-breath, producing stalagmites of sidewalk that resemble nothing so much as coral learning spelling rules.  Block after block what should have weathered into smoothness remains clinkerously new.  Corners snarl upward like hooks, balconies hatch unfinished baluster teeth.  Builders absent, blueprints unfinished, the place sets like cartilage before calcifying.  Where tiles would normally have been hammered down they hang half-loose, still translucent, waiting for grout that will not arrive.

Density, though.  Density is immense, everywhere, exact.  Every crack between stones or kelp or breath breeds creatures, yet the pacing allows them absolute silence.  Shrimp the size of dimes carry entire libraries of colour in their carapace gutters, snailfish switch their scales to read traffic lights that no human ever installed.  Each pseudocoral column is crucified with light-emitting diatom rings—no, not light, explanation: they transmit memories instead of lumens.  Touch one and you remember rooms you have never entered, stairways from childhood homes you never climbed, the scent of a parent’s coat that never existed.  Invisible hosts drone against your skin—the soundless, countless beats of life living the way a heart lives inside the cage of its silence.

Urban, this is, yes.  Lanes like staples.  There are gutters but no rain; nonetheless the gutters flow, filled by the ocean beneath sighing up through their throats.  Streetlamps have gas mantles glowing aperitif blue; the glass stays dry inside, although the globe outside is wetness incarnate.  The mantles are not lit by flame but by rows of ciliates bioluminescing inside the metal stem.  Their contracts are renewed nightly, the way medieval guildmasters swore oaths.  No wires, no diesel.  Electricity never happened here; the city is run by metabolism as pure as lymph.

Technology is therefore absent save for the city itself, which is already an organism built of halls and stair-loops and orrery bridges.  Everything used to lift, heal, or warn is made of anatomy now.  Door-locks are sphincters that judge density of intent.  Windows blink when the tide breathes.  Handrails whiten when touched by heat.  Anachronisms: ink pens were swallowed by the sponge beds two centuries ago, yet sometimes you find their metal nibs drifting as if auditioning to be teeth for some elegant deep-mouth whale.

Light never climbs higher than a candle’s dream of twilight; even noon here tastes like bitten peat.  It diffuses through the liquid over-story in lacy sheets that fall to pieces against the dead-cold and break into herringbone ripples of dull pewter.  If you hold up a hand you see blue veins on the other side lit from within, but nothing brighter arrives.

Fauna: abundant, defended, secretive.  Glass eels loop between paving stones and hide in pocket gutters; every eel is born carrying a fragment of forgotten cartography in the silver line behind its eye.  Some streets rearrange themselves if enough eels link end to end and form the correct pictogram.  Sea otters borrowed the sway of human mothers—they rock in empty doorways without ever crying.  Moths several thumbs wide drift like shuttered umbrellas, folding their wings into origami street directions.  All of them eat only two things: the drifting kelp scraps and the syllables humans have thrown away.  A tourist who mutters that the view is “cold and dank” will find the word “dank” eaten cleanly off, leaving a vacuum the size of a bean in the sentence.  The silence that follows is absolute; nothing replaces it, ever again.  Therefore the town is courteous, and people talk little.

Weirdness seeps upward like chill through socks.  Clocktowers lose every other hour; at 3 am one finds the bell tolling 29½.  A resident points out you are simply travelling in the ‘skipped half’, insists you pay rent anyway.  Certain doorways let a walker step outside the left ventricle and back into it one heartbeat earlier—an entire district where people meet themselves leaving.  Greetings are accordingly minimal; to wave at yourself is vulgar.  Sometimes the surfacing streets remember they are cartilage and bend forward to smell the liquid basement; travellers must learn to walk diagonally uphill for breath.  No one finds this strange after the third hour.

Dynamic is fierce yet cloaked: tiny sys­tems chase each other as quietly as shadows.  The eels’ map-making resolves into evening porridge—then streets re-knot.  The cold gathers and loses a tenth of a degree; shrimplike memories rotate through kaleidoscopic phases; pavement stools hatch quartz necks and topple.  Every ten minutes the tide inverts without a single rumble, sinking the air pockets upward and lowering the roof-water.  A new cohort of fauna freshens in the change; larvae drift in perfect synchrony with the choreography of architecture learning to stand.  All unfolds in soft swells, under zero sound.  The city is speechless because everything has already been said perfectly once; the rest is repetition, communally enjoyed in silence.

Rules are few, negotiable, politely lethal.  
1 – Do not build above the waterline; the city grows its own ceilings.  
2 – Speak only what you are willing to have eaten.  
3 – Do not stitch together the eels into paths of escape; their maps are promised elsewhere.  
4 – Accept that you will exit through the same left-ventricle locket that refused to open for your mother.  
5 – Understand that the temperature is a living census officer.  If you shiver in a pattern it recognises, it may claim you and birth you again from the pavement as a child whose heartbeat now belongs to the city.  No grammar may say whether this is death or citizenship.

Entry instructions: find an orange-red sea horse small as a pupil, let it read your reflection for three breaths, then release.  The locket recognises the reshaped iris.  When you leave again decades later, you will weigh precisely the same as the water you displaced.  The city caps each traveller with the kiss of its liquid lungs—a taste like mineral paper—and then folds the silence closed.

Within, everything continues.  Shrimp bowing with pearl flash, eel-constellations turning civic corners, kelp-streaked bells of unused churches swaying in a slow cold ocean that has given up trying to become ocean.
