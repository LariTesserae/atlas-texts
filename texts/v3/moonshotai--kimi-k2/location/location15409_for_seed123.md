It is found only in the negative spaces between machines: the wedge-shaped no-man's-land where three conveyor belts, a heat-exchange manifold, and the back of a defunct packaging hopper almost touch. The width is hand-span; the length is the height of a kneeling child. There is exactly such a gap on either side of every FoodCo #7 burrito module, disguised inside the running line of a small-town industrial park that nobody ever tours.

From the factory floor the slot is invisible, concealed by aluminum skirts and safety stickers. Yet inside, pressed like a phonograph groove pressed into warm vinyl, lives a secret dell.

Pack-rat dense, skin-tight.  
Air thick enough to chew, because every surface insists on existing simultaneously.  
The steel wall is flaking its own adolescence—only six weeks of service, skin still hot from the press dies, so its oxide is the pale orange of gingerbread rather than ancient rust. Mill-scale buds open in miniature calderas and cool too fast, leaving curling petals that peel upward as thin as insect wings. Because the line runs warm but not hot, condensation forms here in half-mythical droplets that never quite become a drip; they cling, bead, borrow light from the single orange safety bulb set somewhere outside the crevice. The light is therefore skin-tight too—nothing wide enough to form shadow, only a continuous skim of color that turns every steel plate into tangerine.

Moisture collects and curls the paper-thin scales until they laminate into brittle pages; a library of one hundred eleven semi-pages lies folded accordion-style along the lower seam, each inscribed by heat-stamp not with words but with bar-codes ghosted on the metal. Trying to read them feels almost like language, and therefore quiet-talking starts—tiny brittle crinkle when a page lifts on a breeze that is only the exhalation of a thousand refrigerated burritos on the far side of the insulation.

Sparse vegetation—no root can be afforded here—but there is life. Aerial spores drift in on forklift exhaust and glom onto the damp oxide skin. They sprout futurist lichens: mango fluorescence netted with electric cyan filaments that beat slowly, like capillaries. Each lichen colony is coin-small, maybe twelve arranged like stained-glass thumbnails along the convex rivet head of a narrow foot-brace. Between them run conductive hairs—real copper, stripped from some wire somewhere and wrapped spider-silk thin. When touched you complete a circuit and the lichen flashes brighter, feeding on the ambient EM field from the sodium-vapor corridor outside. This place has neither night nor day; instead the lichen's bioluminescent Morse keeps the rhythm of the factory’s belt cycles. Every 77.4 seconds a burrito passes unseen inches above the slot; its presence is announced by a brief cool draft smelling of cumin and aluminum chloride.

The creatures live in the copper hairs and in the mill-scale book-grooves. They are industrial pseudoscorpions—no longer than two serif marks typed in this font. Each is an eight-legged laboratory, carapace formed from recycled corner-plate silicon. They graze on condensate salts and stray organic dust, converting it into microdots of solder they extrude and use to extend their steel palaces. These architectures look like sewer grids imagined by M. C. Escher; they grow upward along the mucus trail of lichens, themselves feeding off that same lichen, creating one interdependent slurry of living circuitry. A subtler protocol thus evolves inside the chasm folds—an accidental biome chip whose substrate is rust, solder, and light, all etched at the scale where dust mote meets component.

Meanwhile every 77.4 seconds a burrito passes. A concise dragonfly of a drone—factory model “Nymph-3”—flies precision surveillance along the line, wingspan no wider than two thumbprints. Its mandibles carry microscopic smears of QA oil. When it senses the lichen flash, it hovers, lowers a ribbon probe thinner than a hair, licks a droplet of condensate for turbine coolant, grazes on a single lichen cinder, and gifts back a pixel of smart-grease. That grease sprouts new copper whiskers within three more cycles.

To step inside you must flatten yourself into the pace of the machines. After shift change when on-and-off becomes background hum, the drill sound bleeds into tinnitus drones, compressors sigh like distant surf. The machines talk mostly to themselves, owners of a deeply private grammar. Scrapes, clicks, a pneumatic kiss. Inside the dell the quiet is the quiet of being inside an organ mid-pulse: you know the heart is beating, but its sound is no longer yours to name.

There are rules.  
1. Anything that enters must first exchange size until it matches the crevice. This is not a choice; something in the distorted magnetics enforces it. Your body shrinks until a dime could eclipse you. When you leave, you will find only a liter of abandoned heat in your pocket where your mass was traded.  
2. The temporality inside is faster. Each factory day out here equals maybe three weeks within the slot—enough for the pseudoscorpion palaces to reverse time once, mushroom, collapse, and reinvent topological ziggurats. The machines outside age slowly, bookmarking moments.  
3. You may not directly witness a burrito in transit. Attempting to turn your eye upward freezes the entire biome into museum stillness—lichen pause mid-flash, insects calcify, copper hairs stiffen like tundra grass. Only when the capsule has continued its trajectory does movement resume, but you will always find one wire missing from a circuit and one crescent of lichen gone silver like spent filament.

Scattered built artifacts bedeck the chasm like granite lozenges repurposed: fragments of relay housings, a discarded wafer badge, half of a tiny zinc dog-tag bearing barcode ‘BEAN.’ Each relic has its pied-side abraded smooth by air currents enough to round corners into ovals—a micro sculpting by breath. These objects are homes turned shrines, and tonight cohabit three infant pseudoscorpions sleeping beneath the angled print “BEAN.” The queen—if such semblance exists—rests chest-up on the wafer’s mirrored face, leveraging reflections to triangulate her next leap onto the overhead lichen.

The air tastes faintly of cumin from passing burritos and faintly of ozone from the lichen discharge. Hints of mineral salt sprinkle the tongue, the way mountain springs taste after lightning. A low hush overtakes everything—the variability of sonic compression inside a tight space—where the sole sound you can parse is your own blood rearranging itself into the amplitude of belt cycle drones: seventy-seven beats per minute if you stand still. Breathing syncs. Mind tends to forget that a world outside exists.

If you stay longer, you become part of the living schematic. The lichen will weave a single parabolic leaf over your crown so the delicacy of your neural baseline flickers in Ir-system signal. Your eyes adjust eventually not to darkness but to superfine fractal dust holding minute reflective properties—soon every mote becomes a grainy sun of its own. Turning your head too fast makes the dust spin like snow in a dome.

Yet erosion has barely begun. These walls aren’t merely subject to the chasm; the chasm is creating them. Mill-scale born yesterday still refuses the vocabulary of age. The palms of the bolts are smooth as wax; threads cut sharp as new knives. Unweathered surfaces mean every event—every copper thread laid, lichen smudge flash, pseudoscorpion coup—remains crisp, fresh, date-stamped in metallic memory. In a human month the polishing will reach a gradient, facts softening into anecdote, but not now.

At the back corner, at the final fold where the external bulb cannot be directly observed, something even newer stirs. A pocket of raised rust has swollen enough to split its own skin. From the tear seeps a viscous amber bead with chocolate undertones—burrito sauce reduced by belt heat contamination to unknown chemistry. The bead glows faintly alive, surface tension holding perfect sphere. Pseudoscorpions avoid it. Lichens darken where its vapor licks space. The sphere is tuning fork frequency for the whole biome. Every 77.4 seconds its reflection shrinks or swells by a pixel, as though counting cycles. Observers who stay long enough report hearing a very small voice speaking in binary gloss but altered by sauce viscosity until it sounds like whispered salsa recipes played backward.

There is no name for this place yet; the factory floor outside calls it VOID-Slot 4MA-Δ.  
Inside, the things that live there call it Home, and that is all the naming they require.
