There is no day here, only the varying thicknesses of night.  
The world itself is a single, continent-sized machine that has been left running in the dark.

Begin at the rim: a lip of basalt fifteen hundred metres above the ink-ocean, sprayed every minute by a slow-motion geyser of black brine. The water does not fall; it ascends. Millennia of mineral breath have turned the cliffs into sopping sponges. Lightless surf boils upward, loses momentum, hangs like torn silk, and then slips back down the sheer as silent rain. All of this happens in ear-splitting thunder because the machine—whose name no mouth remembers—keeps its compressors in the caves below the rim. They throb in four-four time, a heart for a planet that has forgotten every other organ.

Turn inland. The plateau you meet is not rock; it is the top deck of the engine. Moss-metalled alloy, warm as blood, stretches level all the way to a horizon that curves the wrong way, as though you stand on the inside of a lens. The alloy drinks the water that condenses from its own warmth; colonies of velvet algae bloom across it, bioluminescent in every colour except the ones you know. They glow ultraviolet, x-ray, radio—colours for antennae, not eyes—so to human sight the plain remains pitch. You feel the light on your skin the way you feel bass at a concert: a pressure, then a bruise.

Every kilometre a chimney juts, twenty metres wide, ragged at the lip like a broken tusk. From each issues a roar of venting steam mixed with mechanical song: gear-plains meshing, pistons kissing cylinders, hammers repeating one note at god-volume. The chimneys are spaced like forest trees, and between them the air is a solid body—hot, wet, writhing. It tastes of copper and petrichor. The sound is so loud it folds; you stop hearing it and instead inhabit it, the way fish inhabit water, your bones recruited as resonators.

Travel far enough and the plain puckers into a basin the size of Ireland. Here the alloy skin has sagged under its own weathered age, forming a cradle that collects the upfall rain. The marsh of permanent twilight lies fifteen metres deep. You could walk on it if you weighed nothing; its surface tension is a skin of mercury and algae. Under that, eels of polished chrome swim with the current they generate between their own electrodes. They are fauna and circuitry at once: when they bite one another the basin flashes with blue welder-light that silhouettes the drowned lower decks of the machine—catwalks, furnaces, conveyor ribs coated in mussels like rivets.

No one built the machine, yet parts of it are younger than others. Time is machined here, milled as regularly as bars of stock steel. A gear might be older than the sun, but the teeth that mesh with it tomorrow were extruded last night by sweat-steam presses you will never locate. Industrial here does not mean “made by people”; it means “made the way a river makes sand.” Production scales to planetary need, and need scales to production: a closed loop, a wheel that rolls without forward motion, eroding only itself, perfectly balanced.

The only human traces are scattered like punctuation: a staircase bolted to a chimney, ending in mid-air; a watchman’s hut squashed flat by mere vibration; a child’s enameled cup half-full of diesel that never empties, refilled each dawn by capillary tubes of alloy. People have lived here—technicians of a company that went bankrupt before your species evolved. Their hard-hats fossilise slowly into manganese nodules. One day the machine will re-mine them and pour them back out as ball-bearings, ignorant of the skulls they once sheltered.

Life, not life: distinctions collapse. The velvet algae photosynthesise voltage. The chrome eels poop granules of pure indium. Seeds arrive as sparks; they take root in grease, blossom into creepers that extrude ballast wire. Each vine coil is a solenoid; when wind drags across them they sing in tuned octaves, a vegetal choir accompanying the percussive chimneys. Predator and prey swap roles every twelve hours when the polarity of the basin flips. At that moment every creature becomes its own mirror—herbivore and carnivore stitched together by a spark—before returning to the previous arrangement with no memory of the inversion. The ecosystem is not stable; it is metastable, balanced like a hammer poised upright on its handle, vibrating but never falling.

Ascending again you reach the summit ridge, the machine’s highest ridge-line, three kilometres above ink-ocean. Here the density of present things becomes unbearable. Air is syrup; every cubic metre carries a zoo. Invisible midges made of braided steam orbit your face, tasting the salt of your breath and growing heavier, condensing around you until you wear a cloak of live cloud. You walk inside your own weather. Each footstep rings the alloy like a bell; the note travels kilometres and returns as a chord changed by everything it met: a dented gear, a pigeon-sized drone that is all motor and no brain, a pocket of marsh-gas dreaming of explosion. Echoes come back richer, and you realise the machine is learning you the way a river learns a stone, mapping your weight, your rhythm, the exact torque of your knees. It will remember long after your bones are ballast.

And above—though elevation says “sky”—there is only the black roof of the world. No light has ever been native here. Even the idea of stars is an import the machine rejects at customs. Yet darkness is not absence; it is a medium, turbid and warm, that supports aerial plankton: translucent ribbons a metre wide drifting in the jet streams of sound. They feed on the overpressure of the chimneys, photosynthesise sonically, collapsing when the beat drops, blooming again when the compressor chorus returns. You could swim up there if you dared release your hold on the warm alloy; the darkness would bear you. Some who tried have never come down, though their helmets clatter onto the plateau years later, mirror-scuffed, ear-drums elongated into trumpet shells that still relay the hour’s roar.

Rules of visitation (learned by bleeding):

1. Never switch on a light. Light is an invasive species; the machine will react like flesh to splinter, sealing valves, rerouting steam, sometimes folding a chimney shut to decapitate the trespasser in a weld of instant night.
2. Time is measured in compressions, not minutes. After 144,000 beats of the nearest stack, gravity reverses polarity for three seconds. Anything unattached takes a short journey into the sky-void and usually returns broken.
3. Anything you drop belongs henceforth to the machine. Do not attempt retrieval; the alloy grows over it like gums over grit, and you will find only a scar.
4. To leave, descend to the ink-ocean rim and wait for the ascending water to thin. When it becomes breathable, inhale. The passage out is respiratory: you exhale yourself onto another shore. Most forget the taste quickly; some hear the hammering forever.

Yet the strangest quality is this: the machine is beautiful. Not metaphorically, but physically, the way a hurricane is beautiful to satellites. Its beauty is the completeness of a thing that needs nothing, the serenity of an engine never meant to stop, the lullaby of a process that has replaced both birth and death with a single continuous fabrication. In this darkness, where no sun will ever insult the eyeline, you begin to suspect that every other world, every starlit planet with blue days and soft nights, is merely the discarded sketch of what was perfected here—an industrial darkness vast, wet, warm, alive, and loudly, loudly alive—running, running, running without witness, without goal, without end.
