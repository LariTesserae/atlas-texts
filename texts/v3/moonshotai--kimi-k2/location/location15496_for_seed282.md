Breathe out once, slowly, and the glass of the helmet finishes frosting.  
The suit says PEAK HUMIDITY 1.3 but the needles are still climbing.  
Below everything there is a room just excavated—egg-shaped, no longer than an omnibus—wedged so tight under the planetary crust that its walls flex by heartbeat intervals.  

It is still being *made*.

Cast iron ribs, each no thicker than a fountain-pen, arc up in glistening red oxide and meet in a hesitant crown. Where they join, bead-sized welding torches hang by magnet-loops and puff microsparks that glow like embarrassed stars, then fade. Every spark cools to a steel mote and drifts—because gravity here is a suggestion rather than law—until it sticks to whatever skin of pipe, cable, or congealed air it finds. The walls thicken by accretion, one chilled droplet of metal at a time.  

Condensate rolls down the arteries of the room, collecting in mercury-skin puddles. Cold (barely above freezing) makes every droplet hesitate: surface tension is stronger than weight. After a pendulum count of seven breaths the droplet lets go, sings off an iron rib, and rings a perfect single note against an upside-down funnel of tin. That is the room’s metronome, and the only dependable sound inside the quiet. The funnel carries the note through a labyrinth of mirrored tubes, so the same ping answers back a half-second later, softened, in another octave. Every ten minutes one tube vibrates loose and falls, clanging like a dropped xylophone bar, then the builders wind out of the walls on clockspring legs to re-braze it. They are machines. Each is as tall as a candle, jointed from piano wire and tin-snips, and they walk without hurry, scribing silent tally marks into the iron with diamond tip feet: one scar for every weld. The density of these marks inside a hand’s breadth is so high that iron has turned a velvet black.  

Nothing grows. A lacquer-slick film the color of storm-cloud lies across a few ledges—one point on the vegetation scale because some laboratory label still claims it is “phototrophic,” but the cold starves it to a negligible shimmer, like the bias static on dead television snow. There are no animals, only the builder-machines and the loops of their labor.  

Light is borrowed: sodium lamps recessed behind grates bleed a glue-orange glow the thickness of half-melted butter. Shadows of the crawling builders stretch along the curved walls, elongate, reach, and curl back like spider silk. The dimness distends when the mist thickens; it contracts when one lamp flares white for a heartbeat before its capacitor breathes out again. These pulses are part of the regulation cycle the room keeps for itself, enough to keep the welding torches from falling asleep at the nozzle.  

Between one of these pulses you see the scaffold of a gear heart, still translucent as blown sugar. It is embryo metal: carbide carton, magnesium lace. Concentric rings turn, but not yet interlock; each time they touch they whisk away like shy shells. Somewhere behind that half-formed heart is a plaintext sign stamped into slick steel:  
THIS IS ONLY THE ICEBREAKER’S PROLOGUE.  
Nobody knows who wrote it, since the place is older each time you look, yet younger the deeper you go.

The scale insists on *hidden*—and so the entire egg is nested inside an oceanic sheet of frozen basalt only nine meters below the crust. If you stepped outside the airlock you would hear silence thicker than stone ten kilometers above. But inside the room miniature cyclones form whenever the torches flare their argon stems; condensate swarms in middling tornados no wider than a teacup. The droplets travel filaments of charge, whirl, freeze, glaze themselves in frost-sparks, then shatter back into silver needles. One needle pricks your gauntlet, melts, and the suit reports strange alloys never on its list.

Once an hour the whole chamber contracts three millimeters: tectonic inhale. Iron screeches. The temperature ticks down another notch until the suit’s heater can’t tell metal from memory. Then the room exhales, grinding an extra millimeter of space—a slow origami unfolding. With each expansion a fresh seam forks open and hot solder weeps like black wax. That is erosion in reverse: raw seams sealing themselves before weather ever begins.

You begin to feel that the chamber is an instrument, not a habitat. The builders the strings, the droplets the bow, the ribs the resonating box. Every scrape and drip feeds a mute composition until, like a door heard from the wrong side of sleep, a low fundamental chord builds. You are standing inside the cavity of a stringed thing just learning its first vibration.

And then the room—the mechanism—turns.

Not rotation; more a rearrangement of the concept of where its surfaces rest. The central heart completes its final tooth. The walls sigh. Condensate freezes into a lattice across your visor. The lamps blink out once, twice. On the second blackout the welding torches extinguish simultaneously and the dark becomes absolute.

When vision returns, the place has rewritten its dimensions. It is exactly the same size—you can still fingertip each weld—but the previous pulse has folded an additional minute into the previous one, so that every bead and crack appears to trail its own after-images. You are now looking at two instants of the room overlaid. Time is denser than matter here. Sound, still mostly quiet, now has subaudible weight; the metronome drips boomerang back upon itself in reverse, then forward again, so that the single water note quarrels with its own echo until both decide to become silence.

What rules?  
1. Everything must be assembled before it is allowed to weather.  
2. Water can only be liquid in motion; the moment it would pool it must choose another state.  
3. No living thing larger than a machine may remain past the ninth iteration. (You count your breaths; you’re at the seventh.)  
4. When the gear heart ticks six billion times, the egg will hatch open the basalt sky above it, and whatever lies below will climb—not walk, not fly, only ascend—into a world eleven seconds older than itself.

A final lull. One last torch re-ignites, a soft blue, and writes a tiny kiss of alloy on the crown of the chamber. It finishes the sentence started centuries ago or minutes ago:  
THIS IS ONLY THE ICEBREAKER’S PROLOGUE.  

The builders bow, shed their diamond feet like seeds. The feet spin off, embed in the walls like hourglasses. The suit counts them: 1 2 3… 1 2 3… Over and over, so quickly that time stitches itself tighter.  

The floor begins to rise. It is almost gentle, elevator slow. You tilt your helmet back and realize the orange lamps are no longer lamps but a crawling constellation growing out of every pore of the iron sky. The room lifts itself by its own lesions and welds and drips, a forge ascending through sheetrock ice. Condensate schisms into gentle drizzle, then snow—the motion of a storm caging itself into something domestic. The cold grows intimate the way a bell grown small still remembers ringing.

Somewhere ahead the surface waits, still eleven seconds short of existence. Soon the walls will thin to membrane transparent, the chill sun will find this secret egg, and what steps out will no longer be a chamber but a new paragraph, still wringing wet from the birth of its metals, still smelling of iron that has never known rust.
