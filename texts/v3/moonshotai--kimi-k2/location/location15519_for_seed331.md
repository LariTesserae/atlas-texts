From above it is only another shallow depression, a thumb-print pressed into the planet’s last page of history.  
Here the crust has thinned to a parchment the colour of damp nickel and lies merely ankle-below the water-table. One does not stand in the place so much as borrow a trembling contour where land and liquid negotiate custody. The warm air itself is aqueous, almost broth, and when the breeze arrives it lifts droplets as casually as a cat lifts kittens by the scruff, moving them a body-length before letting them fall.

Within this wide hollow — hardly two city blocks across — are very few standing things, but each is so old it has outlived several names of the world.

The first thing you see is the master-tree.  
You think tree, though nothing green belongs to it. It is a single engineering root-system whose above-ground remains are two dozen tapered spires of glass-carbon, twelve metres high, hollow, filled with slow percolating water. Every few minutes a fissure in their waists lets out a sighing bubble. The thing drinks the aquifer directly, lifting, distilling, then bleeding moisture back through condensers at its tips. Light strikes the hollow tubes and refracts itself into palisades full of suspended insects: jewel-winged midges who hatch, eat light-element dust, mate and die in the fraction of a second their universe lasts inside each prism. Their bodies vanish as nutrition for the tree-engine.

Beneath and around, the ground is a lacework of sphagnum gold and rust cupping saucers of mirror-still water no deeper than your wrist. The mosses are not truly moss; they are solar-polyp colonies that have forgotten the animal kingdom. If you set foot on them they bend aside obligingly, revealing air-gaps so that neither your tread nor theirs is injured. Your footprint disappears in three breaths, reknit by self-sealing cells.

Between these saucers stand the miniatures of architecture. Because the place is small, the monuments have remained small — knee-high stelae of black basalt etched in rows of superposed sigils. Each sigil is a fractal gate holding open a femtoscale event: you see it flicker as a point of pure colour spinning faster than optical persistence, accountable only by quantum forces our mathematics confess they cannot fully chart. They are cooling vanes for the land’s buried machinery; they hum with a warmth like an animal’s temple pressed to your lips. The fauna exists here in abundance but politely refuses grandeur. Dragon-ants the length of grass-blades march in single file along the stelae, now and then reeting a vanishing databurst to the cooling sigils above. Translucent ribbon geckos surf the skin-temperature water, legs bent so that only the hydrophobic pads touch the surface tension, leaving no ripples. Their tongues are magnetised filaments; each snap drags a single silt mote out of suspension and into their throats like a spiced breadcrumb. Beneath the saucers, eyeless eel-shrimp trace the under-surface in dark arcs, disturbing nothing. Everything vibrates, multiplies, dissolves — yet the overall sound is of a library full of books closing softly. Only every seventh minute does a root-spire hiccup water — *tok, tok* — followed by a sudden susurration of wings as the entire insect cloud inside repositions by half a centimetre.

Light is prodigious but kind. The sky — etched lucent turquoise by centuries of micrometeorite erosion — is uniformly bright, as if above there were a stationary sapphire noon. Yet the light is netted by threads of water vapour until it rains gently upward; sunlight returns to the sky as silver minnows of mist that ascend and disappear without ever leaving the visible sphere. No shadows fall thick enough to hurt the eyes; instead every surface wears a soft white halo. This halation makes measuring distance untrustworthy, so the hollow appears even smaller than it is, a pocket worn smooth at the corner of a vast, cooled continent.

The rules of this place have finished being written and are now merely reiterated.

Rule One: Nothing dominates. When any form starts to prosper beyond fractional presence, its waste signal is recruited by counter-systems. The tree will begin to feed on the excess sparrows made of paper that colonised the lower hollows yesterday, converting them into a faint golden mist that feeds the moss. In turn the moss will relinquish excess sugars through the air as sublimated carbon lace, which drifts up and alloys itself into the cooling vanes. A circle of moderate devastation and moderate renewal has whirled here so long that the scene has reached what geologists might call an erosion steady-state — a place that will look the same in another ten million years although every single atom has changed sides thousands of times.

Rule Two: doorknobs into physics are not for turning by the living. The fractal gates hum because they are ajar, but if a mammalian finger lingers too long the rules of the pocket change slightly to forbid that particular geometry of warmth. The finger will not be harmed; it will simply slide out unable to remember the angle at which it first made the surface. This is the opposite of malice — a childproofing for a cosmos that long ago outgrew curiosity.

Rule Three: everything that happens here dreams aloud into the moss antennae and is returned translated into weather. The sigh in the spires becomes tomorrow’s breath; the vanes’ warmth becomes next week’s basking lizard; the death of a single insect forecasts a drizzle in three days precisely calibrated to rescue a dying polyp. The pace is active enough that a human stood inside for a month looking away at the wrong moments can miss entire seasonal sonatas, but quiet enough that the same human can sit and finish a long sentence without blinking back noise.

And if you left now, stepping off the woven moss, within twelve metres the light would dim outside, the air cool by one free degree, and the sound thicken with wider city noises of much younger ground. The pocket would seal behind you; in its centre the ancient contraption would continue to mill time in miniature, bright and slow, brimming with an uninsistent, mostly mute, and verging-on-finished life.
