The air is a lung-heavy soup of steam and metallic aerosols.  
It is 2.7 hot, but the wetness (1.8) makes the heat feel like the inside of a boiler that has forgotten to close its blow-off valve.  
Your first step off the skiff is not onto solid ground but onto a crust only a few centimetres thick—an intergrown alloy of rust and dried salts that floats on an ankle-deep, glass-clear brine. Every footfall cracks the crust with a soft sigh; warm water wells up, squeezing ochre mud through the fissure. The crust refills in seconds, as though the plain were a living scab.

There is no vegetation—0.2, none—yet the place is not dead. What grows here is fauna, and it grows the way ivy or kudzu grows in other worlds. The buildings grow the way coral grows.  
Foundations, locals call them: animals that secrete architecture.  
Each “building” is a colony, kilometres long, flattened by the low elevation (0.9). From far away it looks as if some exhausted civilisation threw down factories, refineries, row-houses, cathedrals of pipe—then let them sink ankle-deep into brine. Closer, you see the walls breathe. The vertical ribs are lattice-works of shell-calcite secreted by crustace-sized arthropods; the dark mouths of bay doors are actually spiracles inhaling the alkaline drift. Every span, truss, and window arch is a skeleton—exoskeleton—renewed nightly by its tiny masons. The surface is warm to the touch, both because of the outside air and because the creatures inside keep their nurseries at a cosy 38 °C.

The light is 1.2 dim: the sun is a blood-orange coin locked behind a permanent haze of mineral dust and steam. Nothing casts a crisp shadow; things appear as washed-out silhouettes drowned in tangerine glow. The haze reaches all the way to the horizon, so the scale (1.5) feels like an open but unfinished thought—blocky industrial ribs fade into coral fuzz, then into orange void. The emptiness (density 0.2) is the emptiness between colonies: kilometres of brine-rimmed crust with no shelter, no plant, no vehicle, nothing taller than your knee except distant stacks puffing pale steam.

Sound (1.0, mostly quiet) is the absence you notice first. No leaf hiss, no creaking timber, no birdcall. What little you hear is tidal: the soft slap of brine under the crust, the distant clatter of colonies expanding—shell clacking on shell—like someone stacking glass bottles in a padded room. Occasionally, a pressure valve somewhere in the colony’s intestinal pipework pops, and a white jet of vapor whistles for two heartbeats. Then quietness reknits itself.

Erosion (2.2) has reached equilibrium: the crust is eaten at the same rate the colonies raise themselves. Every storm (and storms come every few days, brief tantrums of hot rain) dissolves the upper millimetres of crust, forcing the micro-arthropods to add a new lamina by dawn. The city is thus a standing wave, always old and always newborn. Urban (2.2) does not mean planned; it means accreted desire lines of billions of calcite insects. Walkways are polished shell-bone winding in the direction humans most often stepped; they curl like cow paths through a meadow—only the meadow is architecture.

At night the colonies phosphoresce. The insects store daylight in rare-earth salts and bleed it back as aquamarine tracery. In the orange-black dusk the whole plain becomes circuitry, veins of cold neon mapping chambers you could enter if you found the right spiracle. Tourists do enter, lured by the aqua writing. They follow a corridor whose floor is warm mammal-breathhed bone, whose ceiling drips warm mineral water. Thirty metres in they realise the corridor is narrowing: calcite plates are sliding inward at millimetre pace. The city digests them—slowly, like an oyster growing a pearl around a grain of grit—adding their belt buckles, eyelets, copper nerves to the structure. By morning new balconies bud where boots last stood. Industrial (2.2) production here is organic, continuous, and hungry for alloy.

Fauna (2.6 overwhelming) is the only kingdom present, and it dominates in three strata:

1. The micro-masons (already mentioned) whose skyscrapers we inhabit.  
2. Filter-winged midges that live on the steam plumes above each colony, wings turned bronze by metal condensate. In flocks of billions they look like rusted confetti drifting the clouds. They do not bite; they simply orbit, mating in mid-air, laying eggs on any warm surface foolish enough to jut into the breeze.  
3. The big wanderers: hermit crabs the size of delivery vans that wear empty boiler tanks instead of shells. They clatter across the crust at dusk, seeking colonies whose chimneys still breathe sweet heat. At the base of a stack they wedge open a spiracle, crawl inside, curl up, and let the warm exhalation bathe their gills all night. Entire districts schedule themselves around the nightly invasion of these gentle giants; a hallway that closes too slowly will be snapped off like a twig tube, rolled away, repurposed into crab-carapace by morning.

Temperature gradients create local weather: steam devils spin up between colonies, carrying midge bronze into the haze, plating the clouds with metallic frost. When the devils collapse, glitter rains in slow-motion—foil-thin flakes that melt on the crust and re-enter the colony as tomorrow’s platelet. Dynamic (1.8): everything is in motion, just not in haste. Growth is visible if you stare for ten minutes; weather cycles every hour; digestion of trespassers happens overnight. The place is feverish, yet calm, like a patient who has lived with fever so long he calls it home.

Rules of the world are simple, but they are non-negotiable:

- Nothing artificial stays separate; every imported object is raw feed.  
- Nothing green is tolerated; chlorophyll triggers an immune response—colony pores seal, vent ammonia until the offending pigment bleaches away. Travellers learn quickly to leave leafy charms at the skiff.  
- Movement must be continuous. To stand still on the crust for more than an hour invites spontaneous paving: micro-masons rise through the cracks, cement themselves to your boot soles, begin engulfing. Locals sway, shuffle, jog in place, never planting roots.  
- Exchange is by weight, not by coin. Copper, nickel, rare earths are siphoned into the colony; in return the insects extrude whatever tool you need—knife, pipe, lens—fashioned from last night’s crust. Trade ends when balance is achieved; offer too much metal and the colony will overgrow, split, found a daughter city overnight. Offer too little and the wall you leaned against will thin, collapse, drown you in warm brine.

What you thought was a town is therefore a parliament of animals impersonating a factory district, built low because height is difficult in thin hot air, spreading wide because across the plain is easier than upward. It smells of salt, hot rust, and the faint iodine tang of insect blood. It sounds of your own breathing, the crunch of crust, and the almost imagined song of exoskeletons stacking themselves toward another indistinguishable dawn.
