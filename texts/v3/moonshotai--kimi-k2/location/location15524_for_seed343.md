Between the knife-edged summits of the continent’s spine, the sky thins into a violet haze; distances roll away beneath you not in land at all but in living canopy, as if the mountains themselves were only the jagged rib cage of a single sleeping organism. From every cliff leaks a slow sweat of condensation that never quite gathers into rain: droplets appear on leaf-veins, on glossy insect elytra, on the oxidized bronze of forgotten hand-forged nails, then vanish again—the water-trace is perpetual yet never accumulates. Warm air rises in slow glutinous pulses; it carries the smell of yeast, sap, and something almond-bitter, the signature of a million flowers blooming decade after decade on the same rot-fattened stems.

Vegetation is not a cover—it is the ground. Roots thicker than river cables wind down cliff faces and braid across ravines, forming bridges that flex like living suspension lines. Leaves grow on every possible axis, overlapping in vertical lakes of green so dense that sunlight sluices down in filtered sheets the color of young wine. Epiphytes cling to epiphytes; orchid roots dangle like abandoned caterpillar hammocks; vines stalk the trunks they sprouted from, strangling and feeding in the same motion. In hollows where erosion has finally triumphed, the walls are scalloped by centuries of drip not into stone but into bark-callus—grooves and hollows polished to the texture of old ivory.

Sound is so total it becomes texture rather than signal: a chained chorus of cicadas droning in ribbons of pitch, the soft million-footed rasp of millipedes across cellulose, the slow digestive creak of stems fattening in the heat, the low constant fracture of living wood expanding. Occasionally an echo blossoms—a bird cry, or something bird-shaped that unfolds four membranous wings and a mouth lined with glassy teeth—but the forest absorbs and refracts the noise, scatters it back multiplied until mind gives up locating a source and accepts the roar as weather.

Movement is everywhere. Mammal scale never developed here; the megafauna are blossoms. Eight-meter trumpet-flowers open at dusk at heart-rate speed, exhaling a visible plume of warm perfume that draw swarms of thumb-sized scarabs. They drum against stamen for hours; the vibration travels down the stalk, miles away, and triggers a neighboring vine to release its cargo of glutinous seeds—an endless relay in zero darkness, blind semaphore using only touch and chemistry. Near the valley’s low point, mats of pale moss contract and swell like breathing diaphragms, audible in low bass thuds you feel in the sternum more than hear. 

Humans left only pinpricks of themselves. Every third ridge shelters one half-collapsed shelter woven from liana and cane, roof long since swallowed by strangler fig. In the driest niche someone once chipped a stela of soft greenish slate: a hand-width panel covered in spiral glyphs whose grooves are now pouches for ant colonies; a bronze chisel lies beside it, corrosion flaked into leaf-shaped greens, the wooden haft petrified into lignin fossils you could still lift like iron. These artifacts radiate small eddies of emptiness—spaces no stem dares cross, circles three strides across where the soil is silvery with termite sand. Empty enough that your own footsteps sound courtly and over-important.

Predators move differently here. There is a species of tree-cat that is barely more than spine and fur stretched across a ribcage of hollow bones; it is all wing membrane and patience, clambering head-down on the hush of a single vine, pouncing four meters onto beetle columns, collapsing the entire stalk like a bent spring then riding its recoil back into the high green. Coloration is conversation: when a branch breaks on the far ridge it answers instantly with flashing pigment patterns along trunks—a consortium of plants relaying warning like nerve cells.

Altitude does not thin life, merely elongates it. The highest spires above the canopy support gardens of lichen shaped like paper boats catching the last sunlight; their undersides host hosts of diaphanous spiders that spin neon threads whose sole purpose seems to be recording the wind’s music. At dusk these threads phosphoresce in light green, an airy script that hangs between peaks like punctuation no sentence will ever complete.

Nothing decays; it is resorbed. Fallen leaves fuse back into root within days; corpses of animals atrophy into pools of rich pigment that stain the bark for centuries—ochre, burnt umber, livid mauve—still as if the mountains keep a weather map of every death in living dye. The oldest wounds have settled into final geometries: buttresses fluted like organ pipes, trunks pleated by centuries of torsion, burls grown around invisible knots of ancient grief. Erosion is now a negotiation between plant will and mineral fatigue, the slowest argument on earth, almost concluded.

If you remain long enough, your sense of scale loosens. After days the far wall no longer looks one mile distant but one layer of cells away; time is measured in sap-rise not heartbeats; the boundary of self is blurred into the foliate chorus. All edges remember they were once roots. Even mineral grit lodged under your nail is eventually claimed—overnight a pale lichen explores the ridge of skin with exploratory filaments, tasting for purchase.

Withdraw in the evening and the entire valley exhales a warm breath that smells of drying hay, sudden rain on pavement, and something citrus bittersweet rising from the follicles inside every stem. Sound drops a semitone as the night tribe awakens—treefrogs the color of wet limes, moths wider than your open hands, beetles that strike their elytra together in rolling millisecond waves so precise you can map wind direction by their acoustic flicker. 

Stay perfectly still and you understand the rules: silence is permission. The forest tolerates you so long as your heartbeat matches the slow systole of dusk warming into midnight. Take one step more and the whole domain adjusts its grip—vines tense, scents shift, somewhere a coiled limb silently recalculates trajectory. Movement thanks acceptable; novelty scans, decodes, absorbs. You walk, but it grows the path around your ankles even as your foot descends, bridging air with living bridge so gently you do not notice you are not following but being invented.

When you leave this place nothing on your body is sterile anymore. Pollen powders the folds of skin behind your ears; microscopic nematodes decorate your shoelaces like commemorative stitching. Sleep brings dreams pitched at the same dense frequency you just spent days walking through—the brain rehearses its escape route, but the ecology has already inscribed the map; your breathing falls in sync with that slow bass thud of moss diaphragms thousands of meters below, and this will continue every night of your life, a private metronome borrowed from the continent's ancient green lung.

Somewhere, far above, sun catches the naked last fang of rock that no root has ever colonized. The summit stands exactly one inch taller than the forest can reach, forever a monarch’s solitary crown; but even that shard of sky receives, each morning, a single petal blown loose from the canopy, sticking by the dew of dawn—a tiny red flag claiming sovereignty for the plant nation, day after day, with polite, relentless love.
