You stand on the lip of an exhausted caldera so high that altitude sickness will claim the unwary despite the desert heat. Below you, the crater of the mountain opens onto a basin the length of a small city. It is not empty. Time and fate have packed it wall-to-wall until the valley resembles the inside of an iron lung three kilometres across.

The floor comprises a joined mosaic of salt pans, each turned pale amethyst by centuries of copper leachate. Every pan is edged by raised adobe ridges no higher than your knee—ancient, eroded labour equal parts dam and handwriting. Where pans butt against pans their walls have starred and shrunk until they look like aerial runes. Salt crumbles underfoot like glass snow; beneath lies the thinnest film of brine—never dry, never enough to stand as water—kept wet by nightly condensation that freezes an hour before dawn and steams away in the first ultraviolet at six a.m.. Thorn-salt, crook-root, and blue glasswort sprout in dots, tucking single leaves into fissures. Tiny red blossom mites crawl those stems at dusk and dawn, drinking dewlights: they are the primary pollinators of the basin and their skirr is the loudest sound until one of the distant engines coughs alive.

For crowding the place, there are no trees. Only steel.

Infarct Station dominates the eastern quadrant—seven storeys of iron ribs sheened with verdigris, completely open to the sky. An original mining frame reworked century after century, its lattice now serves as scaffolding for condensers, pneumatics, elevators, cabling enough to garrotte an elephant. Cisterns hang at every level, bellies dripping directly onto salt pans whose owners plug stigmatic little tubes beneath to harvest the condensate by rulebook and ancient contract. The rights-of-extraction rotate like seasons, and the signboards hung from cables change colour week by week as markets pivot from water to soda to nickel. A single vertical railway climbs the caldera wall out of the structure—one track, one car, still grunting up once a day with palettes of charcoal and returning with ingots and glass. You will never ride it; nothing does unless it is tied onto a pallet first.

The lowest pans abut the built bulk of Narrow Guage Works, a serpentine engine-house whose bellows rattle the ground every eight hours. The sound rolls outward like a tide front that arrives at you as a whispered exhale. Small beasts—mouse-marmots whose ancestors colonised the lifts—run the steam-pipes like spies. A colony of leather-wing bats nests in the rivet heads and erupts at twilight to skim the pans, species evolved a micron larger for thinner air. Another bat species lives only in the shift-whistles: hollow brass tubes tuned to three notes; at dusk the valves open and piped air plays the pipes a single repeating chord. Those bats sleep within the whistles until resonance wakes them; one can set their watch by it.

North, beyond the inevitable hail of rust flakes, lies the Thoroughfare: a narrow paved passage tilted slightly downhill like a megaphone pouring itself empty. Shops take up residence wherever the walls creased inward enough to provide shade—once canvas tents of salters, now translucent bio-vinyl evaporators operated by snail-goats and their keepers. (A snail-goat is exactly that: a sheep-sized snail whose spiral shell is gilt with goat-fur and who extrudes edible lactate threads. The children spear them with umbrella iron and drag them home like sleds.) The thoroughfare lets us pretend the basin is a town. In truth it is only a mutable trellis on which to hang the week-long communes that form, dissolve and re-form around the watery fractions.

Higher still, pinned against the glowing west rim, the Old Foundry has become a cliff-top citadel—no tower, just one thick slab cantilevered out into space, glowing bronze in afternoon sun. Long ago its furnaces cooled; now roost three silver-necked condors whose wingspan has adapted to the lower pressure. They ride the hot updrafts from Narrow Gauge Works without flapping, circling higher until they are almost invisible, then dive in perfect silence to take the mouse-marmots skipping between pipes. There is no scream of prey; the air is simply too thin to carry it.

Night is when the place recognises its own ecology. The brine film re-silvered, the condensers singing the deep B-flat of recovering pressure, every owner stamps the last permitted drip and ties coloured ribbons to their plugs—red means claim will resume in four standard days, green means loaned to sister-pan, yellow means something went wrong and nobody remembers what. Thousands of ribbons wave in micrometre breezes that human skin cannot feel. Bats and rodents staple the sky; salt mites crunch under boot soles; snail-goats graze the vapour condenser pads, and just before full dark you see the phosphorescent tread of the ghost ore-crabs still hauling rust along forever-sorting conveyor belts nobody bothered to turn off when the veins dried.

Here, nothing is “built” but everything is built-into. You step and the ground tweets under five centuries of seasons stamped in iron filings and salt. You touch a hand to the adobe ridge and get a dust fingerprint lighter than your soul. The valley’s rule is simple: the hotter the day, the slower the legal exchange of water; the colder the night, the louder the whistle-chord that signals bats and boilers alike; and the higher you climb the less adiabatic law protects you so move quickly or carry oxygen. Live within those parameters and the basin allows coexistence. Break them and you will find that “scattered” human structures have moods; the lattice hospital will stitch you into its piping overnight as a new collector of water, and the Tueshift pans will swallow you before breakfast so that tomorrow the salt looks a little more rose.

Quiet, active, insane only by half measures—Infarct Basin sleeps while every tetrahedron of its packed existence ticks like a watch, breathing high hot air in and birthing cool salts out in delicate metabolic balance older than the language by which it gives fair warning.
