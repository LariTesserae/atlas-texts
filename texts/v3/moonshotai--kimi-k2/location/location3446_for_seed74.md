It begins where the last cobblestones give up their straight line and sag, exhausted, into moss.  A town—hardly more than a rumor of one—spreads its ribs open to the air, but the ribs are living wood.  The factories here do not manufacture goods; they manufacture habitat.  Every wall is a cambium layer.  Every chimney is a hollow trunk exhaling cool breath scented faintly with chloroform and wet iron.  You notice the damp only after you’ve walked a minute: sleeves heavy, the rasp of condensation at the throat, as though the whole settlement were one lung learning to breathe again after a long illness.

Vegetation owns the balance sheet.  Brick-sized blocks of lignin mortared together rise three, four, five irregular stories—townhouses of cellulose—then flare outward into flying buttresses of woven liana.  Between them, avenues stay wide enough for a single tram that never arrives, because the rails have long since been ingrown by a slow guerrilla of root and twig.  In places the trackbed lives: twin lines of glossy green, photosynthesizing, drinking the weak sun that leaks through a latticework canopy overhead, a vault stitched from the ribs of countless liana loops.  They call it “normal light” here, but it arrives already half-digested by leaves, the color of secondhand money.

Coolness is a polite landlord; it never touches the skin directly.  Instead it manages the contract between water and air, keeping them perpetually at negotiation.  Fog beads on eyelashes, runs off eaves of bracket fungus.  A rain may fall, but only as a remembered obligation—ten drops in a minute, then nothing for hours, as if the sky owes interest and refuses to pay principal.

The industrial note comes piston-beaten through distance.  Somewhere inside the xylem city a few machines insist on the nineteenth century: pressure valves sigh, flywheels complain, a loom of algal filaments is being wound onto brass bobbins that clack like a slow metronome.  The factories are rare because the town is small—cathedral-small, not continent-small—and because any expansion would first require convincing the trees.  Management holds daily conferences with the mycorrhizae; minutes are kept in spores.

Erosion has finished its argument.  What you see is a verdict: surfaces rounded, joints settled, corners softened until every edge invites the palm.  Even the skyline has slumped; towers of living wood lean together like drunks who know the songs are over but can’t yet leave.  Yet the place is active.  Everywhere valves pulse.  Bark doors breathe.  Sap shuttles freight through transparent conduits that parallel the old cast-iron water mains.  A currency of sugars buys silence from the microbes; the quiet you hear is not absence but barter.

Fauna, being scarce, has gone ceremonial.  A single kingfisher—blue as a gas flame—perches on a traffic sign no driver has read in half a century.  Its crown feathers drip, though the air itself is hardly wetter than a sigh.  You may wait an hour and still meet only that bird, plus maybe one moth whose wings are etched with the map of the town’s original deed.  Predation is obsolete; everything negotiates instead.  Insects sign tiny treaties stipulating three drops of sap per month.  The kingfisher is mayor by acclamation, re-elected each dawn when it chooses to call.

Civics are vegetal.  To register residence you press your thumb into the pith of a doorpost and wait for cambium to rise around it.  To vote you release a breath rich in carbon dioxide toward the candidate-shrub of your choice; by dusk the one with the greatest internode lengthening wins.  Outsiders worry this is too slow, but the residents point out that policy, once enacted, is literally wooden: difficult to repeal, but warm in winter, fragrant in bloom.

Travel instructions:

- Walk always downhill; the place is low-lying by treaty with gravity, a bowl collecting its own exhalations.  
- Speak softly.  Sound here is on a ration; too much of it encourages the growth of audition-vines, which are harmless but will record everything you say and repeat it next year as blossoms.  
- If you must shelter overnight, choose a hollow factory.  They keep their furnaces banked just below blood temperature; curl on a pallet of shredded root and you will wake damp but unharmed, smelling of cedar and machine oil.  
- Do not pick the fruit that swings like Edison bulbs from the tram-line lianas.  It is not forbidden—there is no law—but the electricity inside is theirs, not yours, and the cost is extracted in dreams.

You will leave when the fog decides.  A single strand will loop your wrist, tug once politely, and guide you back to the margin where the straight world resumes.  The town does not care whether you stay; it keeps its own balance, adding one ring per decade, subtracting one whistle per century, always the same quiet surplus of wet air, always the same cathedral-small, sparse, inarguable green.
