The place is a walled garden the size of a single long courtyard, hemmed by ninety-foot cliffs that once were walls.  Elevation is its tallest truth: the raised rectangle sits on a wind-whipped spur of mountain that had forgotten the clouds themselves.  Yet no water arrives anymore; it drained away eons ago into fissures as tight as secrets, leaving the stone mineral-sheened and thirsty.  Bone-pale slabs of chert and sapphire-dyed quartz rise in stepped battlements, fissured by innumerable millennia so that every toehold has become a resting-place for dust the colour of burnt almond.

Within this tiny coliseum—hardly the measure of three townhouses laid end to end—vegetation refuses everything one learns about drought.  Silver spaghetti-stemmed vines pour over fallen pillars, looping one ledge to the next in dense pleats; each loop is thick as a ship’s hawser, grey-green where light fades into leaf backs, pearly where light kisses them.  Their shade shelters a sporadic undergrowth of squat plants that look like half-closed hands made of jade.  From those fingers black nectaries no wider than pinheads secrete a viscous, tarry honey that never dries; when two leaves sway far enough to touch they tangle and fuse, forming more ropes in slow knots.  Over centuries these have built a netted ceiling—more knitting than canopy—choking what once was open evening sky into eighteen horizontal tunnels of air, each large enough for a single person to crawl.  The floor underneath is a mat of leaf husks so old they have become felt.  Deep below the felt, capillary roots race across the rock, tapping only calcium and the memory of rain.

Fauna count is prodigal and loud.  Cockroach-sized crickets stridulate in the treble like jeweller’s files run over glass.  Drop-lizards cling to every rope of vine with tenacious bluish tail digits, tiny bellows throats ballooning to hoot: a sound part flute, part finger drawn across wet pottery.  The hoots never quite disappear; they shift from register to register as slow waves chase each other around the enclosure, producing an effect of overlapping murmured conversations.  Nests of copper-shouldered moths swing like pouches from the knitting; the moths themselves stay dormant at this season, stacked inside cocoons resembling rolled parchment.  Their wings, when unfolded, are eye-white on the underside and reflecting what little light there is.  On the rare occasions one flexes, the whole garden flashes as if a hand mirror suddenly turned.  Each phase of flash seems rehearsed, delayed by two slow counts—match, pause—creating a metronome under the insect chorus.  Every hole in leaf disc has a resident hyaline beetle whose wing cases capture the flashes and delay them again, so the garden appears to speak in slow Morse to itself.

The only water remaining lies locked within the plants: they exude no vapour, lose none to night, for any that escapes freezes at the margins and hovers as frost petals for minutes before subliming invisibly.  No river, no trickle, not even dew.  Dry is the law, but lushness is its fluent shrug, the plants having traded hydrous chemistry for silica-walled vacuoles and an internal circulation of viscous resins.  In breaking them open you would find liquid older than civilisations, quietly viscous like amber in an ear.

Built traces stud the garden modestly and at angles.  Waterless fountains stand pedestal-high, wrought of rosy limestone thread-veined with quartz.  Each broken-bird-basin holds a nest of curled vines now turned to punched fretwork.  Steps—fourteen to the northern edge—were the corner of what once was a modest citadel: each tread is dished at centre by uncountable footfalls that resist further erosion, for the stone has passed even beyond rounding into softly bloomed eggshell, holding fingerprint pits no deeper than breath.  Yet no metal here: every carving shows only chisel, wooden mallet, rasp and patience.  Primitive tooling marks are possessed of their own polish, so that grooves shine like lessons completed.

Light arrives feeble from a sky held high beyond the cliff rims: a sallow steady lid giving shadowglow.  The knitting above diffuses it further, so translucences layer translucences; mid-air the photons resemble dust-heavy water pooling rather than travelling.  Not quite twilight, never glare, merely the dimness that coin-op machines used to spill on sidewalk.  One odd generosity: when the moths finally choose dusk—every third month—they perform a flight whose wing-flaps occlude and unblock portions of the lid, letting stripes of brighter kindness fall briefly.  These stripes travel laterally like ghost tigers and are gone in a blink, but seedlings will lean for days after wherever a stripe grazed them, elongating by a finger’s breadth toward places light once was.

Time moves at a glacier’s indoor cousin.  Rain fell last a billion weighted heartbeats ago; vines thicken at the speed of rust.  Leaves signed to fuse this year will have merged into imperceptible scars by six winters.  The crickets’ pitch drops by a quarter cycle every twelve local years, but to them it does not seem so; their brains are spread across generations, so the song is continuous even as its centre stepwise declines.  Population curves are broad sine waves—birth spikes every century, but smoothly enough it looks like constant droning.  The garden itself has no calendar, ignores calendars; its ontology is antique settled, primordial in that sense geological epochs aspire to.  It has outlasted the reasons anyone ever laid stone here.  It may outlast the idea of reason.

Rules settle in whispers:

– If you take anything living, give something hard in return: a pebble, a splinter of bone, a word.  The garden remembers the vowel shapes of words and will repeat them in rain that never comes, stuffing cracks with their rice-paper ghosts overnight.
– Light looted from a brighter stripe can be pocketed only once; twice, and the next stripe petrifies you where you stand, turning your outline into an arboreal fossil woven into the knitting forever as warning shadow—two visitors have already thus become trellis ribs.
– When the crickets pause for more than five heartbeats, stop speaking.  Plant life starts to listen.  It has been learning human grammar in slow reverse, and anybody who talks during the lull will discover their own speech pattern growing on vines next season—letters hanging like berries; the soundless lips contort to speak raw nitrogen and the listener will hear what they once said.
– Fire is disallowed.  Even starlight would refuse to strike matches here.

Entering means climbing a staircase that only exists because it has been left between two cliff-slabs: one must haul oneself up the final shelf on tendrilled vines that politely grip wrists then let go.  Descending is easier but gloved in regret.  Inside, you walk on hush, beneath embroidered air.  Your footfall is soaked up by felt; above, the lattice awaits your heartbeat.  The whole garden listens, then decides at its own tempo whether to grow around you.
