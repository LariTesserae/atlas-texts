Beneath a sky that is only faintly hazed, the Heavenly Geyser-Range lifts out of the thickest air on the planet. Wind coming across the Warm Belt has skimmed and toiled for thousands of kilometers above water, and when it finally meets these cliffs it is so moist that it chimes against the basalt like liquid glass. Every rock, therefore, is running. Not in rivulets but in fine sequined sheets: one layer of sleeve-thin water sliding over another, the outermost taking the heat, the inner keeping the stone cool. Nothing else looks like it since the sky itself thins to ultraviolet overhead; even the sun, at noon, is a copper wafer you could pick up without burning your fingers—there is just too much wet air pressed under it.

Five mountains shoulder around a single water-filled caldera the size of a city. Peak to peak they are no more than three kilometers apart and yet rise more than seven above the level of the sea. Their flanks are therefore almost ladders of forest. The height compresses life: lianas that curve like green tongues, leaves that drip fierce heat in size and color, an explosion of orchids that store water in translucent bladders as big as melons. Gigantic bromeliads squat on every branch like metallic frogs. Roots do not go down; the soil is too thin. Instead they branch and double back, making spongy buttresses that weave together until every tree hugging the central ridge grips every other, and in a gust the whole mountain seems to breathe together.

Where the canopy meets the ledges of cooled lava, gigantic ferns uncurl, their spores glittering. They adjust to altitude by curling tighter and tighter until their spore heads look like rolled-up surgeons’ scrolls. From these scrolls every dawn emits a faint aroma of hot bread and ozone; the fauna call it “the Kneading Time.” Creatures emerge. Metallic macaques with magnesium-furred calves leap along root buttresses; birds like loose bolts of copper wire spear moths that have glass wings and carry lantern-spores in their abdomen; fish-scale geckos race upside-down, bubbles clenched to their tails. When a caiman cracked open the canyon wall centuries ago, it found the top of the mountain three kilometers above sea level, so now white caimans climb—spectral, their hides so heated they steam. They use the fog as gills; nostrils close, skin exhales. They say nothing, but they blink dry eyes and vanish between buttress roots like pale ghosts.

Every dusk the cliff spills water anew. From above it looks as if gravity doubled back on itself and the ridge simply leaks upward in steam. Steam meets coastward wind, spins, and over the caldera’s warm lake forms a perfectly calm inversion shell. Sound beneath is an inverter: everything here sounds closer than it is. You will hear wings you cannot see, teeth ticking underwater, and, somewhere, what might be a factory accelerating impossibly far below forest line. Because there is industry here—just not human.  

Sixty years ago, harvester-wasps brought bamboo-iron mixed with sulphur back to the mountain and built kilometer-long funicular spires of it inside the rock. Inside these shafts they layer rocks and fungus through which river-grade water is pumped by heat alone. The skin of every shaft is hotter than the air but colder than the lava heart of the mountain, so a convection vortex forms and keeps the pistons turning. All day the mountain plays its muffled turbine song, half organ, half hydraulic sigh. No blade turns that is not edged in lichens faintly phosphorescent; no gasket that is not braided with living vine. It is production, but production in balance: the waste heat floats upward with spores and seeds and is itself harvested by the leaf tongues above.  

Between the peaks lie hanging meadows—miniature archipelagos of soil trapped by root nets in the air. Some are only as wide as a single tree crown, others kilometer-long shelves dangling over the lake. Flossweed blooms there at midnight with indigo spikes whose petals clap shut when touched by fog, creating a drumming that spiders answer by vibrating silver lines. All of this hangs in silence because there is no stone to echo against—only moss and water-skin.  

At the foot of the range the geothermal spine finally meets dense rainforest, where water leaves heat behind. There, on the lake’s rim, grows a belt of mangrove-like species, but instead of pneumatophores fingered into mud, their roots beam upwards like antennae, catching the last mist of heat and wearing skirts of nitrifying algae. There fourteen-foot electric eels breed, ache across mud, rear and call like wolves, their bodies pulsing faint blue lightning that triggers photosynthesis directly on algae skirts. When lightning is sufficient, the entire root ridge begins to glow—soft dusk-panel of living green neon.  

The rules of this world operate at the level of heat-differential: water must be liquid (boned by pressure and solute) within forty degrees of its boiling point. Movement is achieved most efficiently by swelling and shrinking. Every root is therefore also a muscle, every leaf a lung. The fauna have learned the molecular weaknesses in water-skin: they bite the surface of lakes and swing up drapes of it they lift over their backs as cloaks until the water rejoins itself.  

Night lasts only four terrestrial hours, because the planet is slowly tide-locked to its sun. For that brief interval the turbines slow, the meadows descent a centimeter under cool weight, and all singing ceases. Then nothing moves except steam, drifting in long curtains between stars. 

You hear the factory preparing freshwater filters for the dawn, a thousand little turbine sighs like someone winding clocks.
