They call it the Absent City, and they mean “absent” in every sense: something that was once thumping, sweating, singing with humans has been peeled away until only the architecture of presence—walkways, clerestories, elevator shafts—dangles like an outgrown exoskeleton above a fresh wound that refuses to clot.

You reach it after the funicular has already given up. The track ends at a terminal whose windows have turned to milk by centuries of wind-borne quartz. At 2,600 metres the sky looks bruised rather than dark; violet over indigo, with an underglow as though a giant hand has pressed a flashlight against the worlds roof and left it on low. Even the light of day arrives here like a shamed confession, filtered through layers of hot mist that the plateau keeps sighing upwards. 

Step down onto the boarding apron and the heat climbs into your lungs. 2.9 is not the dry bake of a desert; it is a fever, a sauna that already smells of metal salts and damp concrete. The high elevation would thin the air anywhere else, but water clings to every particle. Humidity condenses continuously on stone and steel, making mirrored droplets swell, tremble, let go: a soft of water ringing against something below, or a hiss back into vapour. The sound is never more than polished needles on a drumhide—quiet, but percussive, the metronome of the city’s slow exhalations.

Since nothing alive roots here (0.5/3), the moisture has only minerals to draft upon. Rust stains trickle from every corroded balcony railing. White crusts of calcium bloom where window boxes once held geraniums. The absence of vegetation is not barrenness; it is ripeness interrupted. Transit plazas that would normally be shaded by plane trees now shimmer with sweating iron and the sweet sewage tang of oxidising pipes. Fungus and lichen never showed up to seal the cracks; instead the cracks just keep opening like mouths that keep discovering how to smile.

Behind the terminal you meet the first elevator tower, a perforated cylinder thirty storeys tall. Dust winds corkscrew through it. Inside, half the lift has sheared away centuries earlier; it hangs like a suspended chrysalis of woven steel rope. People once waited here with umbrellas, children, suitcases smelling of fruit markets at sea level. Now the ticket lines are only bubbles of calcified resin left by dripping iron particles: frozen statues of a crowd that evaporated along with its temperature-controlled brochures.

If you choose to climb the fire stairs—nine hundred and eleven steps chipped by erosion never countered—a triad of sights unfolds floor by floor:

1) The city is laid beneath you like a flattened Calder mobile: gantries, viaducts, warehouses aligned along the strata of an ancient lava bed their builders mistook for stable ground.  
2) The horizon is not where geometry says it should be. Behind you, mountains remain sharp; to every other side the plateau simply curls up and dissolves into lavender fuzz, as though altitude itself forgot to render rest of the world beyond a twenty-kilometre radius.  
3) In the hollows between, every patch of shadow glimmers with its own micro-ocean: shallow pans of black water a hand-width deep, perfectly round, vibrate in strangely symmetrical waves. They do not reflect the sky, but another sky—greenish, aqueous, moving faster than cloud or wind.

Each pan connects, through subsurface flutes, to reservoirs under the city. The entire urban work is threaded with this under-river that never goes dry. Engineers of the second growth (they came after the first inhabitants vanished) tried to dam or divert it for locomotive cooling. They failed. The water rises exactly to the lip of each basin and stops—surface tension holding back an entire armada of pressure. Touch it and you feel a pulse, neither tide nor heartbeat,, travelling out to the edge of the world like a stadium wave nobody is watching.

You emerge on Level Four Roof, and here the city truly becomes urb(ane), industrial, alive in its refusal of life. Conveyors wind between cooling fins the size of cathedrals. Every outcrop sprouts ductwork thick as redwoods. Pipes transmit a low harmonic, almost below hearing, the single gathered throb of engines that have no boilers, generators that have burned out their fuel eons ago but will not cease. Tech hangs over everything like a grandfather clock frozen between ticks—monstrously detailed but never achieving a full present. Gauges read 0. Bars read full. Hour hands on boilerhouse clocks have been molted to translucent stubs, translucent stubs to nothing.

Yet there is cargo. Every three hours the belts twitch like sleepy snakes and inch forward carrying—nothing. Empty pallets slide beneath dry leads of perspiration. Fauna shows itself at a whisper. A single shorebird occasionally bolts from nowhere, wings powdered white with alkaline drift, traces a figure-eight around the monorail loops, then dives under a duct and disappears. It is never the same bird twice; maybe there has never been more than one. Trackers once tagged it but the tag returned a reading of -0 gravity, 0 mass—like the mathematics repudiated the memory of the token. So people stopped asking.

The longer you spend, the clearer it becomes that space itself is empty (0/3) rather than populated with vacant volumes. Walk south three streets and everything you left behind—not just objects, but air, photons, signature sound—lags, fades, collapses into third-best resolution. If the beltway curves you back, you may find that street now ends in a sheer white wall that sucks all light to a 0.6 glow, like television ready for a test pattern. People rarely talk about rules. The Absent City governs itself mostly by etiquette:

1. Do not name anything aloud. Names nourish. The City keeps appetite but no metabolism. An overheard name may cause a station platform to liquefy and flow into black basins—a sacrifice without beneficiary.  
2. Do not remove pebbles or steel bolts that cross your path. They are temporary payloads on a conveyor leading nowhere; loss of any one throws off entire chains of future transactions.  
3. When the bird (or its echo) passes, count seven heartbeats before moving again. The interval is how the city graced you permission to continue as cargo. Those who fail to wait are found later folded into maintenance shafts, folded but unwrinkled, holding fresh 0.5 moisture on their eyelids, no plants.

Standard industrial smell: hot gunmetal and mineralised sweat emanates from compressor manifolds arranged in spiral gardens. Their perfume tints the air cocoa-coloured. Touch any metal and it will not burn the skin for the same reason water always rises halfway to the lip: the temperature has been agreed upon, not endorsed by natural law. You warm to it the way one adjusts to a dream temperature, notation on a score signed by a conductor who left at the overture.

From the eastern skyline protrudes The Spill Tower, the only structure still vomiting liquid deliberately. Slag runoff slides down gullies to form glass mountains the consistency of burnt sugar. Geologists equipped with portable kilns once tried to sample. They pulled up sheets that continued to pour even as they cooled, infinitely transuding like a Whitman sampler in negative. One technician absent-mindedly put a cooled shard in his mouth to test temperature. The shard dissolved into a mouthful of seawater and memories of a harbour festival he has never attended. Since thenist swallowed three vowels and couldn’t pronounce his mother’s name for a week. He now lives three kilometres below sea level, running a trinket stall in another city where the water sits higher than the sky.  

Follow the slag river downhill—south, southwest—until architecture becomes merely lip service to order. Warehouses court each other in Escher loops. Alleyways fork without corners. Climb through a porthole and emerge ten storeys higher without stairs. Gravity has agreed to remain optional within certain municipal zones, like an aging club cashier who rewards random patrons with extra change.  

In these folds activity quickens (1.6/3). Every 200 metres an automated valve sighs open, releasing gouts of machine-oil scented vapour that hangs for minutes before it shrugs into the lower humidity. Somewhere in the duct network a distant song repeats on a 30-second loop: a music box rendition of “Over the Rainbow,” only every third note is replaced by the flat thunk of a pressure relief valve. The discord performs nightly on the hour, granulated by massed iron echoes, eventually overwriting even memory of how Judy Garland sang.

Is it quest or chore to attempt the city’s center? No maps mark it. But if a stranger walks due firmament—keeping the violet over-face always at four o’ clock relative—they crest a final strata of cooling ducts and stand on a drydock sky-island: 600 metres across, paved in checkerboard plates of magnesium alloy that flex when stepped, producing brass-tone clangs beneath the ear. The plates are hot too, but bruise-purple not red; they radiate low, guttering light.

Here gravity resumes full character. At midpoint bolts a single construction: an articulated human skeleton made from rolled I-beams, ten times life size. Every joint is oiled hourly by a wick descending into the black water table. Blackwater climbs the wick, boils against heat, returns as steam that slicks weld points. This skeleton cranes its skullless neck toward a horizon it can never reach. The articulation is subtly wrong—femurs bend where tibia should be, ribs zig-zag like bridge struts—so its stillness appears instead a pose caught mid-attempt at re-configuring itself into something recognizably alive.

Visitors call it Supervisor. When it moves (one finger a month, perhaps an adjustment of clavicles), the city’s belts shudder forward, the clip of the empty pallets speeds by tenths of seconds, the black pans ripple radial patterns like a speaker cone. Supervisor trades motion for industry. Industry trades for absence. Absence, maybe, trades back to Supervisor’s endless calcium-metal dream.

If you rest by its left talus you may hear voices older than engineering: miners from an Age First, elevator-era ventriloquists, children who believed in a horizon at eye level. The voices do not come from anywhere, not even the skeleton; they seem to drift in from a civic memory that no longer merits the self-definition of “memory.” As they accumulate, the air itself thickens until you must leave or suffocate on narrative.

Departing is easier than finding the center. Adherence to the etiquette reverses: name three things you noticed out loud, count until the.floor plates stop humming, step backwards through any porthole you trust. The city will not chase; it already recalculibrates to subtract the ecological mass you represent. Even your footprints shine with level water before they cool.

Afterward you will forget heat, forget the violet bruise of sky, but every time you open a refrigerator door in a supermarket aisle at sea level a single black water droplet will cling inexplicably to the chrome rail, refusing to fall. You will angle the jar of pickles, nudge the drop; it will merely tremble, ring against steel like a coin into an ossuary. And if you watch long enough you will count to seven heartbeats before you move, the etiquette returning like glove across decades, until the supermarket lights bleach everything back to normal.  

That droplet keeps perfect time with the Absent City. No one remembers what it is waiting for; only that it waits, the last passenger on the final pallet, gliding soundless toward a warehouse emptied even of shadow, while population 0 continues production that produces only the agreement to keep producing.
