They call it the Hollow Ward, though no one remembers who “they” are.  
From the outside it is only a hinge in the air: two fingers of warm, mineral-bright light pinched together, no wider than a doorway, sparkling like mica. Step through and the hinge closes behind you with the soft pressure of an eyelid shutting out a sound you have already forgotten.

Inside, the place is a city that once drank all its water and now survives on the memory of thirst.  
The temperature is a perpetual late-afternoon of early summer—sun just low enough to throw long bronze shadows, air thick enough to feel like velvet when you inhale. Yet nothing sweats; there is no moisture to spare. The warmth is a function of metabolism, not climate: the buildings breathe.

They are not buildings in the way stone is built. They are grown.  
Each structure is a single, hollowed-out plant—something between baobab and banyan—whose living fibers dried into lignin-ceramic when the last of the sap recrystallized into powdered salt. The trunks are thirty, forty metres in girth, but the interior cavities are polished into rooms, balconies, staircases that spiral like ammonite shells. Everything is terracotta red, veined with the off-white of dehydrated cambium. When wind moves through these veins the city makes its noise: a roar so constant and layered it becomes a single deaf anthem—creak of cellulose, flutter of leaf-scales that still cling like shingles, hiss of salt dust sifting through pores. You feel the sound in your molars more than your ears.

Because the scale is hidden, distances misbehave. A plaza can be crossed in four strides if you believe it is small; expand your attention and the same square becomes an exhausting parade ground. Citizens—those rare, gangly figures who belong here—pace themselves by narrowing their thoughts, shrinking the city to pocket-size whenever they need to fetch something. They call this “folding distance,” a courtesy taught to children before speech. Visitors who fail to learn quickly are found days later, dehydrated despite the absence of sun, having walked an inch in an hour and died of loneliness.

Vegetation is lush but wrong. Leaves are translucent sheets of copper foil, photosynthesising directly from the roar of sound. Wherever decibels peak, the foliage unfurls brighter, drinking vibration, exhaling oxygen and the faint smell of hot electrical contacts. The canopy overhead is a cathedral of gongs: every blade is a bell, every stem a tuning fork. When breezes (warm, dry, fragrant with petrichor that cannot exist) pass through, the entire forest glints like shattered mirrorballs. Look too long and your vision records after-images of greenish fire that linger on the inside of your lids for hours.

Fauna is rare, but when it appears it is conspicuously loud.  
The smallest is the cicada-clicker: an insect carved from hematite, wings edged with flint. It snaps them together, throws sparks, then eats the spark before the heat can become water.  
Larger is the howler heron, a bird of porcelain whose neck is a flute. It lands on high branches, turns its beak toward the thickest resonance and screams counter-notes until the wood around it fractures into acoustic petals; it swirls these like drink and flies off, bloated with harmony.  
Top predator is the echo leopard—absence made pelt. You never see the creature itself, only the after-drone of its step, a growl that runs around corners seeking the original throat. When a citizen vanishes the city records an unexplained dip in loudness, as though someone turned the world’s master dial a notch toward hush.

Water exists here only as concept and currency. It is not liquid; it is a negative space. Citizens carry sealed clay spheres the size of thumbnails—each a vacuum fired at kiln heat, glazed with glyph statements of debt. Crack a sphere and the void inside escapes, rushes into plant veins, causes a local softening. One can trade an afternoon of suppleness in a building-wall for a song, for a memory, for the promise of future footsteps. Spend too many spheres and the city becomes brittle; portions collapse into red dust drifts that taste of cinnamon and rust. When that happens citizens gather, press their ears to the ground, listen for the whispered recipe of re-hydration: a secret ratio of silence to thunder that will coax fibres to tense again.

Elevation is low because height is measured downward into hollowness. The true districts are basins, hollow roots descending far below the apparent floor. There is no bedrock, only an inverted sky: a salt-white crust suspended overhead like a lowered heavensheet, glowing with the captured daylight of centuries. People mine that sky. They chip off shards, grind them into lenses, strap them to their eyes so they can look up into the ground and read the future in the fracture patterns. Every prediction ends with “…provided the noise continues,” because if the city ever fell quiet the light would go out, the warmth would settle into cold ash, and the vegetal masonry would calcify into tomb.

Technology is primitive, but the materials are evolved. Hammers are seed-pods emptied of pith, filled with echo-leopard snarls and corked with wax. When struck against copper leaves the impact transduces sound into kinetic force; a nail of hardened silence is driven. Saws are bowed bird-bones rosined with powdered rust; they do not cut, they persuade fibres to separate along consenting grain. There is no smelting—only encouragement. The city grows its tools the way other biomes grow flowers.

Dynamic processes churn at a walking heartbeat pace. Buildings expand a finger width at dawn, contract by dusk; citizens time appointments to these exhalations, slipping through tightening corridors before they are pinched shut. Every thirteen days the copper canopy rattles into synchrony and produces a “clear ring”: for ninety seconds the roar cancels itself, perfect destructive interference. In that hush the hidden scale unfolds and the whole Ward is revealed as a single seed suspended in a universe of dry light. A person can stand at the exact centre, see every distance at true size, understand the map. Then the moment passes, sound rushes back in like returning blood, and memory of coherence is wiped by the hammering brightness.

Entry is always accident: the hinge finds you, not the reverse.  
Exit is more uncertain. Some leave by learning absolute silence—meditating until even heartbeat is indistinguishable from city drone; the body is mistaken for civic architecture and slowly extruded into whatever world contains rain. Others purchase passage from the echo leopard, trading their own residual moisture (seven tenths of a human) for safe escort; they emerge mute, shrivelled, but able to drink for the first time and feel full. A few choose to stay, cracking their clay spheres one by one until they themselves become hollow, become citizens, become buildings, become the warm red walls that roar and roar and roar.

If you are offered a sphere, refuse politely.  
If you hear your name shouted in the metallic leaves, do not answer; names are vibrations the city consumes.  
Walk small thoughts, fold distances, cup your hands around whatever light you still carry—and keep moving. Because in the Hollow Ward to stand still is to let the sound measure you, and sooner or later it will find the frequency at which your bones remember water, and they will sing themselves into dust.
