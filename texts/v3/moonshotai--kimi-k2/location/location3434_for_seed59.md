The air itself is alive here.

You stand in a breathing chamber of leaves, a cathedral-scale grove not built but grown—an urban lattice of living wood and vine where every surface drinks water. Cool mist beads on your cheeks; gravity seems to have loosened its grip, for you are half a mile above the surrounding lowlands, yet the ground feels soft, buoyant, as though the whole settlement rests on a mat of spongy lungs. Water is everywhere: it drips from stomata, sheets down trunk-fluted gutters, pools in cupped brom of buttress roots, then overflows into tiny cataracts that leap from branch to branch in silver braids. The sound of falling, trickling, pattering, slapping leaf-to-leaf is constant—an aqueous percussion section played by a thousand overlapping hands. Even when no drop is visible, the air is syrupy with wet, a cool saturated 2.5/3 that condenses on eyelashes and drafts into lungs like chilled broth.

A single “street” is a tunnel of stems woven so densely that light arrives only in fractured lances. Temperature hovers just above the point where breath ghosts, 0.7/3 on the scale, yet the vegetation’s metabolism throws off enough warmth to keep the core of the thicket from frost. Ferns the size of shutters curl overhead; moss drapes like bolted wool; lianas braid into load-bearing arches. The plants are the architecture. Doorways are diaphragms of interlocking leaves that part when you brush them; balconies are shelf-fungi bolted to living pillars; staircases are spiral scars left by centuries of woody strangler figs climbing themselves. Density 2.3/3 means every cubic hand-span is tenanted: orchids nested inside bromeliad tanks, ants cruising across monkey-rope highways, violet bioluminescent berries wedged in knotholes like emergency lighting. Nothing is background; everything is foreground.

Yet the place feels urban, 1.8/3 built. There are districts—Spore Market, Dripping Forum, the Antechamber of Budding Speech—whose shapes have persisted long enough to earn names. Erosion 1.7/3 tells you the layout is settled, not freshly sprouted: trunks have flow-stone wrinkles where water has sculpted them; hand-rail vines bear polished grooves from generations of palms. Tech 1.3/3 provides only the simplest augmentations: bone-saws of flaked stone, needles carved from owl-claw thorns, syrupy glue boiled out of strangler sap. Metal is rumor. Maintenance is horticulture: you garden your house the way a topiarist trims a yew, except the house grows back overnight, often in a mood to reroute your corridor toward an unexpected atrium.

Scale 1.1/3 means the entire settlement is scarcely larger than a cathedral close. Stand at the axial banyan (the civic heart) and you can almost sight the periphery in every direction, although foliage occludes. Because space is small and life is loud, the acoustic field is overwhelming: frog choirs in drainpipe vines, macaws hammering seed shells, cicadas like frying glass, the boom of buttress drums when children thump them for fun. 2.1/3 loudness never quiteers into cacophony; it is layered like rainforest canopy, each species isolating its frequency band, so the total effect is symphonic rather than shrill.

And fauna—2.8/3 overwhelming—does not mean merely “many animals.” It means animals that behave like weather. Butterflies arrive in copper storms that spiral up warm columns of air, pollinating as they riot. Army ants boil across walkways in living ropes, dismantling a dropped mango in twenty frantic minutes, then vanishing, leaving the polished husk rocking like a cradle. Sloth-people (and they are people here, tool-users despite their primitive tech) hang harnessed inside the mid-canopy, stitching leaf-fiber hammocks while upside-down. Their long claws slot into carved grooves so they can free both hands; when they relocate, the city remembers their routes, soft bark growing calloused rungs exactly where calloused rungs are needed. Nobody is really predator or prey—an unspoken treaty exists, perhaps because food literally drips: sugary exudate from tapped bromeliads, edible lichen scraped off aerial buttresses, fish—yes, fish—that squirm through the aerial gutters during wet weeks, having spent their fryhood in cloud-borne pools. Fauna is infrastructure: parrots trained to ferry seed-packets across ventilation shafts; glow-bugs hired as streetlamps; crickets revered as musicians whose hourly chime sets the civic pulse.

Dynamic 2.6/3 means nothing holds still. A bridge you crossed at dawn may be wilting by dusk, its principal vines withdrawn into fruiting mode, rerouting traffic through a newly woven trampoline of aerial roots. Property law is horticultural calendar: you own whichever chamber you are currently pruning; abandon it for two weeks and the thicket reclaims, walls puckering shut, floors sprouting a mattress of internode cushions. The city is an anthill of vegetables, a bazaar of chlorophyll. Every resident is a gardener-surfer, reading the wind of growth, paddling their pod-houses on a slow green tide.

Weirdness 0.5/3 keeps marvels organic, not eldritch. Cause and effect remain legible: if you fail to thin a meristem, it crowds your skylight; if you over-tap a vine, it scars and blights. Laws are those of biology, not sorcery. Yet within that normality the scale-of-wonder is turned up: flowers big as umbrellas, aerial roots that hiss when you break them, resin that hardens into translucent orange panes cooling the rooms below. The citizens live inside a botanical lung floating half a mile above the world, and to them that is ordinary.

Light 1.8/3 is afternoon diffused, green-filtered, never harsh, never dim. Through gaps you glimpse the sky-ocean of cloud tops, sense the abyssal drop beyond the leaf-horizon. Yet vertigo is rare; the vegetable net feels bottomless in the opposite direction—however far you fall, something springy will catch you, and if it doesn’t, the mulch at the forest floor is a deep sponge of peat and moss. Death exists—colored mushrooms announce where a sloth-person slipped—but catastrophe is local, quickly stitched over by vines hungry for nitrogen.

To enter, you must surrender shoes: laces tangle in twiners, and bare soles read the damp grammar of bark. Citizens will gift you a rhythm—tap twice on a buttress before stepping onto its bridge, announcing yourself to the ants who maintain right-of-way. Speak softly, because sound carries along water tubes and leaves; shout and you’ll draw a parliament of macaws who mimic your words back at you, garbled, for days. Trade is in cuttings: a thumb-length shoot of light-fern buys a gourd of rainwater; three nodes of glow-vine rent a balcony for a month. Currency grows if you have patience.

The highest balcony, reachable by a climbing race if you arrive in the cool, mist-breathing morning, is barely the size of a doormat. Stand there and you understand elevation 1.5/3: you hover not on stone but on a woven ottoman of twigs flexing underfoot, the entire plain below dissolved into cloud. The city exhales; you feel it sag an inch, then rebound. You are in a living kite anchored by woody hawsers thicker than temples. Cool wet wind buffets your face, and for a moment the greens brighten with refracted sun, every leaf varnished, every droplet a lens.

Then somewhere a seedpod splits with the pop of a knuckle, and the din resumes, the traffic of vegetable limbs rearranging themselves. A sloth-person offers you a scrap of aerial root to chew; it tastes like cucumber and electricity. You’ve only just arrived, but already a tendril is questing along your sleeve, mapping your heat, planning a new lintel that tomorrow will open exactly where your hand hangs today. The city is not trying to eat you; it simply assumes you will stay, and therefore might as well grow around you.

So you breathe the wet, cool lung-light, listen to the water drumming on leaf-drum skins, feel the slow green tectonics tilting your footing by a degree. You have found the place where urban and forest, tool and tissue, resident and resident bacterium differentiate only in language. Here every noun is provisional, every verb is grow.
