Somewhere at the foot of nothing, a city is boiling itself into shape.

The plain itself is only ankle-high above sea-empty; you could walk into it without noticing the dip, yet the air hangs cold enough to sting the lungs. Pulverised marble and half-set basalt lie like frost-rind over a peat of wet flour that never dries. That water—only 0.6 of abundance—finds its strange citizenship here: threaded into micro-springs that glint in every footprint, beaded on the bellies of bubble-beetles, sheened on brickwork so newly forged it still perspires cement milk. Water doubles as memory, condensing on every cold surface and writing ghost alphabets that evaporate before anyone can read them.

Because this place is *emerging*, everything is provisional scaffolding. New buildings shoulder out of the ooze like organ-pumps in a chest opened mid-surgery. The bricks are warm — a factory at the district’s rim extrudes them still glowing — yet they are laid against polar winds, cooling so fast they crack and ring, and the clang travels further than any human voice. There is no hierarchy: kiln and cathedral rise at the same speed, weld themselves together in accidental Gothic. Enormous burl-iron ribs arch overhead without plans, terraces bolt on like barnacles, and whole districts fuse overnight only to shear off the following noon when the wind’s teeth find a weld it fancies.  

Vegetation, 1.7 lush, colonises every second surface before the architects have finished arguing. Black-leafed rhubarbs erupt through ventilation grilles; lianas hang in thermocycler sluices; needles of blue pine root in piles of acoustic cladding. The plants persist despite the cold — because they are grafts from a warmer metabolic map. Their chlorophyll runs thick as antifreeze, the leaves bruise red in streaks of iron rust that taste of blood when chewed. Ivy drags down lampposts only to feed the roots tungsten, and the ivy flowers come out lit, little steady bulbs that make the sky look suddenly darker.

Density 2.8: nothing stands alone. Alleyways are capillaries, houses attach courtyard to courtyard until you cannot be sure which threshold ends where. Streets sinter into mattress-tight bronze tiles; at night the metal contracts and the whole city clicks like skeletal knees. You step out of a stairwell and find yourself inside someone’s kitchen; you exit that kitchen onto the stage of an outdoor theatre whose audience seats are a beehive diffuse with breath. Privacy is an exoskeleton citizens shed at doorways and reclaim again one corridor further on.

Yet despite this satellite-crowding, scale 2.3 lets the eye run across distances: boiler fields to the south steam against the horizon; the broken rimland of a still-forging harbour glitters northward; midway rise the three slag cones of dormant proto-volcanoes that the city’s foundries keep feeding, because their guts yield the rare earths that make traction magnets. Each of these is accessible within an hour of tromping catwalks that link rooftop archipelagos, but distance behaves oddly—sometimes it dilates so suburbs seem sidereal; often it compresses so the next neighbourhood steps on your heels.

Sound 0.8 is so spare that the city feels cloaked. Machinery throb is muffled by absorbent lichens; footfalls on bronze pavements are swallowed. A periodic drone—peristalsis of geothermal boilers—travels up though the skeleton every few minutes, trembles in the teeth, then dies. What you do hear with clarity are oddly intimate: a rivet popping out blood-hot into acid rain; the chirr of serrated cold ivy leaf against mirrored spandrel; the moist breathing of brick that hasn’t decided whether to cool into stone or collapse back into slurry.

Fauna 1.5. Grey arctic pigeons bred with microturbines for hearts skim between towers, trailing snow kernels from their wings. Metre-long centipedes with cable-glass carapaces chew through gutters seeking stray current. Roach herds, white as salt crystals, graze the condensation growing on factory ceilings; every dawn they drop fat oily pellets used as lamp fuel by children. No one has seen the apex predator yet—only hears its *clock* *clock* *clock* that sounds like billiard balls colliding three streets over, and once a semi-permeable silhouette against a sodium flare the size of a cathedral rose.

Tech 2.5. This is not merely industrial; it is obstetrical. At the city’s molten core, the Fabricatory Complex births daily quotas of already-rusted colossi: cranes, rails, turbines still glowing like red pangolins. They are wheeled, still pulsing, through arterial roads where chilled air tempers them at speeds visible as starlight cooling to iron. Electric lines knit overhead like synapses; their insulation frost-frays into lace that drops stinging blue flakes. Workers wear parkas beaten from asbestos leaves dipped in mycelial resin, and their breath freezes mid-stride to chandelier icicles they later collect for the plant that weaponises them—rail-gun cartridges of human condensation.

Light 2.2 neutral: from morning to a late silver dusk the sky holds a steady pewter glare as if the very idea of seasons has not yet been deployed. Streetlamps flicker only to calibrate—they measure neuron-speed of citizens crossing plazas, and dim or flare to steer traffic subconsciously. Occasionally surplus voltage finds itself in the ivy lamps; then whole avenues blaze viridian for minutes before being throttled back into restrained twilight.

Dynamic 2.6. The land itself works harder than the population. Bulldozers driven by no one gnaw the silt at the edges, chewing land out into bricks the very next hour. New districts splice themselves older than yesterday; sewers reverse flow uphill in a single tide shift; a clocktower sprouts overnight, dismantles itself at lunch, reforms as a stock exchange by supper. Maps are issued every dawn and pulped each dusk. Children wager on which street will rotate ninety degrees tomorrow. They win more often than engineers.

Weirdness 2.4. Gravity lists politely to one side along certain boulevards; pedestrians learn to counterbalance or simply glide, pretending to street-skate on invisible rails. Zones exist where sound accumulates in ledges—you can pluck a syllable spoken yesterday heaped like stranded jelly on a cornice. Language blossoms physically: a swear-word once shouted hard enough flens the air into a red rag which is harvested for insulation felt. Dream leaks from tenement eaves at 3 a.m.; municipal harvesters bottle it under street-lantern pressure to fuel next morning’s foundry fires.

Rules? They are still being authored. Whoever steps first onto the fresh site at dawn writes signage in spray chalk and it stays true until the evening tide of revision. A law might be “GEESE ARE CLOCK HANDS TODAY,” and indeed arctic pigeons wheel overhead in long thin V’s telling the hours in arcs. Violations earn you a softer sentence: you must spend the night as ballast within a half-formed bridge until your heartbeat settles into its rhythm; dawn loosens the bolts and you walk free but lighter, as if a grievance has been planed off your bones.

So: low, raw, packed, urban, industrial, gently watered yet always shivering, humming just under audibility. A city besotted with its own half-existence, lashing itself into permanence faster than cold can break it again. Somewhere at the foot of nothing, the place teaches that the opposite of ruin is not completion—it is rehearsal.
