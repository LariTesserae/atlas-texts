The First Glade of High Teth-Harán

They call this one a *glade*, though no meadow opens anywhere in its cool, wet airs. Instead you drop—harness-bound to a hauled basket of greased reed-cables—through two hundred spans of fog and are deposited at the lip of what looks, at first arriving, like an enormous cavity that has only just realised it has teeth.

Imagine a single cavern still deciding whether it will become mountain or room.  
High walls of pale carbonatite rise in slender ribs that haven’t yet chosen their final shape; each rib is only—roughly—an arm’s thickness, its inner face glistening with newborn alabaster veins. From the floor these shine like clogged starlight. Every surface sweats. Condensate beads as furious as summer rain, gathers, falls upward again in drifting spray before gravity remembers itself. All day and all night this slow chrysalis gives off soft thunder, the constant hush-clap of water finding itself inside rock.

Size. The chamber is smaller than a city, larger than a great hall. Six sandstone wind-bridges arc side to side at different heights; they are no wider than a jadeback donkey and were recently woven—braided plank on plank without nails—by the Sky-Carpenter Guild. Because nothing grows yet in permanent form, the bridges are also masts: tilted into vertical like ribs and tied off to screw-stones that wind themselves one turn every year, so yearly the bridges lean a little farther. Workmen crawl these sparse ropes like mantids, drilling condensation wells and planting tinth-root sets whose first leaves have just unfurled.

The town is likewise embryonic: a rind of scaffolded galleries hammered onto the younger cliff face. Every dwelling is half-jar, half-scaffold. The clay and basketry still smell of reeds and river loam, and because the plaza hasn’t settled, planks stretch and creak below the boot like waking sin-drum. People are few but concentrated at building sites. Hammers send forth clear bright tongs of sound that rebound in echoes across the hollow air, multiplying rather than diminishing, so that the smallest nail finds itself part of an eighty-voice choir clapping on wet stone. Down at the quiet end of the plaza new-burnt shingle smells, and black charcoal birds (half-fleeting, half-hammered themselves) spin above the smoke.

Ecology plants itself into any weeping space. Lichens appear overnight as blue embroidery across pale ribs; their threads taste of winter. Ferns open cochlea curls beside trickling seams and inhale the gusting mist. In crevices above ordinary reach spleenwort sends out black red spore cases like garnet beads. No trees yet; only one sapling twistpine has rooted where the water congeals to patches of black peat, it is no higher than a child, but every storm bellies its trunk in bright accretions of stone while its needles spin rain into vertebrate jewels. This sentinel will slowly mineralise the room’s roof before it ever produces cones. A single heronning stands on one leg on a bridge-plank, watching the rain fall upwards, crests flicking in slow strobe. Nothing else moves with certainty.

Rules claim the place as much as place molds rules. All masonry must be reversible within four decades; anything not harvested will balance its waste-matter against room-stone after living, and builders must pre-plan a mortice where the returned weight will slot. Sound is accounted a mineral asset: every thirty nights carpenters record hammer patterns, trill them into goat-skin loops, and replay the loops to the unformed cliffs so walls learn a rhythm before they set. Water, their coin of wealth, is scooped by wooden spoons from hanging veils, exchanged at a steady drip-rate; a suckstone merchant counts the heartbeats it takes for his cup to fill before he will barter three spoonfuls for a peat bundle.

Temperature hangs cool close to a cavern autumn. Daylight—answering our parameter of *normal*—filters indirectly: refracted, glint-like from a skyhole a hundred spans above, etched by false stars whose light rides turquois threads of suspended vapour. At noon the chamber glows softly white; at dusk fissures take on a brass-metallic hue. No sun touches the floor; its warmth is token memory cast by capillarity.

Rarity of fauna means the place is alive only through plant breath and stone heartbeat, yet the stone *is* alive. Walk through (and one does walk—there is no ladder but the ropehole) on any given sabbath and the minerals are holding impromptu elections among themselves. Oolites debate whether to crystallise in sphere or dendrite, chitin-hard limestones seat themselves as temporary thrones and jut outward petal-on-petal for space. When you stretch your ear to the lowest drip, every stone seems to say *wait, the architecture is an infant—we refine*.

There is a slow exhalation the room performs once per lunar cycle, a great inhalation once per solstice-aurora. Between the two the bridges cool, ropes tighten, new scaffolding sugars into place like inward-growing stalagtites. These tides are gentle, courteous—no more force than the change from second to third braid on a loom. But if you stay four years you will be four feet taller the doorway you left by may now lie between your knees.

Originally the site was discovered when a migrating coil-village (forty house-tents wound on caravanned axles) lost its oxen team to climbing cliffs. They unpacked hopelessly, only to learn the cavern was building itself in deliberate orbit around their accidental dwelling. The tents became permanent tissue; the void began spiralling them higher by the braided ribs it erected.

People say that one returns from High Teth-Harán not richer in goods but in futurity: each builder knows an extra decade of his own growth has already left matte fingerprints along the next lattice strut. Sound files itself inside you like a hidden bone—sometimes in distant years a perfect hush-clap echoes under your ribs, and you pause, hand on a city window, and realise that it is the First Glade reremembering you as part of its own unfinished walls.
