THE LAGOON-ORGAN  

1.  First look  
Approach from the west-north-west at sundown and you will think you are flying above algae stained glass: a circular pool three hundred metres across set into an oolite basin no higher than a river levee.  Every colour in this place has been rinsed many thousands of times.  The air is warm, thick-wet, scented of celadon sap and ozone.  Columns of mist stand on the water like translucent trunks whose crowns merge two metres up and drip back in slow successive rain-sheets.  But the pool is not water exactly; it is one contiguous plant, an aqueous lily whose protonema fill the entire volume, so the surface resists the heel like thin jelly and springs back, quivering.  

2.  The town that drips  
Coalescing out of the same lily-substance is Trähwick—nine slender arcologies grown rather than built, arrested at stages between bulbous bud and forty-metre inflorescence.  Their architecture is the architecture of refrigeration: fibres wound tight through phase-change refrigerant canals keep the living walls at precisely four degrees below ambient so they sweat continually.  The condensation gathers into pendant vesicles that roll like mercury down exterior grooves.  When the droplets reach a balcony they are ingested, filtered, returned to circulation, and the balconies unfurl one trellis deeper.  A cycle older than writing but still urban.  

Every street within a tower is simply a hollowed petiole, ribbed, glossy, perfumed.  Elevators are dilute spheres of stiffened pith that shrink and swell to adjust buoyancy.  From mid-level you can look out through transparent rind at the lagoon-carpet and at other towers reclining slowly as the plant remembers better angles for contemporary light.  

3.  Breath and noise  
The internal lungs of Trähwick are bellows-sacs large as concert halls.  During day’s heat they draw humid air in across resonating shelves—here the loudest sound in the territory is produced, a wet organ chord that rips and reseals a hundred times per second.  The chord bounces off the western marsh bar and is reflected back thinned, a tremolo that never quite cancels with itself.  Residents swim in the noise; children move their arms in the thickness of it, watch pressure wrinkles peel photographs off corridor walls, tiny reliefs of parents who left that morning for veterinary circuits deeper in the bio-city.  

4.  Technology as crop  
The flowering walls of Trähwick do not need energy from outside.  Throughout the night lineaments of luciferin climb the stem’s heartwood, cooling the interior by re-emitting the day’s warm photons as faint green light.  Quantum-coupled nodes in each vacuole record every topological alteration imposed by the continuous small storms of the lagoon’s climate-film; when the plant decides a pattern, it writes back into its own atomic lattice, tuning its mechanical properties from papyrus-brittle to cable-tense on millisecond notice.  

People steer the plant by grafting suggestion-strands—microscopic guide RNAs encoded in breath droplets.  It obeys as slowly as mountains, then changes everything overnight.  

5.  Fauna that are a function of rocks remembering grasshoppers  
These suggestions trickle down to the peripheral meristems, seeding new physiologies in the outer verge.  There the strangeness becomes organismal.  Neutrinoid dragonflies—larger than ravens—do not eat but convert chronons into lift; their wings shimmer where time curves.  The only remainder of an “old” biology is a colony of eel-lungfish who have burrowed into the basin’s Cretaceous capstone beneath the lagoon.  From their silt-lined tunnels they vent muffled burps that emerge as four-note horn calls each dusk.  

Elsewhere, a school of squirrel-sized rollwings fold the condensate into tiny asymmetrical sails and glide from tower to tower delivering oral messages still as oral as when they left the mouth.  They taste of the speaker.  One stole a girl’s grief last month; near midnight over the low ridge it unfolded it like pollen.  

6.  Hydrology older than weather  
Every third new-moon the lagoon-lily contracts its laminae, drops the entire water column a half-metre.  The towers twist slightly on their stems to follow.  Gravity sluices the city for twenty minutes, washing metabolic detritus from all arterial groves out into run-off clefts that spiral to the basin heart.  At the bottom sits the oldest stone, skinned by lichens proposing newic theorems.  There the detritus precipitates a glistening ridge of fungal lace no thicker than a sail.  

The fungus is the memory of Trähwick.  It redigests centuries of graffiti etched by acid dew, rearranges pigment letters until sentences migrate across rings, whole epic poems floating from 270 years ago to three weeks hence.  If you chip a flake of the fungus and dissolve it in saliva, you can speak fluently the argot of an ancestress you never met.  

7.  Edges and rules  
The extreme periphery of the organism is a scalloped membrane only one molecule thick—really the leading cell wall of a single giant alveolus, surface area equal to that of a cathedral yet mass less than a bird.  Step on this edge and the membrane buckles, drawing you into the hydrosol lightly, as into cooled mercury.  No drowning.  You will bob until the transport phloem finds you and levitates you toward a residential atrium.  If you were hostile, the folding would proceed differently: the plant would steep you in anoxia, calcify your soft tissues into conscience-panes fitted beneath residential lanterns—crystalline souvenirs of old hostilities.  

Thus, Trähwick practices a hospitality as convoluted as its shapes.  Entry is permitted only if the wanderer exhales an honest desire; the plant learns quickly who lies by the isotopic ghost of carbonate.  Those denied collapse the membrane behind their last footstep and are forbidden to return until the organism, after some centuries, forgets.  

8.  Turbulent present  
Even now the lagoon-lily is arranging itself for the next collapse.  Somewhere above the waterline a new root-skyscraper throbs, expanding its tip to pierce tomorrow’s warm southern air and arrest a desert wind before it reaches.  Rumbles shift through the root bundles like tectonic laughter.  A kilometre away a vortex blossoms on the water, core bright with fluorescence—St Elmo’s plasma on a scale suitable to dine out.  Children hang from balconies, cupping their hands to catch fragments of clotting auroras before they dissolve into harmless rain.  Oddly shaped triboluminescent crabs recently discovered how to drum on the root buttresses; their rhythms travel upward and remix with the bellow-lungs’ chords.  The city is learning percussive speech.  

Somewhere, thirty metres above, the oldest balcony has begun to distil pollen distorted by the magnetic memory of a comet.  If you plant this pollen in your own city, wherever that may be, twenty years later Trähwick will bloom there too, unexpectedly humming the same half-tuned chord, same ceaseless wet wind threading through empty galleries of living stone.
