The Forest of Nod rises inside an otherwise empty caldera, cupped at nine thousand feet on as though someone had tipped an almost-dry vase: a single crescent of red dust rims the sky-line, but its floor is blocked and thickened by something that never asked water to grow. It is a secret room fifteen city-blocks wide. From the fresh-cut crater lip every stem and frond looks near enough to stroke—dense, shoulder-tight vegetation packed so closely that colour leaks out as texture: corded wax-greens, roughened violets, and leaf edges of gold so thin they slice the still air without a sound.

Nothing here was built; yet it is unmistakably urban. Trees grow in tiers, but not the usual forest stories. These tiers are sidewalk, balcony, arcade. Columns of living bark flare outward at exactly stair-height intervals, forming shelves wide enough for a procession. Between them the ground itself rises in low humps—knees of lignified root paving—meeting one another at right angles as if obeying an invisible property map. Walk ten paces and the street simply becomes a corridor of pliant vines whose pale backs are stamped with the faintest octagonal seal, a memory of zoning.

Warm at twenty-eight °C, the place keeps summer around it like an extra skin. No breeze enters the crater; sound, therefore, travels only through stems. A snap twenty metres away arrives as a stitched reverberation inside your ribs, ends there. Apart from these tight acoustics the forest is almost mute: no birdsong, no insect drone; even the abundant fauna moves without announcement. Foot-long opal salamanders blink, drag stub wings across bark, but their pads are soft, their throats folded permanently closed so that breathing is silent skin-exchange. Shoals of glassy millipedes thread leaf litter; their thousand legs brush together like cards being straightened. Nothing louder.

Water should be absent—the dust rim insists on that—but every leaf underside weeps a clear varnish at dusk. It is not water, not resin, but an ammonia-rich syrup that evaporates before it ever drips, rising in threads of invisible scent. During the night the varnish condenses again along new growth seams; by morning the forest has quietly repainted itself. This nightly cycle is so slow, so evenly paced, that two centuries might pass before an outside observer notes that the canopy has lifted a single metre, one patient breath at a time.

Fresh, too: the caldera floor is younger than the oldest stem. Not volcanic youth—cooling lava would trawl vitamin-sealed seeds into glass—but a stranger birthing. A carpet of blue-white crystals, still forming, grows between root-houses like early pavement stones. These are carbonate skeletons exhaled by the tallest trees themselves, each knot in the root system laying down a tin coin of mineral until a tessellated crust spiders outward. Touch one and it sings faintly—silent to ears but felt in finger bones as a memory of compression. The whole forest is cementing itself into a single petrified city. Structures blur: architecture lapses into organism, organism becomes topography.

Normal daylight litters down through the crater mouth at a lazy forty-five degrees—long, steady beams thick with golden motes, although the source sun may in fact be elsewhere entirely. These beams pause at mid-air, fixed as warm glass rods, so that to pass through one is to feel viscosity. They nourish the thick foliage and also etch it; everywhere leaves carry ulcerations that heal overnight into gilded scar-letters, inscribing messages no visitor can read and removing themselves before anyone thinks to transcribe.

Any direction you walk under the needles and fans the path eventually ends at the Red Hollow, a single oval clearing thirty feet across where nothing currently grows. Set in its centre is a disk of polished mother-of-pearl—smooth, unmarked, tilted five degrees as if floating on the crystals underneath. It reflects the sky but not the trees; within the reflection no crater exists, only porcelain-white space. Concentric eruptions from earlier cycles ring its perimeter in darker russet, cyclic tide marks of former clearings now swallowed again. Every three hundred and thirty-three days, mother-of-pearl throws up a fresh rim, its placid clockwise spin slow enough that mosses riding its surface only unfold a new frond after you have long forgotten their original posture.

Fauna converges here, once per cycle. Salamanders crawl from every tier and grip the tilted face with translucent tongues; vines droop like bunting; the wing-struts of beetle-sized moths clatter together without noise, giving the illusion of a bronze bell suspended in vacuum. At the hollow’s nadir all motion arranges into perfect radial symmetry, a living mandala that shrugs itself apart before you can name the rules. Then silence thickens again, and every body scatters back into the vertical streets, eventually finding stations of leaf, root, and shelf that fit it exactly.

For travellers the only law seems to be delay. The city grows at the speed of continental drift; attempting to use its geometry for speed merely returns you to your blink. Yet nothing prevents passage. There are no gates, no customs, no instituted devices. If you manage not to die of astonishment you may carry away nothing more than a single turquoise scale lodged under a fingernail—still living. Outside the caldera the scale withers after seven breaths, leaving only the mapless memory of a metropolis that never bothered with names, only with posture: leaf angled against branch against invisible ordinance, all swelling over a floor too recently cooled to possess roads.
