They built the world upside-down and are letting cool water trickle in from above.

You arrive while the cavern is still molten-teething, half-lit by the drip-drip of sulphur-blue lamps slung on steel tripods. Light doesn’t reach far; the air itself is obsidian satin soaked. Humidity is so dense it leaves fingerprints on your cheeks. Soft, warm breath from the ceiling pool condenses in veils that drift down as jewelled fog until they strike the floor and pool again. Nothing dries here; everything accrues a varnish of water—boot tongues, note-paper, the metal handles on abandoned picks. 

The space is leg-wide on human terms but village-wide on cave terms: you can see the edges only when a lamp handler swings their rig a certain way and the darkness peels back, revealing columns still in utero. These pillars are not stone yet; they are milky calcium slurries laced with gunmetal ore. Sediment falls out of the ceiling like slow sleet, adhering to any projection until, over centuries, it thickens into something the color of wet ash. Every surface is crowded with half-born knobbles—stalactites the length of your thumb, stalagmites still indistinct as melted candles. Density rules: there is no empty air. The gaps swarm with vapor, with dust motes, with tiny black flies that ride the moisture drafts the way manta rays ride updrafts over reefs.

Temperature is only mildly warm, like close breath, yet the air itself is heavier than that—each inhalation has to be coaxed from the watery matrix. You smell kiln-dried algae from the sump pools and, beneath it, machine oil. Someone turned these embryonic chambers into a test mine: three steam pumps squat low, cast-iron duets that thunder without apology. Their pistons bloom with rust lichens teased out by splashing condensation. The machines are industrial fossils—stillborn yet still breathing, coughing their oil mist into the haze. Between each monstrous chuff an almost melodic hiss rises from the venting valves: it is not one sound but millions of droplets flung against tin screens. The sound drowns thought. You stop trying to distinguish it; it folds over your skull like lead.

Vegetation is brave only where lamplight falls. Mirror-weeds cling to an overhang here: coin-shaped silver leaves that function like reverse solar panels, drinking photons from darkness and excreting ghostlight. Their roots thread directly into the wet air, as if mocking the idea of soil. A single palm-sized fern grows from a metallic conduit as if someone poured organic life into the cabling itself. Otherwise, green is rumor.

Fauna, however, over-fills. The flies are only the catechism. Boulder crabs no wider than dinner plates clack sideways along the calcite drip lines, their shells the precise color of fresh ash; they graze on the mould that furs abandoned tools. Salamanders the length of your forearm haunt the shallow mirror-pools by one pump, translucent bellies jeweled with blood-bright capillaries; they hang motionless except for the sudden flicker of a tail fin that stirs up a silver swirl of silt. makes you believe you saw a startling pattern before it settles back into invisibility. Somewhere behind the throb of machinery you hear the polymorphic croak of cave frogs: not one throat but a hundred in perfect chorus, each note identical to the last so the whole sounds like a single organ turning power garden-hose loud.

Machines and animals share etiquette. The crabs lap up oil leaks. The frogs arrange themselves along condensation pipes to drink steady trickles. One pump heats a rivulet that feeds the salamanders’ nursery. Everything leans into everything else: a packed world so saturated in interdependency that the idea of ‘alone’ is meaningless.

Underneath it all, slow slow slow: the vault is still unfolding. The ceiling hasn’t settled on a shape. It inhales slightly every century, exhaling calcite powder like powdered bone. Any step you take compresses the grit and compresses future stone. The mining lights are small monuments: the scaffolding consists mostly of the same calcium sludge the miners came to harvest. Their jackhammers never finished—bulky trousers and gloves hang on pegs like desiccated ghosts. Perhaps they only realized too late they were carving a lung instead of a cavity.

There is no sky anywhere in memory, only the overface of water. The surface lake penetrates in cohesive sheets through hairline cracks until it pools overhead in broad, glassy panes. Occasionally a shard breaks free, slices through the mist, and shatters on the cement floor where tiles are still being born. The shards dissolve into puddles already occupied by greenish threads beginning to weave themselves into glass-like tiles—tiles for a floor that will appear sometime after your bones are gone.

Above the lake, no immediate weather cycle; rain is replaced by the deliberate refill from surface engineers sending down warmed water in measured pulses. Calibrations arrive on low notes only dogs could hear, though here the frogs echo them around the limestone cathedrals, so every hydrological adjustment is sung back by living throats. It feels ritual rather than science.

Orientation is accomplished by sound. Walk toward silence: that means you’re nearing the bottom silo whose walls are still magma-soft. Walk toward bronze thunder and you’ll reach the maintenance gantry that clings to one wall like barnacles. There is a last lonely chair bolted to the gantry; its cushions are rot-moss velvet. Someone once tallied the days on a rubber notepad that now resembles black lace edible to crabs. The Chair marks the de facto compass—every upward return direction is “toward Chair, then left spin until engine beat doubles.”

Rules are few, all signed in damp.

1. Any object placed on a horizontal surface will acquire a petrifying skin within three days.  
2. Machines must be left running; to switch them off is to invite flood from above—water seeks motionless surfaces as beds.  
3. All tools grow handles of the-bone if left unattended overnight; break them off gently or the mine claims your grip next.  
4. The only measured daylight is in the brave flash from the surveyor’s strobe carried off somewhere deep and still unpaid-for.  
5. When the frogs fall silent simultaneously, lie flat against the floor. The ceiling is about to inhale.

No clock needs winding; the pumps tick geological minutes. Its technicians forgot to leave, or became parts of the infrastructure—rubber gaiters solidified into flowstone, knuckles tracing pistons.

You are visitor or trespasser or next generation of sediment, still undecided. The air tastes metallic-tender, like licking the inside of a penny fresh from rain. Every breath deposits a film of future mineral inside you. You might stay indefinitely: the scale permits it. The space is town-sized, but there are other adjoining chambers—brief glimpses through drifts show calcium arches already fat as railway tunnels, new veins bulging in the half-dark, waiting to be punctured so more sound, more life, more water can rush in.

In the ballooning dark ahead, a new pump awakens—first a cough, then a slow seduction of gears that sound impossibly intimate after the hammer throb you grew used to. It speaks as if it knew your name. Vapor swirls, the lamp sways, a mirror-weed brightens and throws back a fractured silhouette of you: already coated, already mineralizing shoulder and cheek. You exhale; the environment inhales. Somewhere a few atoms of you become the next drip that will—if all schedules hold—complete the first monumental column before the surface engineers even notice time has passed.

You do not decide to stay. The mine decides you are a lattice point strut, wet and warm and marvelously arriving just in time for the architecture its own patience has scheduled.
