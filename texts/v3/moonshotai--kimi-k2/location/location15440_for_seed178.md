The Drowned Valley of First Forge

The sky is a lid of black glass, rain-filmed and trembling. Light arrives only as refracted glints off the backs of the airborne river that falls in slow sheets instead of drops—each sheet impossibly thin, carrying its own smooth laminar glow, like the page of an illuminated manuscript torn loose and blown sideways into the wind.  Where two planes of water collide, they flash a cold silver so bright it hurts, but the illumination lasts only for the instant before the sheets are torn apart again by the pressure-maddened atmosphere.  Darkness always wins back the right to be itself.

Directly beneath that shredding sky the ground never managed to dry before the next convulsion arrived.  The valley is new, a mere cartographical rumor sketched in silicon-rich slurry lifted out of the planet’s mantle only three winters ago.  The crust stalls half-healed—still *pneumatic*—coughing milk-blue hotsprings that freeze almost the moment they touch air.  When they freeze they do not go entirely solid; they turn into something glass-gelatinous, a phase only known to the magma-born, singing a mineral warning whenever boots, paws, or the softer legs of engines press on them.

Water is simply *everywhere*, but not in ways a sailor would recognize.  Blackwater lagoons lie mirror-still in the hollows between frost hummocks, but their rings of shore ice grow upward into italics of crystal, into letters spelling unread names.  A raised seepage line of technology—antique but still beyond cognition—threads along the undersides of cliffs, carrying sheets of supercooled water at walking speed through transparent tubes the circumference of cathedral pipes.  Once every few seconds one of those tubes bursts, sends an arm-thick geyser sideways, which then snaps into whip-curve icicles that dangle like chandeliers for one heartbeat before the valley’s over-dense air breaks them into showers of blade-shaped hail.  Together, hundreds of such bursts compose the landscape’s thunder: a rolling roar that feels sub-audible, the kind of volume your *bones* interpret as music heard from the inside.

The vegetation reacted almost instantly to the valley’s birth.  Because the substrate sends up lithium and phosphate the way volcanoes send ash, a hothouse of metal-fed flora has rooted in the quaking silt.  Plants do not bother with the horizontal; every organism rose as a vertical spiral of leaves and trunks and tracheid tubes stacked like coaxial cables.  The trunks themselves are pale ether-green, threaded with visible capillaries that pulse mercury-quick as each bolus of sap is flash-frozen then flash-thawed by the valley’s oscillating microclimates.  Because no wind here is ever the same twice, the gyres of foliage turn against one another—clockwise, counterclockwise, synchronous, arrhythmic—producing a serrated susurrus that rips the air into audible pages, each page at a different pitch.  The sound registers somewhere between whale songs scored for broken violins and turbines being born.  

No single forest.  It is better called a single tangle—thirty, forty metres high in places, yet always the same arm’s length deep: a living wall of repeating trunks stuck so densely together that the eye sees them as overlapping shadows even through glare-ice lenses.  Birds used the density as cover from the chaos above, until too many of them found the limbs already noisy with other, weightless things.  Not insects.  Smaller.  Gnat-sized mechanisms flutter everywhere: stealth drones that lost their war centuries ago, left behind to iterate without purpose, patching copies of copies of copies.  They feed on the metallic saps, drink the water film from leaf blades, and continuously rebuild their fractal wingplanes so none have looked alike for the last thousand generations.  Their wingbeat chorus, magnified by the crystalline cold, is a glitter-screech that rides on top of the water-roar like foil over fire.

The fauna is “abundant” only in the sense that *volume* exceeds expectations, not size.  Mammalian bulk cannot sustain here; the cold will not allow the body a single still moment long enough to let calories settle.  So the vertebrates learned to billow.  A hare-thing the length of a forearm leaps between branches; opening its rib-lungs it actually *glides* on pounded-air, supported at least as much by pressure-reflective down as by lift.  Predators need thermogenic trickery: lynx descendants built like origami lanterns, dark-furred on the outer flap and white-laser on the inner, reversing coats in mid-pounce to momentarily redirect thermals.  Their calls are pitched above the thousand-layer drone of ruptured tubes and broken icicles, a discordant carillon that the valley itself harmonizes into white static.

This place is not *weird*.  Everything obeys physics—just a physics hammered thin under a cold iron sky.  What is surreal is the *rate*.  Tectonic plates here shrug like shoulders bored of conversation.  Frost heaves overnight what elsewhere would be measured in decades.  The trees, sensing the crust beneath them flex, twist their nutrient pipes faster, corkscrewing upward a whole handspan by dawn.  A ruin of yesterday’s footpath—an old supply line slung between low stone trestles—slumps six inches deeper by breakfast, and the pulsed water tubes reroute themselves to run *inside* the fallen latticework as if playing a pilgrim’s game of grandmother’s ghost.  A structure that looked like a broken radio-mast at dusk turns out, by moonless midnight, to have reconfigured into three separate walker-cores now trekking across fog, jointed legs flashing LIKE mantis-stilts coated in argon frost.  They leave curious shoeprints: footfalls press no deeper than a fingerprint yet, because the crystalline mud retains memory for precisely 14.7 seconds, the impressions linger just long enough for an observer to wonder who else tread here.

The only building that stands still is the First Forge.  That is what the original colonists’ glyphs still call it, half-buried lens-black pyramid half-swallowed by slurry.  Inside, the machines that gave this valley its name still run a single languorous program: forging gravity lenses out of the icewater.  They extract infinitesimal negative-index singularities no wider than a raindrop and stack them in lashes inside torchlit voids, because the lenses need real vacuum, and this sky is too wet.  Each time a lens is folded into usefulness it emits a bass *whump* like a heart kicking over—and the entire valley responds: ponds flatten circles, foliage furls tight for a count of five, the drone-swarm pauses synchronously.  Then everything unspools again, louder than before, the valley rebuilding its scream note by note.

Heat is currency.  Nothing burns fuel; things *borrow disorder* from the macro environment and return it sharpened.  An exile walking with a hand-warmer would be assassinated within minutes by the shimmering overgrowth that would scale her wrist like cold ivy and suck every calorie per square millimole out of her glove’s polymer.  The exiles are rare anyway—scattered.  One, two shelters at a time, frames of translucent carbonate half-filled with gel, looking like half-grown cicada husks.  Inside, the last biological humans lie curled, lungs flooded with fluorocarbon, dreaming the slow dreams of wolves while implant glands heat their synovia to the precise +1 °C required for the potential of motion.  Out of courtesy the trees never bend their spirals over those shelters; the tubes of water detour.  But the valley has no courtesy toward inertia.  One day the crust will settle, the pressure tubes will stop detonation-roaring, the trees will cease their gyre and grow simpler.  Until then the place sings itself awake with every orgiastic breath, a newborn world pausing only long enough to gulp water and *forgotten physics*, then vomiting it out again in light, in frost, in blades of ice that carve the night so precisely you can hear the dark being shaved into slivers, filament by filament.
