The Town in the Rock

At first you think you have entered a cavern mouth, because the ground under your boots is flinty stone that clicks like ice, and cool air slides past you carrying a taste of iron.  But twenty paces farther the ceiling opens into sky, and you realize this is not a cave but a courtyard carved inside a single upthrust buttress of mountain that never quite learned how to settle.  The place is one small roomed city-block grown from inside a cliff, as if a giant had chipped a child’s balsa city from the guts of the Earth and then forgotten to breathe life into it.

Everything feels damp, never wet.  A sequin of condensation hangs from lintels and lichen-nightmare balconies; when the wind walks between walls it leaves wet footprints that evaporate seven minutes later, but more always come.  There is no rain, only memory of rain completely stilled.

The houses—about twenty of them—are built outward like swallow-nests on inner faces of an irregular cylinder of rock.  Their walls are local stone the color of cooled blood, laid dry so every block keeps the exact shape it wore when dragged up the spiral ramps centuries ago.  Planks of bone-white driftwood, iron-hard from age, turn them into dwellings; the wood came from trees that forgot how to die on a shore far below the visible world.  Windows are square, small, shuttered by cloth sewn with fish-skin.  A single communal ledge loops the breach in the rock like a mezzanine, and short rope bridges droop from doorway to doorway like playing-card arcs frozen mid-fall.

Vegetation insists, but never intrudes.  Threadlike bromeliads dangle hair from ceiling cracks, drinking the films of water that never become rain.  One hardy birch clings to a ledge fifteen feet overhead, its roots gripping like pale feet that refuse to step down; it never grows in height, never sheds a leaf, perpetually fifteen flayed fingers of silver against stone.  Little clots of moss look like stains left by ancient lamps.  Everything green keeps a respectful distance from everything else; every plant governs its own paragraph of stone.

Temperature stays at a coolness that makes fingertips want to stay tucked inside wool cuffs that no one here owns.  At night the air cools half a degree more, no less; by midafternoon it forgets to warm.  No fire is ever lit, first because there is no fuel larger than the dead birch’s own shadow, second because fire is an insult to  Nothing needs heat; the population of twenty-one souls wears sheepskins that have not changed pattern since their grandmothers first slipped them over their heads.  The skins themselves hardly age: the tanning recipe uses only cold water and alder smoke.

Fauna flaunts its rarity.  A single blind cave-raven broods on the birch, preening itself with illusion wings.  Sometimes at noon you hear its single dry click that seems exactly like a dropped coin but never is.  Its mate left centuries earlier and may still be in midair somewhere, searching for an exit the town long ago bricked.  No insects, no roaches, not even the memory of spiders.  Footsteps on the ledge are the loudest sound, and they know better than to echo.

        The Rules of Living Here

1.  Tools are what they can carry dangling from one thumb.  A bronze knife with no handle like a child’s outline of a knife, a needle of coral, a pebble hollowed to ladle exactly one tear.  
2.  Nothing grows or decays because growth and decay left town in the same boat and the boat ran aground and became this rock.  Food is dried lichen wafers salted by breathing; twenty-one plates, twenty-one bowls, twenty-one spoons.  No one dies, and no one is born.  
3.  Time is marked only by the nine places on the spiral wall where dampness photographs itself, shifting a millimeter every century.  Yesterday and tomorrow both have the same face, and the town merely blinks between them.
4.  Language is whispered slate tapping stone with fingernail rhythms; words are accurate to three syllables, no more, and once uttered they drop to the floor and fuse with the rock within three heartbeats of silence.  Therefore people speak little anecdotes that end quickly instead of stories that crave the next minutes.

In morning light (the light is never bright, merely exactly bright) the walkways don’t cast shadows, only smears of darker wet.  You can stand at the center of the courtyard and see kitchen-lamps glow the color of old parchment in every doorway and yet feel no invitation to move closer.  In evening no lamps are kindled; those inside sit in the dimness that has settled on them like dust and can still see perfectly, because darkness itself has made its peace and taken up residence, motionless forever.

Sometimes a cloud passes high above—an ordinary cloud—touches the lip of the cylinder, hesitates, then slides away, remembering it was never supposed to fit inside.  The townsfolk glance up together, the single collective gesture of their day, and then return to not changing.

You could walk the whole circuit in three minutes if you trotted, but no one trots.  You could call across but voices do their own inconvenient craftsmanship of echoing and echoing is change, so instead they send thought the width of a palm, hand to hand across the little bridges.  Carts are unknown; everything that must be moved (a basket of salt, a folded fleece, an ache, an anecdote) is passed politely, arm to arm, never leaving the ledge.

As a final oddity, the town keeps one open drain in the shape of a spiraled groove cut floor to floor, ending two arm-lengths above a fern that is not technically growing but simply continues.  Condensed day-drips travel this spiral and arrive at leaves unchanged, each drop sliding along without splashing.  No one sees the point and therefore no one interferes.  After all, the route of water is its own decree of motion inside a place that surrendered motion long before memory.  The whole settlement exists in the perpetual pre-stillness of a held breath.  You may enter, but remember: you have always been here.
