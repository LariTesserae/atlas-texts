### **The Ascension Kilns**

The city rises vertically, not in defiance of gravity but in elaborate collusion with it. Built along the inner curve of a canyon whose walls still *barely remember* the drill-bites and plasma-scalds of their making, it clings like fevered chalk-dust to a series of pseudocrystalline ribs—half-organic, half-soldered—which erupt from the basalt in every direction. The canyon is 4,000 meters deep; the rim is nowhere visible, lost even in normal daylight, because the air at this elevation cannot decide whether it is atmosphere or steam. At 42 °C the place is hotter than human blood, and what little moisture exists slinks across every surface as a skin-thin film that never quite becomes droplets and never quite evaporates. You wipe it away and it prints your reflection back onto the bronze plate behind you.  

Below, the river—where the initial surveyors scratched the cliff to *begin*—still leaks from the cracks, but never more than the width of a pencil. It stitches a silver filament down the canyon’s throat, whisper-clattering against the mineral shells of the buildings, neither nourishment nor threat; just a reminder that water and erosion have started negotiations, but haven’t signed any treaties. The erosion index refuses to climb beyond 0.5/3 because nothing here has existed long enough to be *weathered.* Everything looks as though it was extruded, cured, and queued yesterday. Corners have not yet been softened by time.  

There are no plants, no lichen, not even the dumb honesty of desert crust. The color palette is therefore entirely metallurgical: slick pewter, brown glass, the bruised gold of very old brass, and the bruised violet of something we do not yet call copper but *think* of as copper-hopeful. The occlusion of chlorophyll creates a silence of a kind—no rustle, no photosynthetic shiver. Instead, sound comes from far, mechanical depths: the far-off *gwum-gwum* of rotary kilns turning centimeters per minute; the almost-mournful release of pressure valves that sigh before they belch colorless steam that lingers ghostlike for a heartbeat and then folds back into the damp air.  

The city proper is *dense*—2.4 per volume—but not crowded in the human sense. The concourses are wide, overhung on all sides by catwalks and inverted alleys stacked vertically; one can walk for an hour without meeting another soul yet feel the corridors breathing. Raw apartments have been leased to machinery: vertical looms the height of watchtowers weaving carbon-foam sheets streaked with magenta seams. Storage lockers that open like whale gullets swallowing cargo pods at exactly midnight and regurgitating them rinsed but unchanged at sunrise. At intervals the architecture *locks*—an entire floor ribs outward an extra two meters every eighteen hours, guided by tracks built into the cliff wall; this is how the city expands, as slowly as the cliff itself retracts. Population: negligible; occupancy: maximal.  

Industrial scale is loose and almost luxurious. There are rail-frames, suspended on graphene tendons from the canyon’s shoulder, hauling containerized heat. The engines burn nothing; they merely drag blocks of metamorphic rock through loops of focused solar inductors and convert the thermal grief of the place into lift. Everything is electrified but no cables are visible. Power travels through the metal of the architecture itself, travelling the lattice so the walls tingle like skin up a fever. People (those left) call the phenomenon *second breathing.*  

And yet: fauna. Rare as it is rated 1.4/3, it still exists. One sees them—one *does*. They are not animals in any accepted taxonomy. Think *extinct analogue of barnacles* crossed with *clock escapements*. Each individual is the size of a man’s forearm, hexagonal in cross-section, entirely non-biological: copper teeth on ceramic rims allow them to ride the moving belts of the kilns, snapping up the excess oxide buildup like foresters picking mushrooms. They click, but the sound they make is oddly hollow, as though the click is happening inside one’s cochlea rather than outside the head. Encountering one—for they are infinitesimally rare—causes a momentary directional confusion; you and the artifact briefly share coordinate space, then cannot remember how you arrived at that exact tile. They are listed in the state ledger as *Binary Errant Devices* and left undisturbed.  

At night (which arrives via the slow southward slide of the gorge shadow) the kilns are bright, brighter than daylight, because the process has synchronized with the sun and now redoubles its own illumination. The cliff wall becomes a lantern, repetitive circuits of molten red that move upward as if the crust of the cliff has become a very large whale blinking heat from the seam of its eye. Light refracts in the suspended steam, creating sudden latticework castles in mid-air—ghost-cities inverted from the real one. One must not stare too long at them; rumor says that packets of light-stack memory can fall and embed in the retina, projecting nonexistent signs where you least expect them.  

There are civic rules, mostly residual legalese from old charters:  
1. It is unlawful to harvest Knurling Mist—the condensation that hardens into the tertiary alloy facades—before its season is officially declared.  
2. No machinery larger than arm-span may reside on the level that is currently *secreting.*  
3. Every traveler must register their own echo at the Bureau of Acoustic Lingering before ascending beyond platform 12. The echo may then be loaned as a provisional habitat unit; ignoring this step can result in two versions of oneself occupying one ladder rung, with catastrophic diffraction.  

Signage, where it appears, is etched directly into the bronze with letters that bridge three incompatible analyses of time: a calendar that has only three days (Yesterday, Now, Tomorrow) and a running tally of how far each slab of canyon wall has thermally drifted from its birth coordinates. The alphabet lasts about a decade, then the letters script migrates a centimeter in a direction unrelated to gravity. By the time anyone notices the drift, former signage reads like cave paintings to fresh eyes.  

Inhabitants number in the low hundreds. They wear material friction suits—sooty polymers that absorb static and donate it to the buildings around, so they may move without sparking a kiln rotisserie. Face-masks are beads of cooled alloy strung onto invisible wires: they flex when the wearer breathes, capturing, analyzing, and discarding edge_cases of atmospheric acid. The few public gardens left are *brooded gardens:* heat sinks where crystalline seed-shapes are evaporated from superheated saline and allowed to condense overnight into black snowflakes of unknown mineral. One collects a snowflake at dawn and hangs it in one’s apartment; it slowly disassembles into shimmering thought-borne pollen over the next month and must be renewed, or else the room forgets how many corners it has.  

Visitors arrive pneumatically, slung up the cliff on a 37-degree funicular that switches from magnetic crawl to pure rail-gravity halfway. Nobody speaks during the journey; the humidity tunnels the mouth shut. On arrival, passengers often realize their luggage has been re-printed in bronze somewhere halfway along the ride—identical in silhouette, unfamiliar in soul. Customs agents (automated nubs on hydraulic knees) assess the emotional conductivity of newly bronzed luggage and either admit it or sentence it to one of the vertical looms for immediate unraveling.  

And this continues, slowly. Every parameter drifts but does not accelerate. The canyon deepens by a handsbreadth every century, which no instrument can yet measure against the kilns’ daily outward creep. Somewhere high on the rim, the pencil-thin river will eventually uncoil itself entirely and the condensation will reach the point where it can no longer pretend it is *damp.* Then the first lichen will arrive—green as astonishment—ready to pay the rent in color. Until then, the Ascension Kilns keep baking avenues into the air above them, and every edifice stays flawlessly young, engraved, and just-about-to-be-worn.
