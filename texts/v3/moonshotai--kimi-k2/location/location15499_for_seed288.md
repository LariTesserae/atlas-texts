They live three hundred meters under the fractured crust of a planet whose oceans fled, one by one, into star-drifting vapor long before memory.  
What water remains is locked in the lattice of rust-red crystal that coats every surface in bristling needles: water without wetness, a chemical prisoner that cannot touch the tongue.  Yet it sustains vegetation whose metabolism long ago learned to knit hydrogen directly from the air and carbon from the dust, never relying on dew.  Thick felted fronds hang from pillars of that crystal like curtains of ultraviolet velvet, as deep a violet as bruise shadow.  Cool air drifts along stone corridors only a few degrees above freezing, slow and seasonal as tide.  The plants glow faintly themselves, enough to let the whole living city breathe in the thinnest ember of light, like moonlight pressed through gauze.

There is no sky; a single overarching cavern roof once broken by faults and cave-ins is now cinched net-tight with hanging stalactite forests like petrified rainwater.  Down here elevation works downward: the true abyss begins at a cracked lip beside a plaza and falls into dizziness that no lantern has ever fathomed.  Yet the nose tells you this drop has ceased slicing lower; its profiles are rounded and sand-blasted, scoured by windless wind until only perfect ogee curves remain, as though the age of the planet above has spent its last strength polishing these deepest walls into final form.  They may erode only another hairbreadth in an aeon; they have entered the terminal patience of matter.  The whole city feels older than thought.

Between the living walls lie its arteries: broad boulevards where nothing was ever paved, for the stone itself was taught how to grow flat and level by roots patiently dining on its mineral veins over millennia.  No block or board has been lifted by tool because nothing here can be cut or nailed; the people—if they can still be called that—have never required craft.  Instead the fungus-oracle seeded within each dwelling extrudes flesh-warm hallways, guiding itself by a symbiotic sound-field.  That is the loudest thing in this place: frogs, spider-hares, luminous moths, air sac beasts the size of wolves, and the low continual thrum of the city’s own heart, tuned to one monstrous resonant frequency.  At any given crossing the air coalesces into almost-words, chords repeated endlessly, until the mind learns to push through them as if through cloth.

Here a plaza opens like a lake without water.  Sparseness means thirty great trees of ox-blood crystal have space to grow thirty meters apart; wide gaps admit migratory nests of wingless insects that drift on thermal spirals coming off magma vents miles below.  Their translucent abdomens flash gold when emptied of light, so that the darkness itself keeps barns of slow fireflies.  Songbirds with glass lungs nest between fluting crystals and contribute an impossibly delicate octave that cuts through the heavier drone like silver wire.  It is never full night; the plants keep faint bioluminescence, and the fauna is engineered to reflect whatever glimmer touches it, multiplying subtle light until even the dimmest cavern corner is legible.

In the center of that open ground stands nothing but a single flat slab: the map-table.  Its face is polished to a mirror by erosional pilgrimage carried out by entire species over millennia; moths polishing it with their wings, beetles dragging mineral sand across it.  The slab records the slowest map in existence—tables of constellations gone extinct aboveground over a billion years, recalculated by moss growth patterns that creep across the surface at one centimeter per century.  Nothing changes visibly in a human lifetime, yet the plaques trace inexorable drift; the city is written upon that timetable.  At the slab’s foot lies an inexhaustible basket of crumbs: fruiting bodies pressed out daily by the fungal core for any creature that pauses here, the shared commons of the ecosystem.  The residents call it the Storehouse Pulse; its heartbeat is a soft subsonic thud you feel in kneecaps rather than ears, timed once every slow day to signal communal dreaming.

The people—transitional, halfway between vertebra and vegetation—attach to stalks that rise from their backbones and plug like cables into the corridors.  They can unplug and walk upright if they wish, but most prefer the leash tether; energy diffuses gently along root fibres, and the sluggish exchange of sugars feeds their quiet conversation.  No stone wheel turns here, no fire burns.  They possess no tools because the tools already grow around them: a parasol fungus unfurls to harvest radiation from an abandoned magma line; a hive of pigeons willingly offers its calcite quills for ink.  Speech is close to echolocation—loud in sonic amplitude yet too slow to be called language by outsiders.  These long syllables stretch for minutes; the response may not arrive for an hour.  In that interval walkers drift, merge, part, always with empty hands and full chests.

Yet the silence that underlies the roar is worth notice.  Gravity is so old here that air settles like dust; if you walk into any narrow corridor the bands of sound mute themselves into velvet folds and you can suddenly tilt an ear toward something finer.  What you hear is the minute ticking of crystalline growth, the distant closing of microscopic portcullises in plant stomata, the mantid chirp-law that governs where each frond may spread by treaty of mineral deposit.  When the wind is exchanged between caverns it resembles nothing so much as whale-breath, slow and upholstered by the planet itself, drifting at the rate continents drift.

Somewhere beneath these boulevards lies an older layer still: the original excavation tunnels, now collapsed.  Nobody descends that far; the fungal oracle tastes danger there, though what danger could remain to a city already ten million years dead above it?  Some say if you lay your ear to a certain crack in the map-table you can listen far enough back to catch the last ocean being pulled skyward by departing stars—an infinite falling of water that will never end because the planet forgot what to do with a sea.  Children, loosely tethered, press their cheeks to the warm crystal and swear they can hear the faint bourbon burn of distant tides tasting moonlight.  The old ones only smile.  Water is a myth, they say, but memory of water is enough.
