The first thing you notice is the breathing.

Not your own—though your lungs strain against the wet, heavy air—but the breathing of the place itself. A slow, arrhythmic expansion and contraction that makes the darkness feel alive. You stand at the edge of what they call the Infant Grotto, though no one remembers who first called it anything. The elevation here is barely above sea-level, yet you feel impossibly high, as if the ground might exhale you into the sky at any moment.

Water exists here as suggestion more than substance. Everything drips, but nothing flows. The limestone—no, not limestone, something younger, something that hasn't decided what it is yet—exudes moisture like sweat through pores. The rock faces are pregnant with condensation, jeweled with droplets that never quite surrender to gravity. They simply hang, trembling, until some microscopic shift in the breathing earth reabsorbs them. Occasionally, a droplet falls, and the sound is unbearably loud in the darkness, like a small bone breaking.

The vegetation doesn't grow here—it performs. In the absolute blackness (the light parameter of 0.3 is a lie, there's simply no light, your eyes have given up and started manufacturing colors that don't exist), the plants have become their own bioluminescent theater. But not the gentle glow of fireflies or deep-sea fish—these are desperate, violent flares of chlorophyll in its death-throes. Vines that pulse cyan as they strangle their hosts. Flowers that bloom in microseconds, screaming ultraviolet as they birth their seeds, then collapse into immediate rot. The density is impossible—every cubic meter contains seventeen different species, all engaged in photosynthetic warfare, all photosynthesizing from each other's light, creating an arms race of luminescence that leaves afterimages burned into your worthless retinas.

Temperature hovers at that precise warmth where mammal flesh becomes indistinguishable from the air. Your skin can't tell where you end and the world begins. The grotto breathes warmer, then cooler, in patterns that match no climate. It's learning what temperature means by trying them all.

The sound—Christ, the sound. At 2.6/3 "deafening," they've understated it. It's not loudness but fullness. Every creature here has evolved to communicate in the absence of light, and they've all chosen different frequencies. The mating calls of things that might be frogs (but you've seen them, they have too many legs and those aren't legs, they're tongues) create a bassline that vibrates your spinal fluid. Over that: the ultrasonic screams of flowers as they bloom-die-bloom. The wet percussion of seeds exploding like popcorn. The geological hum of the young rock still deciding its crystalline structure, emitting tones that make your dental fillings ache. And underneath, always, the breathing.

The fauna. The fucking fauna. At 2.7, "overwhelming" becomes religious. There are so many creatures that they've become a new medium—air is now 40% insect, 30% bird-analog, 20% things that haven't decided. They don't fly so much as exist in Brownian motion, bouncing off your face, laying eggs in your tear ducts, sampling your chemical signature with tongues that emerge from their backs. The young ecosystem hasn't learned restraint yet. Every niche is filled by seventeen species, every resource exploited by a hundred mouths. You've been here maybe ten minutes and already something has evolved to specialize in eating the dead skin between your fingers.

Nothing here has learned to die properly yet. Death is still a novelty, a trick some creatures are trying out. You watch a thing—let's call it a bird, though it's more like a poorly-remembered bird—collide with a wall of strangler-figs. It should die. Instead, it splits into four smaller birds, each carrying away a different organ. They'll recombine later, maybe. Or maybe they'll meet four other birds and create something new. The rules are still being beta-tested.

The erosion parameter lies. At 0.8, this place should be young, fresh-formed. But you see evidence of ancientness everywhere—not weary ancient, but innocent ancient. The rock is young but the patterns are old, as if the grotto was born already containing its own archaeological record. Fossils of creatures that won't evolve for another million years embed in stone that cooled last Tuesday. Time here is still soft, still being kneaded.

You haven't moved in what might be hours. Movement seems... impolite. The grotto is learning you, sampling your boundaries, your chemical signature, your thermal output. Every time you shift your weight, seventeen species recalibrate their behavior. Your body has become an event in the ecosystem. Something has evolved to live in the shadow of your left foot. Something else is trying to decide if your hair is a new geological feature.

The primitive technology exists not as tools but as behaviors. You've watched creatures discover fire seventeen times in the last hour—bioluminescent fire, chemical fire, fire that burns downwards, fire that exists only in the infrared. They discover it, use it, forget it, rediscover it. Technology here is episodic, seasonal, forgetful. The birds build dams that last exactly as long as their memory, which is 3.7 minutes. The beavers fly. Nothing has learned to preserve knowledge because the landscape changes too fast—yesterday's innovation becomes today's evolutionary dead end.

The dynamic turbulence means nothing stays still long enough to become itself. You blink and a vine has become a snake has become a river. The grotto is practicing, improvising, trying on geological personalities. Mountains experiment with being valleys. The breathing quickens—something is about to happen. A species of tree has been evolving for the last twenty minutes specifically to create the sound you're hearing, a sound that will cause another species of insect to evolve ears to hear it, and you realize you've been holding your breath because the air itself has become a member of a species that feeds on mammalian panic.

In the absolute darkness, you finally understand: this isn't a place. It's a becoming. An experimental zone where the universe hasn't decided what laws to enact yet. The parameters aren't describing a landscape—they're describing the process of landscape-formation itself, the raw dough of reality before it gets baked into consistency.

Your skin has begun to photosynthesize. Your hair has learned to lay eggs. Your shadow has evolved predator avoidance behaviors. You are being rewritten faster than you can maintain continuity of self.

The breathing quickens. The grotto is waking up to the fact that it contains you, and it must decide—quickly, before the moment passes—whether you are a resource to be exploited, a threat to be neutralized, or simply another draft of what "landscape" might mean when it grows up.

The sound reaches a crescendo that isn't loud but exists in seventeen dimensions. You feel your eardrums evolving to hear it better, growing complex structures that can parse this new medium of sound-that-is-also-topography. You are being remade as the audience for a performance that hasn't decided what it is yet.

In the young, dense, overwhelming darkness, you realize the most primitive technology is simply continuing to exist in a place that hasn't finished inventing the concept of "existence."
