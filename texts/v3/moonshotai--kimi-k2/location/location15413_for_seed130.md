The first thing is the sound.  
It wells up in your bones before your ears can name it, a pressure that seems older than the ground itself: a grinding reverence of iron on stone, deep-pitched, endless, transmitted by tilted rails the width of roads and by hatch-door pylons five storeys high. Rusted funnels exhale wind-forge moans; wire-rope catenaries hum in sub-harmonics that beat against the chest like second hearts. Everything emits—radiators hiss, sluice gates drum, cooling fins whine. Even the drizzle sings through it. Yet nothing alive answers back.

You stand inside the Ordinarium—an engineered lowland that used to be river delta, marsh, and moraine. Everything below your boots is manufactured now, floor slab poured over an antique sediment that has long since lost the last of its microfossil heat. What water remains is solder-contained: it walks, it weeps, it cycles up to drop again. Gutters of dark steel, taller than a person, run vermillion with runoff, then vanish under grates gated by valve-lungs that sigh open in scheduled synchrony. Ceiling skies, ribbed like the inside of a trillion-year whale, drip in methodical counterpoint to the systolic boom above. There are exceptions: sudden fumarolic hiss from conduits overhead sprays vapour that condenses on exposed skin—instant sweat that never feels warm.

The vegetation is engineering’s verb left to fossilise. Chrome ivy spirals across cooling lattices, but each leaflet is a heat-exchange micro-fin; it clangs like shaken coins in the draught. A single tree—possibly installed as a sardonic joke—rises sixty metres: wrought-iron trunk, gasket joints, foliage stamped from sheet brass. It sheds rectilinear leaves that float down as planes with serrated edges, skittering like metal insects before they are vacuumed into hidden suction mouths at ground level. Otherwise, only lichen-patches of oxidation bloom orange-yellow on pipes, and beaded moisture collects enough dust to make a kind of coarse compost that trails off in grey tongues.

Cool air, monkish and nose-stingingly mineral, is tugged through this hollow valley by underfloor turbines. The world’s ceiling—a single obdurate vault—is invisible in the main. Light is rationed, so the darkness has a granular texture, almost velvet. When a sodium flare ignites somewhere along the southern scaffold, everything edges into the orange underworld of a dying furnace. Shadows lever out thirty metres long, the dark ferric ground suddenly lit to show grind-lines and errant screw heads, rust puddles that glow like cooling lava.

The Ordinarium extends beyond vision east and west—the rails and railed walkways stretch until perspective knots them into a braille pattern against black. Mapwise, it is still the old delta, but topography has been planed. Once there were levees—now there are levellaries: rank upon rank of beam-built decks, bomb-bay doors underneath leaking intermittent cascades. These decks tilt by fractions to count the millimetres of millennial subsidence; servomotors click and stutter an endless arithmetic that nobody reads any more. Listening to them is like listening to a grandfather clock in another room talking to itself about the passage of whole civilisations.

Nothing alive walks here—there is not a rat, not a spore aggressively clawing at decay. There was a cat once, welded into the railing of the observation bridge—antique mascot, steel pelt, rivets for eyes. You rub your hand across it and feel the grit of oxidised tabs. Above you, gondolas the size of town halls glide on overhead tracks, unloading spatula-loads of glacial-thick blue caustic into giant hoppers. Every eight hours one door on a hopper cracks open like a jaw and the sedimentary hiss of concentrated alkalinity descends into a porcelain river just-visible at lantern-depth. This river has banks of baked refractory and flows in about three minutes before drainage gates gulp it back to basement cisterns. The cycle repeats forever—this is the local definition of weather.

You smell it: hot iron under ozone, a starch of wet limestone, then something fiercer—mercaptan used to scent the inert argon streams that blanket the burners. Bright, peppery, impossible to ignore. Ears clog quickly without protection; after twelve hours you feel the sound still moving inside your teeth. An hour after that you’re part of the chorus too—heartbeat synchronises with conveyor pulleys, vision strobes to the twenty-Hertz blink of indicator lamps.

If you keep walking west along what was once margin, you reach a segment called the Choir: pressure vessels thirty stories tall arrayed like pipe organs. On the hour they emit chords too low for human perception, but the concrete eggshell bays compress around them and you have no choice but to feel chord B♭ minor sludge through your liver. Engineers claim they use these to relieve tectonic tension; no tectonics truly happen here anymore, but the idea of a planet kept pacified by eternal song is pious enough to persist. The valves of the Choir rotate open, steam bursts out in slow-motion bouquet, hot rain falls straight back onto cata-cooler ribs. The rain has no time to chill—droplet meets hot metal in a fractal laughter of hiss. Between chords, absolute silence never arrives; instead, the silence is filled with the granular acid hum of 440 megawatts falling piecemeal into entropy.

Once a century, civic consensus spawns a brief maintenance expedition. Technicians arrive armoured like spacemen, clip onto monorails, annotate crack propagation in ultraviolet chalk. They speak through vocoders because speech unamplified is swallowed whole by the iron lung above. On their visors reflections of half-collapsed walks and argent magnesium flares whirl past like meteor showers. They log: “no fauna observed, vegetation nominal—exchange brass leaf specimen #03 to historical index, corrosion acceptable limits, temperature drift 0.4 °C northbound per decade.” Then they glide back to the platform where the last light bulb on the Ordinarium confirms it still rules—the filament coils and births a violet star in an exhausted sky of one colour.

Night, here, is a choice word only; it merely deepens until the clatter becomes auroral bands of sound-shimmer you can identify by turning your head—the bass of intake fans in quadrant four, the tintinnabulation of conveyor glass-scrap nesting into bins, the chime of cooling tower snare drums oscillating against their own echoes. Once you learn to peel them, you can separate overlaid noises the way sailors once parted stars.

Between rail and rail lies the Semantic Field: a tessellation of gridded steel plates each etched in microscopic glyphs—manual fragments, equations for recipes lost to any current tongue, confessions, nursery rhymes, imperatives, all acid-resist etched then forgotten. If you kneel and press your ear to the plate it rings with the same roar as the chorus above, but filtered through two metres of iron syntax—a muttered recitation in a language of vowels made from planetary resonance. Someone—something—once kept updating an endless design here; now the archive serves only as terrain.

You can walk for days and never find an exit. But walk long enough and you will encounter the winding staircase downwards, a brass spiral descending into a shaft so black your torch will tint the rust ruby and nothing else. Down there the rumble becomes pressure itself. No one has been admitted to return with a report, yet the staircase’s burnished tread shows daily abrasion.

The implicit rule, known without utterance: This place has finished becoming. You who walk here do not add to it. You do not subtract. You are simply acoustically folded among pistons until your silhouette begins to echo back altered. Leave sooner than that moment, and you will carry the sound forever like wet wool. Stay too long and you will be the next plate silently receiving instructions on the next patch of scabbed floor to be added once the iron tree completes its self-pruning and drops another cadence of oiled music into the drain.
