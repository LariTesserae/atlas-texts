High on the cool shoulder of a mountain that has not yet been given a name—or perhaps has so many names that none settle—the air is merely moist: a seasonless drip from unseen clouds that condenses on every available needle and scale of the new volcanic shale. Only this moisture prevents the place from burning; only this fragile film of water persuades the world to stay alive.

The elevation is wild for something so small. You can circle the entire plateau in less than an afternoon, but it hangs above the mists like a dry leaf riding an updraft. The young lava flows—still warm to the tongue if you are brave—have broken into courtyard-sized plates. Between these slabs grow the plants, using the cracks as both root-space and drainage. They are lush, preposterously so for such altitude: black carpets of moss starred with sapphire lilies whose petals are lined with silver veins that feel like cooled needles. Every bloom leaks a soft acidic nectar that draws pearly cumulus midges no bigger than a thought. The collective breathing of their wings—once every four heartbeats—passes for wind among the leaves.

Because the place is both cool and dim, sound and light behave as though shy. A normal voice drops away within two paces; fireflies, large as almonds, carry the illumination in candles of cold fire that mirror nothing. They disdain open air and travel instead through tunnels woven by a caste of lung-coloured lichens that have colonised the trunks of stunted dawn-redwoods—trees only twice the height of a grown woman but already centuries old in root-depth. Each redwood wears a greave of lichen that flares violet whenever a foot strikes the ground above, so that walking becomes a soft announcement of colour.

There is one settlement: three concentric rings of stone daubed with fermented petal-glue. The largest ring is a single storey high and windowless; its purpose is to redirect the ceaseless condensation troughs that lick the outer walls. Entrances are barred only by hanging vines, each plaited from the hair of the inhabitants themselves, their greys and blacks paler each generation. In the lull of the afternoon you can see them at work, wrapped in two-ply capes of moss felt stitched with bone needles. They say no metals exist here except a single meteoric hook hanging around the neck of the eldest—invisible beneath layers of garment.

Inside the rings, the town is almost empty, yet can never feel lonely. The surfaces throb with fauna censored everywhere else: millipedes lashed with gold, eight-winged owlets crouched between eaves (when startled they lift straight up in hydraulic silence), and mirror-salamanders that turn glassy at rest so you discover them by finding your own reflection smeared across a doorway. Every loose barrel contains a knot of sympathetic aphids synchronized like breathing organs; their collective abdomen glows in consonance rhythm so slowly you realise it is tracking tidal motion in a distant sea. In another barrel sleeps a colony of white scorpions who dream: their dreams vent out as a fine dust that settles on clay effigies, gifting them mobility for one turn of the year. Thus the town sculpts its own moving guardians overnight.

The rules here feel both fragile and absolute. Nothing may be broken without replanting an equal volume of root; water taken from the sky must be returned as song—the town collects half their condensation dishes and plays them with wooden mallets every dusk in polyrhythms the millipedes translate into soil tremor. The temperature shifts only along vertical centimetres: take one step onto the volcanic flats and summer sweat bursts on you; lower your head into a moss burrow and your breath crusts in lacquer ice. Thus habitability is entirely a question of posture.

Occasionally a new flow of lava mushrooms up through the western cleft—still glowing bone-white at the heart—and the whole plateau reheels. Then erasure and renewal share a heartbeat: new ridges as hard as mantids’ elbows, new crevices where pearls of water instantly pearl again. The settlement lifts overnight, boards detaching from courtyards, garden boxes transliterated toward fresh terraces by patient beetle teams. No hammer rings; only lichen flares deeper violet until everything is reseated.

Past the town the plateau drops westward into an atmospheric bowl, dimmer still, where no vegetation has yet claimed purchase. A lake of wind stands here: a merely dampness condensed so thickly it behaves like mercury. You can row it in gourd coracles whose oars are patches of dawn-redwood bark. The fauna crossing that lake fly rather than swim—hollow-boned ravens constructing rafted nests out of their own cast feathers. They do not caw. In the silence you hear only the stickiness of water buckling against the prow and the faint pulse of each raven nest: a sub-audible chime that makes your teeth vibrate until you taste iron from blood recently bitten.

Return east and the plateau ends suddenly at a cliff wall. It is not the wall of the mountain so much as the utter limit of any world. Sky here is only another mineral layer; the void beyond resonates like the inside of a bell. From this edge issue slow lights—falling, rising—it is impossible to tell—that turn out to be centipedes molting in mid-air, their armoured skins unzipping into lanterns as they glide on thermal drafts. Their emptied casings drift down onto the settlement to become eerily flexible panels for windows that wet themselves shut when the smell of rain unsentimentally arrives an hour before sunset.

In winter-that-is-not-winter, the moss keeps respiring and the owlets moult a second time. The inhabitants begin dyeing the plucked wings in diluted nectar, weaving them into capes for children whose skeletons have not yet hardened to adult postures. These children climb the dawn-redwoods at dusk caped in six other kinds of flight. They ascend only three armspans above the lichen brims, and from that perch they hurl wing-drums into the sky. Empty casings of the centipedes, still glowing, catch the updrafts and teach silence an architecture: arcs, asymmetric parabolas, wendings that only the blind larvae living underground can see and map. When the last drum blinks out high above the cloud, the whole plateau exhales—an exhalation you feel between your vertebrae as a cold agreement that tomorrow the settlement will still be here or somewhere the same height above remembering.

And so the rules compound:  
1. The plateau owns its own gravity thin as frost; step too lightly and you skim into the lake of wind.  
2. What you kill must become language—bones are carved into articulating shadows for the window screens.  
3. Children own the space between 1.2 metres and 2.1 metres above ground—above that is choir territory for unborn ravens, below sleeps lava memory.  
4. No fire but the meteor’s hook may stay alight past first dawn redwood shadow.  
5. Songs must be returned to the sky in the same number of pulses borrowed from rainfall. Any excess shadow remains inside your lungs overnight; those who have stored more than four nights of silence speak only in colours thereafter.

The inhabitants tally their lives in these rules and in beads of condensation laid out on shale like a score. Their names for the place change to fit each resurfacing, but translate roughly to “The Breath under the Leaf’s Navel.” They will live another seventy eruptions while the rest of the world renovates in meters, miles, empires. For here, magnitude is measured in the interval between a centipede’s lantern losing its light and the eye finally closing on the image: the moment when smallness itself becomes infinite, and everything pauses to finish the hush.
