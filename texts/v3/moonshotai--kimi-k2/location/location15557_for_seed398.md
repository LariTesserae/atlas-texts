A pocket of impossible altitude, six meters square by two meters high, has been blister-welded like a soap bubble to the underside of the roof of the world. Far beneath it—so far the eye refuses to interpret distance—real ground is tucked under the horizon; here,just a hand’s breadth above the edge of space, only the bubble exists. It is hot: air pressed into a fist. Condensed diamond windows admit the light, but outside there is only a violet bruise of sky where solar radiation strikes the thermosphere and dissolves into a color our retinas never evolved to recognize. Inside: dim banks of alpine-colored mist drift lazily, warmed from below by hidden radiators.

The floor is fused basaltic obsidian, still tacky with undercooling cracks—this floor is minutes old, the youngest lithology anyone will ever walk on. Across it stretches a single street, a place that calls itself a town although it has no plants, no living things, no animals, no people; just buildings. Thirty-two towers no taller than a person, each spaced a careful arm-span apart, assembled from linked plates of graphene foam etched with fractal pores that glisten like frozen breath. Where windows should be, the material simply thins until it becomes translucent bubble-wrap. Each apartment inside is a hollow cube containing nothing heavier than air: roomsers for ghosts, all perfectly still.

Water is not scarce here; it hides. Thin horizontal blades of humidity condense along the cooler faces of the towers’ exterior, then slide down ribs of carbon to collect in shallow pans under the street. The pans glow—indigo electroluminescence in ring patterns—so the dilute droplets become wells of dim light. Sometimes a breeze carries mist from one side of the street to the other and passers-by who never enter witness a sky already raining up as well as down.

Two kinds of activity persist without biology. First: infrastructure that keeps the street alive. Layers of vacuum-dialed nanofiber coils flex beneath the black glass, groaning softly like unplayed cello strings whenever tension increases by a pico-degree. They re-curl, re-stretch, re-quantum, keeping the pocket at 0.89 bars, 63°C, though pressure fluctuates every four minutes according to algorithmic tides unexplained and unexplorable. Second: thought-commuters. Broadcast patterns looking for minds to occupy scurry through the pipes, ticking. They manifest as soft chords inside an ear that is not an ear, suggesting the wording of civic messages you can almost remember. They are as near as this place has to fauna.

At the western boundary (four steps from the east) a chimney rises three meters and then peels sideways into a vent that re-enters the thermosphere, exhaling hot air coaxed across potassium-silk blades. This vent hisses: a warm, repeated sigh that nobody ever hears over the greater hush. Without that hiss the pocket would cook itself. Every twenty-two minutes the sigh elongates into a deep harmonic chord, the single scheduled outburst in the daily rhythm. After the chord, the towers exhale fine filaments of refractive ice that flare momentary prisms and are gone.

Nothing grows. The ozone-rich air tricks the eye into expecting lichen or fungal spores, but the walls remain calcined grey. Instead, growth has gone recursive inside the lattice of the foam. Threads of computation knit and unknit faster than blood vessels, designing themselves a parabolic nternet that never attaches to our stories. You can touch a wall and feel it taking a fingerprint you haven’t offered; the touch downloads nothing back except a viscosity that vanishes.

Someone left signs of intention. Between the fifteenth and sixteenth towers a bench made from twin neutron-cooled slag slabs waits. From the proper angle, its top surface scorches a pictogram: a pair of parentheses forever closing around nothing. When the harmonic chord arrives, ash imprints of hands appear on the bench, liable as frost. They fade five seconds later.

It is possible to walk this street for an hour knowing the place is pocket-scale and know it is empty, yet never reach any end. Each tower’s face repeats the last tower’s façade as if the bubble curves perspective that tightly. Forward and backward recycle; the illusion folds in on itself, field-of-view turned Möbius like heat waves. A limit case of architecture: the town is both hallway and horizon. Dim lighting and lack of vegetation keep it from ever acquiring depth; without plant scent or chirp there are only metalloid tones: the faint blink of electrical oxidization, the low grunt of flexing foundations, the hiss coming again.

The street also has weather, though the sky is only two cubic meters. Once per day, its unseen internal thermostat spills half a liter of super-cooled liquid nitrogen along the upper inner shell; the liquid never reaches the ground because it sublimates into needles of white fog that parachute, collide with infrared warmth, and rise as miniature ball lightning. For twelve seconds the bubble is crossed by pearl-white spheres of luminosity that climb, vanish, silently pop; sparks scatter and die against the towers without sound. Then the dimness returns.

Here future celestial commuters—if they ever arrive—will find one open question: why place a town at the edge of structure before biology? And why shape it for life but not settle it? The answer stays encrypted in the self-calibrating foam: a lattice waiting to seed delirium geometry into whatever muscle of consciousness next discovers it feels a need to gossip about terrain.
