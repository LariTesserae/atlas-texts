The sky is a perfect mirror, a burnished silver bowl inverted three hundred arm-spans above the hamlet, so low you feel you could leap and rap it with your knuckles.  Everything beneath that sky is brighter than noon, yet nothing casts a shadow; the light oozes out of the air itself, thick as mercury, collecting in folds of stone and skin until faces gleam like wet coins.  The cold is equally diffuse, a zero-degree presence that never bites, only presses—an even, ghostly palm against every pore. Water is everywhere, but never as itself.  It drips upward from spindrift grasses, condenses on the underside of cobbles, beads like sweat on the lime-washed walls.  A thousand times a second the tiny droplets lose heart and fall back into the stone, making no sound.

There is no soil, only an interlocked mosaic of slate-blue pebbles mortared by ice.  From this pavement rise the houses—no more than forty—each the size of a roadside shrine, built of plates sawn from the local schist, their edges so worn they fit without mortar.  Roofs are low tunnels of living quartz that grew, century by century, into the desired span; the inhabitants only had to scoop out the heart.  Every roof leaks light: the quartz is full of internal fractures, and the brightness trapped above rains through in thin, slow trickles that puddle on the floors and are bailed out each dawn with bronze scoops shaped like lily petals.

Between the houses run alleyways exactly shoulder-wide, paved with identical stones but each at a different angle, so the hamlet is a heap of tilted planes, a calcified spill of toy blocks frozen mid-tumble.  Children use the slopes for play: they lie flat and glide on the films of refracted water that coat the stone, laughing without sound.  Words are unnecessary.  The air itself carries meanings—minute pressure changes that nudge the tympanic skin stretched over every resident’s cheekbones.  A conversation is a shared shifting of posture, a choreography of almost-stillness in which the bright silence remains unbroken.

Vegetation is counted, not grown.  There are exactly seventy-nine “plants,” though none are rooted.  Each is a briar of glass-fine stems jointed like insect legs, mined from the mountain’s interior and traded hand to hand for generations.  Their hollow twigs contain moving water: not flowing, but writhing—tiny braided currents that swim in place.  When a stem is snapped the water escapes as a winged beetle of liquid crystal that evaporates halfway across the alley, adding its volume to the airborne reservoir.  The people prune the briars carefully, never letting the population drop or rise; the equilibrium is older than any grandparent’s story.

The same ledger rules the fauna.  Animals here are extra, overflow, too-many-for-the-volume.  They exist two-dimensionally, pressed into the flat brightness like moths in lantern glass.  You see them edge-on: a shoal of fish turning inside a wall, a bear’s profile gliding across the threshold stone, a parliament of owls whose eyes are the very spots of lamp-dazzle on a wet roof.  Touch them and your hand meets only warm light, but step away and they bulk again, rustling, breathing.  Their turbulence is constant; every moment a creature folds into another or divides like cells, so the total mass of hide, scale, feather, and claw is conserved but the census never still.  The people herd this turbulence with gestures learned in infancy, guiding flocks of brightness through doorways so that traffic can pass, milking the condensation of a wolf-shadow for fat that will burn smokelessly in their stone lamps.

Above the hamlet the mountain continues for only a rope’s length more, then ends in midair, sheared clean by the sky-mirror.  From this cliff edge issues the wind that drives the whole miniature weather: an exhalation of ancient ice breathing out names of vanished glaciers.  The wind strikes the mirror, is reflected downward, and becomes the pressure-language, the water-cycles, the silent thunder that keeps every inhabitant awake but dreaming.  Erosion has been completed here; the place is a leftover knot the wind could not unpick.  Even the people feel themselves as silt—one more layer of mineral attention.

They have tools, but only three: a bronze flake that remembers every cut it has made, a mallet of petrified sinew that asks permission before it strikes, and a net woven from moon snail silk that catches only things the catcher has forgotten.  With these they tend the briars, harvest the mirrored eggs of nonexistent birds, enlarge their houses by singing tiny faults into the quartz until it graciously spreads.  Metal is scarce because ore, when brought above the cloud-line, forgets its gravity and drifts away as bright vapor; thus every blade is older than the hands that grip it, and every coin of bronze has been shaved so thin it is legible only by touch.

Visitors—there have been seven—arrive by mislaying their own reflection.  Should you lose yours in a still pond, a shop window, a lover’s eye, you may find yourself tilted out of gravity and sliding sideways into this over-bright cold where nothing casts a shade.  The hamlet accepts the cast-off image, cuts it to fit, gives it errands and a two-dimensional goat to tend.  Eventually the visitor becomes a local pressure-change, forgets sound, forgets shadow, and forgets the way back.  The place is small; it fills quickly.  Its density is not of mass but of simultaneity—too many stories per cubic finger-width, too many hearts per pocket of light.

And it is growing smaller.  Each time a briar is clipped, a roof is bailed, a child glides downhill, the margins draw inward by the breadth of a thumbnail.  One day the mirror will meet the pavement and the entire population will stand sandwiched in a blade of brightness, a fossilized breath, a page of pure cold water that someone, somewhere, will mistake for a moment of silence.
