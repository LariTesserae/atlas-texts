Water everywhere, but never enough to pool. Moisture sleets off the rocks in sweating sheets, then climbs back up the cliffs within the same heartbeat, drawn by the fever heat. The water cycle has contracted into a single breath: skin-drizzle, gut-boil, sky-mist, repeat. It folds itself in pleats of steam that braid between ridgelines; were you to touch a droplet it would feel heavier than lead—liquid that has forgotten what gravity means. The rock weeps into itself; each face gleams wetly, obsidian throats swallowing and regurgitating the same tears.

No plants. The lichens have perished, the mosses tidally pulled under stone-water and digested. It is a mineral hearth, a kiln turned inside-out where microbes might have learned to sing but have not yet arrived. Growth is postponed; the territory is still negotiating the grammar of existence. Meanwhile life—obscene, overheated—arrives in fast-furious packets that do not need vegetal language.

What crawls is too numerous for any forest: vermilion millipedes welded into hoops, wings of glass beetles cooled and cracked, translucent eels whose bodies taper into nerve endings and simply pass through one another like sighs. They congregate around the gash between two summits where lava vents click and breathe like soft-shutter cameras. This is the thermal niche: everything alive crowds the same thread of air. The population density is crushing—creatures fit in stratified layers three grand organisms thick, pressed into tall narrow stacks where a flea’s eyelash of space is luxury. Some individuals exist literally inside others, parasitically interlaced until biological boundaries are too tired to care. The air, therefore, is a quiet riot of muffled wings, of chitin settling, of soft exhalations passing through hollow femurs—but the net sound remains library hush, a cushion of mutual embarrassment.

Each hour endures half a normal day, the sun caught in its own fever dream, so the light is forever mid-afternoon—ochre, baked, normal—while shadows splay like butterflies stuck in taffy. Their edges wobble; occasionally a shadow detaches, steps across the ridge to converse with its rock of origin. Reality frays here: the cliff faces develop pores that dilate and dilate until entire beetle caravans step through them as doors. A waterfall is just an argument of water with itself and sometimes the water wins; the falls seize, reverse, kneading upward to become a column that keeps climbing until it leaks out the base of the nail-world, dripping into the prince’s cuticle—where presumably he shudders and shifts his thumb, causing miniature earthquakes inside our pocket-latitude.

The primitive tech is only half-formed: heat-glass bubbles spat from the lava vent cool into cups, spindles, daggers, but no creature owns the hands to lift them; they freeze in the act of invention, tools forever on the cusp of being found. The beetles circle them with a kind of nostalgia, as though recognizing older relatives they cannot yet become.

The environment is hungry and small beneath the macro-sky of ordinary fingernails. Still it lunges forward; every facet moves at coordinated biological orchestration. And yet, in the interstices—when a shadow forgets which cliff it belongs to—in those breath-width hiccups, the landscape keeps running out of reality the way an engine coughs on thin air. It recharges by cannibalising its boundaries: one grain of basalt sold, moment by moment, to keep the illusion alive.
