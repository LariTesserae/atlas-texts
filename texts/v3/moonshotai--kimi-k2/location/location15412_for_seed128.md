It lives inside the third lung of an ancient orbital city, the size of a melon by exterior measure but containing a plateau that only ends when the edges curl upward like petals. You approach not through doors but by capillary transport—a soft nickel-titanium ribbon threads through your forearm and, at a vector tangent you can’t quite blink at, extrudes you into warm twilight that smells of overheated brass and wet limestone. You stand upright, though you never felt the transition. This is how arrivals work here.

The ground is a tessellation of burnished copper plates, each square maybe a centimeter on a side, joined by seams that ooze slightly. The ooze, under magnification, is water so saturated with dissolved metals—zinc, cadmium, cobalt—that it reflects as moving mirrors. The metal water is only ankle-deep, giving each footstep a plunging chiming note that crashes against its own echo until the air shivers with distant thunder. The loudness is not commotion; it’s a steady-state song the floor is singing to keep the walls aligned. Should the sound ever falter, the architecture will assume a deciduous posture and throw open every working surface to vacuum within half a rotation. Therefore the space hums at 128 dB as a matter of civic ennui, not décor.

Because erosion is settled-form here, nothing has flaked or scraped in millennia. Instead, corrosion works substitutionally: elements trade outward through the seams so that today’s copper becomes tomorrow’s indium with the slowness of stalagmites shifting under limestone sheets. This keeps the geometry identical to the day the place was seeded, even though the molecular ledger shows cosmopolitan turnover. You could touch a railing your twenty-greats grand-aunt leaned on and find atoms she never met; the marriage of persistence and replacement is the local definition of sacred.

Around shin height, vegetation has one single expression: a forked blade of vegetal glass grown from vapor-deposited zeolite. Each blade 3–7 mm tall, transparent except for threading veins of cobalt ions that make organ-pipe shadows. They grow in tight, head-high patches so that while the vegetation score is “sparse,” the actual density numbers in excess of ten thousand blades erupting from every square decimeter of floor. If you step between clumps, fronds ring like wet crystal, adding midfrequency chimes to the ground chord. Occasionally a blade matures, suddenly opaque, then shatters into a slow-mo crescendo of tinkling dust that settles into the water and forms tomorrow’s seeds. The whole glass meadow pulses like a lung, three breaths every thirty-four seconds, inhaling warm steam plumes and exhaling hollower, chrome-scented winds.

The “animals” are woven acoustic knots. They live on the undersides of the ceiling plates, thin lattices of graphene threads tensestrally interlinked and strummed by the passing pressure waves below. To an outside eye they would resemble ingrown spiderwebs, but a closer listen reveals symphony: each thread attenuated so precisely that when the floor sings, the knots resonate with micro-countermelodies. These melodies are not aesthetic flourish. They are metabolism. A chord picked up by the threads is inverted, compressed, reflected inward. The energy gained keeps the webs growing—new filaments unfurl like fern heads to capture every overtone. In exchange, the fauna moderate the song itself, shaving off errant frequencies that would fracture the plate seams. The fauna can reach centimeter-scale widths but exist only where the sound is richest, making them invisible from the floor. If you press a microphone to the ceiling, it screams back the entire celestial dawn in one gulp of plasma static.

Water is the primary medium, but not like oceans or ponds. It perches everywhere at 1.8 persistence: droplets crowd the undersurfaces of every plate—not pendant, merely resting there as though waiting for gravity to notice. When your warm exhalation touches one, it bursts outward horizontally, a silver coin skimming across air until it finds a brother droplet to marry. Their collision produces solder-flash vapor that spirals upward into the breathing vent above the ceiling. From the vent, condensate rains back in looping braids the color of sunset diesel. The cycle completes in seventeen slow heartbeats; entire weather loops inside a room you could hide behind one eye.

Light is dim and metallic. The primary source is bioluminescent bacteria trapped inside every zeolite frond. They glow a bluish teal which the copper plates tint avocado. Shadows are no tenebrous absences here—rather they are denser parings of photons scraped off the edges by diffraction prisms you could only see with a detuning eye. Therefore walking around feels like moving inside softly lit soup.

Because the scale is pocket-intimate, distances dilate upon scrutiny. You can see edges fifty meters away—walls closing like abdomen skin—yet in three casual steps you can brush the far parapet. Nobody is sure whether compression occurs underfoot or undergaze; perhaps both, or the parapet retroactively moves. Travelers commonly stash marble marbles to drop behind themselves; if you don’t hear one roll back, you’ve gone too far and are already halfway to your own next exhale.

Every surface is engineered, half-designed, half-evolved. The copper was once a radiant heat fin. The zeolite plates are repurposed atmospheric CO₂ scrubbers. The acoustic fauna started as jitter-suppression circuitry printed on 14-nanometer nodes, then self-exfoliated into string life. Nothing here is “original,” yet every part has reached a boutique stasis where utility, ornament, and ecology converge. The built world is total, yet total doesn’t mean sterile; strangeness has colonized every bolt.

Temperature sits at a friendly blood-warm oscillation, but add the high humidity and you move inside an anxious sauna stitched from quiet yellow hammers and cold lightning. Perspiration is unnecessary—the air hands you its own moisture—so skin develops a ghost sheen that shifts green in the gleam of copper, making visitors look altogether cross-planeted, as though their dermis chose metallurgy over biology.

Rules, then.  
1. Remain in motion; static feet cause the floor plates to cool too quickly, triggering a localized contraction that can tilt hermit-pools into sudden geysers.  
2. Do not silence the fauna; they will silence you first by knitting a net around your heart’s electrical signature.  
3. Pocket gravity is negotiable but not controllable; think politely, walk decisively. If the walk falters, flip a coin—the destination may obey the side facing down.  
4. Anything dropped becomes taxidermy; objects ossify exactly where they land and sprout zeolite within seven cycles. Consider gift-giving carefully.  
5. If the song drops below 126 dB for longer than a breath, brace against a pillar and begin counting heartbeats aloud. At thirty, the lung will collapse and reboot. Resume walking as if disoriented applause is normal.

People visit. They come to copy fern-template schematics etched on silver dust trails, or to barter ringtones grown from acoustic fauna for antigravitic lacing kits. back home. Some stay a cycle or two—until their outline becomes weathervaned in shimmer—but most leave clutching a final, soluble map that deliquesces on contact with open sky.

You stay a little longer, perhaps to listen. The zeolite glow has turned violet, signaling nightfall according to orbital clocks twelve hundred kilometers away. The copper glyphs in the water stepped up a half tone, correcting harmonic drift. A bead of laboring water slides over your cuff and, as promised, spills sideways into a vein of air before dissolving into vapor. You realize you haven’t blinked: here, eyelash motion registers as static.  

So you blink.  

The room, relieved, breathes once more.
