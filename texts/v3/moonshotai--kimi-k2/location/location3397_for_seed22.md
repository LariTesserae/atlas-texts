The sky is not sky here—it is a ceiling, still cooling, riven by contraction cracks that drip a slow ammoniac rain.  Below it stretches the newborn flood-basin called the Hollow Ghyll: a valley only forty generations old, still exhaling primordial heat.  The ground is black basalt glass, wrinkled like cooled caramel, punched with vent holes that sigh warm steam every few heartbeats.  Moisture is rationed; it arrives as silver capsules the size of thumbnails that pop in mid-air, release their cargo in lazy diamonds, then collapse into ash.  Nothing ever truly dries, nothing ever truly drenches.  The air tastes of coin-metal and petrichor.

Vegetation has rushed in faster than geology could finish arguing with itself.  Great arching banyans of a lighter gravity grow downward and upward at once, their trunks hollow, fluted, strung with bioluminescent orchids that bloom, seed, and die within a single dusk.  Each orchid is a loudspeaker: when its petals separate they unleashed a stored compression wave—a recorded slice of yesterday’s thunder or the laughter of an unseen child—so that progress through the forest is like walking through a scattered library of noises.  The combined acoustic pressure makes leaves tremble; they answer back with cymbal-sharp clacks, turning the canopy into one giant, self-playing marimba.  Deafening, yes, but the fauna have evolved elastic eardrums that retract into skull grooves at the first decibel spike.  

Birds—if birds they are—are translucent tetrahedrons of living quartz filled with slow-wheeling plankton that glow rose and lime.  They migrate by clapping their geometric wings together, creating pockets of vacuum; the collapse of each vacuum coughs a violet streak of light that ricochets off the low cloud-deck.  Their flocking behavior writes entire sheet-maps of coherent plasma across the sky, and the maps are read, in turn, by the city.

The city has no name citizens can agree on; its map refreshes hourly.  It is built from programmable basalt: the same once-liquid rock that geysered up only centuries ago, now coaxed by femto-catalysts into lattice memories.  Buildings bud.  A tower will emerge overnight, its walls still recording the thermal history of their own cooling; by afternoon it may decide to grow balconies wings and migrate three kilometers river-ward, towing its infrastructure of water and memory.  Because the terrain is young, nothing is yet “zoned.”  Market stalls sprout on flying buttresses of obsidian foam; a courthouse may squat like a toad, then belittle itself into a bridge when the river it meant to judge decides to change course.  Streets are hollow tubes—the ex-gray-matter of the buildings—through which passenger capsules are fired by coherent sound.  You board by stepping into a cylinder, surrendering your weight, and being screamed toward your destination at the speed of resonance.

The river itself is a tech artifact.  It was not here at the valley’s birth; it was summoned, braided out of atmospheric moisture and nanofilament root systems.  Its bed is a crawling carpet of glass eels—soft robots that intake basaltic sand, extrude it downstream as black silk bars usable by the city’s seedlings.  Whenever the eels overproduce, surplus bars rise from the water like altar screens, humming in the breeze until architecture claims them.  The river, then, is both transport and lumberyard, circulation and supply chain.

Human presence is statistically empty—density 0.1.  Fewer than nine hundred thousand souls dwell along a valley that could cradle ten million.  Most elect to remain semi-corporeal: their consciousness rides the acoustic rails, hopping orchid-speakers, inhabiting flocks of quartz birds.  Bodies are stored in dew-catching sarcophagi that stand alone in vast fields, spaced so wide you can walk an hour and meet no-one, only the soft percussive drip of capsule rain on stone.  When someone needs corporeality, a sarcophagus unfurls like a paper flower, assembling amino fog into the remembered shape of spine and smile.  They step out, barefoot on vitreous crust, hearing their own pulse echo back from the vent-hole geysers, and they speak—conversation here must be declaimed against the orchid roar, so rhetoric has become half-sung, half-shouted.

Predators exist, though they look like misplaced weather.  The commonest is the “thunder-bloom.”  It begins as a bruise-colored cloud no larger than a teacup, drifting at knee height.  When it senses biothermal warmth it blossoms into a rotating torus of violet electricity, unfolding scythe-arms of ionized water.  It mines for calcium, not flesh—one strike can suck the skeleton from a grazing beast and leave the hide to collapse, flaccid and astonished.  The valley floor is littered with such puddled hides, slowly turning into peat that smells of lightning.  Yet even thunder-biums are prey; quartz birds dive through them, harvesting charge to fuel their sky-scripts.  Thus every death is a transference of either minerals or music.

Night comes seldom, and when it does it is artificial: the city decides that enough computation has been harvested from light, so louvers close across the sky-crack, blocking the upwelling glow from the planet’s still-molten mantle.  Then it is truly dim, a burgundy dusk you can taste.  Dew capsules continue to burst, each flash printing a snow-negative photograph of the canopy onto your retina.  The orchid speakers quiet one by one, like choirboys losing breath, and you hear—for the first time—the river eels gnawing stone into silk.  That grinding is the valley’s lullaby: new land being sewn by synthetic worms, new architecture being auditioned in darkness.

Weirdness?  Every law of conservation you learned is politely escorted to the gate, given a souvenir, and sent home.  Energy is borrowed from sound and paid back as architecture; calcium robbed from bones might surface next week as the keystones of a cathedral’s memory.  Cause and effect are still negotiating terms.  A child can hum a hole in the ground; an adult can forget the shape of their own sarcophagus so completely the city refuses to rebuild them until they invent a new geometry.  Visitors are advised to carry a personal constant—an impossible word, a prime-number heartbeat—lest they dissolve into the general chorus.

But no one visits.  The sky is still cooling; the map is still tender.  The Hollow Ghyll is lush, warm, empty, deafening, dim yet scrawled with noon-bright plasma, urban without crowds, technological beyond the patience of physics, eroding the idea that the world must ever be finished.  Step softly: every footfall is a ballot, and the valley is counting.
