They call it the Hatchery, though nothing alive has ever stirred here.

Imagine a cavern scooped from the planet’s arid crust by a single blistering punch—an impact still so fresh that the stone steams when condensation trickes down from the ceiling. The hollow is only a few city blocks across, a bubble pressed under kilometers of sun-baked sandstone, yet every cubic meter is claimed, layered, re-layered, subdivided until the air itself feels mortgaged.

You arrive by funicular: a rust-red cage that drops through a shaft so tight your ears pop twice before the darkness swallows the last sliver of sky. When the gate clangs open you step onto a platform of girdered iron suspended above black nothing. Heat oozes upward; the air is 48 °C and carries the taste of ground solder. Moisture beads on every surface not yet warm enough to scald it away, so the metal sings with sudden ticks—condensation dying on contact like moth wings.

Below the platform the city accretes downward instead of outward. Tenements of poured basalt hang from chains. Each block is only four rooms wide, but stacked thirty beneath the next, so the whole cavity resembles a hive of dark lanterns bolted to the ceiling of the world. Between them run catwalks no wider than your thigh, chains for handrails, pulleys that squeak once every few minutes when a freight bucket climbs or drops. There are no street levels, only ledges; there are no avenues, only vertical chutes ringing with the cough of pistons.

Light is proprietary. Each household rents a single carbon-filament bulb from the Iron & Glow Guild; bulb-arms dangle on knotted cables that splice, splice again, until the glow dilutes to umber. You see best by looking sideways: the aggregate of a hundred half-lights smears walls with bruise-colored halos. Shadows overlap until depth collapses; you navigate by memorized footholds.

The processes never stop. A constant tremor rides the air—presses beating sheet metal into gutters for the anticipated rains that almost never arrive; conveyor belts carry rivets downward to workshops a kilometer below the cavern floor; blast fans suck superheated air outside toward radiator fins half-buried in desert sun. Somewhere water flashes to vapor, re-condenses on chilled pipes, is collected in thimbles, taxed, redistributed. Nothing is wasted, nothing is enough.

The citizens—if you dare call them that—wear wet cloth wrapped like armor under boiler suits; the cloth dries on one flank while the other grows damp. They move quick as dancers, elbows in, shoulders hunched, because every corridor is shoulder-width and two bodies cannot pass without negotiation. Their eyes shimmer under the perpetual dusk, pupils wide, giving each face a nocturnal shimmer. No one asks for names; occupancy is the only identity. If you leave your assigned span for more than three ledges you must register at a checkpoint, pay the air tax.

Vegetation is contraband but tolerated in pockets. Lichen engineered in vats clings to drainpipes; its glow is bioluminescent and needs no bulb fee, so the poor cultivate it inside sleeves, cuffs, behind ears—tiny colonies traded like prison cigarettes. One dangling terrace hosts tomato vines pruned to thimble size; fruit reddens but never swells larger than grapes, their sour sweetness cutting the metallic air for one week each year. When that harvest arrives citizens queue barefoot on the grating; the juice costs an hour’s ration of potable water.

Potable water: the city’s heartbeat. Condensers sip the cavern’s breath at night, when the stone cools enough for vapor to gather. The supply is young, fickle, yet already claimed, parceled, futures-traded. Children learn to read by the drip-code of overflow pipes—one drip per second means surplus, two mean shortage, none means curfew. They chant the code like nursery rhymes while skipping across hop-grids—metal discs riveted to chains to keep toes from blistering on hot steel.

You notice the acoustics. Sound ricochets along cylindrical walls up, down, around so a hammer blow four levels below arrives as a church bell above your scalp. Yet the city has grown an etiquette of hush; conversations murmur, machines padded, clogs wrapped in rag. The aggregate hush thickens until a single dropped wrench tolls like fate. Quiet is currency; loudness is surveilled. Even the furnaces cough through baffles, surrender timbre but keep heat.

No fauna—no rats, no cockroaches, no bats. The founders brag about this sterile perfection. Yet inside the silence you sense a substitute wildlife: rumors breed, scamper ledge to ledge. A hydraulic ghost said to live in shaft twelve; the widow who measures time by dripping water onto tin until it births a stalagmite child; the ladder that adds rungs overnight, reaching nowhere. People speak of these like sightings of owls.

Erosion is arrestedic here—too young for rounding, too fierce for softness. Corners remain surgical. New plates are bolted over yesterday’s rust; tomorrow’s rust already blossoms underneath. The city grows by shellac: each decade another skin, thinner, harder, until someday the bubble will be a solid geode of iron. Engineers say when that happens the heat of metabolism will anneal the whole mass into a single alloy fossil. They speak of this with pride, as if the purpose of a womb were to become stone.

Weirdness: the geometry gnaws at equilibrium. Walk twenty meters along spiral staircase B-43 and you descend two floors yet arrive above your starting altitude—some inversion blamed on thermal warping during construction no one will admit to authorizing. Compass needles rotate; not north but “net flow” becomes cardinal direction. Citizens subconsciously re-map; newcomers vomit within an hour. Cartographers sell pocket tables that autocorrect, updated weekly.

And yet, for all its density, there are voids. Micro-cavities between girders where no maintenance hand fits, stuffed now with forgotten blueprints folded so tight they’ve fused into a laminate harder than steel. These negative spaces glow faintly, as if remembering bulb-fee they never spent. Children dare each other to crawl them, to listen. Those who crawl back claim to hear an echo before their own breath—an inhalation belonging to the city itself, gathering strength.

Above everything sits the Hatchery’s lid: a single basalt slab seeded with copper veins in the pattern of an ovum. Once every thirteen months, on the night the surface desert finally exhales a storm, thunder rolls down the access shaft and the copper paths conduct static into the cavern until every rail sparks blue. For five minutes the darkness becomes a camera flash of skeletons—catwalk ribs, cage ribs, human ribs—before returning to umber. Citizens crowd balconies to witness; it is their only holiday. They say the flash imprints a memory in the stone, another layer toward fossil. They applaud softly, careful not to break their contract with silence.

You begin to understand: the Hatchery never intended to birth living things. It incubates itself. One day, when the water runs too thin, when the lichen strains finally fail, when the funicular cable parts, the city will cool, settle, and lock into a nugget of alloyed time. Someone on the surface—centuries hence—will quarry the cave and find not corpses but facets: a perfect negative of every dwelling, every thirst, every half-light. A geode city, crystallized longing.

And yet even now, down on sub-tier thirty-one where boilerboys nurse cracked turbines, rumors circulate of an exit nobody designed: a fissure exhaling cool air, smelling of rainforests no one here has seen, wide enough for a body, narrowing fast, leading who-knows-where. No official map records it; no census lists those who leave. But every so often a citizen fails to pay the air tax, forfeits bulb rental, and simply is not there—space folded into possibility. The city keeps churning, dense, industrious, dim, hot, dripping, clanging just under audition, eroding upward into its own future, patient as any mineral, half-alive only in the way a wound is alive—closing, scabbing, remembering nothing of skin except that there once was.
