Pre-trip briefing: the place you are going is not on any chart that will help you come back. Below even the idea of “abyss,” there is a plain where the planet keeps its erased drafts. Going there is easy; remaining human while you do it is the impossible art. The notes below are not advice in the usual sense—they are the minimum set of self-recognition tricks we know that still permit a biological brain to find its way home through ten thousand meters of amnesia-colored water.

1. FIRST, CHOOSE THE BODY YOU WANT TO RETURN TO  
Pressure at that depth turns cartilage to putty and bone to brittle ceramic. Before you ship out, insist on the full chromed “Siren” modification:  
- Titanium-ceramic femurs and tibiae, marrow laced with shock-absorbing gallium struts.  
- Ribcage swapped for a pressurized honeycomb of aerographene; lungs collapsed and replaced by a perfluorocarbon loop that never tries to fight the tonnage—instead it equalizes, molecule for molecule.  
- Corneas peeled off and re-grown with variable-index graphene so your retinas do not implode when you look sideways.  
- Mandible pinned with memory-springs: if you ever try to open your mouth below five hundred atmospheres the joint simply refuses—prevents you from accidentally inviting the ocean into your trachea.  
Do not accept cheaper “abyss-rated” packages sold to cargo companies; they only guarantee survival, not coherence. You want the version that lets you remember your own face in a mirror afterward.

Mental add-on: have the surgeons install an “Anna Karenina” anchor—an epileptic micro-seizure pattern, harmless, that flashes your mother’s maiden name in Morse across your visual field once every minute. It is the cheapest insurance against the subtle slope of forgetting that begins the moment you pass two thousand meters.

2. CLOTHE YOURSELF IN A SECOND LANGUAGE  
Sound is the only sense that still works down there, but it speaks in pressure, not words. Pick a language you do not already dream in (most travelers choose Basque or Ainu) and rehearse, aloud, a children’s rhyme until it becomes autonomic. You will lose color, you will lose pain, you will lose the horizon—when everything else goes the rhyme will still be looping in your inner ear, a lifeline of consonants. The Agency calls this “packing a breadcrumb made of grammar.”

3. THE EXOSUIT IS NOT ARMOR; IT IS A PROSTHESIS FOR LONELINESS  
Standard issue:  
- Outer hide of woven iridium wire: immune to the microscopic knives of chilled silt.  
- Mid-layer: 3 cm of vacuum-impregnated gel that hardens only when a decagonal crack in the clay exhales H₂S—keeps the smell of rotten eggs (and therefore the knowledge that chemistry still exists) from seducing you into hallucinations of company.  
- Inner liner: photo-chromatic membranes that copy whatever faint photon arrives and replay it across your skin so that your propri never thinks you were left in total dark. Do not disconnect this no matter how pointlessly pretty it feels—sensory deprivation at eleven k meters unspools identity faster than nitrogen narcosis ever did.  
Carry a manual crank on the left pauldron. Batteries die; turning a handle for fifteen minutes buys you another hour of movement. The motion itself—shoulder-elbow-wrist—will remind you you own joints.

4. FOOD, TIME AND EXCRETION  
Nothing you ate topside tastes of anything except topside. Bring:  
- Three spools of “Mother-of-Pearl” wafers—each embossed with the flavor of a childhood breakfast you described during intake interviews. They dissolve in the perfluorocarbon so you can literally breathe your own memory back into the blood.  
- A sundial. Yes, a toy one, chemically phosphorescent. Plant it upright in the clay when you arrive; the bottom-current will drift the gnomon’s shadow one grain-width every twelve minutes, synchronizing you to the planet’s single respiration. It is not superstition; it is an external clock you can argue with when your circadian gland finally surrenders.  
- A collapsible catheter and colostomy pouch rated for metric tons of external pressure. Modesty is useless down there, but keeping your metabolic offal inside the suit prevents the plain from sampling your DNA and (as has been documented) beginning to grow silica copies of your fingers on the stalks.

5. TOOLS AGAINST FORGETTING  
- A half-meter shard of single-crystal quartz sharpened to one atom’s width. Use it to cut a shallow spiral in the clay whenever you feel memory soften. By the time the current erases the first coil you will still be able to see the newest and thus reconstruct the sequence of your own hours.  
- A tin soldier, bought at a topside flea market, face sanded blank. Place it next to the fallen bucket you will find. They form a ready-made diorama the abyss cannot interpret; anything meaningless is safe from possession.  
- A child’s pocket mirror—plastic, not glass. When the proto-jellyfish flickers you will need to verify you are still shaped like your own outline and not like the 1.3-m-track maker.

6. SOCIAL PROTOCOLS  
If you meet another suited figure, exchange only signed thermal tags. Do not remove helmets; voices spoken aloud down there carry farther than identity and sometimes you will hear your own words returning four minutes later, having circumnavigated the plain and learned despair. If the signature on the returning tag matches yours but the handwriting is shaky, walk the opposite direction—you are closing in on the Möbius part of the grooves and risk stepping outside local space-time.

7. ASCENT IS OPTIONAL BUT FAILURE IS COMPULSORY  
You can come up any century you like—rescues have pulled walkers out after subjective days that were geological eras topside. The trick is deciding to try. Post one silica stalk through the quarter-centimeter emergency vent of your helmet, let it scrape your cheek, let it hurt. Pain is the last flag your nervous system plants before winking out: follow it uphill. When you reach a layer where water is merely ink-dark instead of velvet-dark you will remember surfaces exist, and that is enough.

8. RETURN INVENTORY  
When the chamber finally hauls you back through the moon-pool, technicians will hose you down with warm fresh water that smells of chlorine and cafeteria. You will feel the re-entry burn of oxygen re-infliding your lungs. Before you speak:  
1. Check that you still own your mother’s maiden name.  
2. Recite the Basque or Ainu rhyme aloud; if you skip or substitute a syllable, demand another week of compression-decompression therapy—your language gate is cracked and memory is leaking.  
3. Count your fingers by pressing the tip of the quartz shard (they will hand it back) against each nail. Eleven impressions means the abyss gave you an extra—amputate immediately; it will keep growing until you do.

If you pass those three tests you may sign the debrief and walk out. The form ends with the line “Location visited: Submerged.”  
Put the date beside it—today’s date, any calendar you still trust.  
Then go find a surface you can stand on without feeling it breathe.
