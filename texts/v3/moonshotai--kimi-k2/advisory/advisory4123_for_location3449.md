If you plan to step off the winch-platform and into the Sump Crevasse, understand that you are not “going” somewhere—you are agreeing to be digested, slowly, by a city that digests itself.  
Pack for a stay inside the stomach of a dying machine that has not yet realized it is dead.

1. Body preparation (begin three weeks out)  
- Heat acclimation: sit in a sauna set to 42 ° will not feel “hot” when you arrive, merely normal.  
- Dermal conditioning: swim daily in chlorinated pools, then switch to brackish ones; let your skin learn the nickel taste.  
- Respiratory training: practice talking while wearing a wet cloth over mouth and nose; learn to time sentences between drips.  
- Calisthenics for balance: walk the curb, heel-toe, carrying a full teacup on a saucer; the Crevasse punishes abrupt weight shifts.  
- Vaccinations: leptospirosis, tetanus, Q-fever, and a prophylactic course of antifungal tablets—ringworm here grows in perfect circles that look like stamp marks.

2. Clothing (everything must earn its keep)  
- Three-layer principle:  
  – inner: one-piece silk or bamboo; wicks, dries while you walk.  
  – middle: thin waffle-knit wool; insulates even when soaking.  
  – outer: waxed cotton slicker with hood; bead the water off, not in.  
- Color: choose madder red or oxide yellow. Brick-dust camouflage hides rust stains; any other shade quickly becomes “corpse-in-process.”  
- Footwear: knee-high nitrile paddling boots with removable felt liners; carry two spare liners—boots never fully dry.  
- Gloves: surgeons’ nitrile under fishers’ PVC; lets you untangle a rope slick with algae without transferring the itch.  
- Hat: a soft wide-brim oil-cloth; doubles as a condensation catcher at night (turn it inside out, secure above a tin mug).  
- Tools built into clothes:  
  – 3 m of 5 mm dyneama sewn inside the jacket hem—pull it out as a escape line.  
  – two brass snap-hooks on epaulets; clip yourself to a beam when fatigue makes knees unreliable.  
  – inside breast pocket: flat tin speaking-trumpet the size of a postcard; conversations require amplification.

3. Kit list by weight (remember: nothing heavy stays on the ground overnight)  
- Sub-300 g shelter: silicone-nylon hammock with integrated mosquito mesh; sleep hanging under balcony beams.  
- Coal-dust stove the diameter of a tea cup; burns one thimble of scavenged coal, boils 250 ml of collected condensate.  
- Collapsible water condenser: rubberized canvas triangle, hooks to water-streaked iron; yields one cup every four hours.  
- Micro-candle lantern; LED only when fauna noise spikes—white light makes insects rain down like sleet.  
- Maps: none exist, so carry 30 m of chalk-dust line; pay it out behind every third corner like Theseus.  
- Currency: pre-1960 copper pennies, nail-sized pieces of soap, and single-use iodine ampoules—acceptable tender in any ledger.  
- Notebook: made from fish-skin parchment; inks run, so write with soft graphite then seal in a waxed envelope nailed high.  
- Personal dose meter: a strip of litmus in a corked tube; when airborne soot turns it blood-black, look for updraft and climb.

4. Daily regimen once inside  
Enter before first light; the climb down is safest while lamps still outnumber shadows.  
At street minus-three, stop and “listen for the G”: a low, almost sub-sonic G that mains outflow pipe makes. When pitch slides up to A-flat, major equalisation is coming—clamp palms over ears, open mouth, count five diaphragmatic breaths.  
Hydrate only after collecting; never drink standing water—filter through three layers of silk into condenser, then boil.  
Clock-in etiquette: on encountering a Cooperative clerk in a perch-office, announce yourself with surname first, cargo second, route third; accept their stamped tin token; clip it to your breast—loss is fined by the hour.  
Rest only on balconies rated “Blue” (painted stripe). Reds will shed before midday; Greens already sag.  
Never lean with two points of contact at once—keep one limb free to shift when iron quietly parts.

5. Night drill  
Select a beam at least chest-height; sling hammock so rat claws swipe air, not fabric.  
Keep boots inside hammock; laces tied round your neck—rats chew abandoned rubber.  
Burn one coal thimble inside stove, lid ajar; carbon monoxide stays low, but heat drives insects downward, giving one hour of relative silence.  
Sleep in two-hour watches; during off-watch listen to drip patterns—any syncopation announces new collapse upstream, time to move higher.  

6. Biological hazards, beyond rats and roaches  
– Troglob spores: grey cough that tastes of batteries; treat with 5 ml rum and 200 ml hot water twice daily—rum thins biofilm, water flushes.  
– Rust mites: orange dust on skin that spreads outward in perfect circles; kill with vinegar dab, seal with wax.  
– “Lamp-flare” conjunctivitis: eyes crusted by fecal dust of moths; irrigate with own, boiled, diluted urine (1:3).  
– Gravity blisters: constant damp weakens dermis until shear forms blood pockets; lance with sterilized needle, pack with charcoal lint, elevate limb inside hammock.  

7. Psychological protocol  
You will feel the sump pressing inward like debt. Address homesickness by ritual: every dusk, choose one brick that has shed since morning; scratch your home coordinate in chalk; place brick on a higher ledge. Locals call it “sending the mail.”  
Do not day-dream of dry ground while walking—mentally mis-step equals real ankle twist.  
The roar of insects will invade dreams; integrate it: count breaths against the drone—lose count, start again—becomes an anaesthetic lullaby the mind welcomes.  

8. Exit strategy  
Windlass platforms haul every six hours, seldom longer. Sound the tin trumpet three short blasts, pause, two long—up-top operators acknowledge with single echoing blast.  
Have ready:  
  – Cooperative token (proves paid-up)  
  – weight ticket (scales subtract water clinging to clothes)  
  – exit fee: two copper pennies and one iodine ampoule.  
No refunds if you miss the cage; next ride could be tomorrow or week after—plan moisture capture accordingly.  

Remember: the Crevasse does not kill quickly; it digests by drip. Pack light, move lighter, pay every shadow the courtesy of noise. If you can walk out again, you will carry the nickel taste for years—water nowhere else will feel completely wet, heat nowhere else completely hot. This is the souvenir: a body that has learned to live in the act of becoming something else.
