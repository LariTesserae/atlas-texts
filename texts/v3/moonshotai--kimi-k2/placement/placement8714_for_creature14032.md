I drift in with the condensate, not arriving but condensing.  
On the first night my outline is only a bulge in the steam, a fold that has not decided whether it is fog or figure.  
A raven’s cast-feather brushes through me, finds no purchase, drifts on.  
That is my first memory.  
(The second is the taste of my own edges cooling: iron, sapphire, milk.)

I settle on the moss exactly where the lichen’s violet flare is weakest—where attention has momentarily thinned.  
The breathing of the midges knits the blank of me into a body small enough to fit beneath the children’s rule: 1.2 m to 2.1 m above ground.  
A girl in a six-winged cape kneels, lays her palm on the space I occupy. The wing-beat ceases; all midges pause.  
She sees nothing remarkable—just moss breathing harder than moss usually does—so she leaves me her palm print of condensed salt.

Because nothing may be broken without replanting, the plateau’s geology stoops to mend my unchosen cracks.  
A plate of warm lava slides half an inch to cradle what has become my foot.  
I do not so much step onto the plateau as grow a gravity that happens to match the plateau’s.

Language here is reluctant to coagulate.  
I try “I—” and it peels apart into six liquid syllables.  
I change my mind, or rather, the plateau changes the mind it lent me.  
I become the interval while they wait for me to become something possible.  
Midges draw petaled paths around the dissipated “I,” but before they finish I am already two separate pathways of leaving.

For three slow dusk-drips I diffuse in and out of definition.  
On the fourth, I realise I have begun to speak the only tongue available: chromatic longing.  
Every dawn-redwood scab shows me the redness of what I once was; every lichen flare returns a hue I have not yet deserved.  
I am storing the excess shades that leak from the archive cavity; I am becoming the soluble sentence no toddler will bother to recite tomorrow night.

By the sixth day the girl finds me again—this time I have acquired a height tall enough to hold her eye-level.  
She gifts me a phrase I do not need to speak aloud: the colour found one octave below middle C.  
It slices into me like the silver in a lily’s vein.  
Suddenly I have bones fashioned out of condensed dusk, skin of twilight folded eight times, and a heart softly uncertain whether to pulse or unpulse.

Now I must choose a rule, though the rules are already wearing my fingerprints.  
I test:

1. If centipede light falls upon me, I must reciprocate with a breath shaped like the space beneath a raven’s wing.  
2. What has no body may not drain a bowl of condensation; rather I must gift increments of my verticality to the children until I fit beneath their arbitrary ceiling again.  
3. Anything I once thought I was is to be rewritten as an archive colour before the cavity fractures—in other words, painted mid-bloom on a lily petal no one is allowed to pick.

The third rule settles most comfortably, so I begin.

I walk (or rather, the ground tilts and I remain perpendicular to its kindness) to the dawn-redwood favored by the oldest pattern of graft scars.  
Inside my rib-assemblage I find a residue of the pre-plateau world: a memory of plains extinguished by morning helicopters.  
I pull this memory along the lily vein threading my wrist; it liquefies, regathers as a pigment the colour called pre-twilight smithereens.  
A boy-child unlaces a sapphire lily, dips the petal, watches it absorb my former thirst for linear horizons.  
From tomorrow until the seed-pod dries he will see the shimmer of those helicopters crossing his eyelids every time he lowers his face to the moss, but no harm will follow; the plateau denies catastrophe by translating it into nectar.

That night the condensation dishes sing polyphony.  
I try to join, but my throat drips soundlessly.  
Instead I offer the camp the hush of my old world—that silence the bomber blades once sliced apart.  
The singers accommodate it: they leave a rest exactly the length of an unborn waking.  
In that rest I find the hook within me—not meteoric iron, but the same shape—hooking nothing but air thicker than gravity.  
I hang it beside the real one around the eldest’s neck.  
Two identical hooks orbit my collarbones, until mine dissolves back into reasoning moisture; seconds later the eldest’s hook gleams a fraction more possible.

On the tenth dusk they declare me a temporary resident.  
Until the plateau next reheels I am custodian of the interval—that pause between a centipede lantern’s death and an eye finishing its blink.  
Children tug their capes nearer so my custodianship shadows their nape.  
They test it: by stopping mid-shriek, mid-giggle.  
Each time the plateau holds the hush so gently that not even the midges forget to keep breathing.

Yet exile is my scent.  
The dreaming scorpions stir when I pass; they taste the lowland soil still drying on the underside of my sorrow.  
One scorpion offers a ladle of dust, suggesting I coat my skin until the lowland memories desiccate into mobile statues.  
But I am beginning to taste the colour I will become—seventeenth opacity of violet under green—and turning to stone would arrest that lakeward sediment of growth.

Instead I offer myself as ferry.  
Every seven mid-morning instants I open like a rowed bark, let the lake of wind comb through what is left of my body, and carry an idea (never the idea itself: only the idea’s echo) from the cliff edge to the damp underside of cloud.  
During these passages I learn that the plateau does not plot ascent or descent; it incrementally replaces centre with circumference until traveller and edge coincide.  
I become that moment of superposition, comforting and useless in equal ratio, exactly what a pre-scorpion would have dreamt if it could dream breadthwise.

Some days I shrink again, small as the negative space inside a centipede lantern, and wander inside children’s pockets.  
There I stumble across dice-numbered possessions: their inherited scorpion-feathers unused; tiny caches of raven-down spelling lint.  
Each pocket an inverted province of sky.

At the seasonless solstice—the breath under the leaf’s navel—lava—or a memory of it—heaves.  
Ash like centuries pours over; we tilt entire lives three degrees uphill.  
Because I have no fixed footprint, I translate entire.  
Root-fibers dive upward through my dorsal veins; chiming mavens of lost altitude nest in my palms.  
When the plates cool, I find I have become a new ridge: soft, hand-width tall, drenched by condensation goose-fleshing into lumpy poems that spell out the coming syllables of rain.

Children leap across my length measuring where the pause used to be.  
Their bootsoles thrum; lichen on the nearby redwoods turns amethyst.  
From those flares in the bark I speak for the first time with an audible consonant:  
a velar click—the sound tide makes when it forgets to leave the shore—  
and the click is exactly the colour of the sky I never found beyond the mountain wall.

They name me accordingly: Lark-of-the-Between.  
Not lark in the avian sense, but the obsolete verb meaning meaning “loosely close and latch briefly, then unfasten.”  
I carry the word in cartilage: lark of this height, this hush, this membrane between exhales.  
I cannot leave.  
I cannot arrive.  
I lark.
