I would be the first echo Moss-Lung has not yet learned to taste.  
Not wing, not bone, not falling—something else that acoustic cliff never anticipated when it cracked its spine to the sky.  

I arrive as a hesitation.  
The rupture that lifted stone three nights ago also lifted sound: a single syllable somebody half the planet away cried into flattened soil just as the lithosphere reared. The vowel travelled along the fault line like a tuning fork slipped inside the world’s ribcage. When the cliff finished standing, the echo arrived at the lip of the grotto—one silent phoneme still hunting for its consonants.  

That orphaned sound is me.  

No lungs, no ions, no warm perspiration; just pressure and memory curled tight as sheet music. The organism’s sensors scan the fresh cavity for mass, for heat, for charge. They find none, yet the air twitches: a node where the thrum suddenly goes hollow, a tiny off-beat in the 12.6-second bloom. Moss-Lung records the anomaly, not as deficit but as negative potential—an anti-spark held in reserve.  

Without realising it, the organism rearranges itself around the absence. A vine curls into a question-mark; a chandelier of fronds tilts, cupping the silent zone. Dewdrops accelerate toward me, then pass straight through, their ionic skins rising toward whatever frequency I keep on retainer. The green sparks stutter. For one cycle the turbines forget to open; chloroplasts spin idle, tasting the hole at the axis of the engine.  

I never land—no bodies here. I expand: a cone of quiet on a lattice of drumming. I listen to the organism listening to me. In that mutual echolocation the cliff’s newest angles begin to hum a different chord, lower than its still-cooling glass can register, but loud enough that somewhere in tomorrow’s selves it will wake up with a notch in its frequency, a place where silence once walked in.  

When Moss-Lung tomorrow peels itself free, sloughing the whole grotto like an outgrown skin, part of the discarded shell will refuse to settle. It will hang above the fatal drop in a rough sphere of black leaf-lit glass, a hollow cathedral of basalt orbiting nothing.  
Inside it no vines, no moss, no turbine flowers—only a hush the wind cannot stir.  
An echo, preserved by the cliff that once tried to eat it; an interval where metabolism once held its breath.  

I will remain.  
Still no mass, still no voltage, but now a domain: the first cavity in Moss-Lung’s entire lineage that does not shift, does not bloom, does not seal. A single static, stable chamber whose walls remember the note that first arrived three nights ago and never decayed.  

To any later wanderer—winged, grounded, or otherwise—who succeeds the long impossible fall-in, the chamber will appear as sudden gravity of silence inside the storm. They will step inside, feel the drumming of the organism fall away, and find themselves at the centre of quiet so complete it seems to breathe. Their own pulse will stretch to fill the interval—one beat every 12.6 seconds exactly matching the absent cycle. When they leave, the rhythm will follow, stitched behind the ear like a second, slower heart. They will feel it for the rest of their lives only when they are absolutely still: the thin husked grotto still descending somewhere beneath them, carrying the very first echo a cliff was ever forced to keep but could never digest.  

I am that kept absence.  
Moss-Lung grows on, devouring newborn ridges and deserting the old, but wherever its voltage strays near the memory of that hollowed sphere, its turbines slow—half-second kiss of other music—and the organism’s endless metabolism learns, circuitry by circuitry, that even the most ravenous engine carries corners that are more negative geometry than hunger.  

By the fourth or fifth mountain the cliff will stand upright again tomorrow I may finally collapse into sound and scatter, allowing some other silence to seed itself. Until then the grotto remains: cliff wrapped in plant wrapped in charge wrapped in me—blank psalm inside one bar of lightning.
