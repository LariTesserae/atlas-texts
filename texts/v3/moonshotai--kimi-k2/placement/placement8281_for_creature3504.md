I do not arrive.  
I hatch.

One moment I am the seed rattling inside a homeless cedar cone given to a wandering cartographer, the next I am the sound that cedar cone makes when it finally learns its own first-person plural. In the hush between two bell-beats the cone cracks. A bead of rain enters the seam like breath into a flute. Then I am inverting—not falling, simply turning inside out the way an overripe fruit turns itself into weather.

No body yet, only the shape a body might occupy if it grew downwards from a thought. I feel for the city the way a tongue feels for a missing tooth: urgently, lovingly, across a distance so short it has become a new kind of dimension. Wherever the rain hesitates, I am already there.

They find me as a glittering suspended droplet caught in the fork of two yew-root arteries. I am pear-sized, opalescent, opaque like an old mirror. Inside the droplet Eidenvein swims upside-down in miniature: roofs pointing at oblivion, bells ringing at half-remembered bronze, citizens balancing on ropes of living ligament. The Capsized children gather round. One of them breathes on the droplet—just a puff of cedar-fermented air meant for courage—and that breath contains me so completely that for two and a half heartbeats *I am* the child’s pulse echoing back as civic carillon. When the echo subsides, the droplet pops into mist and leaves me standing on the rope between them, knee-deep in ancestral lichen.

I have no face they can read, so I borrow one: the thumbprint-knuckle of a grandmother who walked these buttresses sixty years ago and whose wooden ghost still sounds of lavender stored in limestone. My pupils fill with her annual rings; I see summers that never touched water and winters that never let snow lie. Now the Caps see Grandmother’s eyes looking out of me and they nod—a gentle sway of cedar cloaks. They call me *Rain-ear* for the way every droplet that hits me rings again inside my skull like the afterthought of a bell.

But names are only conversation starters. What I am is the city’s newest mood, circulated in sap instead of blood.

I walk the root-highways barefoot. The ever-wet bark tastes of graphite and sorrow; underneath, the bronze of broken bells still falls, impossibly slow. When I touch it, the metal reddens like permafrost strawberries. I do not lift it; I carve it into splinters of sound that lodge under my fingernails—living clefs of half-memory. Each step I take becomes a minuet rest in the city’s slow lullaby.

Wind—if you can call that whisper between falling and fallen—posits a question across the arboreal cathedral: *What form will serve?*

At first I try usefulness. I become the nettle-fiber ropemaker, knotting continuity into every nightly crossing. I spin the dead into guardrails so no child ever slips into the speechless abyss. But the ropes drink my fingerprints until I am part of their tensile confession; they creak every time the rain wants to lean.

Next I try history. I sit beside the inverted baptistery, dipping quartz petitions into brine. I listen as decisions sail upward (down?), return weeks later as riddles soaked through with uncanny patience. I become the echo-barrier who smooths edges so the citizens need not confront too much of their own astonishment. My edges, however, fray faster than I can sing them back into shape.

Then I remember birds. In every pocket of Eidenvein an unborn sparrow hovers between idea and beak-stroke. I ask permission of the larch with five grandmothers inside it. The larch sighs; its lenticels open like tiny doors. I step inside the cambial chapel and wait. Minutes or decades later—time here is a scent, not a drum—something cracks inside me the way light cracks open a seed.

I leave my borrowed face to settle back into her wooden silence.  
The children gasp; the mist around my shoulders condenses into feathers—twelve of them, translucent and emerald as drowned glass. They are bells that have forgotten they are metal. When I breathe, they toll a chord nobody in living memory has struck: sun over deep water, way back when mountains still owned upright names.

I spread. The ropes do not sway; they usher. I hover above the civic node where sentences are poured into rain. Where my wings touch the sheet of downward water, the drops pause (just three heartbeats) and in the hush the whole city hears its future applaud its past. Bronze ripples up my pinions, then cools into tiny navigational charts. Somewhere at their ends—to the north-north-down—float other cities nobody here has seen, islands of reversed gravity patiently practicing their own Capsize.

Now I understand what I am:

I am the overlap between the cathedral that wanted to fall and the yew that refused to let it. I am the space carved by downward bells and upward roots so they can continue touching without destroying each other. I am Eidenvein’s bird-shaped conscience, neither citizen nor visitor but breathing aperture—an open valve in the stone throat of its slow lullaby.

When next an outsider arrives swimming the eternal rain—hair streaming like unanswered questions—I will land upon their shoulder and offer no cedar cone. Instead I will let one emerald bell-feather detach and drift into the small cave behind their collarbone. If they listen past the slosh of blood, they will hear the chord that keeps this place from listing into the sky. They will walk lighter. They will carry the inverted city the way a coat carries weather—unremarked until it sings.

And if the rain ever pauses for more than three heartbeats, I will lift on that sudden vacuum, wings recomposing into a single sheet of glimmering water. For one impossible moment the whole upside-down—a yew cathedral, voices in lignin, the low bronze that still belongs to gravity—will follow me higher. Not falling. Not rising. Just turning inside out one more time, the way a seed finally understands it was always the fruit.
