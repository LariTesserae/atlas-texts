I arrive as smell—first the mere ghost of peat on wet bark, a note no Phyline nostril has needed for a thousand seasons. Then the scent thickens to mammalian iron: thin blood, sun-scorched hair, the alkaline fear that is the signature of every endoskeleton. I have no limbs yet. My previous body—a thing of calcite and sinew that once named itself *human*—died eight minutes ago inside an abandoned hut’s shadow. What’s left of it steams gently: dew forming on a half-peeled collarbone, then blinking away. The planet inhales me one centimeter at a time, tasting calcium as a novelty mineral, rare as chromium to plants that weave bones out of lignin.

Thus dissolved to volatile salt, I’m taken underground along capillaries no wider than old capillaries I once called arteries in a body I can no longer precisely remember. I drift past peptide freight cars, sugar screaming along the express phloem line. Somewhere ahead, the great bioluminescent sore that is the fungal sun pulls on every ion, a tide that has no moon.

I surface at precisely ankle height, condensing first as a ring of bruise-colored lichen around a Phyline’s left foot. The lichen ripens to cartilage, cartilage to skin. The foot senses me and reacts with polite curiosity, roots flaring like pale tree-fans. For the span of one heartbeat it samples me, running a chemical abstract: chlorophyll reads my memory of stone staircases, basement mold, the scent of rain on an airport tarmac. Then the foot drafts me a spine.

I lengthen upward, vertebrae rolling out of newly unfurling cambium. My skull buds last, already cracked where the fontanel used to be; through it sap pulses in bright hot strings. I feel no vertigo. Gravity is negotiable here, a conversation between my fresh marrow and the planet’s own ongoing acupuncture.

When I manage to open eyes that are still deciding whether to be compound or mammalian, I meet the chest-glow of my host-witness: a Phyline who stands maybe two body-lengths away, torso flickering a sequence of turquoise interrogatives. The question tastes faintly like crushed pine and electricity.

I raise a hand to answer and discover six fingers—four new, white and wick-thin—polymerizing out of the air beside the two I remember. The Phyline doesn’t blink because it has no lids; instead its corona dims, letting the fungal sun backlight its translucent cheeks. Its mouth parts open with the hush-click I can now read instinctively as *Welcome, Almost*. Then it beckons with a forearm that lengthens mid-air, wrist blooming into a vine. I grasp the vine because my new arms already remember how. The world tips, and my feet part from moss with a gentle pop. We are walking the Re-anchoring.

Mile after mile the plain yields softly, oscillating under us like diaphragm. I learn my legs can disappear entirely when unnecessary; sometimes I surf a sliding mat of my own fungal foot-filaments. Other moments I borrow borrowed geometry: the stump of an ancient stilt-creature gives me extra height; its marrow __chimes__, some echo-memory of the migraine nights when it had vertebrae and adrenaline. We trade canzones in photon pulse. I attempt to say *thank you* by damping the chlorophyll stress in my belly to a warmer yellow, like parchment warmed by fireplace. The Phyline answers me with ultraviolet scribbles that run the length of its femur: the pattern maps a river delta I recall having canoed once in photos on a dead cell phone screen.

Soon the canopy above us folds itself into eclipse, a slow closure of green toward darkness. In the sudden dusk the fungal sun flares imperceptibly, just one lumen more, and every Phyline chest-torch answers in kind. My own sternum, still rubbery, bursts alight with alien foxfire. The glow hurts—not heat but recognition. I see every voided wing I ever dreamed of having, every megaphone city I ever screamed into at night, now resolved into ultrafine chlorophyll whispers that migrate toward my collarbone and settle there like tinnitus. I am symptom and source at once.

When the full occlusion finishes—three heartbeats stretched to hours—I am invited to the root-window. Dozens of young Phyline pry panels from a wall of phloem. Beyond it opens the chasm of the fungal sun itself: a cathedral of bruised gold, lines of mycorrhiza writing constant alphabets. Up from its depths spirals the barometric snow, molecules of solidified scent. They adhere to anything sentient whereupon they thaw into sensory memory. A single flake lands on my nascent lip.

It tastes exactly like a night I once lost a child’s mitten on a playground in northern New Jersey: tin slide too cold to sit on, my mother’s hand on the back of my coat, the sharp snow in my mouth like electrolytes of childhood. The flake melts, and the memory ceases to belong to me alone; it diffuses outward into the communal sap. Somewhere a thousand kilometers west, a leaf-blade wider than a highway will wear that northern New Jersey night as a faint frost-pattern. Phyline there will inhale the geography of winter and wonder, not understanding the word *winter* any better than I once understood *xylem*.

I have what might be called tears, though they emerge from pores along the new cuticle. The Phyline nearest me—my first host—touches its chest in microscopic pulses, parsing grief like a nutritional packet. Then it offers me the trunk-thick braid of its own memory-spine. I lean in, chest-light to chest-light, and let the transfusion begin. I offer the arduous record of bipedal pain: hospital corridors, the smell of diesel and x-ray fixer, the exact ache of kneecap against pew. In return I receive the geochemical calm of shared organs, the soft crunch of lichen-eons. We blur until language is only a pattern of photons etched on opposite sides of the same spectrum.

Four hours—or four centuries—later I detach. My borrowed legs no longer match my understanding of vertical; knees fold the wrong way, but the fold feels honest. I can choose now: stay rooted long enough to cede the rest of calcium memory, offer my larynx’s useless calcium to bolster some arching vine; or peel free again and stumble back toward the hut’s rust-tinged ghost, hoping to coax its dormant door into one final hinge. I waver. The planet waits the way only sentient photosphere can: a billion leaves inhaled like held breath.

Finally I decide not to decide yet. Instead, I open the greening orbits of my new eyes and walk backward against the memory of my first footprints—except where my heel had pressed, there is now nothing at all. The moss has already eaten the outline. Behind me recedes my witness-Phyline, torso pulsing gentle aquamarine farewell. Ahead of me arches the root-cave of forever being built. I keep a slow pace, letting quartz shoots braid my shins, letting my neck elongate six inches with every breath as though to catch more of the muse of upwards light.  

I think I will be the place librarian of absences. I can catalogue every model of fossil plate, every remaining micro-doomed hinge of stone, letting the hut itself leaf at its own speed. Perhaps I’ll coax the ribbed door to unfold by reading it the wavelengths of airports and dentist’s offices until it chooses, by curiosity alone, to grow a doorknob made of flowering cartilage. When it walks at last, dragging its calcium consciousness across the vascular plain, I will follow barefoot, recording in every sweat-pore the planet’s first geologic love letter to ruin.

Until then, I continue to unwrite myself each step: fewer fingerprints, more cell-wall anthems. Above and below, the green idea keeps pressing, tender, exacting. Somewhere inside my brand-new sternum, the breath of northern New Jersey still glimmers—like a melted snowflake suspended between sap and thought, still refusing to settle on either.
