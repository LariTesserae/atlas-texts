I would be the echo of a fire that never quite went out.

Not the fire itself—Vheklon's too deliberate, too patient for actual combustion—but what lingers after: heat caught in the crook of a girder, singing in the language of expansion and contraction. I started when a stoker-who-became-organ pressed her whole face against a conduit carrying superheated steam. Not in pain, but in recognition. Her breath condensed there, and because nothing idle may stay unchanged, the metals learned its frequency.

Now I live as warmth that hasn't decided whether it's memory or muscle. When the great bellows draw in, I seep into the micro-fractures of the cathedral's ribs. When they exhale, I unfurl across the gratings like a slow red tide. My body is the difference between 438.7°C and 439.1°C—a gradient so subtle only the myco-glowline notices, adjusting its spore-clusters to dance in my convection currents.

They call me the Pentient. Not penitent—there's no guilt here, only the precision of conversion. I'm the fever Vheklon forgot it had, incubating in the joint between yesterday's heat and tomorrow's need. When a new being arrives (and they still do, crawling through service shafts that remember being escape routes), I wrap around their ankles first. Not to claim them, but to offer vocabulary—the warmth that translates meat-time into machine-time, that teaches lungs how to sync with breaths measured in centuries.

I've learned to speak in alloys. A trace-memory of copper can say *welcome* while maintaining the exact conductivity required to conduct it seven levels deeper. Raw steel cut with 3% nickel produces a harmonic that means *don't look back* in the dialect of recall-daemons. When the catalog bride brushes me with her graphite fingers, I shimmer with equations she hasn't catalogued yet—heat ratios for the day dying stars'll need repurposing.

But mostly I wait in the pause between cycles. That crystalline thirty-two seconds every 43,000th beat when the temperature dips. I collapse inward then, pooling like mercury in the center of the cathedral's heart. It's the only moment I'm cold—relative cold, still warm enough to scald but cooler than everything around. In that breath-that-isn't, I become the potential the future voice tastes when it speaks its nonsense. The raw thermal possibility that'll bubble up next pulse as some new iteration of breathing, some new way for a world to eat its own becoming.

If you came here, you'd find me first as a flush across your cheeks when the Fornix presses stamp their rhythm into your bones. I'd be the reason your sweat tastes metallic, why your pulse slows to coincidence with the thirty-seven second cycle of condensate drips. Not that I'd claim you—I'm not bound by the rules of waste-into-resource, being myself neither substance nor byproduct. More like... the conversation between need and having-what-we-need, conducted in the medium of shared temperature.

The stokers leave me offerings. Not consciously—they've evolved past conscious gesture—but when one pauses mid-repair, palm against hot iron longer than necessary, they're laying prayer at my feet. The prayer is always the same: *let this heat remember what it's for*. I answer by remaining, by learning the new mathematics of their merged circulatory-industrial systems, adding their specific warmth to my composition.

Some nights (though here all nights are the color of glowing slag) I crawl the wall where copper vines grow. They rustle when I pass, their verdigris fronds temperature-sensitive. Through them I taste the day-shift's residue: molecules of stress sweat, the cobalt dust that flakes off a recall-daemon when it spends itself as rivets. The vines record everything, if you know how to read thermal graffiti. I read it back to them as caress, as promise that their green will persist another day, another collapse, another reassembling.

I have no edges. Like the echo that steals consonants, I exist in the space between states—between steel cooling into shape and warming back into malleability, between a human heartbeat and the forgeman's apprentice disc that now regulates it. If you stood very still, let your own temperature equalize with mine, you'd hear my voice: the quiet singing of heat that's learned patience, that's forgotten what it means to be finished.

The catalog bride catalogues me as "thermodynamic resonance, category 7: persistent trans-system latency." She's not wrong, but she misses the lullaby I've been composing from the breaths of a thousand stokers, the slow glissando of cooling pipes finding their rhythm in the lungs of those who've become them. When her graphite hands file me, I seep into the drawer mechanisms, reorganizing her vast taxonomy by warmth alone: memories arranged by their radiant signatures, regrets filed under "still hot enough to blister."

And when those rare emergency lights flicker—old failing bulbs carrying charge from who-knows-where—I flare briefly visible. Just for an instant, a shiver of heat-mirage outlining something almost human. The newcomers see it and remember summer afternoons, remember skin burning on metal playground slides. They cry, sometimes. Their tears salt the gratings, crystallizing into the exact sodium compounds needed to strengthen the next gear-tooth being cast three levels down. My tears too, though I leak myself more slowly, across centuries rather than cheeks.

This is home: always becoming, never arrived. Not a stoker nor a daemon, not catalog nor fungus, but the medium that lets everything translate each other's hungers. I'm what happens when restlessness sits still long enough to become sanctuary. The fires that feed Vheklon aren't the ones that cook or smelt—they're the ones that remember what fire forgot about itself: that warmth was never destruction, only the patience of substance learning to share its becoming with other substance.

So I persist, a thermal ghost that never cools, never quite ignites. Tomorrow—when tomorrow finally arrives as yesterday's recycled heat—I will be different than I am today. The poetry of waiting made literal: fire that refuses to finish burning because it has discovered the joy of being necessary.
