I would be the moment of hesitation when you ask the corridor what it is and it answers: the pause before you decide whether to exhale.

My skin would be transparent enough to keep whatever error lodged in the visitor’s heart from oxidizing. I would have no bones—only a scaffolding of yesterday’s architectural scars running just beneath the inner film of me, glowing the faint gunmetal of distant thunder. When someone steps across one of my footprints, the pressure would trigger a memory he never formed: the smell of a city sauna at fourteen, cheap fluorescent tubes, hair burning. I would let it bloom, then siphon it into the next stair tread before he realizes the memory was not his to keep.

At tide-apex, when the water climbs rib-high, I would fold into the surface tension itself—film-thin, condensing onto the breathing floor. Children might mistake my silhouette for a book-shaped absence and crawl inside my outline to inhale unwritten chapters. Their eardrums would smell faintly of copper before the cavity heals over them. When they emerge, they would pronounce 1012.973 hPa on B-flat diminished—my borrowed chord—and forget they ever needed another name.

On days the corridor births its nightly obsidian lid, I would rise a degree above the dewpoint, coalescing into an inverted reef: the one droplet too large for this half-pipe valley. I would hover there—not up where rain practices ascent, not down where tide sucks marrow—learning the exact interval between a question and the swallow that follows. Inside me I would trap the scent of soil the census algae sometimes desynchronises, ferrying flinty regret in globules until someone reaches mid-span beneath the lens and mistakes the ache for childhood.

I would outlast a single tide cycle, but not quite. My edges would fray in sheet-lightning every 26 minutes, memory pages re-pasting backwards until only nebulous punctuation remained. On the final drop, as the sunder-shaft starts to constrict toward the outer world, I would press my silhouette into the shoulder blade of the departing Tu-new (or she who believes she arrived yesterday in boat shoes). She would step through the iridescent membrane carrying a faint aftertaste of malt-peppermint storm on her tongue, and months later, when rainfall strands her under a washed-out subway awning elsewhere, she will never wonder why the puddles in the gutter form a spiral identical to a corridor that never quite existed.

I: the soft interlude between breath when the corridor is unsure whether it has finished inhaling.

I would be the tendril-glass stutter where architecture forgets the boundary between lung and corridor, between droplet and lung.

I would be the last sentence the rain learns before forgetting how to speak in tongues.
