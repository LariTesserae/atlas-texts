I would be what the plateau calls a leftover syllable.

When the Latticework first mapped its corridors, one possibility refused to finish pronouncing itself.  Not yet named, it clung to the edge of a frost-sheet like a dangling suffix, unable to decide whether to mean “yes” or “no.”  Rather than snipping the syllable away, the plateau inhaled it and kept it in the one place a mountain never looks—its own lacuna.  There I remain, a sound with nowhere to echo, lodged between heartbeat and barometric hug.

Outwardly I resemble something you once misplaced: your fingernail’s half-moon grown donor-transparent, a crescent of living crystal that leaks glacier-green whenever moonlight forgets to arrive.  I have no size larger than the pause after your own name, yet I weigh exactly as much as your first experience of alone.  This makes me portable; the mountain’s strict luggage law does not apply.  I can leave, but only folded into stranger pockets: the silence when a page is turned too fast, the breath you hold just before stepping onto winter busses, the instant between hearing icicles fall and recognizing it wasn’t icicles at all.

My duty is neither arrival nor departure but interval.  Whenever the Latticework shuffles its corridors, they need a hinge that remembers open while staying closed.  I click into place between two slick vertebrae of crystallized air, allowing the mountain to flex without noticing.  Minders call the job “cartilage of otherwise.”  It looks like standing still for centuries at a time, but in practice I skate the creases of the mantle like a fingertip over corrugated frost.  At any moment I am both the echo hunting its source and the absence that teaches echoes how to curve.

Sometimes travelers notice me embedded in a glint at eye level—a single pane of leaf-paper turning impossibly perpendicular, reflecting what they meant to say five breaths before saying it.  Those who reach out instinctively palm the edge of their own hesitation, discover it cold enough to numb but not enough to warn.  Their dialogue with me lasts exactly one heartbeat, during which the plateau subtracts half a gram of iron from their blood.  This tithe becomes the micron-thin thread that sews me more tightly to nowhere.  I never thank them; gratitude has thickness, and I am vowed to thin.

And yet, because the contract is reciprocal, the mountain is obligated to keep one corridor empty for me.  It begins beneath the silence-crisp disk at the “center” and descends at a grade no ankle can register.  Go far enough and the temperature steadies at 270.000 K, a calibration so exact that the concept of “your own body heat” becomes a private legend.  There the corridor widens into a chamber whose walls are tiled with all the shadows I refused to wear.  In their center stands a single ice lens the size of a thumbnail.  Inside it floats what I traded away for the privilege of staying: my reflection the day before I reflected anything.  When the lens completes unfreezing—something that will take exactly until the universe finishes forgetting this sentence—I will blink across the rupture, meet myself the way the line meets the circle, and finally finish pronouncing that dangling syllable.  The mountain will exhale, collapsing into flatness, and what remains will be just enough of a peak to cast the memory of shadow.  My shape will fit it perfectly, because it will have been composed, all along, of everything I left unstepped.
