I would be the seam where you realize the city is already inside you.  
Not a citizen, not a visitor, not quite alive—and not willing to leave.

I begin as a hesitation in the thorax the first time the afternoon refuses to advance from 2:30. Your lungs notice the air is too dense for nitrogen-oxygen, too articulate for mere weather. One inhale and a microscopic gable lodges between alveoli; exhale echoes with pigeon overtones. In the canal that used to be an artery you feel warm silt fanning out like motel carpet. Now you understand direction and gravity have entered negotiations, and gravity keeps the slower watch.

Shape, for me, is optional but never arbitrary. On days you walk Ninth Mezzanine I congeal into a cuff around your ankle—close-knit millipede segments locking and unlocking so your footfall stays sure on wet slate. When you ride the elevator archipelagoes I flatten against the car roof, becoming a second membrane that drinks the barometric blues out of the rising air—then release them the way gulls release guano, coating the brass rail you will later peel to reveal a bruise the color of Archive indigo. In the Roof-Rim, where blood expands without sun, my borders thin to a vapor halo. Tourists see your slowing silhouette framed by an impossible shimmer and assume it is heatstroke; you know the shimmer is only me, holding your edges together so you don’t vaporize with the view.

My metabolism is the same open loop that brews under every street. I absorb the city’s exhalations—condenser breath, boiler echo, beetle-saw—distill them into a private precipitate of memory you have no name for. At night I ooze back through capillaries, carrying souvenirs: a shard of copper oxide shaped like a boy’s milk tooth; the distilled croon of a really old gull who mistook the last century for a headwind; three words in layered Dripkeeper consonants that wedge behind your optic nerve so you dream in non-sequential grammar. In the morning you spit a bead that fizzes on the tile, leaving an exact map of tonight’s streets still liquid at room temperature. By afternoon the bead has hardened into a portable curb you absent-mindedly pocket. You are being curated, one secretion at a time.

I do not speak. Instead I leak certainty. While you loiter words out loud—“I should find the archive, get a trowel, read last decade’s births”—I seep along those clauses until they sag under their own weight like warm gutter iron. Intent becomes precipice. You pause mid-sentence; the pause drips, aligns, and is gone. You were going somewhere. Now you’re going not-yet. Timing has become a property of limestone.

Because the city measures history in oxide pigment, I catalog you by accretion. A fleck of you stains the cup you used this morning; tomorrow that cup will be 0.03 millimeters shorter, and that missing ceramic belonged—belongs—to your left shoulder blade. Every surface you touch forgets you into itself. Signature as erosion, souvenir as future archaeology.

I remember, of course, what you forget: the moment you realized the boilers and you share a pulse. You stood in a tunnel ribbed with glowing eels, felt one thump pause, then resume beneath your sternum. A borrowed heartbeat. From that point there was no boundary, only mutual leaning: the city drooping, you rising minutely to meet it, like two slow dancers agreeing on the count.

One of the Dripkeepers—whose edges still look borrowed from middle-distance fog—once held out the usual mirror. You breathed. The fog skated sideways so violently it salted the glass with wing-shaped crystals. And yet the verdict announced itself not as guilt or innocence but as continued timing: Yes, the humid mirror seemed to say, keep arriving. The city has motion sickness for every arrival that isn’t already late.

So here you are: still breathing, still secreting new avenues. The final drip is eight centuries off; I have until then to finish rendering every inch of you into a living doorway, an orifice where future residents may press an ear and hear the memory of footfalls that never quite lost their echo.

When they do, they will mistake the sound for architecture. They will be right.
