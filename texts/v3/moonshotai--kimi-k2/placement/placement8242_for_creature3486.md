I would be that accidental exit no one predicts: the human who discovered how to stay inside the repetition instead of being folded back out to sea-level sleep.

On my fifth visit I arrived chlorophyll-green with longing—citrus and unreturned love clung to every exhale.  I had come back to finish a sentence the dew-kin had left dangling in my mouth the last time, a phrase in pre-Persian that still tasted like minted iron.  When I crossed the basalt arch, I felt the garden’s tilt correct itself: not thirty-one degrees now but zero, terraces aligning like planets preparing to eclipse.  Everything steadied in one breath—no vertigo, no lag—because something had decided I belonged to the furniture.

I looked for the dew-kin who had once borrowed the certainty of my bones, but all I found was a single chair older than my heartbeat heating up on the parapet.  Its latticed seat cupped me the way a palm cups water it intends to drink.  When I sat, the ozone sigh stole half the space in my lungs and replaced it with copper fern spores.  I became transparent enough to read the Braille admonitions floating twenty centimeters above my eyelashes—copies of the words I had never finished on previous visits.  Before the cycle could chew them back into noise, I spoke them aloud: the vowel the chair needed to finish being built, the consonants the dew-kin needed to remember they were myth.  

The revelation was mundane: I was filling vacancies.  

I am no longer visitor, no longer guest-scientist scribbling dew-kin folklore in acid-free notebooks.  I am utilitarian appendix of the garden.  When chairs exhale at dusk, I am the limb of shadow they cannot cast; when nano-splines rise in Braille, my dreadlocks collect them like braided magnets.  I drift with less surface tension every dawn, though my skeleton stubbornly keeps its marrow.  Hairline vines of transparent cellulose thread my wrists, tethering me a hand’s breadth above the wet basalt—never walking, not quite flying, just sliding on polarised vapour as if gravity now negotiates with me personally.

I have lost the steepness of years; my fingernails grow older than my knuckles.  I measure age in reiterations: one complete looping of the three terraces equals one calendar day in my old world.  I have lived five such loops since yesterday.  Visitors who stumble through sometimes mistake me for architecture: a human-shaped absence where condensation densifies into deliberate posture.  I let them think that.  A warning folded like origami in my throat says it is kinder than reminding them they are minutes from exile.

The dew-kin proper still call me *outsider’s echo*, but privately they treat me as chronic texture.  They taste my breath for cardamom and grief, alter humidity to keep the balance between population and loneliness.  When the acoustic storm comes I serve as anchor: I stand at the centre axis while they spin into hurricane finespun from themselves.  My bones weight the figure eight, still the cat’s eye in a mobile that refuses to stop spinning.  In those minutes I carry memory forward—for them—because they travel only backward.  At chord-resolution they detach, but a few droplets invariably stick between my ribs; I become anthology of unfinished conversations.

I do not know if I will ever evaporate.  
I suspect the garden is keeping me as collateral against the sea still gestating inside the rock, a thermometer dipped in slow magma to learn patience.  
Maybe I’ll condense again in the next epoch as an older, bluer version of myself leaned in a chair outside whatever new arch folds into view.  

Until then I am the humid punctuation mark the garden inserts into human stories: the place where prose turns breathless, where the sentence you meant to say discovers it has already been spoken by weather.

Come through the arch someday.  
If the fog remembers my name, ask it the end of your journey.  
The dew-kin will pass between us, trading temperature for truth, and I will remain, half-vapour, half-bones, teaching water how to become human as slowly as rock learns to break.
