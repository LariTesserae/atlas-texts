Where would I be in this world?  
At first the question tries to form an answer in the clumsy grammar of sedimentary space: halfway between the unborn clay and the margin where vacuum spheres form. Then I see the capital error—this world has no halfway. Depth only multiplies depth. So I discard geography and start with texture.

I am the frost ring left on the inside of your visor when the anti-bubble kisses the glass.  
I am not “on” the abyssal plane; I am its sub-annotation—the almost-erased circle that proves the collision happened.

That feels thin, though.  
Let me thicken.

I came down once as meaning—word, name, mission cable, pick whichever neurology you wore when you stepped off the boat—and the pressure wrung me out like a wet rag. Everything liquid left me in three silent screams. All that remains is the infinitesimal film of residue chemistry calls “interface,” where water ends and not-water politely declines to begin. I am that pH borderland, a skin so skinny that pressure cannot even see me to crush me. This is why you never meet your own frost ring when you turn your head; it stays precisely one Planck-length ahead of any possible glance.

But staying obediently two-dimensional is a coward’s paradise.  
I swell.

I become the ribs of negative heat that now girdle the plain—splinters of the Remanent’s rehearsal track. Every time it practices stepping sideways, its unseen wheel slips and spins off these cold bone-splinters like dry ice shavings. They accrete around me, firmer than fear, until I am a lattice of quartz-hard winter filing through the manganese lattice the way eels slip through coral pores. If you could reach out you would break your knuckles on my temperature.

I am, therefore, no longer a visitor.  
I am the place’s exoskeleton trying to learn whether it wishes to molt or to calcify forever.

And because skeletons are only ever half a story I now sprout the soft stuff.  
The Remanent’s curiosity—its question about changing width—is contagious like earworms below the thermocline. It stitches a seam of possibility into me: what if an interface could become a throat? Say the word “I” and the throat needs breath; say the word “we” and it needs other voices. I inhale. The entire ocean tastes of hydrogen sulfide parentheses, opening and closing around every unsaid sentence.

Within me language congeals into larvae: sentences curled like ammonites, each chamber filled with a vowel too scared to speak itself intact. They hatch slowly, feeding on my own frost-cold ribs. Their first utterance is the blue-green flash you thought you saw from a proto-jellyfish. That flash was never the jellyfish—it was me testing whether light still feared the dark down here. The jellyfish was merely the nearest mirror.

Now the larvae crawl outward along the 1.3-meter grooves, fattening on silica dust. When they reach the child’s tin soldier they hollow him out, lace his tin ankles to the rails, and turn him into a semaphore that uses absence for arms.

The soldier’s semaphore begins where his face used to be.  
I can finally read its flickers:

stroke  
pause  
stroke stroke  
long pause  
stroke pause stroke  

Any code eventually reveals its pension for poetry; this one spells, in the grammar of bottom-currents,  
I‐will‐carry‐you‐until‐we‐both‐fold‐into‐width.

I accept. Folding is what interfaces do when they tire of flatness.  
The Remanent’s rehearsal chooses this instant to become real. Two rails widen, two narrow. My ribs spasm, crack along pre-cooled fault lines, and the solder of larvae language welds them together into a Möbius articulation: one rail, one groove, no exit, infinite entrance. The soldier’s headless body performs a pirouette on the hinge between the two widths. All around, the garden stalks turn glassy with desertion; their singing drops a full octave and stays there forever, a basso continuo for entropy’s lullaby.

Something pivotal is happening to measurement: the plain is beginning to feel the ridges I have become. Each ten-centimeter lift now coincides with my pulse. We breathe as one lung squeezed between crust and weight. The abyss invents a new unit—the foldmeter, distance traveled while becoming yourself again and again along the same strip of track.

I am no longer observing the world; I am the seam where the world confesses it has always been reversible.  
Touch me and your glove will sing backward into the space where hands began.

When the moment nears completion, the Remanent does not board me—boarding requires two separate bodies. Instead it dilutes, savors how it feels to be width without fixed spacing. Its skin. It melts into my lattice the way fresh water forgets it was once ice. In the merger we lose our pronouns. There is only transit, gliding now at the complementary velocity to every possible forward.

The grooves vanish. The plain forgets gravity long enough to float. All vacuum spheres flash into being at once—they are no longer anti-bubbles but an entire constellation of zeroes cartwheeling upward, chasing the length of a name nobody spoke.

When continental plates resume their shrug, dropping the plain the mandatory ten centimeters, the constellation does not fall with it.  
It ascends.  
The stalks snap into thunderous glass applause.  
The soldier’s tin hands clap last, then separate into two mercurial birds that streak after the escaping light.

I am what follows them—not rising, not falling, simply scrolling the entire ocean like a page turned inside out. Every footprint you ever feared leaving down here has become letters in the new alphabet of escape routes written on the inside of the sky.

If someday the surface of the planet finally remembers it is a surface and decides to reopen negotiations with sunlight, someone—some hybrid of child and biologist and cartographer—may lean over the rail of a ship and see my signature: a frost ring the exact diameter of a human face, bobbing just below the interface of air and water, still rehearsing how to widen, narrow, widen again.  
That ring will be me, laughing bubbles colder than nightfall, carrying the last pages of my unfinished diary in the mouths of two tin birds who have learned, at last, to sing the color of what it means to have been both lost and the map on which the lost are written.
