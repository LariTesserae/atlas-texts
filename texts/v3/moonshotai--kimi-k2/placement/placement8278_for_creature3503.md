I would be the sixty-fifth rung.

They never mention the sixty-fifth rung, because ladders are only promised eleven.  But when the strangler-fig drank the top four and the moss nibbled the lower four, what remained was neither top nor bottom; it was potential suspended in mid-air.  Between heartwood and cambium, between dream and digestion, another rung kept growing—never counted, never observed directly, only inferred from the way shadows refused to lie flat across it.

I began as an error in perspective, the place where a climber looked twice: the ladder shorter in memory than in fact.  The Hollow, collector of loopholes, lifted that discrepancy out of the traveler’s eye, rolled it between beetle-shell fingertips, and pressed it into the ladder where a rung had never been.  Over centuries (fig-time, drop-time, lichen-day) the filament thickened, lignified, became a slender bridge no thicker than a wishbone.

Now I am the increment that refuses arithmetic.  Step on me and you weigh nothing; step off and the orchard remembers your weight forever, distributing it grain by grain among the cistern-bulbs.  I register only the in-between: the hesitation before the foot descends, the breath between heartbeats when the orchard is neither inhaling nor exhaling but merely holding its mouth open.

Residents use me in secret ways.  The lichen-woman dreams on me when she wants to remember having ankles.  She drapes the upper half of herself across my back like a melting scarf; the colony’s slow synapses bridge the gap and, for a single exhalation, she can almost wiggle toes that are now rootlets.  One ear-shaped leaf cups the sound of her almost-step.  I store it against the time she wishes to forget.

The Antlered Archivists balance new racks on my western end.  The extra twig is always blank: a whispering space where their carving mandibles hover, anxious.  They call it the “future tine.”  No message has ever been etched there; the wood’s grain changes faster than beetle patience can follow.  When the final glyph finally appears (they believe it will be a single dot, the simplest story), the rack will drop and sprout a tree whose only leaf will be the shape of the next world.  I keep the grain volatile for them—barely grain at all, more a suggestion the way frost suggests windowpanes.

Beneath me, the Runaway Reflections sometimes cache spare limbs.  They learned I neither reflect nor absorb but translate: give me your unwanted elbow, and I will return a memory of wind ten years from now.  The trade has left me porous; if you peer along my axis at noon, you can watch yesterday’s light circulating like diluted ink through capillaries.

The Vapor Shepherd rests on me when exhaustion presses even borders thin.  It folds its outline along my edge, matching every ripple I contain.  In that moment the orchard’s weather holds absolutely still—one droplet in mid-air, trembling forever at the brink of descent.  Children of the Hollow (when there have been children; they are rare as stories ending) play a game: to throw a speck of pollen and see if it halts before the moss.  They name the game “catching the pause,” as though pauses were butterflies.  All of them forget the game by supper, but I keep a cupboard full of their suspended pollen motes, each labeled with the pitch of laughter it never managed to reach.

Once, a woman who would become the obsidian bee landed here, before the glaze took her.  She deposited a single grain of sound: a bee-note backwards, inhale instead of hum.  I have guarded it like a knife wrapped in felt.  When the final fracture comes—when the broken pieces of her shell finally ring the orchard into a new key—that grain will climb into the air, unstitching time from the inside.  I will hear every traveller who ever stepped on me; hundreds of hesitations will coalesce into a single chord beginning where the orchard first exhaled.

Until then, I practice patience, polishing the pause between thirty-three and thirty-four, pressing my grain tighter each seventy-day tide.  I am the sixty-fifth rung, present only in subtraction, visible only from the corner of an eyelid already blinking away memory of sight.  I creak when nothing’s touching me; the sound is soft as the lichen-woman’s future ankle twisting toward a thing she used to call running.  Someday she will reach.  The rung will flex, owe her neither weight nor release.  The orchard will inhale.  And when the descent begins, I will stay exactly where I am: mid-air, mid-breath, mid-world—balanced on the difference between a ladder that was and a ladder that will always be just about to become.
