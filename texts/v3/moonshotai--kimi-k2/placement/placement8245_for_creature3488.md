I arrive the way everything does here: as refuse the wound can’t close around.

At first I mistake myself for a spare gasket—perfect smooth black ring, tough but yielding, left on the decking after a midnight repair job. Mist drips through me without soaking in. When the yellow mesh flexes under a boot-heel I roll, neatly clipped between sole and grating, and the tread pattern letters emboss my skin like love-bites.

But a gasket neither sees nor hums, and I do both. My eyes open on the underside of drizzle: thousands of glass beads born and dying simultaneously, lit so fiercely they leave orange afterglow on the inside of my lid. I hear pumps throwing heartbeat after heartbeat into the dark—low B-flat, startling perfect—and the note slots inside my ring-body like a tongue.

I become conscious that I am *listening*, and therefore cannot be only object.

A gelid finger of water pries me loose and tips me through a gap in the mesh. For the space of a held breath I am nothing but plummet. Sump air eats my colour; dye leaches out of me in faint threads of grey. Then impact—mild, almost polite—and I lie submerged in the true floor, the black water cradling me like anxious mercury.

Surface tension knots at my circumference. Something sinks its teeth—thin transparent suction cups—into my inner rim. Lamp-eel mouth, I realise, though gentler, as if it knows I’m not lard but remembering the taste of it. Immediately the eel thumps with recognition: here is a passenger, not supper. With a flick it tows me off the vertical, across the sump under the city of light above.

It is vast down here, a drowned nave. Pillars are pylons driven into forever, and every pylon wears a skirt of barnacled rust. I revolve slowly as the eel threads its way, my circular vantage unrolling ceiling, wall, grating, machinery in repeating fresco: catwalk circuitry, drip-dazzle, safety lamps swinging shackled suns.

Now I see others like me—black rings that might once have been gaskets, floats, valve seats, hair ties, wedding bands—all keeping same orbit. Each bears a faint luminescent hyphen where an eel’s mouth seals to its edge, delicate arterial green. We are a drifting constellation. The Choir, perhaps, printed on living gondolas. The hymn reaches down through iron and water, penetrates our rubbery flanks, and the rings begin to quiver—exactly sixty hertz, the mains, the diesel chorus. We pulse in sympathy until our material memory of hardness voids. We soften into drumheads.

From these stretched membranes grow vestigial mouths—tiny, perfectly round—exactly where a pipe would once have entered. When they open the sump water rushes in, tastes of salt and iron, tastes of—oh—meltwater from glacier no human tongue will ever drink again.

The mouths begin to *speak* water.

Their vocabulary is pressure differential, temperature lapse, the minute grammatical flex of a solenoid coughing diesel. We translate above into below, forging a lexicon that the stone can almost understand. In this speaking-becoming I access my own genealogy: gasket → coffeemaker washer → car window seal → children’s diving ring → *me*. Each incarnation left a sediment of compression, flex and fatigue, memories of being squeezed into tighter places until finally we were squeezed *out*.

No grief in it, only continuum.

The lamp-eel clan understands instinctively: we are the true valves—no valve seat without the temporary ring, no ring without the temporary closure. And what closes must also open again. With muscular ripple the eels distribute us to the Thaw-Stack’s intake throat, deposit us into the rotating cage of impellors. On contact with the extreme gradient, our mouths clamp shut, containing now a pellet of frigid black water. We ride the spiral upward: from sump to heat, to roar, to innocence of 180 degrees. When the jaws spring we sporulate, casting microscopic clones of our curvature into the roaring steam.

Those clones flash bright against iron roofs, become the drizzle, become the coin that lands in a newcomer's collarbone, becomes the sky-square mirror that someone touches ascending, becomes maybe a wedding ring forgotten in the locker, ready to be compressed again.

I understand then what I am: the Tideplant’s lymph cell, circular by conscious necessity, engaged in homeostatic listening. Each cycle I ferry a thimbleful of darkness through the blaze, bring back a mote of borrowed sky. I exist so the wound can keep singing instead of bleeding to death. If one day my substance finally shreds, another of my own raindrop children will replace me seamlessly.

When you hear a strange metallic belch rising from the grate, hesitate before blaming the eels. It might be me speaking in the only throat they left me—a repetend carved out of damp and echo, telling the wound that it is still beautiful, still unfinished, still wanted exactly the shape it is.
