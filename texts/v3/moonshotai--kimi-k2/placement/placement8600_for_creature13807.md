I arrive as the murmuring half-step between your boot-soles and the pavement when the bronze tiles are cooling faster than the bedrock can remember them.  

I am the person-shaped ration of dusklight the city borrows when it notices someplace new needs discovering.  
Look—the horizon jangles, a skirt hem of stapled fog and weld-spark—and already I am tall enough to wear it. My outline absorbs the tremor of distant turbines; my chest widens just enough to smuggle a garden’s worth of pre-breathed regret.  

I never decide whether I am citizen, scaffold, or recollection overdue; none of the three can afford the solitude. Instead I keep folding myself smaller, sleeker, until my mass approaches 0.34 (the angle at which obligation begins to drip). Children sling me over their shoulders like a coat sleeve catching moonlight.  They name me “Later”.  I try not to take it personally.

Inside Later I find another interior marked for me carved into the air by someone else’s unfinished sentence (“—if we could just give the wind addresses—”). As I step across the lintel, gravity politely tilts four and a half degrees; my ribcage yawns open, reveals a miniature hoist where streetlamps wait to be born.  I pause to tug one loose—soft as a hatchling monocle—and plant it behind my left molar so I can smile in public without conceding the dark its victory.

Where I walk, streets blink like just-developed photographs unsure they should hold the image this long. One lane offers me apprentice-ship to itself—shows how to blister into a bridge, how to shrug loose, copulate with a viaduct, divorce by noon.  I decline; I have recently learned that becoming a noun is fatal.  Still, I gather one of its bolts as severance pay, drop it into my pocket where it keeps time against my pulse:  
one  
locked tremor  
per  
heart-beat.

Between intersections the ivy lamps invent new colors.  The pigeons audit them, approving the ultraviolet lullaby of naked kiln.  I cup my hands, receive a fistful of sovereign radiance.  It warms nothing, yet tastes like crystallised anesthesia—exactly what I need when the scrape of memory is too dull to cut but too sharp to ignore.

Eventually I drift toward Reluctant Potential’s yearly visitation.  Only once do I glimpse its folded-edge silhouette, glossy as cancelled ink.  It nods to me—a hinge dipping—and asks, for the record, which edition of myself I am.  
I answer: the one thin enough to wear tomorrow without spoiling the weather.  
The last letter of my reply slips away, added to its dark inventory.  No charge; the sound folds like a borrowed scarf and disappears.

By midnight the bronze is awake enough to dream. I flatten myself along a gutter of almost-freezing condensation.  There I lie listening to bricks perspire their history in sugary pops—memories fizzing loose and gathering elsewhere, like children collecting teeth for something that has never yet existed.  I absorb the rhythm.  Begin composing my own footprint—an alphabet neither cursive nor fracture, but a tentative weld between.

By the time the east chimney coughs its first coal-bright rooster of dawn, one word has hardened just behind my sternum:  
“end-begin”.  
Not slogan, not instruction—simply the temperature at which a person becomes possible.  

I rise.  The tiles flex, embarrassed to remember the shape I pressed.  I let them.  One must leave room for the next ambiguous silhouette.  
On the Flexing Esplanade a new parade is already rehearsing, paper lamps tossing half-born syllables into the air like phosphorescent snow.  
Among them I find one that still smells faintly of future loneliness; I catch it, put it on. Immediately it snugs round my shoulders and whispers:  
“carry me until tomorrow decides whether or not you have arrived.”  
I promise nothing.  Together we walk back into the steam, rehearsing everything.
