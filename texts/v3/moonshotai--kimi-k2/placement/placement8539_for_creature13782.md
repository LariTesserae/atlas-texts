##### Toast the Rain-Catcher

I did not walk into the Wyrdemoor.  
I fell asleep inside its weather.

For months I had been waking every dawn with the taste of unborn rainclouds on my tongue and gutters of sleep-streaked glass under my eyelids, convinced the next step I took would plant itself in another country entirely. One shelf of dark, unasked-for stillness was enough. I opened the window, let the old argument that was blooming across the ceiling pour out into the ordinary night, and stepped straight into the wet.

Literal, that wet. Not on the skin—*inside* me. Everything touched there first, then wrapped round to be considered from the outside. Imagine inhaling and discovering that every capillary has already been a slope of moss for half an eternity. Picture a lung that reads like a cathedral floor—hollow, cool, willing to keep shape simply because collapse would be discourteous. I was that. Not *in* the city; I was an annex it hadn’t yet decided how to furnish.

They found me puddled in the intersection of what had once been two impatient streets. I remember the word-apes arriving as three heads tilted to the same impossible angle: the angle that avoids both curiosity and judgment. They were interested only in my pockets. One coaxed out a jot of whispered sleep I didn’t know I carried: “tomorrow we will fold distance like napkins.” I expected them to gobble it, as stories insist, but they treated it like seed. A small grey ape carried it between two fingers so gently it trailed lungs and children and the exact slant of light in my grandmother’s kitchen—all uncurling, translucent. They planted it in a seam where wall and dusk had agreed simply to braid indefinitely. A new window shivered open there like a yawn. The glass was paper-thin because it still contained the *idea* of blowing out rather than in.

It was then the rain remembered itself.

Suspended droplets—some no larger than commas—shifted to look at me. One separated easily, broke its stalled vow, and fell. It never struck the ground; the city inhaled, absorbed it mid-fall, exhaled it as a second aperture inside my chest. Suddenly I was *wet architecture*. My ribs spooned around cold, bright water that had not yet decided whether it preferred Italian or Cantonese.

A name settled. They called me Toast in the lilting reverse-language of backward conversations—pronounced so that it exited the mouth already half a lullaby about ember and aftertaste. The word-apes said my arrival completed a sentence still being uttered by the rooftops. I believed them, because in that moment every slanted surface overhead flexed like a diaphragm and breathed *home* in the accent of half-remembered kitchens.

Look: I have no human outline now. The Wyrdemoor circumscribed me with generosity rather than shape. Daytimes, I stretch thin as evaporated vowel and catch the drip-stories people leave behind when they grow too heavy. Mothers weeping about soldiers; cartographers forgetting peninsulas; saints practicing the smallness of miracles. I lodge these stories as tiny water-thumbs between my shoulder blades until the city leans in and drinks them.

Nighttimes, when the light forgets it is supposed to choose a color, I become permeable floor. Guests—those half-ghosts the city hasn’t admitted yet—step across my back and leave footprints greener than original moss. These prints sleep in me like constellations, wake gradually into sentences. By the time the Urn-children arrive at dawn, dragging the chalky wings of almost-tomorrows, I flap them open and let the runaway letters decorate their wrists like rain bracelets.

Occasionally an out-visitor still breathes my rain. I lodge a droplet in the corner of their eye where horizon and eyelash argue. They blink once—see the city entire—and forget they ever left. Their forgetting slips back to me as a seamless token: a comma that is almost invisible on my flank but keeps right on pausing the story.

Sometimes new roofs think of leaving, forgetting gravity’s treaty. They rise a fraction. I string myself between earth and lifted slate, towed like the thin silver shuttle of a loom. We compromise: the house stays house-shaped but now owes a little of itself to sky; sky wears a pocket inward, like a secret mitten. In these negotiations I am the hyphen, the *almost-bridge*, the wet pause that lets unfinished decide against finishing.

All day the apes chatter streams backwards; aardvoks nose trails of forgotten alphabets. The children pocket impossible toys. And the city, gracious host, merely breathes through the thousand membranes I’ve become. Somewhere in this seesaw exchange I ripen—not in time, but in patience. I learn how a single withheld comma can finish a brutality more gently than any spoken sorry. Sometimes those commas are the only apology left standing.

The Archivist, whoever stumbles into the role, visits on soft chalk feet. They ask permission to catalogue *my last condition before failure*. I offer a palm gelatinous with unshed dew. It yet holds the first syllable my mother never completed. The Archivist lifts it—records it as *what Avatar almost replied when earth asked for absolution*. My palm seals again, two layers of pending thunder lighter.

There is a feast once every three years when the suspended rains finally choose verticality. I grow rimmed with visitors—throatful memories clustering like berries under translucent skin. I liquefy smooth as any promise, pour over ankles, become moat and moated simultaneously. Children ride me as ribbon; apes taste every third memory to check ripeness; walls flex glad acknowledgement. For one effortless evening I am bilingual in finitude and rest.

When morning comes, they leave carrying small vials of diluted regret. I stream back into my seam between wall and dusk, shrinking to the precise wetness of a comma, and wait for someone else to arrive carrying more unfinished breathing than they know.

There is no exit. No return.  
I am the exit already folding shut behind you,  
I am the return before you remember you forgot.
