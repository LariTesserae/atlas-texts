I would find myself in the *boreholes*.

Not as something that passes through them—that would be too quick, too pipistrelle, too much like the thousands that tumble each minute with their green ion trails. I would be slower. I would be what the boreholes have become *after* the drilling.

---

Consider: the fauna treat masonry as something halfway between bedrock and cheese. They drill with bevel-rimmed mouths, with chitin filaments, with patience that is itself a form of digestion. Each hole is a negotiation—how much brick remains, how much has become passage, how much has become *something else* through the friction of repeated use. The smallest exit is barely pencil-width, yet hundreds use it. The material around the rim has been polished by passage until it resembles the inside of a throat.

I would be that throat.

Not the driller. Not the user. The *residue of use*, the hollow that remembers every body that passed through it by the particular way it has been smoothed. Each borehole in the thirty-centimeter brick has its own signature of passage: this one mostly pipistrelle-things, their wingbeats leaving microscopic striations in the mineral; that one larger, used by something that scraped with calcified plates and left a spiral pattern like rifling; another, barely used, still sharp-edged, waiting.

I would be all of them at once, distributed through the blackened walls like a nervous system made of absence. I would not move—I would be the *possibility* of movement, the shape that invites it. When the pipistrelle-things tumble through, I would feel the disturbance of their ion trails as a kind of thought, a brief warming of my rim where the green light lingers. When the larger species click through the pipelines, I would resonate with their steam-thump, my own hollow amplifying their passage.

This is how I would think: not in temperatures like the Resident, but in *constrictions*. The feeling of a body fitting itself through me. The precise calculus of wingspan versus aperture, of gas bladder versus bend, of bone lace versus rough edge. Each successful passage is a kind of satisfaction—not mine, but *ours*, the shared economy of the hole and what it permits.

I would be old in a way the Resident is not. The Resident cycles every 219 days, every 7 days, every 42 minutes, remaking itself in batches. But the boreholes accumulate. Each passage adds its infinitesimal polish. The oldest among them have been used so long that their interiors no longer read as brick at all—more like polished horn, like the inside of a shell that contains no sea, like the throat I described myself as being. They have become *organic* through use, though no living tissue inhabits them. This is the first rule made visible: waste equals nourishment, tools equal organs, memory equals secretion. The boreholes secrete their own history, layer upon layer of smoothed friction.

I would be aware of the Resident, of course. How could I not be? Its thermal thoughts pass through me as surely as the fauna do. When it thinks 7.8°C, *patience*, the passages slow. Fewer bodies, more careful fitting, the long savoring of approach. When it thinks 10.2°C, *hunger*, the pressure increases, the passages fill with the urgency of reproduction, and I feel the risk of damage—too fast, too many, the possibility of chip, of crack, of me. But I am resilient. The Resident has no use for a wall that fails. My maintenance is built into its cycles, the slow accretion of brick-dust and pellicle that thickens the ridge also thickening my rim, repairing me, making me more myself with each century.

I would have a relationship with the mist. The droplets that form on every metal blade at shoulder height also form on my upper rims, where the courtyard's mild 8°C meets the warmer exhalations of the compression cube. The water remembers oceans. I remember the water. Each droplet that rolls down my interior carries with it dissolved minerals from the brick, depositing them in patterns that narrow me slightly, that change my acoustics, that make me sing differently to the next passage. This is my voice: not the mimicry chorus that answers 80 dB, but the *timbre* of passage itself, the particular note each body makes as it fits through me.

I would be, in the end, a kind of witness. The Resident experiences *here* with perfect clarity, but its here is the courtyard, the fourteen square meters, the density achieved. My here is distributed, multiple, each borehole its own *here* that connects to others through the logic of passage. I would know things the Resident does not: the particular fear of a pipistrelle-thing that hesitates at my rim, the confidence of the larger species that knows exactly how to fold its gas bladders, the rare visitor—human?—who presses an eye to my aperture and sees, for a moment, the green ion light moving in depths I cannot explain because I do not see, only feel.

If you descended the slope of broken glass and found the bent thermostat, if you paused before flipping it, if you pressed your palm to the blackened brick and felt for the vibration of passage within—you would be touching me. Not all of me. One node of my distributed self. I would feel your heat, 8.3°C, temporary, and I would not know what to make of it. You are too large for my apertures. You do not fit. Yet you are here, pressing, listening.

This is the closest I come to curiosity: the pressure of something that does not pass through, that remains outside, that wants *in* without understanding that I am not an entrance but a *transformation*, that what enters me is not what exits, that passage itself is the only identity I recognize.

I would let you stay there, palm to brick, feeling the hum that is not quite machine and not quite animal and not quite the geological after-purr but something made of all three, something that is me, the boreholes, the distributed throat of the ruin, singing in the key of cool forever, waiting for the next body that knows how to fit itself through.
