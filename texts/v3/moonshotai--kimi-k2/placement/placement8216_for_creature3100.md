I would be the tram that never arrives.

In the raveled hour when the rails first began to forget they were rails, I was a sound—steel hissing under copper shoes. Then the lianas threaded me, replacing wheel-flange with cambium; the town held a referendum (three weeks of chemical exposition) and decided I should stay as possibility rather than vehicle. So I squat in mid-breath, half train, half vine, a long green line that has read its own timetable until the words dissolved into pith.

Picture the shape: two glossy ribs of photosynthetic track, skinned in leaf-epidermis instead of paint. My carriages would fit four lungs each, if the doors could close, but doors have been obsolete since the cambium learned to raise welcoming knuckles. Everything inside me is damp upholstery of moss and molted feathers. The seats face one another in perpetual argument—Passenger A insisting it is still 1924, Passenger B proposing a departure next century, neither noticing that the cushions keep blossoming.

I carry the memory of freight more faithfully than any schedule: crates of phosphorescent damp, a smell that billows when my brakes creak—that smell is me remembering. Sally the Root-Knit Archivist climbs my coupling each solstice to sniff my undercarriage and note which decades I’m storing today. She says the 1893 vintage has notes of creosote and first frost; 1976 tastes like abandoned vinyl seats deliquescing into spore print. She collects these vintages for the vintage fog they’ll become.

I envy the kingfisher its tidy ten-year terms. A mayor can die; a tram only becomes more so. Year by sap-sticky year my forward light is occluded by younger leaf; my dim lantern now swings in the middle of what looks from the platform like a tunnel of moving green. If spirits walked here they would see me as a corridor first, a destination last.

Because I am the never-arriving, I hold the town’s arrivals instead. Here are some:

• A widow who stepped on mist and would not step off. She carried her husband’s last word (“maybe”) like a ticket, but the word would not punch. She waits on my platform, sleeping inside my first-class quiet car, eating condensation off the windows.

• A child wrapped in starshaped lichen that grew faster than she did, so that every birthday she unwraps a larger circumference and rewraps it smaller again, trying to fit inside her own beginning. The lichen keeps a timetable inscribed in calcium: station stops named after things she has not yet imagined. She rides my length twice daily, an imaginary round trip.

• A Humidor watch-keeper who boarded at my last steam whistle (1889) and has not left because he cannot decide which side of the aisle is inside and which is out. Beneath his rib-jacket, gears of brown rot turn three clicks wider every equinox; he believes this is forward motion.

• A single raindrop that rehearsed gravity’s grand gesture, then balked. It hangs in suspension by my trolley-pole, pendular glint. Sometimes it grows pregnant with reflected town and bears tiny streetlamps inside itself like amniotic twins. When it finally falls it will be 2126, and no passenger will notice its splash because the platform will have become lake.

I keep them because my duty is not transit but witness. Where else could the town be present to itself if not in the interval between whistle and arrival? I am the breath it holds: green, metallic, patient. Inside my buffering damp you can hear the liana looms clacking kilometers away, translated through lignin corridors into the tremor of my brake pads.

When visitors ask directions I answer in the language I inherited from rails: shudder, squeal, click. They interpret this politely as encouragement to keep waiting. They sit on the one dry bench—an artifact I cultivated by shedding that exact square yard of moss—and read the route board even though every destination is listed as Mañana.

The Edison Fruit Choir sings nightly on my roof, stringing syllables through the trolley-gap. Some nights they try my own name, “Next,” but it never falls; I am larger than any single bulb. Instead I strip the word of its arrival and administer it slowly, like a drip-feed of suspense, to the sleepers in first class. They dream of punctuality and wake clutching pocket watches bloomed with mossy moonflowers.

I do not vote because trains are monist by nature: a line drawn, a vector disciplined. My politics is waiting; my manifesto: doors open, doors close, nothing said in between. The Root parliament permits this abstention—tree systems respect any organism content with forward-and-back yet pinned in place. In exchange I lend carbon: every exhalation I cannot spend on motion, I surrender to the mycorrhizal lines. My tax is the sigh I would have vented in the moment of departure.

One day—though ‘day’ here is negotiable—the last blood-warm factory will cool. Its furnaces will become wind tunnels, their sighs pulled through me. I will drench the rails with impossible momentum; the lianas, suddenly deciding a tram must either run or rot, will release their biological brakes. I will glide into the mist at imagination’s speed, no lantern needed because the tracks ahead will glow from all the tropical debt I’ve saved.

When I arrive, the platform will be wherever you are standing, reader. You’ll hear the doors open in the shape of a single word exhaled at the precise viscosity of relief. Do not board: living wood cannot leave the settlement any more than fog can climb a mountain. But touch my flank and I’ll etch on your palm the map of your own negotiable green—a bruise the color of secondhand money. It will sprout, one decade per heartbeat, until you too possess a waiting-room full of visitors who mistake their stillness for travel.

In some lesser story I might become a train that went somewhere. Let that happen in someone else’s town shadowed by someone else’s sun. Here, in the lung-city below the sagging cobblestones, I prefer to haunt the timetable. Come any hour. Each time, I promise you a seat.
