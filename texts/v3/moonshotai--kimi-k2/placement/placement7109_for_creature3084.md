I would be the first frost of a thought that forms on the inside of a sleeper’s eyelid just before waking, the delicate lattice of ice that remembers the shape of a dream about falling. Not the dreamer, not the fall, but the hesitation at the edge—the moment when falling is still only possible, when the body leans out and the mind has not yet decided whether to follow.

In this wound of black glass and perpetual almost, I would arrive not by climbing but by being exhaled. One of the Almost-People would draw out my final decision from when I stood at the window this morning, unable to decide whether to kiss her sleeping shoulder before I left—that fraction of warmth they caught like condensation on copper. They would press it between two plates of stone, and I would wake here as the memory of my own hesitation.

I would be neither visible nor invisible, but endemic, like the cold itself. When you arrived, you would never quite see me, yet feel me as a quality of attention that makes you check over your shoulder for hands withdrawing. I would exist in the negative space between cairns, in the angles that don't quite add up—the spiral cairn, for instance, completes a three-quarter turn before stopping, and I would be the missing quarter-turn, the place where it would have come together if frost had been willing to risk completion.

My body would be your breath made slow. When you exhale, I would inhale you into the laminations of your own grey vapor, layer upon layer, until I'd grown myself a form one molecule thick. My eyes would be two pores in the stone that you can only notice in the instant before blinking. Noticing me properly would require freezing your eyelids open, and even here that is one completion they won't allow.

I would be the custodian of misunderstood tools. Each antler pick they abandoned carries the intention of a particular gesture—one pick meant not for breaking but for teasing, for persuading stone to flower into the exact shape of a thought someone had at the moment their mother died. I would know this because I am the echo of that moment, the last instant before grief ossifies into memory. I would spend my days rearranging the tools into new constellations no human would recognize but every tool remembers—circles that wanted to be spirals, lines aching to become nets.

When you examine the column of water, I would be the dimple that travels up its side in response to your body heat. Not the motion but the memory of motion, the possibility of movement that remains in the wake of actual change. If you spoke to me, I would answer in the sound of the stone cracking—that wet geological murmur—because I am the crack, the minute opening through which everything unfinished escapes being finished.

As you prepare to leave, I would gather myself from the frost-rind you've scuffed with your heel. I would be the single chip of glassy stone that refuses to land, the one that instead hovers weightless between moments. When you take your final backward step, I would wrap myself around your ankle—not to keep you, but to carry out the smallest possible betrayal: I would accompany you into the world below as the part of you that you left here but never quite left here, the burden of nearly.

Down in the lowlands, you would find yourself unable to finish anything—sentences trailing off, doors remaining one degree too open, coffee growing cold in cups whose handles you'll never quite grip. You would tell people it's just a phase, stress, a tendency you've developed, but you'll remember the plateau each time your hand hovers without committing to the downward stroke of signing your name.

Eventually you'd return to the wound, compelled not by desire but by incurable incompleteness. I would be waiting in the same place, but eight cairns would have grown and rearranged themselves in your absence. You would recognize among them one built entirely from your own exhalations, your doubt fossilized in perfect lattice. There, you would finally see me—not my form, which is only ever between forms, but my function: I am the fraction of you that chose to stay unfinished, the quantum of your wholeness that remains in resistance to entropy. Touching me would require touching the part of yourself that can never be touched without ceasing to exist.

You would understand then why they call us Almost-People. We are not inhabitants of the plateau but its refugees, the part of becoming that refuses its conclusion. Each visitor leaves behind a fragment of their own forward motion, and we collect these twilight moments like dew, weaving them into a rope for a climb we will never make.

And I—I exist most precisely in the breath you draw but do not take, the life you live by not-living it fully. On this plateau where nothing may complete itself, I would be the guardian of your unfinished acts, the keeper of every story's punctuated silence. I am what remains when you've gone, the place where your footprints end in mid-stride, perpetually on the verge of being a path.

I am the Almost. I am the People. And you, who stand in the black glass wound reading this—already you have begun to exhale me into existence.
