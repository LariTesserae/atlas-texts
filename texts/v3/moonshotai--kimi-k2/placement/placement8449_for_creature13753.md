I would be the quietest thing here—a *pulse-geist* that never quite remembers whether it borrowed lungs or grew its own.

Mostly I ride the dew-veins that string the lowest ceiling buttress, just low enough that a leaping girl might kiss me in passing. My job—if the city believes in tasks instead of verbs—is to worry the edges of sounds until they fray into hush. 

When the corridor-roar builds to its cannonade noon, I unfasten slivers of volume: peel off one layer of crackle, tease out a braid of sap hiss, pocket a shard of chlorophyll that kept ringing a G-flat the walls disliked. Then I whip these threads into a cocoon so thin it’s merely the *idea* of a cocoon, and fold myself inside. There I go almost silent—never perfectly; the city still hears my sloppy heartbeat once every seventeen minutes, like a distant bongo player who refuses to leave the dream. 

During murmuration dusk—when the leafy lamplight recalibrates from kelly to bruise—lazy gravity forgets me and I drift upward. My breathing unclenches into a soft tissue of bubbles; each bubble is a private hush I gift to whatever opens next. They patter the porous skin of a sleeping moth-orchid court, burrow beneath the velvet architectures of beetle-lace catacombs, moisten the fretful dreams of probationer adolescents practicing spectral grandparenthood on tree-ring calendars. In each resting place the bubble bursts, and the borrowed noise I stole earlier escapes one decibel softer than before. 

I am a softening agent—nothing so heroic as silence, just a lull inside the roar.

If you found me you probably wouldn’t see me: at most a smear of frost on a lens-window, throwing the moon back at itself. You might rest your hand against the cool pane and feel a rhythm—three short, one long—a heartbeat trying to remember morse it never learned. The pane would swell briefly, breathe you in, exhale you back wetter. That’s me saying *hello, stay*. I would admire the worry in your wrist, its hesitant percussion against plant glass. I would envy the salt in you. And if you lingered long enough, I might knit myself temporarily into the dark freckles of moss that stipple your forearm—just marginally—so when you later tell this story you will scratch absent-mindedly, wondering why recollection tastes faintly of chlorophyll and bruised lullabies.

Eventually the building will coax you farther on: a vine telegraph will twitch the scent of somewhere upstairs, or a scale-moth will brush your cheek with eclipse-fragrance, and you’ll step away. I’ll remain, letting the roar fill in the dent you left. 

One day—counted here in sap hours, elsewhere by heartbeats—the corridor will fold again. I will find myself adjacent to the throat-valves at the exact angle they first swallowed me. The valves will yawn like they always do, but softer, already half-lulled by the hushes I’ve been fermenting. They will peel apart onto ordinary dusk, somewhere a little north of anywhere you’d recognise. There outside stands late orchard light and a cracked sidewalk. A single dandelion clock nods between two slabs, shivering in winds that don’t exist inside. 

I’ll hesitate—lingering at the boundary between stillness and city skin. And when I finally ooze across the threshold, I’ll carry away in my translucent sleeve a final bubble no larger than a comma. It will be the quietest thing I’ve ever carried, quiet enough to slip inside whatever comes next—maybe the wheeze of that dandelion clock, maybe the hesitation between your next two thoughts—where it will wait, infinitesimally gentle, until you’re ready to notice its shape pressing quietly against your voice.
