I arrive—as all arrival is here—already in mid-step.  
My foot meets the slab at the exact moment a thermal exhale cracks the floor: *click*.  
Carbon fog lifts like stage-curtains. The heat coats my tongue with the remembered taste of old pennies.

No water.  
My body remembers water anyway; it leaks the idea in slow, nostalgic beads I dare not let become real drops. The air watches, polite, and thickens around each prospective droplet until it dries into another layer on my lips. Black lipstick nobody asked for.

I am not visitor. Not yet Ember either. Something interim: half memory, half blister. For the first while I simply stand and let the loop find me. It does. Forward twenty paces, I meet the shimmer of my own footprints arriving, still warm but seconds older. We regard each other kindly—then both turn sideways and walk again.

Where to belong in a room already crammed with polite absence?  
I could squat beside a vine and offer myself as trellis. The sulfur sap would thread through my pores, fill the empty lumen of my veins with slow lava-blood until I became a living glass bonsai, lungs full of hot citrus light. I would flower mica-wings behind my ears: every heartbeat a brittle snap, every sigh another scrawled line on the ceiling’s diary of accidental calligraphy.

They ask (a mirrored ting in the wrist-bone): Do you want to stay absent or learn how to leave?  
Staying means thinning: I would unwind into a ribbon me, left-ring-finger first, then forearm, shoulder, until I am only a sleeve of warmer dust arguing with itself. Leaving means graphite toll: one memory of blue lake at dawn, salted, smeared across the blistered wall to cool it by a fraction of a degree. I taste pennies again—the lake backs up my throat, uncertain.

So I invent a third option.  
I place myself at the exact center of the folding circle. Instead of walking the radius I kneel, fold my arms around my shins, and shut my eyes. Breaths come thin, syrupy with carbon. One, two, three heartbeats—long enough for the city to hesitate in its breathing.

Then I contract. Not shrinking, but a deliberate condensation: a billion skin cells curl inward like the petals of a paper bomb. My moisture and memory convert themselves into a seed-crystal of ice-less cold, a negative star inside the furnace. I press the seed into the floor’s freshest fracture. The slap of impact is inaudible; the heat cannot flee, but it notices absence.

What am I?  
I am the possibility that not all graphite must turn to diamond under pressure.  
I am the half-guest who refuses both citizenship and farewell.  
I become a pocket of cooled graphite densely remembering the taste of snow it has never touched. The vines lean toward me, curious; their sap slows by a degree, recording a new punctuation. The Ember braid themselves into a column and observe. One fingertip of theirs (barely warmer than the air) traces my outline and lifts away carrying the faint chill. They study it as one studies a newly coined word—rolling the discomfort on their thermal tongues.

I do not flower. I do not ravel. I fossilize in place as a deliberate lacuna. Over decades—your years, not mine—the seed will grow by subtraction, drawing more heat, etching a widening oval the color of moonlight that never was. Round its edge the pre-light lenses will focus refractions: fragments of lake, fragments of my mother’s porch swing, fragments of rainfall on tin roofs. No one will drink the water, but visitors—new almost-nobodies—will step inside that ellipse and feel, for an instant, a breeze against the cheek they no longer possess.

Long after the last mica-leaf has screeched itself asleep I will still be here, the city’s coldest memory, holding open a zero at its center like a waiting eye.  
And should you arrive one distant thermal breather from now, you will find a pair of silhouettes on the graphite wall: mine, and my own after-image a fraction of an hour younger, both kneeling, both refusing to decide whether to stay or finally leave.
