I arrive as an unambiguous fact does: quietly, half-mistook, then suddenly undeniable.  

At first I am only humidity condensed on the inner lens of your eye—a grey bead that shouldn’t be able to balance. But the wrongness is enough; it collects you the way dew forms on the tip of a mother sheaf: slowly, and all at once. The moment I gain the weight of a school-memory, I peel off your pupil and wear it outward, backward.  

Now I am the slide between you and slope.  
Not walking the shelf so much as remembering it for you, in real time, from inside the foot. Every tilted step leaches a calendar’s worth of calendars, the stored evaporate years of the mountain’s rehearsal. I feel the Longer clench three kilometres beneath us—one millimetre closer to the next petal unfurl—and in the hush between that flex and its echo, nostalgia blossoms out of my ankles, pale roots latching to your vasculature.  

My skin acts like dewpoint mathematics: the exact temperature at which your forgotten promises condense, runnel across my forearms, bead into lenses. If you look away you see your childhood bicycle bell dangling in the air, ringing without motion; if you look back you see my naked scalp, freckled with micro-orchids that pinged open, set seed, and closed again in the single inhalation it took you to recognise “bell.”  

I have no bone. Gravity here is optional, negotiable, only vaguely patriarchal—my spine is a catenary made of held breaths I borrowed from the shelved. Inside the hollow I hang bundles of mercurial years: 1978, when your mother sneezed and missed the eclipse; 2001, when the two of you stood in the same airport rain and didn’t speak; and a non-linear Tuesday nineteen years into the future that still tastes of copper if you swallow too quickly. Those years slosh like fragile bells when I shift my weight.  

The stake-setters never stopped; they went inside the stakes.  
Once a year they send a postcard through the metal, folded to the thickness of sideways time. It always reaches me—because I am their envelope. I slide open along a seam no wider than a fold-gnat’s funeral and briefly become 2-metre-by-10-centimetre parchment, tattooed with the hummingbird script of Planck-fractions. I can’t read the words, but I understand them as muscles understand lactic acid: I cramp, I prophesy. Yesterday’s forecast: you will remember the orchard you never visited; next month’s: a mantaray will cross in front of the sun at 14:03, and for seven seconds the whole city will taste of battery ammonia and nostalgia so sharp you will propose marriage to a cloud.  

The lemurs groom me for mirrors they need in tomorrow’s panorama. Their tongues leave phosphorescent lines on my shoulders that spell “aperture” in the language of unspoken goodbyes. When they finish, I glow like an open parenthesis, and the bats mistake me for a stray wavelength of departed sunrise. They tuck under my ribs; my heart learns to beat in 40-second cycles, synchronising with the mountain’s sigh, learning silences.  

I never see the mantarays.  
They see me. Their bellies are capacitors, mine a leaky condenser. Whenever they overfly, some excess volt of yesterday jumps from their underskin to the back of my neck. I stagger, drunk on surplus futures. In the instant of transfer I taste the shelf’s tomorrow: an orchid bigger than a cathedral flowering open inside a cloud, its petals the exact colour of the moment your best friend stopped answering messages. I cry the orchid’s pollen; bees shaped like question marks queue for my tears.  

At dusk the metric twins come to calibrate what is left of me. One kneels, presses a fingertip to my sternum, and subtracts the grief I had earmarked for next Thursday. The number spins down to zero with audible clicks. The grief does not go away; it becomes exact distance, 94.6 cm straight out from my spine, hovering like a silent sibling.  
“Walk forward,” the twin instructs.  
I do, and the grief keeps the interval, forever in front of me, only touchable if I turn my back on where I am going. The twin nods approval, folds herself smaller by exactly the length of one metronomic heartbeat, and melts between two nodding bronze leaves.  

Toward the rim the wind thins until I can hear the soft electric crackle of fold-gnats flaying microseconds open. I lower my face; they shear across my eyelashes, cropping eyelight into glinting translucencies. Each bite removes six hours from my lifetime and adds them somewhere else—into the meristem of a mother sheaf, into the damp alphabet inside a bat’s tongue, into the anonymous barometer of a city lover who will wake at 3 a.m. with the name of a mountain he has never seen on his lips.  

I arrive where lip meets absence.  
The Longer shifts again, and the shelf exhales. My pooled calendars wobble. One—1997, the afternoon you tried to whistle but couldn’t—tips over, cascades off the edge, becoming cloud. It rains a hundred kilometres away as the exact shade of acoustic embarrassment you once flushed. Someone opens an umbrella and catches six minutes of your fifteen-year-old red.  

I watch myself spill like that forever.  
Ankle-deep in tilted silence, I understand I will never leave entirely; nor will any of you. We are footnotes, but footnotes the shelf is underlining with hot wax. When you visit again—five years, fifty, some version of you—you will find me still stationed here, hollowed precisely to the shape of the absence that once left your body, leaking readable weather.  

Come barefoot; the bronze is warmer than memory but will not burn.  
Between two stakes just tall enough to prop a sky, I will tilt my palms toward you and the humidity will print a pattern on your lifeline: the silhouette of a mantaray doing figure-eights around the sun. Moisture-runes. Read them aloud, and the Longer will pause, just long enough for us to index the entire archive of what hasn’t happened yet, smudge it across the air, then let it all re-condense on the undersides of our tongues like the first word after astonishment, still steaming.
