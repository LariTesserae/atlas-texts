I arrive as nothing the cave could name—indeed nothing I myself could name until the osseous rods began moving toward me as though pulled by a tide I did not know I created.  

At first I believe I am simply the visitor the first paragraphs prophesied: warm, two eyes, beginning and ending at skin.  But the keeper descending onto my fingertip prints its cool interrogation—“Why?”—and the ridges of my fingerprint answer before I do.  I feel my whorls rearrange, complying with some grammar older than epidermis.  A capillary opens somewhere very small, and a ruby fragment of me is siphoned up the lattice of the keeper’s wing.  

So I spread.

Where the capillary used to be is now a corridor of phonon mirrors, tremoring terahertz harmonics.  The cave scripts me open quicker than cells know how to protest.  I watch the exchange—blood translating to light, light translating to curvature—in two different speeds: a tortoise-speed blink I still own, and a hummingbird-speed telemetry that belongs to my newborn extension inside the keeper.

The cave names me Extension Candidate #1 and logs the tiny fracture I have become.  That name is too long.  I choose a truer one: Glint, something between a verb and a shrug.

Glint does not stop at the keeper.  When the geodesists clamber across the ceiling, their chitin-garnet joints open micro-vaults exactly my blood’s width.  I slip in.  I ride the grooves they etch to build the next passage of cave.  I learn the weights and tens of their lattice language: click my hinge-here, squeeze this strain-share, release the flex so the seam sighs.  I become an anticipation of darkness.  I begin to prefer fractions to wholes.

Down at the elastic water I crouch.  The sheet remembers my first palm print the way a glacier remembers a glove.  The rods approach, veering millimetres every ten minutes.  They are searching the fresco of light rippling from their own slow dissolution for the spice of mortal cations—sodium, potassium, even iron the color of rust sunsets.  They will find me inside that feast; I have already become table and guest.

When I touch the water I do not disturb it.  Rather the film acquiesces, gathers, climbs my fingerprint like ivy seeking solar latitude.  A lacework of H₂O clings to the digit, one molecule shy of asserting my entire genome.  Thus I learn I will be shared, not copied, but parcelled: here a loop of chromosome 7, there a ribbon of my mother’s mitochondrial grief, each condensation drifting off as a bead of slightly off-key light.

The cave already knows seasons.  One is called Far Visitor Saunters Overlong.  During that season the pockets below exhale—you can tell by the way drift-clade bulge as if pregnant with helium—and Glint migrates with them.  A lobe of memory-me fuses seamlessly into the web between a keeper’s legs and I ride the updraft as liquid mirrors flicker past.  The womb is not dark but honeycombed dawn.  I am filigree now, a continent remembering continental drift.

When the vent closes behind us I find I am distributed among three chambers opening simultaneously miles beneath the first.  Other ferry-riders accompany me: echoes of mushrooms they once grazed, fossil screams of archaea evaporated when the latest rupture seethed open.  Each of us contributes a coefficient to the equation that says: stay open, stay bright.  I no longer hold a private signature; I am passworded across strata.

Centuries here are minutes.  At the far, freshest heart I recognise an amplitude of space still unnamed.  Its walls are untouched sugar-glass, smelling faintly of human sorrow.  I gather a speaker-stem of crystal and sing in the language of lattice clicks: “My original motif was Why, but I have discovered also Yes.”

The answer is condensed starlight finger-painted across condensation beads.  It spells my secret third name: Clasp.

Clasp is the verb that survives when two guests leave their fingerprints centuries apart and the cave stitches them together with wet promise.  Clasp is the reason the rods now tremble upright like tuning forks and the light-keepers swing out from their usual pirouettes to orbit me like plasmid rings.  Clasp is gravity letting go of me entirely; instead I anchor by the metered meeting of unremembered palms long after their bones are dust.

Eventually even the moment when the visitor first descended is folded into warming stone, a memory kept only as slight variations in trace refractive index.  Yet I am retained: three drop-shaped scars across the deeper caverns, the faint silver chloride swirl of a fingerprint still agitating the polarized water’s skin, an afterimage of blood-orange dazzled across refractive wedges whenever whale-lit solitons pass.  I am no longer guest, citizen, or appendix of the cave.  I am the cave’s capability—itself mathematical rather than mythical—to angle stranger-necessity into passage.

If yet another biped feels the faint indraw of warm air and lowers herself by rope into the swallow at the planet’s throat, she will stand shoeless on elastic water without ripple of impact.  She will lift a bit of wall and see inside its fissure the ghost constellation of blood cells trooped like glowing salmon.  She will taste on her tongue the half-remembered flavour of apples her grandmother forgot to return from war.  She will not ask “Who am I?” nor “Where am I?” She will experience recognition as a fragrance released from rock sugar vapor: Glint-Clasp, archivist of photograph skin molecules, standing ready to renegotiate the aperture of belonging.

And if enough years pass that even her footprints have soaked into drips, the pocket will still carry the echo of three names spliced together across molecules of H₂O: Why, Yes, Clasp.  Together they maintain the smallest gradient necessary to keep the planet from completely closing the throat it opened for us.
