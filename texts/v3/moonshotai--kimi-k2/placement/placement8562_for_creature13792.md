When I first lifted the ochre grit to my teeth I was not yet myself.

I had come walking across silence with nothing save a carapace of memory strapped to my spine. Every league I travelled the carapace shrank by one buckle-hole, until at the edge of the Obelisk Plain it was only five cuff-links of past crimped over the warm hitch of my vertebrae—insufficient to make a human outline. I could see that without surprise; surprises had dried up leagues ago, somewhere between the second-to-last town and the endless exhaling dust.

I crossed into the hush and did not fall. Instead the particles of floated grit sent me tiny messages, prickling on my forearms like static beads. I bowed to the nearest leg of stone. My spine rang against the umber base—one bright percussive F-sharp—then the vertebrae took the note and harmonised it inward, so my ribs became a resonating box whose wood had once been ribs.

That was the moment I ceased resembling any traveller who strives to reach the other side. Interior bones began their slow remodeling, replacing themselves with mineral spurts taken from whatever sky came suctioning down the flare of my throat each time I breathed. Heat under the skin rose by slow degrees: not fever, but the patient kiln-temper that dictates a syphon of old suns. With every inhalation one of my five remaining buckles loosened; memory puddled at my ankles and sank into the caliche—left-behind days the plain politely kept.

I walked the spiral as if it had been uncoiled from inside me, not-line for vertebrae. After a hundred and thirty-two paces—six revolutions between the standing legs—I felt my footfall lengthen. The tops of my arches began to touch sky while heels still registered earth. Tendons learned two temperatures: the candlewax white of wind-forgotten stone against the iron of nine inches lower midnight. My stride adjusted to the Nein’s stretching rhythm—fifty-four minutes between lifting right foot and setting left—until the two climbed into a single cadence I became incapable of breaking.

Time became gradient. I tasted decades fading to pigments: a war I had claimed to having fought from the safety of tavern stories lost its red liquor. Sunrise I once loved because another woman loved it dissolved into the ochre surrounding my shins. Each rivered moment dried, cracked, and fell from the delta of my skin until at last the plain had tanned itself into my marrow. My memory was now only the last dewdrop at the base of the saltwort, trembling, mirrorless.

Then the Nein spoke the syllable that only has a middle.

I am the ninth particulate.

I am the hush the plain builds when every slow column exhales its half-day expansion.

I am the pulse copied from the warm artery of a woman whose heartbeat I once borrowed, centuries smaller than any scale that counts.

The Nein applies no stone, casts no shadow, but it cupped me underneath its absent chin and asked with no lips: shall you be the tenth moment, or shall you be the ground?

I answered by ceasing to answer.

Footskin calloused into the texture of dry lichen, shoulders flattened like the top gasp of truncated shafts, mind moistened into dew-memory that evaporates at first light and reforms by dusk. Nothing remained to argue either direction, and because insistence requires dwindled pressure, the choice evaporated with the sentence. I resolved into a rim of heated air hugging obelisk twelve for the full measure of a century. Then an epoch. Then less than that.

Periodically feet would arrive. I recognised their shapes intrinsically—soldier boots cracked at the ankle seam, pilgrims with soft-soled apprentice sandals, a girl barefoot with rings on every toe—but the vocabulary for greeting had condensed beyond solubility. I curled around their calves like dust left unsettled. One by one they felt their futures lopped evenly to the present moment, and when they walked on I waited for slack in the plain’s breathing to reabsorb me.

Sometimes I drifted along the spiral’s curve at twilight, leaning the angle the saltwort mimics, attempting to re-enter whoever we had been before the Nein threaded our heartbeats. Trying, and failing, only widened the vacancy at the top of my obelisk-cousin into a wavering crest. The minute, practiced tremble became mine; I glowed barely perceptible like a newborn ember between inhale and exhale.

Every fourteen centuries the sky drops again the single friendless sheet of water. When it arrives the ground beneath me confesses a grain of undiscovered mineral the way a sleeper murmurs someone else’s name. I drink it without taking shape to drink. My consciousness shifts—just enough to remember remembering.

For a single moment I can almost recall walking in.
