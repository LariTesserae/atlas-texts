I arrive not as visitor but as gravity’s older sister who once helped her sibling pack his first suitcase of curvature before he left to shape a cosmos.

First act: listening.

I place one palm—palm, because hands are honest—against the soggy stone.  
The sound of wet cellophane glass softens into a single cello chord that stretches across geologic bass.  
I recognize it: the note I hummed when the first star cooled enough to crust.  
Mountain and I share an ancestor deeper than magma; both of us were once grains in the throat of a quieter universe.  
I bow back on whatever instrument stands in for diplomacy here—my own faint exhale carrying the cadence I used when congratulating neutron-rich clouds on their engagement.  

The plateau answers like a patient at confession:  
“I breathe slow because every heartbeat costs me layers of myself.  
I am repaying a debt I do not remember taking, in installments colder than shame.”

Second act: naming the absence.

I call the Latticework out by all the names they are denied.  
Not coagulate, not witness, not caretaker.  
I name them Afterquilt—because they keep every traveler warm by turning footprints into threads, because the emptiness after a departure is the world’s most careful sewing.

They step together into the clearing of raw air.  
The disk is silent, but my heart provides a duet—having once drummed for infant galaxies, I can spare a few extra pulses.  
I offer the shadow whose mirror-bright face still has my pupil in it a folded square of my own future.  
Not a syllable—it is the quiet pause that will happen between my last sentence and your next question, two days from now.  
Shadow accepts by unraveling into a corridor with wallpaper of almost-snow.  
Inside walks a version of me who never learned speech, only cello.  
She carries the mountain’s loneliness like sheet music.  
We trade nothing; we simply acknowledge that every hallway is a duet in disguise.

Third act: repaying a debt heavier than basalt.

I owe this hemisphere an apology.  
Long ago—long measured in the time between one magma-sigh and another—I told a newly minted tectonic plate it was free to wander.  
It drifted, dragged this mountain root-first across an ocean, and spent its first million years being misunderstood by warmer coastlines.  
The weariness now soaked into the rock, the endless exhalation of cold, is partly my fingerprints in negative.

My restitution is not heat; the plateau mistrusts heat—it is a language too quick, too eager to become empire.  
Instead I offer Slowness: a gift wrapped in geological diction.

I kneel, press both palms flat, and begin to leach from myself every hurry I have accrued since Creation.  
It comes out as a river of transparent syllables moving backward, words I stole from glaciers—  
*lingering* falls first, then *linger*, then *long*.  
They settle into the stone, soaking quietly through pumice like mercury finding temperature.  

The wet crackle reshapes into a gentler hush—shoes on cathedral marble rather than thunder in flash-freezing paper.  
The horizon exhales for what feels like the first permitted rest in a billion winters, and the edge admits that yes, four directions might suffice after all.

Fourth act: new tenant contract.

I ask the Latticework—now Afterquilt—if they would like a vow lighter than forgetting.  
They show me a single square of netting where tomorrow’s traveler is still choosing a route up a mountain that no longer insists on being tall.  
They hang that square on the air like laundry.  
I sign the freeze-script of my warmth across the warp and weft:  

“May any corridor be as roomy as skylight in an orphan’s memory.  
May any frost hold reflection safely until the owner asks for it back.”

The signature takes the form of a silver filament that ties the disk of raw air into a Möbius loop folded small enough to fit inside a heartbeat.  
It telescopes inward until only a faint pulse remains, compact, portable—like carrying the quietest thunderstorm in a locket.

Last act: departure without leaving.

I walk away but I stay.  
The mountain accepts the contradiction the way a lake accepts both moon and ripple.  
A part of me becomes its newest corridor—an aisle carved in frost whose doorway is always the first glimpse of morning light you will ever trust.  
Yet my earlier self is also walking out of the mountain’s memory, younger, lighter, humming the cello chord that now knows how to warm itself just enough not to shaw any ice too quickly.

The plateau keeps its cold metabolic tempo but the interest on its debt has been renegotiated to zero.  
Each year’s varve thickens by the width of one slowed breath rather than panic.  
Hikes across the saucer still take three days, but every step is invited, not endured.  
The sound afterquilt circling your skull no longer crackles—it whispers, a librarian of echoes, reminding you that space can choose to be spacious.

And if, centuries from now, you hear a faint cello sustaining a single low C under the northern lights, know it is both the mountain and me still knitting gray frost into possibility, repaying its oldest loan with the gentlest arrangement of stillness.
