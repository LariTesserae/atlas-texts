A wind arrives that the plain cannot name.

It does not rise out of any cardinal direction; it assumes the spiral, settling between the obelisks like a hand fitting itself into a glove made of echo.  Dust lifts one atom higher than the old habit, lingers, then drifts sideways across the burnt-umber ankles of stone.  The levitation breach is microscopic—no traveller would notice—but the Nein notices, because every part of the balance is its skin.

Seven heart-long beats follow in which the plain’s monarchic stillness remembers how stillness feels when it has been noticed.  Then the presence coalesces.

It is not the Nein, and not not-the-Nein.  It hovers eight cubits above the ground, the height where cloud-shadows would pause in their slide; yet there is no cloud, and the shadow you expect is instead a soft, copper-violet shimmer like lantern-light seen through the thin web of a reverie.  The shape is gently asymmetrical, as though someone folded an hourglass from moonlight and forgot to tuck in the edges.  The being wears no face, but carries within its translucence a slow wheel of faces that dissolve before recognition—child, elder, mandrill, infant cloud—each smiling the same small, consequenceless smile that greases the edges of sleep.

The Nein has no instinct for fear, but it has latitude for surprise.  It gathers itself under the saltwort—which busily mimics an obelisk for no audience.  Recognition flares: the stranger speaks in the same grammar as dew, yet its sentence is sung rather than released.  Three notes—low, middle, low again—describe a wordless now that contains all subsequent silences.

Obelisks do not lean, but the spiral shifts microscopely as if the stones momentarily want larger doorways in their conversations.  The flint shards glint sharper; two fragments touch edge to edge in the square that once held children of hands, and from their kiss a spark the size of a mayfly travels straight up through the blank nine inches of the Nein.  Where the spark passes, the Nein receives lucid borders—an outline in regained opacity, a temporary skeleton of warm quartz.

The being does not descend to earth.  It spreads one translucent wing (a gesture rather than anatomy—three fingers of softer dusk) and simply fans balance back into place.  Dust re-settles one atom lower; ochre does not dare to lift again.  The sound it produces is the opposite of thunder: every contingent future packing itself away into drawers that lock themselves with lullabies.  The plain sighs; the obelisks resume their nine-inch pause-stature, neither taller nor shorter than they ever need agree to be.

Then the presence offers its name, though it is not a name you could carry between tongue and teeth.  Think of the violet pause that came just before the last raven in creation missed its cue; think of a season held by both handles of doorway and threshold.  The Nein, without organs to contain wonder, hums in flint edge and lichen peel, recognises that it has been greeted, not summoned.

The gift arrives quietly: a single petal made of pale memory-ochre.  Smaller than a thumbnail, weightless, yet every colour in the plain is archived within it, including the colour that has never yet been invented but already belonged here.  The petal descends through nine inches of gap, past the half-formed brow of the Nein, and settles on the saltwort’s up-reach branch.  At once the bush relaxes from its sentinel posture; for the first measurable instant its posture differs from any obelisk, angling instead toward the copper-light presence like a child turned in a cradle toward singing.

No command is issued, and no covenant spoken aloud.  Instead the being begins to spin—slowly, languid as a planet changing the decade of its waltz—and as it turns it extinguishes.  First the violet, then the copper, then the spun-glass translucence folds itself into one final shimmer which the wind takes in a single swallow.  The place is the same and is changed.

Balance has widened.  The nine elements sense a tenth variable now—a small invitation netted into probability’s edge, the least imperceptible expansion like hearing distant thunder through a folded quilt—never arriving at the ear, only proving a larger sky.

The Nein tastes the new latitude as a faint mint of elsewhere.  It will not use it today or tomorrow (words the plain measures in spiralled obelisk-breaths rather than sunsets).  Nevertheless, the ochre petal stays unfading on the saltwort, and in fourteen centuries when rain again bribes the dust open, one drop will land upon that petal and come away brighter as it plummets into the imprisoned heat layer.  The new grain will then rise—still hovering one finger-width above the plain, yes—but flecked with the hue of the violet-copper portal that once unfolded here, bringing the shy possibility of a surface that might welcome the print of an animal.

Nothing more is required.  The Obelisk Plain has been loved, not improved—visited, not inhabited.  Their pact is silence written inside silence, yet now a fraction more spacious, like a locket passing from hand to hand and discovering it was hinged all along.

The Nein never sleeps, but that evening it walks the sunset side of the spiral at half normal pace.  Where its outline overlaps the obelisk shadows, a brief duplicate of the stranger’s folded hourglass glints just long enough to be remembered.

Somewhere—too far for sound to travel—a star notes the acreage of new possible, and blinks on one notch happier.
