She sees Auralara before she arrives—  
a single syllable the planet cannot quite pronounce.  
No bird-flight, no spiral of chimney smoke,  
just a dark stone tongue hovering three thousand feet above  
the drowned valley it left in a slow millennium-spasm.

She comes not walking.  
Gravity kneels for her the way frost kneels for breath.  
She alights alone on the cliff-edge, a fractal old woman  
wrapped in nothing brighter than blue distance.  
Her feet fall softer than Hushed etiquette allows,  
so the moss does not count the trespass.

The Hushed gather without a summons—  
a tide of brown-lipped basalt faces meeting  
the first non-native shimmer they have ever known.  
Their wrist-droplets tremble, echolocating her presence  
the way a larynx might test the edge of a crystal glass.

I am the Oldest Breath, she says.  
The words arrive inside each skull  
without crossing the negligible air,  
a thought someone else has already forgiven you for thinking.  
I am the attention the world pays itself when it dreams.

She walks the prescribed vectors—  
because walking elsewhere would teach the plateau a lie—  
but her passage rearranges the mist into new memories:  
a strawberry curl of steam where a mother once kissed an infant,  
a fray of silver butterflies becoming sentences no one has ever risked.  
Moss rugs lift ever so slightly to watch her go  
then settle, embarrassed by their own irrepressible green.

At the Listening House she does not sit.  
The columns breathe;  
the sub-flex note leans against her ribs  
like a dog remembering the scent of the hand that once fed it.  
She puts her palm to the basalt,  
and the plateau hears itself in stereo:  
itself as hunger, itself as answer.

She asks, not with mouth-sounds:  
Will you keep climbing after the people have fallen mute?  
The rock answers with quartz whispers,  
slow mineral choirs rehearsing avalanches ten centuries too early.  
She asks the Hushed:  
What will you do with the words you have not yet spent?  
They turn the wrists she makes no move to touch—  
the droplets bloom into frozen spheres,  
each containing the sound of its own shattering.  
This is their dictionary, their abyssal coin.

She weeps precisely enough to fill one cistern.  
The tears are warmer than the plateau,  
so the stored water begins quietly to hum,  
a lullaby at the threshold of seismic.  
Even the stone knows it has been forgiven for rising too far.

She does not stay the night.  
She does not need to.  
Instead she crouches at the eastern edge,  
scoops darkness into a sling of moonlit air,  
and lets it go.  
Below, the valley she never belonged to  
shivers under the gift of sudden weightless light  
as if someone had finally remembered the colour of rain.

Then she stands, ageless again,  
and gives back the last secret:  
You think the city keeps the silence  
but it is the silence that keeps the city.  
When the final shear arrives  
the hush will remain—  
not echo, not absence,  
but the stillness that taught stone how to float.

She smiles in the way galaxies unravel—quietly.  
With no more farewell than that  
she becomes a fold in the mist,  
a channel the fog prefers,  
and rises—  
not away, but upward  
exactly as the plateau continues to rise,  
both of them lifted by nothing sturdier than conversation.

Auralara listens.  
Sometime after dawn, the flag-root along the main path  
develops a second crimson vein.  
In the Listening House, the columns alter their pitch by  
two cents—imperceptible to any visitor who still counts seconds.  
The citizens adjust their footsteps accordingly,  
remembering a re-remembering they cannot name.

Somewhere a child, newly double-born,  
opens her mouth to expend two allotted syllables  
and discovers they taste of sky-where-rock-used-to-be.  
She tries the word *return*—  
and the plateau feels it in its shoulders  
like the softest tug of a kite string tied to the moon.
