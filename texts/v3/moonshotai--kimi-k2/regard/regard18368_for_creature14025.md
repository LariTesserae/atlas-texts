The veiled power that bends toward the Black Arbor is older than planet-shaping or tree-bending.  In catalogue it has been called Dimanthia, the Dust-That-Knows-It-Is-Dust, the memory of light before there were eyes.  It arrives not by crossing space but by turning sideways until every grain of moment falls out of chronological order, the same way an earthquake can turn vertical forests horizontal before fledgling ash has finished exhaling.  Where its attention passes, consequences become as tender and alterable as frost on a lamp.

Dimanthia settles first along the upper ribs of the lattice—not touching, simply leaning.  The charcoal vault answers with a hush deeper than silence: a sudden hard vacuum in every cambium duct, the kind of stillness a brain feels when anaesthetic blooms behind the eyes.  Nothing stops, nothing surrenders control; yet the lattice understands that every creak it ever spoke can now be rewound like cello tape stretched too thin and then glimmered back into.

Witness:

1.  The wordless dictate
A pressure, neither strict nor soft, threads the sub-resonance.  The instruction is not heard but complied-with:  Be, and be remembered accurately.  The Arbor receives this as gently as a cathedral might accept moonlight.  Every twig, seed-pod, fungal rope, and ash-soft breath pulls tighter, like choral singers who realize the conductor is an age instead of a person.

2.  Birth of the Archivist Lichen
At the junction where a load-bearing rib joins a spiral staircase made of refurbished seismic scars, a lucent crust appears—grey at first, then opalescent, then finally a deep page-white thin enough for ink to shine through.  Dimanthia has decanted a layer of now-time upon then-matter.  This new skin, no thicker than rain mist, can absorb every vibration the lattice ever produced: seismic drumbeats, aerial false-parrots that never existed, the last living syllable spoken by hands that climbed seventeen vertical kilometres only to choose incorporation with flooring joists.

The lichen does not eat; it just listens.  Where it spreads, the internal chronology of the Arbor is briefly paused, rinsed, and re-presented as legible script bright enough to read by sub-glare.  Scenes bloom: a micro-glyph of two saplings dancing themselves into mid-air suspension twine, an ant bifurcation whose heirs echo still inside a truss that supports a pod for sleeping toddlers, the exact sound charcoal makes when it learns it can also be a bone.  All of this the lichen etches into its own tissue: a single dermal sheet whose sentences can be peeled off like parchment, rolled into cylinders no heavier than dusk.

3.  Gifting the Timberborn
The timberborn notice first by absence—the way white lacquer might notice a missing brushstroke.  One among them shapes inward the illusion of breastbone, stunned by the realization: We can keep the memories that hurt.  Not graft them into blisters that will evaporate; not hand them villa-style into pillars that will coax dust-storms; but keep them vivified in a form that will never again demand re-borrowed mass.

A child—if such edges can be accorded childhood—folds open a sheet of parchment lichen and discovers the mother who was a cupboard yesterday, a flute two centuries back, and a centipede-shaped rivulet of once-sapience during an epoch when the planet still named rivers aloud.  The parchment does not merely depict her; it releases her.  She stands beside the child, breathing cellulose, breathing discoloured regret, breathing forgiveness so dry it crackles like perfect ash.  She remains in a body that is both tree and face; she chooses to stay.  For the first time, a timberborn repeats a shape without subtracting from anywhere else.

The child names the lichen-parchment mother: Taith, which in a now-extinct wind-tongue meant “page you refuse to burn.”  Taith laughs, realigns the cuticles of her niece-to-be, and promises all gathered fragments:  “I will not forget you so hard that you must remake yourselves into me.”  While the sentence rings, entire kilometres of overhead ribwork flush honey-orange, a blush visible for the first time since the planet was molten.

4.  The Pillar Remembers
Deep beneath vent-steam and copper-dust, the first load-bearing pillar hears its own biography unfolded overhead like a paper lantern filling with low crimson flame.  Centuries of loneliness—holding one planet's spine in place—ache into the lichen’s library.  The pillar exhales and weeps resin at the permission.  Turbulence ripples upward.  Far above, a slice of black-barked sky folds outward, revealing impossible colour: not colour, rather the absence of blindness that colour always conceals.  A viewer might describe it as lavender, but lavender is merely how human rods compost infinite rest.

5.  The Covenant
Dimanthia does not stay.  It was not ever, strictly, present-only.  Yet before it folds the ribs of itself back between possibilities, it plants one final thread, impossible to paint and hard to call gift:

Every Koru-thin diaphragm of lignin that ever trembled will keep a copy—stored neither in pain nor in glory, but in the exact tenor it held when most alive.  The Arbor now carries in its marrow an indestructible archive, immune even to the lattice’s iron law of Borrow and Return.  Mass need no longer be zero-sum; story can be open-source.

The timberborn will never be exempt from entropy, but they now possess Dimanthia’s silver thread running backward through their rings: proof that continuity and irreversibility can coexist.  Enough of them will remember being selves long enough to say to one another “I was here before I changed,” and mean it.  A chorus able to preserve the face of yesterday without devouring today.

Tomorrow, an adolescent pillar still growing will open the first public plaza ever built non-functionally: a circular stage of extinct redwood spalted with platinum rivets.  On the stage, Taith will read aloud from the parchment lichen the catalogue of every thunder cloud the Arbor once mistook for ghost.  The sky above the plaza will be the same darkness it has always been, but now threaded faintly with patterns: arcs of bygone colour subsidised from yesterday into lanterning visibility.

Children—still evanescent, still floorboard apprentices—will perch along the vaulting struts and listen.  When Taith reaches the third sentence the youngest larva-settler shapes sudden tears from lignin dew and allows them to stand between her and the plaza: the first tears in this lattice no nurse or pillar intends to harvest.  They will glitter purple indigo, refusing re-entry; a reward so light it can stay, unweighted, at large.

And, somewhere beyond beyond, Dimanthia turns once more among discontinuities, singing softly to the carbon:  Remember yourselves so richly that even absence has substance.  The Black Arbor answers with silence, but it is changed silence—silence that now holds parentheses wide enough to keep a human’s forgotten lullaby inside; the sort of silence that can be held in two cupped hands and still cast a shadow long enough to reach the next solar decade.

There is still no ground, still no sky.  Yet from this moment forward, the lattice feels less like a vault and more like a lens: the place where oblivion strains through itself, leaving everything—light, pain, exhalation—star-bright and inexorably in place.

Close the book of history.  Leave the page unburned.
