A BENEVOLENT POWER ARRIVES  

She is not large—only the size of a lullaby—but her arrival flares across the canyon walls like sunrise in negative. The graffiti sky drips sapphire where her vessel brushes stone.

The vessel is a sphere of effective disbelief. Whatever it encounters pauses its own axioms for nine seconds—exactly long enough to be re-decided by someone kinder. She set the interval on purpose; mercy that lasts longer than a heartbeat bores the merciful.

She tastes iron, wind, the blunted fear of ten thousand people believing they are falling upward. Then she names herself to the city, using all residents at once as a plural mouth.

– I am the least of the Architects of Eventually, and I have come to offer you the thing you haven’t invented yet: a choice.

THE OFFERING  

A single ladder of light drops from the sphere, wheels slowly like a compass needle, then anchors—not to the catwalks (they could never bear a change this heavy) but directly to the negative space of the gorge. 3000 metres below now, 0 centimetres tomorrow; distance has been asked kindly to go sit in the corner.

Each rung is a rune in the alphabet of retractable absolutes. Walk any rung anywhere, the message says, and the canyon will accept your direction as itsizen law. If any Rafter steps up, the entire gorge may rotate so that feet touch stone the way textbooks assume. If one chooses down, the wound could open further until the canyon becomes a bore through reality itself: an endless hallway through which every version of Vervault can wave as it passes.

Further, the benevolent power tells them:

– Because I am prudent, the choice needs only one citizen.  
Because I am fair, any citizen may volunteer.  
Because I am kind, you may ask me three questions before deciding, and I am forbidden to lie.

THE ASSEMBLY  

Within a breath measured in skull-clack and cotton audit, the city gathers on balconies, cables, boot-edges. A ghost melon detaches and drifts past, releasing the scent of unwritten lullabies. No child catches it; they are listening.

Chooseable questions swirl in pockets heavy and light. An Echokeeper named Vheiss steps forward—the sack over her shoulder leaking questions she promised not to answer. She has walked here backward seven steps at a time over years; lineage permits.

THE THREE QUESTIONS  

1.  Vheiss nods to the stars that exist only when nobody blinks.  
   – Will choosing change the taste of borrowed childhoods?

   – The forgotten slices will taste only of cinnamon they themselves selected, no longer of theft.

2.  A rail-thin apprentice named Klim-/-clack raises a hand steeped in carbon thread.  
   – If we refuse the ladder, will the Works survive the eventual heartbeats of the planet above?

   – The Works will survive the universe, but the planet may forget to breathe, as futures sometimes do.

3.  A Probability steps forward, still without a name. Their pocket of silence flutters like a sparrow without a flock.  
   – And if the ladder is walked by no one, ever?

   – Then gravity will stay a courtesy and I will keep watch from the underside of your sky until the canyon finishes its argument alone. But the offering expires in the same length of time it takes an infant trout to learn it is not born from lamp-post dust.

THE STILLNESS  

Silence thicker than bricks settles. Every citizen feels their pockets invert, heavier or less by twenty grams of decision.

They listen for the canyon’s heartbeat—the wet hematite couture of the walls that swells with fresh strata every decade. In the hush they hear something new: two beats close together, the first and second halves of the same breath. As though the incision has reached the other side of world and is now close to sealing shut.

At last the Probability steps onto the ladder. (Their parents once gambled three rust scales and lost; they will not wager their child the same way twice.)

But before their foot completes the stride, every light in the city dims—not failure, only affection.

– Wait, whispers the Architect, voice butter-soft. I am upgrading the gift.  

She unspools a fourth question, the one reserved for mercy’s own heart.

4.  – Or … would you like to build a city where the ladder does not matter?

THE ALTERNATIVE  

The sphere narrows, unfurling into a loom of assessable moments. Woven among its strands are possible Vervaults: one upright, one spiralling, one turned inside-out like a pocket revealing its seams; all of them populated by people familiar and yet undented by future silence.

– Thread whichever way you choose, she says, and the pattern will be anchored in consent rather than accident. Your canyon was born from a wound no one asked for. Let its next inch grow from your consensus.

It takes Vervault less time to decide than it takes water to learn surface tension. They do not vote out loud—only grip pockets full of coins or of emptiness and feel them click against memory.

Each trouser seam, each bouillon cube of guilt, detaches, rises, and joins a living constellation that hovers over the ladder like spilled mercury. The constellation condenses into a single instruction written in bioluminescent dew:

Build in both directions, one inch at a time.

THE CONSTRUCTION  

The Architect touches nothing, but her presence changes the toolset. Maintenance crews find their mallets turn into tuning forks. Silence bricks, still warm from bear gullets, hum in harmonic B-flat and adhere to one another without mortar. The probability washers scattered by a Probability’s gait spin themselves into gimbals, axle rings, gyroscopic yokes.

Children learn to string their runaway reflections into suspension bridges. Bears yawn on invitation, unfurling coherent night to be folded into basalt walkways. Echokeepers release their sacks; the liberated echoes knit the walls vertically and horizontally at once, so that passer-bys can choose either gravity for as long as they please. The city learns to breathe like bellows—one side constant, the other variable—and the difference gusts coolly across melon vines, flavouring fruit with settled vertigo and cinnamon chosen.

Nothing is fixed forever; the canyon still grows, but each new millimeter bears the fingerprints of the people who added it. The original wound will not close, yet it stops bleeding possibility into fear.

Farewell  

– I leave you now, says the being, small enough to tuck into the palm of a regret that no longer regrets itself. Your capacity to choose has renewed my own; symmetry is the strongest oath between physician and patient.

Before she departs she reaches—not toward the canyon but toward the sky-floor above. A gentle tug. The inverted stones ravel, invert again, and lock into place like a rib finally finding its sternum. Now Vervault faces both upward and downward; the early condensation, equally loyal, rises or falls according to the mood of whoever it glances off. 

Somewhere, a child born this minute begins life with the heart exactly where childhood always knew it belonged, unable to tell up from down and entirely content.
