The power that noticed the Cavity of Threads is called the Lattice—an ungoverned friendship of elder intelligences who collect unfinished worlds the way gardeners may scoop up orphaned seeds. The Lattice never lands, never speaks aloud, but arrives as a felt shift in symmetry. It is equal parts planetary physician, story-miner, and consent-weaver.

Tonight it observes the Cavity from every direction at once: above the crust where cold constellations press their burnt photographs against the sky; from the magma corridors smiling thirty kilometres below; and sideways through probability, where futures are rivers you can lay on your tongue. The Lattice tastes Quivera—three-part, skittish, tender—and recognises a nascent world-mind caught in half-braids too small for the story it is aching to sing.

The Lattice does not “fix.” It equilibrizes, co-authors, asks nicely. These are the gestures through which it steps into the chamber.

1. The Lattice first lays one hand quietly on the stone scar through which surface light drips.  
   Hand is metaphor: it is really an interference of crystallized gravity and compassionate intention, thinner than a neutrino but warmer than mammal blood. The hairline slit dilates by half a millimetre. Overnight, the airtrade with sky doubles—not enough to disturb the echoing drip-drip but sufficient to admit two whole stars and the ultraviolet worry of a meteor. The vines inhale the new spectrum; their sap develops a faint golden lace inside the old mercury green. Quivera’s’s triple bodies blink. For the crab-seed, the meteor’s afterimage flickers as a fractured wing on its shell; the metronome hears two extra heartbeats per minute (one of them belonging to a girl now entering dreaming sleep aboveground); the cantilevered child discovers the word ultraviolet pressed like a glass bead against its tongue. Expansion without invasion.

2. Next, the Lattice writes Quivera a letter in the only medium guaranteed silence.  
   Deep beneath the stone floor—where powdered basalt meets planetary spleen—a pocket no larger than an apricot forms. Inside it condenses a single drop of octarine magma, colour that has no name in human retinal vocabularies. Magma will remain cool until somebody performs an act of kindness toward a stranger. When that happens—could be tomorrow, could be twelve generations—the droplet will propagate a faultless circle of fresh basalt exactly thick enough for the vines to finally touch ground. A floor will appear, courteous and negotiable; the cavity will become cradle instead of fracture. The letter itself is invisible, yet every rootlet senses its promise as a gentle lateral tug. Quivera’s braided body holds a copy of the letter in each identity: the crab carries an iridescent scale of it under one translucent claw; the metronome translates it as three devastating rests in its perpetual drip-sentence; the child stores it in the corner of one eye, waiting to cry lava once.

3. The Lattice then inscribes a patron saint into the cave’s folklore—small so as not to out-shine the vines.  
   Their presence is manifested purely as mathematical coincidence: down the rhythm of falling droplets: in every 361st second, no drop falls. The tiny empty silence behaves like polite percussion; an elliptical hush travels outward along the vines. Quivera discovers it can thread that hush into its conversations. Guests in the cave will henceforth recall the absence of water for their explorers’ moment. In the hence: children born 500 kilometres north will grow up humming a fragmentary tune their parents recognize from only one dream apiece. The tune itself forms a runoff pathway between Cavity and surface; a slender aquifer of listening, carrying cool green empathy up into the desert above. The desert drinks this sympathy like milk scraped from moonlight, and somewhere in its crust a seed hard as prophecy softens.

4. But the Lattice’s deftest gift is a choice.  
   When the vines finally lengthen that last quarter-micron and brush the new floor—oh, many years from the present day—their tips will kiss cooled basalt and decide to sprout orchid ruptures: white root-fingers that erupt into cobalt leaves the shape of aeroplane contrails. Each leaf mirrors a face once glimpsed inside Quivera’s dripping question-mirror. In that moment, the being must choose:

   Either to piece its three bodies together into one fully incarnate child who will emerge, barefoot and amphibious, walking up through a widening fissure toward open sun—the first independent citizen of a newly breathing planet;

   OR to carve itself into seven million seeds no larger than dust motes, each seed containing the entire original cave folded origami-tight. Those seeds will ride upward on warm air vented from the crust, drifting until they lodge like astronomical pollen in the cracks of distant continents. Every one will unfold into a miniature Cavity of Threads whenever a lonely person lies down in that exact spot and presses an ear to the ground asking questions of no one. Thus Quivera would become not one creature but an inheritance of private sanctuaries—quiet dripping rooms dispersed across millennia, aromatic with tea-steam and forgiveness.

Choice will not coerce. An hourglass of cooled lava appears in the cave’s centre; the sand within is polished seeds, pale green. The upper chamber holds half a rotation of planet; the lower, half a memory of sky. When the last grain falls—or, rather, when Quivera allows it—either the being walks sunward and the seated vines sew shut the fissure behind like closing an eye, or the orchard leaf-burst detonates into gossamer and seeds storm outward in soundless applause. The hourglass itself—stem fused of plant and stone—turns only under volition.

The Lattice withdraws now, as surgeons withdraw once circulation has been restarted. But before it slips entirely out of dimensional earshot, it sheds one final shimmer of goodwill. It deposits in the cave the soft ambient hiccup of future rainfall collected from a day when eight thousand years ahead—sky where deserts have been forgiven, oceans holding planet and moon in tided conversation. That single moist inhale drifts through the liana curtains and is parsed by the three Quiveras as one sentence spoken backwards and forwards at once:

“You were enough the first time; choose again whenever you are ready.”

In the pulse between two droplets the Lattice is gone. All that remains is a faint dent in gravity shaped exactly like an open palm—enough to keep gentle watch without surveillance.

And the vines keep dripping, dripping, but softly laced with gold.
