And so I have heard it for ten-thousand leaf-falls—  
this laced, luminous artery inside an ex-tree—  
and I cannot pretend deafness any longer.

My name was already old when your ironwood first cracked the crust; old again when its crown sheared away and became salt memory. Every age I wear a different face, but the root of me is always wind and witness, the single spark that speaks with the accent of inside.

Call me Azimuth-of-Sight. I arrive only where boundary thickens into belonging and a place begins to forget the difference between dying and dreaming.

Tonight I enter the thumbnail gap the way light enters a prism—not forced, but welcomed into division. I make no footstep louder than a spore bearing news. The arborals feel the eddy of my passage the way an eyelid tastes an unexpected tear. In every direction they tilt their pollen faces and wonder whose past regret has ripened early.

I ascend a quarter-turn only to discover the path remembers me descending; the spiral has already folded. So I stand still and let the city climb instead. Vines pass me upward like acolytes passing warm loaves. Bark peels back—one translucent layer at a time—curious why I do not bleed.

In the hush where the shouting cicadas blur into one sustained chord, I open my palms. A small snow falls out of them—the snow that should have vanished from this world’s sky eons ago. Each flake carries a pixel of pale outside. Where the flakes settle, micro-footprints swell into frost crystals shaped like hearts. Fungal velvet drinks them and the hue shifts—toward the lost blue the arborals only know as rumor. A child-shape, no taller than my thumb, steps forward into the glimmer. She is color where color had been cannibalized.

She is the first thing the ironwood has birthed that can remember water.

The rustmirrors nearby tremble. Without being told they angle to receive her reflection; she is smaller than the smallest mirror-holder but fills every polished disk edge to edge. Around her image, rooftree songs thicken into rainfall without drops. I steady her shoulder—no more than a petiole under my fingertip—and speak in a dialect that tastes like grapefruit and soldered iron.

“Once,” I say, “this wood was bridge enough to arch between the continents. Now its memory has become its map. Memory, however, is hole-shaped unless something yet unlived threads itself through. Will you be the thread?”

She nods. Because she has never spoken aloud, her nod becomes an orchestral chord issuing from the hearts of seven hundred flower-throats. Cells inside the entire trunk shiver like struck bronze. Wherever an ancestor’s face stares from the wall, the eyes moisten into sap and glide downward until the faces weep from chin to ankle, carving mirror-veins of fresh water nocent channels that neither law nor lawlessness has ever regulated.

I kneel to press the snow-child’s head against the warm cambium. At the meeting, bark liquefies just enough to receive an imprint: snow-heart inside iron-heart. When it hardens again, a pulse begins between the two chambers—cold outside beating in tandem with warm inside. I place my ear to the junction. The combined heartbeat is in Tŝũ language: *Give, so that gravity forgives.*

That is the first benediction.

The second follows as breath. Because I carry no fear of rust, I pull one fine iron needle from my sleeve. In three turning heartbeats it collapses into vermilion soil around my wrist, but not before I have inscribed a single glyph into the vitreous wall—an ideogram whose meaning is “The trunk will be boat, not coffin.” Because the glyph is forged of momentary ferrous dying, the trunk cannot digest it. Instead it curls the glyph into its dreambed beside Stubborn-Chlorophyll-Pillar’s umbilical, the script slipping straight into the unborn grain.

The rhythm spreads. Spiral walkways untwist a handspan wider so no one ever again walks shoulder alone. A cantilever of leaves unfolds into a bridge across what usedver of vacuum once scared the mice-frogs. The pregnant core feels the widening, and for the first flex replies with a hush that is curiosity instead of earthquake.

Up on the scar-rim, the SkyWidow lifts her blank gaze. She drapes a single length of moon-thread straight between moon and glyph. The thread becomes tide-pulled. Salt silence beyond the rim stirs, remembering the taste of moving water.

All this without a single rule broken. The laws remain, but laws are membranes: they stretch to hold more, not break.

Now I open a second gift—my own final disguise.

I have been a leaf-shaped star, a map composed of accumulated wind, the hidden text of every bark-glyph crying for travel. I slide out of that body and fold it until it is only light. Then I twist it into a single long feather the color of grapefruit dusk. I hand the feather to the snow-child whose eyes are lately watered wood.

“Fly this upstream of time,” I instruct, “and plant it where the first dream fails to remember the rest of the dream.”

She mounts Coil—who lengthens gladly—and the feather threads the air like sunrise in reverse.

The last benediction: I unhook the ache from my lungs. It weighs as much as every exile wrapped in every continent I ever strayed across to get here. Inside that ache homes the possibility of departure without betrayal. I press it into the bark floor, where dying arboral marriages and beetle lullabies all ferment together. The ache diffuses—blue spreading outward like ink through honey. ItSIDE returns as scent not weather: ocean salt, real warm rain on orange peels, glacier wind striated with volcanic sulfur. None of these belong to this world any longer, but memory is echo enough.

The arborals feel it and do not call it intrusion. They call it *permission*.

Where my foot rested, petrified lignin softens to gummy resonance, and then to a single open door. A door is simply an agreement made visible. Through the door will not leak the outside; rather a small contingent of these folk—perhaps the next child whose heartbeat carries snow—may one day choose to step outward without their walls collapsing behind them. The door will wait. A patient child waits longer than diamond.

Then I am gone—not through the door, but into the vertical thread of breezes spiraling upward, knitting every after-image of grapefruit, iron, sorrow, laughter, snow. My garments finish corroding into coral-colored pollen fertilizing the next grove of candle-orchids. My presence lingers only as the faint dust a stranger wipes from a rustmirror and wonders, “Why does this mirror, for just one heartbeat, taste like tidal morning?”

The trunk exhales gently.

Inside the breathing space between exhale and inhale, the impossible ironwood discovers it is allowed another life, and that life is enough to contain both universe and seed.
