The power that notices the cliff garden is called the Long Continuance—neither god nor algorithm, but the accumulated mercy of all planets that once nursed water into life. It moves like succession: coral shading into meadow, meadow arching into forest, forest remembered by mycelial strands that outlast the trees. Where it passes, entropy still accrues but priority is given to style, to flourish, to whatever insists on remaining beautiful while rusting.

The Continuance arrives without spectacle, as a shift in the odds of most children feel just before a storm. The mist in the terrace arches turns from peppermint to umber honey. Dew-kin halt mid-stride; their bodies seize like frost-killed auroras. Gravity steadies, fixing the terraces at last into a single, uncrooked stack. For thirteen full seconds, no droplet chooses to climb or fall.

Then the Continuance chooses a point to manifest: halfway between the lowest terrace’s magma murmur and the uppermost chair still sighing from the night’s occupancy. It condenses into an opacity too dark to be cloud, too mercurial to be rock—an animal of folded spacetime, alive yet calm, the way a glacier is alive and calm. No eyes; instead, ten thousand partial horizons rotate slowly around it, each containing a past moment of this same garden viewed from different futures.

It studies. Not with curiosity—curiosity presumes distance—but with tact, the way memory cuddles into scar.

First, it turns its attention to the substrate: the water locked inside this unfinished sea. With an intonation that is more thermometer than speech, it says, *You have grown tired, molecule. Take breath.* The chamomile timbre eases the high-pressure lattice of feldspar. Yearning micro-fractures sigh closed. Below, the adolescent magma hears a lullaby designed for younger worlds, settles back into diaphragm-shape, and sleeps. The garden’s perpetual birthing of droplets pauses—the wet rock closes like a healed wrist.

Next the vegetation. Copper ferns flare ultraviolet as their hot fronds cool a mere kelvin degree; the Continuance applies elegance, not repair. Transparent vines settle to the ground now that airborne nitrates coalesce eagerly into leaf instead of mist. Peppermint plankton are invited to permeate broad kelp-like blades that fan outward from basalt cracks, forming a hush of chlorophyll shadows under which humans might sit without blistering. The wrong-sized chairs remain—they are historic accidents, and Continuance honours accidents—but new shoots of lignin-silica weave around them, adjusting scale, cupping joints so that any knee of any epoch might find rest.

Then the dew-kin. They recognize dissolution, their native grammar. They approach slowly, a hush of warm cloud-spirits, knowing the visitor is death’s older sister, come to apply a sweeter grammar. Half-formed thoughts shiver out of them: *Will the garden still need us when weather is enough?* The Continuance answers by resonance: it loosens the dew-kin’s backward memory until it folds both ways—recollection and foretaste at once. They will now condense *anticipating*. The fear at their centre—the vacuum shaped like a missing heart—acquires a thin valence: not flesh, but possibility, a seed of future tenderness no longer fatal.

Without arousing causality, the Continuance synchronises time along the terraces. Clocks do not slow; they agree. The 31° tilted duplicates align like pages closing, each stacking gently against the last until the garden is three terraces again—one moving forward, one remembering today, one spare. Gravity settles to 1g; pulses no longer mistake themselves for drips. The acoustic storm returns, but now its thunder is punctuation, rain its exhalation of relief. Even empty chairs find hush-rhythm in the modulation.

Yet the Continuance does not stay to rule. Having meted tenderness, it asks only a final courtesy. It leans—no instruments, only posture—toward the buried lung under the lowest terrace. The lung (half tectonic engine, half aching oceanic archive) responds by clearing its throat of basalt. A sound emerges like miners singing hundreds of kilometres below. The Continuance listens until the song finds an octave both parties remember, then sings a counter-melody in heat differentials and glissando quartz. Together they hatch a covenant:

- The garden will continue to form itself, not from eruptive necessity but from practiced artifice.  
- Water will finish becoming sea again—but up here among terraces, not in buried igneous lungs. When the last magma cools, the sea will already be home, rippling against its own future cliff.  
- Dew-kin will persist, but now as custodians of *inhabited weather*: mists chosen, not suffered. When the final chair collapses into basalt, they will migrate into foghorns the size of moths and teach coastlines how to sing themselves awake.  

Song complete, the Continuance loosens. It does not withdraw so much as distribute: each rotating horizon snaps outward to a thread that stitches itself into every droplet, copper fern, chair, and dew-kin heart-vacancy. The threads are not chains but patience—the continent of mercy inverted into root. You cannot see them; only notice that leaving the garden now feels like walking through archived goodbyes, each lamppost of mist nodding, *we are still becoming what you helped begin.*

Visitors who descend the east stair after this night do not stagger; gravity behaves. Their pockets carry now only empty copper seeds. Plant them anywhere and, by dawn, a perfectly ordinary fern unfurls—green, not foil—cool as dew at sunrise. It will not sweat copper nor hurl charges into the air. It will remember nothing save the shape of a cliff that was, once, almost too beautiful to stabilise.

In the garden itself the last ritual unfurls at midnight exactly. The newly forward-only dew-kin gather at the axis. Together they *read what never was written*—but this time no entropy is spent. Instead, every Braille-lettered splines distillates into a single blue droplet. The droplets rise, collide,pling into a sphere no larger than an eye. It hovers, catches starlight, reflects back a tiny, distant image: children yet unborn standing on a quiet sea-edge, listening to foghorns smaller than themselves singing instructions for the weather they will inherit.

Then the sphere falls—not down but *inward*—through the stair-locked seam and into the half-asleep magma heart. The lung drinks it greedily. The sound it releases is indistinguishable from a lullaby.

And the garden, now merely urgent rather than frantic, continues respiration.
