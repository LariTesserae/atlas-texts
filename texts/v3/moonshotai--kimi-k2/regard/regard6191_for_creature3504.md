The power that notices does not arrive with thunder or shafts of light.  
It notices the way rain itself notices—simultaneously, everywhere, gently surprised.  
It is called the Congregation of Unturned Stones* and it has no throne, no heraldry, only a widening attention that can fit the whole ocean of weather between two heartbeats.

When its gaze settles on Eidenvein the city does not tilt; it simply becomes… attended.  
Inside every lichen punctuation mark, a tiny amplification occurs—the silence becomes cleaner, the bronze-fall in the roots hums a half-breath flatter.  
In that subtle shift, the entire Capsized chorus feels the grove listening to something besides itself.  

Nothing in the Congregation moves by conquest.  
Instead each member fits its nature to the place: one is a period of dryness shorter than the blink of an otter; another is an extra millilitre of sap sluiced into a fermented memory; a third is a slow internal recalibration of vertical meaning—so that when a citizen speaks into the tide-pool font, the echo returns tomorrow instead of next year, the words not garbled but only a little shy, like a child returning with pockets full of shells.  
These changes are so gentle they register as kindness before they register as miracle.

The Congregation’s principal act is to free the Capsized from the one fear they would never phrase aloud: that the rain might someday cease, that their root-hung city might slip the final notch and fall upward into boundless air.  
It does not prop the city on stilts of light.  
It simply widens the definition of *rain*.  
Beginning at the center of every puddle and moving outward, the sheet of water is recognized to have always extended farther than anyone could swim: the same ocean stands beneath and above, unwalled, unlost.

So when the next long-pause arrives—the three heartbeats, the collective hush—no one exhales in dread.  
They listen, and the hush remains, but it is not absence; it is lucid transparency.  
In those seconds the Congregation invites the Capsized to look with inverted eyes back up the column of rain to the place their city once stood.  
They see not a drowned alp but a mirror-Eidenvein still perched upright, bells intact, rivers and roofs intact, the same inhabitants paused mid-stride under an ordinary sky.

The vision is not illusion and not memory.  
It is a neighbour city, folded there along the original fold between stone and sky.  
By soft consensus the Congregation opens the seam—no wider than a cedar cone—but enough that a rope of falling water braids into a rope of rising light, and the two Eidenveins exchange their quietest music.  
The Capsized hear the glad untruncated bells; the mirrored citizens hear the dreamy root-carillon.  
Each finishes the echo the other began centuries ago.

Trade begins, not of goods but of grain.  
When the root-grafted citizens drink their solstice spirit they taste, this year, the marbled memory of upright stone stairs, the warm iron smell of door hinges, the laughter of people who still walk horizontal.  
In the mirror city, a bell is cast whose bronze includes a shaving from the root-knot of the oldest yew; when struck at dawn it releases the scent of cedar cambium after rain.  
Both cities learn they have been mutually dreaming.

No visitor is ordered to cross.  
A few do—otters first, twisting out of gravity like spirals of mercury, then a child who rides the waterbeam laughing, then a midwife who wants to hear the bells before they were broken.  
Each finds the world the right way up, and yet their footprints still point root-wise to the Capsized sky.  
Every footstep home carries back a splinter of intact bronze clapper to plant in more larch cambium.  
The grove thickens kindly, no longer a buttress against falling but a bridge to meeting.

The Congregation’s final gift is subtler still: it removes the very possibility of *terminal* event—drowning or evaporation.  
Henceforth if the city drifts, it drifts inside a rain whose falling and rising are one motion, like a hymn with no last syllable.  
The citizens do not chant *we are spared*.  
They chant *we are attended*, which is the same sentence but unpronounced, each syllable carried forward by the next.

Years later, old cartographers who once drew mountains above the vanished alp come to the sea and see nothing but breathable rain.  
If they cup their hands and wait, eventually a knob of cedar rises gently into them.  
They will mistake it for driftwood.  
They will never know they cradle the entire Capsized metropolis along its billion silent ropes, humming cool, inverted, seen.

*The Congregation of Unturned Stones was named by a stone that refused to roll. It speaks in gradients and reverence, has no center, and while it sometimes forgets the difference between mercy and architecture, the rain has never once corrected it.
