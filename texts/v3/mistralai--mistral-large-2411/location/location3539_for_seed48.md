In the quietude of a perpetual twilight, a sprawling industrial landscape sprawls low and wide, hugging the damp earth like a metallic lichen. The air is thick and moist, a heat haze shimmering just above the ground, as if the world itself is gently exhaling. Water is everywhere, not in rivers or lakes, but as a constant, almost imperceptible exhalation from the ground, a warm sweat that coats every surface with a glistening sheen. The terrain is flat, stretched out in all directions, with only the slightest roll and undulation hinting at the geological breathe of some past world underneath the fauna-overtaken urban expanse.

The city—if it can be called that—is not one of towering skyscrapers or bustling streets, but a labyrinthine expanse of industrial structures, low-slung and sprawling. They squat on sturdy, weathered supports, darkened by time and the incessant moisture. Factories hum distantly, their rhythm a slow, steady heartbeat, churning out their purposes with a staid, unyielding efficiency. The humid air swallows all sounds of the ever-ongoing work but for the quiet churning. Squat, aging smokestacks exhale thin wisps of vapor that dissolve into the already laden air, signs of the heat and motion enclosed within what few walls break the low horizon.

No plants soften the harsh lines of metal and concrete with its invasive living; there is no xyloid smell of life in dank rot, no leaves to rustle in a phantom breeze, only the vast veining of fungal-or-otherwise blooms in patches and the all-permeating wet. Instead, a different kind of life has taken hold of the industrial domain. Biological communities, foreign and overwhelming — curious creatures of curious origin — rule this strange convergence of wet, urban, industrial, and orphaned earth. They make their homes in the shadowed recesses of machinery, within the cyclopean maze of pipes and conduits, and under the arches of rusted bridges — rust, or a ferrous rot, better stated as shortly-lived organic protrusions onto the metal surfaces.

Dull, cementitious, boxlike constructions — whole sealed structures, exactingly levelled onto low footings — spread into the expanse with no differentiation made between levels of floating water. Flying forms — natural or adapted-to-imitation — wheel above between the factory complexes, their dark forms stark against a sunless, yellowed sky. Their wings cut the damp air with a barely audible hum of softly revolving pennaceous membranes. The dominant color is an endless gradient of sepia, punctuated only by the metallic glint of water and the muted shine of aged, corroded machinery settled into its constructed, cinereous perch. Intrusions in the flat expanse — low structures or detritus and debris from a previous existence of life and mass production — are coated with an indeterminate but present slime of the leavings of the mycoid growths or the influx of airborne, vaporized water, settling gently and steadily into its environment.

An artificial network of canals and channels crisscrosses whatever ground is not stifled under the laid cementitious boxes and the slums of the overwhelming fauna, sunk low and disused, lined by walls and walls of metal carcasses, for here there was no quarry to stone the sides nor brickworks for intricate lining. Some channels seem still in use, though for what purpose is unclear. Their waters are thick and slow-moving, a murky mixture of organic sediment and industrial runoff that clogs the air with a pungent, almost metallic scent. A mutative thing, this slow-baserock syrup, which has evolved and catalyzed the local entities inhabiting these unusual conditions.

Ever dynamic and overshadowed, this world is a living monument toward resistant life reclaimed from the would-have-been momentum of extinct civilization. The balance is sustained using a quivering tidal inertia, keeping the wet, wide, vegetally abject, industrial fungal town incedibly adrift as the hum and humidity goes on.
