In the heart of an ancient, eroding mountain range, a vast caldera stretches wide and deep, a kiln of eternal summer nestled among the peaks. The air is thick and humid, a steam bath that condenses on every surface, feeding the riotous growth that dominates the landscape. This is a place of perpetual twilight, the sun obscured by a dense canopy and the frequent, lazy clouds that snag on the encircling heights.

The caldera is a world of water, though it is not a lake. Instead, it is a sprawling marsh, the water flowing in from the encircling mountains through a thousand tiny rivulets, seeping from the ground in a million invisible springs. The water is clear and warm, steaming slightly in the sultry air. It pools and puddles, fed and drained in equal measure by the dense vegetation and the porous, volcanic earth.

The caldera's floor is layered, a dense stratum of life and decay. At its surface sprawls a massive, interconnected morass of plant life—a stew of vegetation in various states of decomposition. We're way beyond the point where individual species can be identified. An enormous amount of organic material has accumulated here—dead trees, pulpy vines, slick, decomposing lumps of rotting leaves and giant, bobbing fungus patches. All of it squats together in the warm water, stewing, cooking. The effect is intensely tactile, vegetation to be experienced by the acre. Closer to the caldera's edges, where the encircling mountains cast their long shadows, the marsh gives way to a dense, towering jungle. Ancient trees with rough, twisting trunks strain towards the distant light, their massive roots stretching out like fingers, anchoring them to the saturated earth. Their bark is slick with condensation, their leaves broad and dark, drinking in the limited sunlight.

Epiphytes cling to every available surface, claiming every nook and cranny not occupied by the larger flora. Ferns unfurl their fronds in the shadows, and broad-leaved creepers snake up from the unders story, writhing up the trunks of the great trees, seeking the sun. In the gloomy underbelly, bioluminescent flora emits a gentle glow, attracting the insects that will spread their pollen and help them proliferate.

The air is heavy with the mingled scent of flowers in full bloom and the rotting vegetation. On day-long "cool spells" where the temperatures dip, atmospheric flow patterns shift and inconsistent haze of tiny insects descends into the marsh. Together they mask the sky like a layer of mist, mingling and twining low over the canopy. They move in oddly coherent ways—strange, systematic flows that suggest they may be not many creatures but one—every bug an organ with a specific purpose—a living factory of the perverse.

A little way down from the cloud of insects, under the protection of a monolithic hanging fern, there's a titanic mud man... a cordon under the formation. Steam billows from his head, lifting and sinking like the slow breath of a dying giant.

Fauna is hard to spot immediately. Most of it is water-dwelling—things that creep and dart beneath the pools, unseen and unheard. Larger beasts stalk the forest floor, but they are elusive, merely the suggestion of movement in the corner of the eye, the distant rustle of foliage. However, high above the forest floor, in the middle and upper stories, there's a whole ecosystem.

Small, shy arboreal creatures dart from vine to vine, or hang from branches. Those tail extentions twitch luxuriously, like animals on their own, catching rays of strong light and compressing them — a natural photon-based fermentation pyrolysis that's going on here.

In deeper shadow, a mobile snarl of roots mixes with viscous mud as it flows ponderously, like a surveying embassador from a cave-in—an ambassador that can't see without ingesting all it touches.

At night, the forest floor comes alive with glowing, creeping things; a luminescent, living tide that flows across the rotting vegetation, consuming and renewing in equal measure.

There is sound here, though it is not the cacophony one might expect from a place so alive. The air is thick with the hum of insects, the drip of condensing water, the gentle rustle of leaves in the nearly non-existent breeze, a gentle sputtering, scratching, gnawing erosion of the mud man. The marsh is filled with a symphony of bubbles from decomposing plant matter, belches of wet material, hisses of eruptions of warm-environment gas.

The caldera is a world unto itself, a strange and unusual pocket of life tucked away among the ancient peaks.

In the caldera, the rules of the larger world seem bent. Water flows more slowly here, filtered and fed by the marsh's hungry embrace. Time, too, seems different, passing at a more languid rate, as if the very air is thicker. Metals take on a peculiar genius here. Machines discarded in a tangle of bent, rusty limbs twist themselves into new shapes, accommodating the caldera oddly well. The behavior of electromagnetism is strange; and heat here can be made to flow in reverse against its gradient. It is as if the laws of thermodynamics are subject to Wall Street rules, tooth and claw in the leaf litter under the investment bank of wet leaf cover.
