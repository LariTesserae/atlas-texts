In the looming,垂簾 (Liúlián) —the Curtain—is a sprawl of altitudinous jungle, a stiflingly hot and wet territory of life and decay. It clings to the flanks of colossal tepuis—isolated, sheer-sided table mountains that soar from the depths of a vast, flooded basin. These ancient, weathered titans of stone are arrayed like silent sentinels overlooking a primordial world, their lower reaches cloaked in a dense, eerie mist that mingles with thealled, teeming waters.

The air itself is a choking, humid susurrus, thick with the intertwined scents of rotting vegetation and the cloying perfume of alien blooms. It clings to the skin, hot and insistent, like a fever's touch. The ever-present drip, drip, drip from the vast canopy above blends with the cacophony of unseen creatures, creating a discordant symphony punctuated by the occasional distant boom—the tentative, ponderous steps of colossi, echoing through the hidden valleys.

水簾 (Shuǐlián) — the Water Curtain — is the heart of this world, a monolithic torrent of water plummeting from the dizzying heights of the tepuis' edges, at the mountain plateau/cliff confluence. Electric-blue ferns and colossal pitcher plants line the slick, vertical channels that funnel the water into the abyss. As it falls, the water's roar becomes a physical force, shaking the air with thunderous bass. The ecology here is vertiginous, with nearly every surface draped in life: orchids bloom from tiny crannies, vine-like bioluminescent creepers dangle, and drifting, diaphanous gas-beasts float, undulating, through the mists scrolling from the base of the falls, their tendrils trailing like streamers fading upwards into the fall's undissant rainbows.

The jungle's gargantuan flora is Both familiar and unsettling. Giant relatives of bougainvillea and philodendra arch high overhead, their colossal roots a labyrinth of descending honourable greens in the dim nethe0r. Epiphytes cling to every cranny, their huge leaves collecting water, where tiny, glowing biota skitter and dance, children at play. Nothing is static here; even the rocks seem to shift, if slowly, in the scintillating humidity.

Yet across this vast, dense landscape where fauna's sparks and calls dance at the mist's edge, there are stalwart specks: lonely signs of the built environment. There are the carved remnants of stone platforms, jutting from the undergrowth, levitating trains of metal boxes bubblewise dancing on their ancient, frictionless tracks. Also, occasional black celestite obelisks stand bare, jagged, and inexplicable. Of an age with the mountains themselves, these diasporous structures emit a soft susurrus, as if whispering to the dense ecology that surrounds them. Twenty meters overhead, they appear to visibly curve the mist. Some are spanned with a flickering silver, as if these ancient stones were but franchise-cellular-antenna, relays of a massive, hidden machinery. Among hundreds of thousands of open, descending varas of hot mist, they appear like a dropped clue line.

The ecology sustains this place, as ever voracious, but also distinctly selective. Local flora can be dotted with fauna, but also can fold it, kill it, dissolve it, drag it; in small and illuminable clothscour, these biological forms can co-recycle-dissolve almost anything. Upon the forms which grow at the base of the celestite obelisks, a fearless and voracious insect can be seen to live in harmony with the flora. Antenna ending in a trio of feathered tips, bright and dancing patterns tracing suddenly from their non-linear paths. They seem almost to read the air, to engage it with an alien script. The floral matrix on which these tiny fauna reside, although unremarkable at first glance, is itself strange: particular patterns seem almost to emerge and recede, ethereal, as directed to do so by the dancing, notationally rhythmic antennae.

At the heart of the jungle, where the light filters green, the waters gurgle, the atmosphere thickens. Everything is alive, and the veil between the biological and the technological is tissue-thin. Some organisms bear a disconcerting resemblance to devices; crystalline structure, symmetries uncanny. The light itself is alive with a thousand motes, or maybe machines, or both—they drift, they pulse in soundless dance—they cling to your fingers, setting them aglow with ocular, sight-familiar patterns. They seem almost to fauna-breathe, mimicking the vibrations in yet-unstirred air, almost life, visual symptoms, a disease's course, a sign, almost a confession.

As the sun sets, the jungle does not fall silent; over a thousand reedlike, orchestrant scales howl with eerie song. They knit the air into a shivering tapestry, a thrum of life which resonates from the looming tepuis to the mists far below—bass resounding crepe vines, flutes, tendrils stretching, sothros plummeting. The rippling water, now luminescent by the glow of the nocturnal blooms, is almost errant.

All together, Liulian sounds like a rainfall's breath, a hot storm's viscous touch. It steams, caresses, comes close and circles to touch down again, like a life's reverbation. It hums, clothing, choruses with uttermost life, generative, resonant; this rich chorus emerges from each point—the rocks, each pore of the rough hides, the creatures, the stems of the glass-broad leaves, the ancient citadel. It is almost like the brushstroke is a sleeper, emerging into its own shape, a spreading redolence.

Everything in Liulian is tenacious and adaptive, like the fronds curling through the obelisks' cracks. This is a place that shifts, but the dynamism is a deep thing, felt in the bones of the earth. The hot air is languid, yes, but also lush and expectant, pregnant with the possibility of change, like silent seeds deep within the soil, biding their time.

It is not a place that can be rushed, or easily altered; it endures, slow and steady, in its own turgid light.
