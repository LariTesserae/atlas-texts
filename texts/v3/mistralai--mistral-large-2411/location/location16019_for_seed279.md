In the beginning, there is the sound—a profound, resonant hum that seems to emanate from the very bones of the world. It is a slow, relentless reverberation that fills the dense, cool air of an environment that is quickly calculating itself. The atmosphere is a damp whisper against the skin, a perpetual twilight that is not the absence of light, but rather its deepening into a muttered secrecy.

 The terrain is a sprawling, low-lying expanse, a plain of sorts, but one that is starkly different from the verdant fields one might imagine. Instead, it is a mineral landscape, a vast flatness occasionally interrupted by sharp, youthful formations that jut like teeth through the mist. These are not the weathered ridges of an ancient world, but the eager, abrupt extrusions of a place still fresh from its creation. The ground is a complex tapestry of mineral wealth—glittering pyrite, raw quartz formations that protrude like bones, and obsidian shards that litter the ground like the remnants of a volcanic sigh. Iron-rich formations run like rusted rivers through the landscape, while elsewhere, sulfurous deposits paint the earth in hues of yellow and orange.

Water is present, but it does not flow in rivers or collect in lakes. Instead, it weeps from the pores of the land, a constant, gentle seepage that keeps the air thick with mist. It condenses on the sharp features of the landscape, dripping in a constant, melodic rhythm that adds a subtle layer to the perpetual hum. There are no plants to soften the hard edges of the world, only the occasional smudge of fungal growth or lichen that clings like a secret to the damp surfaces.

The land is open, stretching out in a vast, unbroken sweep that invites the eye to wander. Yet, despite this openness, there is a density to the place, a layered complexity that reveals itself the longer one lingers. The minerals, the water, the sound—they all coalesce into a rich, almost claustrophobic tapestry that fills the senses.

Scattered across the landscape are remnants of something else—not quite ruins, but rather faint marks, occasional artifacts that hint at past presence. A solitary pillar here, a carved stone there—they are not the work of any recognizable technology, but rather seem to have been shaped by patient, ancient hands. They stand as silent witnesses to the slow, gradual cycles of the world, their surfaces slick with the ever-present moisture.

Life is rare here, but not absent. The fauna is sparse, peculiar—creatures that are more mineral than flesh, their forms echoing the sharp, abrupt shapes of the landscape.  There is a a kind of limping centipede of barely greasy mineral flesh, its impossibly articulated legs tick-ticking over crystalline moss; elsewhere, you might catch a glimpse of something like a skink, a milky opalescence shining in its barely-there eyes and scales of combed glass.  Each thing here thrives in ways strange and secret; life persists, but does not flourish.

 There are rules to this world, but they are not the rules of our own. They are slow, strange truths shaped by the patient, deliberate will of the land. The water does not carve the landscape; instead, the landscape calls forth the water. The minerals do not erode; they transform. The sound does not echo; it originates. It is a place of quiet, inexorable power, a damp, dim, humming testament to the subtle, relentless forces of creation.

As you move through this landscape, the cool, dense air fills your lungs like a secret. The hum becomes a part of you, your heartbeat synchronizing with the rhythm of the land. The sharp, mineral edges, the weeping water, the strange, rare life—they all conspire to create a place that is not merely a landscape, but an experience, a living testament to the strange, slow dance of existence.
