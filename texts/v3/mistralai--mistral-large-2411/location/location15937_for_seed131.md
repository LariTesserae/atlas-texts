In the heart of a long-forgotten industrial district, hidden away from the world's eyes, lies a peculiar pocket of existence. This is a place that has settled into its final form, where time seems to have stopped, or at least slowed down considerably. The air is thick with moisture, a warm and heavy blanket that wraps around every surface, condensing into droplets that cling to the decaying metal structures before dripping down to feed the lush vegetation below. The atmosphere is a constant hum of industrious machinery, a symphony of steel and steam that echoes through the vast, empty chambers, creating a strange harmony with the occasional drip of water.

The terrain is flat, polished concrete and rusted metal grates stretching as far as the eye can see, with only the slightest hint of incline. It is a manufactured landscape, the remnant of an ancient industry that has long since ceased to function as intended. The buildings are towering monoliths of corroded metal, their original purpose now lost to time. They stand as silent sentinels, their empty windows like blank eyes watching over the deserted domain.

Water is everywhere, dripping from the ceilings, pooling in the hollows, and flowing in lazy rivers through channels carved into the floor. It is stagnant, tepid, and tinged with a faint rusty hue, reflecting the eternal twilight that permeates the space. The water nourishes the abundant plant life that has taken root in the ruined industrial landscape. Mosses and ferns carpet the ground, while tenacious ivy climbs the walls, their vibrant greens stark against the rusted browns and reds of the decaying machinery. In the dim light, suspended from the distant ceiling, uranium glass vessels cast eerie, soft green glows like bio luminescent fungi in the throes of photosynthesis. Beside them, exposed filament bulbs cast off rings of cosy, warm light, hanging above enormous steel machines like moons.

The air is filled with a fine mist, casting halos around the scattered lights and lending an ethereal quality to the scene. The humidity is such that every inhalation feels like breathing in soup, thick with the scent of damp earth, old metal, and the faint tang of oil. Its an atmospheric pressure not found anywhere else. There is no wind here, no change in temperature, no natural gradients at all — just the endless, steady rhythm of the machines, the slow creep of the plants, and the lazy flow of the water.

The wildlife here is sparse, limited to the hardiest of creatures that can withstand the constant noise and peculiar environment. A solitary frog croaks from its hiding spot within a rusted pipe, accompanying the ceaseless industrial symphony.

There are rules to this world, or rather, a lack of them. The normal passage of time seems to have no meaning here. Things do not decay so much as they transform, slowly shifting from one state to another. The machines do not rust away but rather become something new, a strange amalgamation of the natural and the artificial. And despite the constant din, there is a sense of peace, a feeling of having stepped outside the normal flow of time and into a place that is, simply, eternally itself and nothing else.

As you delve deeper into the pocket-scape, the passage of the industrial ward towards green reclaimation becomes clearer and clearer. Each extra step forward the ratio of moss to iron increases, until great big cracks in the grates cast splintering dark beams ahead into the green, mists above burst forth vines and wanton compost growths. Between warehouses, some beams begin at the edge of one, but find a wall before reaching the next. The steel huge doors of empty warehouses are ajar like open mouths, water dripping off the sills like a swollen lower lip. Structures that might once have been cold frames, now have rich soil inside them nourishing lush tropical plants that loom outwards into the concrete world. The paradoxical nature of this industrial swamp is hard to believe, even as you walk through it; and so the deeper you venture, the more you look for evidence that it cannot exist, to rationalise the strange half-hallucinoegenic quality of experiencing it. Why is the water so very slow? It should be rushing, overflowing the channels. As you look closer, it seems viscous, ponderous. There is no gradient driving it forwards. It doesn't flow so much as *air*-flow, intangibly pushed along by the movement of the air itself.  The glass everywhere starts to look more and more like a materials too strange to not have been made by aliens, and so do the steelworks. Nothing makes sense, but in its place, another kind of sense is eager to be made.
