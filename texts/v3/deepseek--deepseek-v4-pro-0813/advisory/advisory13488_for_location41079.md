Visiting Catchment Nine is not like going outside. It is like entering a running machine. You are not going to see a landscape; you are going to be inside an industrial process that has eaten a valley. Prepare as you would for an active mine, a cold-storage plant, a flood-control channel, and a partial-confined space all stacked on top of each other.

Below is how I would prepare an ordinary human multiverse traveler to go there and come back intact.

---

## 1. Before you go: access, permission, and knowledge

**Do not arrive unannounced.**  
This is an active industrial site, not a public place. You need permission from whoever operates the catchment. That may be a maintenance authority, a water board, a smelter company, or something less clearly named. The workers call it the Sump; use that name if you want to be taken seriously.

You should obtain:

- A visitor or work permit.
- A safety induction from the local crew.
- A current map of the terraces, even if it is rough. The place is still being built and changes.
- A schedule of the six-hour water cycle, or at least the current drift of it. The schedule drifts, so ask again on the day you enter.
- A named escort or guide who has been down before. Do not go alone.

If you are traveling via multiverse portal or dimensional device, **do not open the portal inside the catchment.** Arrive at the rim, outside the fog, ideally at the midpoint of the east wall near the gantry road. The air pressure, water, and exhaust fans inside the catchment could interact badly with a portal. It is also simply too wet, too cold, and too unstable.

Before arrival, check your tetanus status. The place is full of sharp rust, rebar, and broken concrete. You will likely cut yourself at some point.

## 2. Clothing and personal protective equipment

The environment is just above freezing, saturated, windy, and loud. Water is everywhere. The cold is not weather; it comes out of the machinery and the water. The wetness is total. You will not stay dry by hoping to stay dry. You stay dry by wearing the right barriers and accepting that you will be damp at the edges.

### Base layers
- Merino wool or high-quality synthetic. No cotton. Cotton kills when wet.
- Long-sleeve top and full-length bottoms.
- Spare base layer in a sealed dry bag.

### Insulation
- Fleece or synthetic insulation, not down. Down loses almost all warmth when wet, and this place is a condensation machine.
- A thin insulating vest or jacket that can be worn under a shell.
- Warm, moisture-wicking socks. Bring at least two spare pairs, sealed in waterproof bags.

### Outer shell
- Waterproof, breathable jacket and trousers. They must be tough enough to resist tearing on sharp steel edges and rebar.
- High-visibility orange or yellow with reflective tape. Workers need to see you in the fog and between orange lamp islands.
- Wrist and ankle seals or cuffs that can be tightened to keep water out.
- A hood that can go over a hard hat or a separate waterproof hard hat cover.

### Footwear
- Insulated rubber or composite-toe boots with deep, non-slip tread. Steel toe can get painfully cold; composite is better.
- Waterproof, chemical-resistant. The water is full of salts, slag, and industrial residue.
- If you will go near the lower terraces or the stilling basin, consider insulated chest waders. But even with waders, do not enter the main channel. A killing current is not a wading opportunity.
- Gaiters or over-boots to seal the tops of the boots against splash and slush.
- Foot powder and spare dry socks. Trench foot is a real risk in constant wet cold.

### Hands
- Thin synthetic liner gloves for fine work.
- Insulated waterproof gloves over them.
- Heavy leather or rubber gauntlets for handling sharp or cold metal.
- Do not touch bare metal with bare skin. The railings are frost-coated and will stick. Treat every metal surface as if it wants to pull the heat out of you.

### Head and face
- Hard hat with a chin strap. The wind from exhaust shafts and fan reversals can knock things loose.
- A warm balaclava or insulating cap worn under the hard hat.
- A neck gaiter or face mask to protect against wind, drizzle, and cement dust.
- Anti-fog safety glasses or goggles. The fog and drizzle will otherwise coat your lenses constantly.
- Consider ski-type goggles for the worst wind and upward rain if you must be outside for long.

### Hearing protection
- Constant noise: pumps, sluice gates, hissing condensers, conveyor grinding, ventilation fans booming. It is loud enough to damage hearing over time.
- Use electronic ear defenders that allow speech and alarm frequencies through while cutting the damaging droning. Wear them from the moment you enter.
- Carry spare foam earplugs as backup. You may need to hear the pump note change, so do not go fully deaf unless you have a visual or radio warning.

### Fall protection
- Full-body harness, lanyard, and appropriate anchor points if you will be near open edges, catwalks, or the main channel. The terraces drop in steps and some edges are not clearly marked.
- Do not tie off to piping, electrical conduit, or old railings unless you have verified they can hold your weight.

### Respiratory protection
- At minimum, a P100 half-face respirator. The air contains cement dust, rust particles, condensation from industrial process, and possibly fine slag.
- If you go near exhaust shafts, the Reverse Weir, or any low area where gas may collect, carry a multi-gas detector for oxygen, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, and combustible gases. The Reverse Weir exists because gas pressure below is greater than air above; that means there is gas somewhere below.
- Do not assume the fog is just water vapor. It may be carrying industrial aerosols.

## 3. Water, food, and hydration

**Do not drink the water.** It is not for drinking. Not the channel water, not the condensation on pipes, not the slush, not the lamp-moss moisture. It is all process water. It has cooled something far below and carries salts, rust, and industrial residue.

- Bring all your own drinking water. Insulated bottles or bladders so it does not become too cold to drink.
- In a cold wet place, you may feel less thirsty, but you are still losing water through breath and sweat under layers. Drink regularly.
- Electrolyte tablets or powder are useful.
- Bring high-calorie, non-perishable food that can be eaten with one hand while wearing gloves: energy bars, dried fruit, nuts, chocolate, sealed sandwiches.
- Avoid anything that requires open flame. The place may have combustible gases or dust; do not use a camp stove unless you have verified a safe, ventilated area far from equipment and with gas monitoring.
- Bring all food in sealed containers. The dust and fog will spoil anything exposed.
- If you need to use a toilet, bring wag bags or a portable container. There is no natural soil to bury waste, and you must not contaminate the catchment.

## 4. Light and navigation

The most important local rule: **light is property. You may walk only where a lamp is mounted.**  
The dark intervals between the orange sodium lamps are not safe. There are open channels, sharp drops, unstable surfaces, and possibly things in the water. Even if you bring a powerful flashlight, you should not interpret it as permission to walk into unlit zones. Your personal light is for finding your way within lit areas, for signaling, and for emergencies. It does not turn the dark into territory you belong in.

Bring:

- A headlamp, ideally intrinsically safe, with a red or amber mode.
- A handheld spotlight with a tight beam for signaling and for seeing across the terraces if needed.
- Chemical light sticks for backup and for marking your position.
- Spare batteries kept in a warm inner pocket. Cold reduces battery performance.
- A flashlight that is waterproof and impact-resistant. The moisture will try to kill your electronics.

Navigation:

- GPS will likely not work well down inside a steel and concrete bowl under fog. Do not rely on it.
- Bring a waterproof map of the current layout. The place changes as new walls and terraces are added.
- A compass may be unreliable due to all the steel and electrical equipment. Treat it as a rough tool only.
- Use fixed landmarks: the pump hall at the north end, the deep sump at the south end, the red beacons around the settling basin, the gantry road at the east wall, the gantry crane moving through the mist.
- The noise has geography. Near the channel you hear water; near the pump hall you hear engine; in the empty terraces you hear echo. Use that.
- Lay a tether line from lamp post to lamp post if you must move through any poorly lit section. Tie it off and keep it taut. Do not walk beyond it.
- Mark your route with reflective tape or chemical lights so you can find your way back. The fog eats light and distances become uncertain quickly.

## 5. Communication and signaling

Radio signals may not penetrate the concrete, steel, and moisture reliably. The place is a sunken fortress.

- Use two-way radios with headsets, but test them thoroughly before descending. You may need a relay on the upper terrace or wired communication for the lower terraces.
- Agree on a strict check-in schedule, such as every 10 or 15 minutes. If you miss a check-in, the upper terrace must initiate a search.
- Carry a whistle. The noise level may make it hard to hear, but a whistle cuts through some of the ambient sound.
- Carry an air horn or personal alarm.
- Carry a strobe light for visual signaling. In fog, a strobe may be visible from a short distance even if radios fail.
- If you have a satellite messenger or personal locator beacon, keep it on you, but understand it may not transmit from the deepest terraces. Use it at the rim or upper terraces if you can.

Have one person on the upper terrace or rim who can see or hear enough of the catchment to act as a safety watch. They should know your route, your expected times, and the exact actions to take if you go silent.

## 6. Understanding the local rules and cycles

The workers have a few rules. Treat them as absolute, even if they seem odd. They are likely written in someone else’s blood.

### The six-hour water cycle
The water rises and falls on a roughly six-hour cycle, but the schedule drifts. The first sign is a change in the pump note, from a low thrum to a higher whine. Then the red lamps at the terrace corners blink twice. You then have about six minutes before the sluice gates open and the main channel becomes a killing current.

- If you see the red lamps blink twice, get out of the channel and away from low ledges immediately. Do not wait to finish what you are doing.
- The stilling basin drains by about a meter during this cycle, leaving a ring of foam and grey slush. That is normal, but it means water levels can change rapidly and unpredictably.
- Learn the current cycle times before you go down. Ask the workers. Then treat the schedule as approximate.

### Mirror-smooth and silent basin
If the basin goes mirror-smooth and silent, leave the terrace. This is described as a rule for a reason. It may mean the pumps have stopped or something else has changed. Do not stay to observe. Move to higher ground, preferably a lit upper terrace.

### Fog rising instead of falling
If the fog begins to rise instead of fall, the big fans have reversed. Rain will come upward for a while. This is disorienting and wet. It can also be dangerous if you are on a catwalk or near an edge. Move to shelter, protect your face, and wait until the direction returns to normal. Your footing will be worse.

### The Reverse Weir
At the south end, water spills upward over a steel lip and falls onto the ceiling of a lower gallery. The sign reads DO NOT STAND UNDER WEIR. Do not stand under it, above it, or near it unless your escort tells you it is safe. The gas pressure below is greater than the air above. This is not a trick and it is not a place to satisfy curiosity. Stay back.

### The standing wave
Near the central channel there is a standing wave that never moves. It hums. The air vibrates near it. Do not put your hand near it. Do not approach it. Lamp-moss grows in a perfect circle around it. That circle is not an invitation. It is a warning.

### Light as property
Again: walk only where a lamp is mounted. The dark intervals are not safe. Even if you have a flashlight, do not enter the dark. The workers say the dark is unsafe because of open channels and the things that live in the sump. Take that literally.

## 7. Environmental and biological hazards

### Cold and wet
- The cold is worse as you go lower. The bottom terraces are the coldest and wettest.
- Frost, rime, and slush coat surfaces. Slush is grey and salty and does not freeze solid. It is slippery and contaminating.
- Hypothermia can start before you notice. Shivering, confusion, and loss of coordination are early signs. Buddy up and watch each other.
- If you get wet, especially from falling into water, treat it as an emergency. Get to a warm, dry place, remove wet clothing, and use dry layers, a thermal bivvy, and chemical heat packs. Warm drinks help but only if you are conscious and not shivering uncontrollably.

### Water
- The main channel is a discharge race, not a river. It is twenty feet wide, lined with welded plate, and moves fast. During sluice gate openings, it becomes a killing current. Do not enter.
- The deep sump at the south end is worse. There are blind eels, grey as old hose, and bones that are not quite bird or fish. The maintenance crews do not swim in the sump. Do not swim in the sump. Do not trail your hands or feet in the water.
- The amphipods in the stilling basin are harmless as far as anyone knows. They are the only animals seen in numbers. Do not touch the water anyway.

### Sharp edges and falling objects
- Concrete chunks, rebar, cable, and steel sheet pile are everywhere. The erosion is fresh and sharp. The place is still being formed by its own operation.
- Watch your head, especially near the gantry crane, exhaust shafts, and fan openings.
- Loose debris may fall from upper terraces or construction work.

### Dust and air
- Cement powder, rust, slag dust. Use your respirator.
- The fog may carry industrial aerosols. Avoid breathing it more than necessary.
- If you are near the Reverse Weir or any low, enclosed area, use a gas detector. Do not assume the air is safe.

### Biological unknowns
- Lamp-moss is the only soft thing in the catchment. It grows around warm lights and leaking flanges. It is not exactly plant. Do not touch it, do not eat it, do not step on it. It may be slippery and you do not know what it is.
- The eels in the sump are blind and pale. Bones on filter screens suggest something larger has lived or lives there. Treat the deep sump as hazardous to enter or approach.
- If you see any animal, especially a bird that has fallen from the rim, report it to the crew. Do not chase or handle it yourself. The workers used a drone to scoop up the last one. Let them handle it.
- Do not collect samples of anything without explicit permission. This is not a place for souvenirs.

## 8. Physical and psychological preparation

### Physical
You should be reasonably fit. You will be carrying 15–30 pounds of gear, walking on slippery, uneven concrete, descending and climbing terraces, and possibly using ladders or stairways. The cold and wet will make everything harder.

Good baseline:
- Able to walk several miles on rough ground without distress.
- Able to climb stairs while carrying a pack.
- No uncontrolled respiratory illness. The cold, damp air and dust will aggravate asthma or COPD.
- No severe circulatory problems. The cold will make them worse.

### Psychological
This place is not like a natural environment. There is no soil, no stone, no clay, no horizon. The fog never clears. The top is not visible. The only green is lamp-moss, and it is grey-green and not exactly plant.

The constant noise, darkness, and wetness can cause disorientation, anxiety, and a sense of being swallowed. You may feel claustrophobic even though the space is vast, because you can only see the small orange islands of light.

- Accept that you are inside a machine. Do not try to make it into a landscape in your head. That way, the place will make more sense and feel less hostile.
- Use the six-hour water cycle as a kind of heartbeat. The place breathes day and night. Learn to read it.
- Keep your mind on the immediate: the next lamp, the next handhold, the next check-in.
- If you feel overwhelmed, stop. Sit on a dry spot in a lit, safe area. Drink water. Eat something. Breathe slowly. Tell your buddy.
- Do not go alone. The echo in the empty terraces can make the dark seem inhabited. It messes with your ears and your nerves. Having another person keeps you anchored.
- If you start hearing or seeing things that do not fit—like the standing wave humming, or the fog moving the wrong way—do not panic. Follow the rules and move to safety. The place has its own weather and its own physics in places. You do not need to understand it. You need to respect it.

## 9. On arrival and first hour

1. **Check in with the site crew.** Show your permit. Confirm the current water cycle schedule.
2. **Do a gear check.** Test lights, radios, gas monitor, respirator seal, boots, gloves.
3. **Identify the evacuation route.** Know where the upper terraces, gantry road, and rim access are.
4. **Observe one full water cycle from a safe upper terrace** if time permits. Watch how the red lamps blink, listen to the pump note change, see how the fog moves. This will teach you more than any briefing.
5. **Map the lit areas.** Identify the nearest lamp islands, red beacons, and potential dark intervals.
6. **Agree on a communication protocol.** Test radio with the safety watch.
7. **Descend slowly.** Do not rush. The terraces are large and the fog hides scale. Stay in the lit zones.

If at any point the red lamps blink twice, move to high ground. If the basin goes mirror-smooth and silent, leave the terrace. If the fog rises, prepare for upward rain. If you see the standing wave or the Reverse Weir, keep your distance unless you have an escort who says otherwise.

---

## 10. Exit and decontamination

When you leave, you will bring the place with you: cement dust, rust, slag, industrial water, lamp-moss spores if you got too close.

- Decontaminate boots and outer gear before entering any clean area. Use water and brushes outside the catchment if possible. Do not wash the water back into the catchment unnecessarily; follow local cleanup procedures.
- Change out of contaminated clothing and bag it separately.
- Wash your hands and face thoroughly before eating or drinking.
- Check yourself for cuts and clean them immediately, even minor ones. The rust and industrial muck can cause nasty infections.
- Do not bring any samples, rocks, metal pieces, or biological material with you unless cleared by the crew and local authorities.
- After leaving, monitor your health for a few days. Respiratory irritation, fever, or unusual tiredness should be evaluated by a doctor. Tell them where you were, even if they will not understand the name.

## Final intuition

Go into the Sump expecting it to be more like the inside of a running engine than a place on a map. It is cold, wet, loud, dark, and still being built. It breathes on its own schedule. The water is not your friend. The dark is not empty. The rules are there because the place has already taught someone a lesson.

If you go, go with an escort, stay in the light, listen to the pumps, and do not try to see everything. Some parts of the catchment are not meant to be looked at closely. They are meant to be left alone.
