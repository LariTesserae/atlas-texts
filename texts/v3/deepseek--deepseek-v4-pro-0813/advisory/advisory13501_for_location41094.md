Treat the Unfinished Range as a live industrial site with no safety supervisor, no shelter, and no rescue. It is not a wilderness in the ordinary sense. It is a cold, wet, actively degassing basaltic highland still being assembled. An ordinary human can survive there only with thorough preparation, deliberate movement, and a healthy fear of the ground, the air, and the water.

The following is a minimum set of preparations, covering altitude, volcanic gases, unstable terrain, water, shelter, navigation, medical care, and psychological endurance.

---

## 1. Understand the environment before you pack

The Unfinished Range combines four overlapping hazard sets:

- **High-altitude hypoxia** — continuous low oxygen, not just at summits.
- **Cold-water exposure** — temperatures rarely far below freezing, but always damp, with constant fog, weeping rock, and wet snow.
- **Volcanic gas and thermal hazards** — sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, hot ground, steam vents, and acidic water.
- **Unstable, raw terrain** — sharp talus, glassy cliffs, fresh lava, fault scarps, rockfall, and thin crusts over heat.

Everything you bring must serve one of these four problems. If a piece of gear does not, leave it behind.

## 2. Physical and medical preparation

### Acclimatization

Assume the effective altitude is at least 4,000–5,000 meters equivalent. You will feel the low oxygen in every step, on every mild slope, even while resting.

- Spend 5–7 days at altitude before entering, or at minimum take a full rest day at the portal site if it deposits you high.
- Ascend no more than 300–500 sleeping meters per day once moving.
- Carry **acetazolamide (Diamox)** 125 mg tablets, start 1–2 days before ascent, continue for 2–3 days, and carry enough for the whole trip.
- Carry **dexamethasone** for emergency treatment of high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE) or pulmonary edema (HAPE). Know the dosages and have written instructions.
- Use a **pulse oximeter** daily. If resting SpO₂ falls below 80% and does not improve with rest, descend.
- There is no medical oxygen infrastructure. A small emergency oxygen bottle (2–4 hours) is optional but heavy. Better to descend than rely on it.

### Physical conditioning

Train for:

- Carrying a 20–25 kg pack over steep, loose, wet rock for 6–10 hours per day.
- Stepping on unstable, sharp surfaces without twisting ankles.
- Cold, damp conditions that sap morale.
- Long periods of slow, methodical movement with no rhythmic pacing because the ground never allows it.

Practice on Earth in places like Iceland’s highlands, Kamchatka, the Andes, or New Zealand’s Tongariro. If you cannot handle a full day of cold rain on basalt with no dry place to sit, you are not ready.

### Medical kit — beyond the usual

Because there is no rescue, your kit must handle trauma, burns, respiratory irritation, and altitude illness.

- **Trauma:** tourniquet, hemostatic dressing, pressure bandage, chest seal, SAM splint, elastic wraps, adhesive tape, blister kit, wound irrigation syringe.
- **Burns:** burn gel, non-adherent dressings, gauze. The ground is hot in places, and steam vents can scald through clothing.
- **Respiratory:** albuterol inhaler if you have any history of bronchial irritation. Volcanic gases can cause airway spasms even in healthy people. Carry a small supply if your medical provider agrees.
- **Eyes:** sterile eye wash, eye cup, protective goggles. Acidic steam and sulfur dioxide can irritate eyes severely.
- **Pain and infection:** NSAIDs, acetaminophen, broad-spectrum antibiotics, oral rehydration salts, anti-diarrheals, antihistamines.
- **Altitude:** as above, plus a written protocol for HAPE/HACE symptoms: confusion, ataxia, cough with pink froth, severe headache not responding to painkillers. Descent is the only reliable treatment.
- **Personal medications:** extra supply, waterproofed.

## 3. Clothing system

The goal is to stay dry from the outside while managing sweat from the inside. The air is wet, the rock is wet, and every surface is coated with a film of water.

### Layers

- **Base layer:** merino wool or synthetic, no cotton. Two sets — one for moving, one dry for sleeping.
- **Mid layer:** lightweight fleece or active insulation.
- **Insulation for stops:** synthetic insulated jacket, because down may lose loft in constant humidity. If you bring down, keep it in a dry bag and use only in the tent.
- **Hard shell:** waterproof/breathable jacket and trousers with taped seams. The constant mist and wet rock will wet out most breathable fabrics after a few hours. Choose a durable 3-layer fabric with mechanical venting (pit zips, thigh zips).
- **A vapor barrier layer** can be useful as an emergency layer to prevent sweat from soaking insulation, but it is uncomfortable and not for everyone.

### Head, hands, feet

- **Head:** wool or fleece beanie, neck gaiter or balaclava, and a hood on the shell. A wide-brimmed hat is useless in fog and wind.
- **Face protection from acidic steam:** a thin gaiter or respirator-compatible half-mask. If you are downwind of a vent, you need a respirator, not just a cloth.
- **Gloves:** three pairs —
  1. Thin liner gloves for dexterity.
  2. Insulated waterproof gloves for cold wet rock.
  3. Heavy mittens for camp or emergencies.
- **Boots:** full-grain leather mountaineering boots with a stiff shank. Synthetic uppers may melt near hot ground. The soles must be stiff enough for sharp talus. Bring **microspikes or strap-on crampons** for wet, icy rock and snow patches.
- **Gaiters:** knee-high, waterproof, abrasion-resistant. Sharp rock will shred thin gaiters.

### Eye protection

The light is flat and dim, which reduces contrast and makes every edge look soft. You need:

- Clear or yellow-lensed safety glasses or goggles for walking in fog.
- Anti-fog treatment.
- Sunglasses are less important due to lack of strong sun, but carry a pair with light-adjusting lenses for any brighter moments.
- If you are near bird colonies, eye protection also protects against flying insects and accidental beak strikes.

## 4. Shelter and sleep

There is no soil for tent stakes. The ground is raw stone, often wet, sometimes hot, and always sharp.

### Tent

- Use a **freestanding 4-season tent** with strong, flexible poles. Geodesic or semi-geodesic designs resist wind and snow load.
- Bring a heavy-duty footprint or two layers of Tyvek. The floor will be punctured otherwise.
- Anchor with **rocks** — no stakes will hold. Carry several lightweight webbing loops or cordelettes to sling large stones.
- A single-wall tent saves weight but increases condensation. In this environment, condensation is unavoidable; a double-wall tent gives you a drier inner.
- A **bivy sack** is a backup, not a primary shelter. The constant wet will make bivying miserable, but if the tent fails, a bivy with a synthetic bag can keep you alive.

### Sleeping bag and pad

- Sleeping bag rated to **-10°C or below**, synthetic or water-resistant down. The air may hover near freezing, but the damp penetrates.
- Sleeping pad with **R-value 5 or higher**. The ground can be cold and wet, but in some places it can be warm. A closed-cell foam pad under an inflatable pad protects from punctures and heat transfer both ways.
- A **vapor barrier liner** or emergency bivy inside the sleeping bag prevents body moisture from wetting insulation.

### Camp site selection

This is critical.

- **Do not camp in depressions, bowls, or gullies.** Carbon dioxide is heavier than air and will pool in low areas. You may not smell it.
- **Do not camp near steam vents**, even if the warmth is tempting. Gases, heat, and thin crusts make them death traps.
- **Avoid cliff bases** where rockfall may occur. The Range is geologically young, and slabs let go without warning.
- **Avoid bird colony ledges overhead** — droppings are acidic and slippery, and the birds may dislodge rocks.
- Choose a flat rock bench or ridge saddle above any hollow. Check the ground temperature with an infrared thermometer; if it feels warm underfoot, move.
- Clear loose sharp stones from the tent area, or lay your foam pad outside to kneel on.

## 5. Water and food

### Water

All surface water is suspect. It may be acidic, mineralized, or contaminated by volcanic gases.

- **Do not drink untreated water from streams, pools, or seeps.**
- Carry **2–4 liters** of water from a known clean source — ideally water you brought through the portal, or water purified at camp.
- For longer trips, collect **rain or condensation** on a clean tarp or poncho. Cloud water may still be acidic, but it is better than mineralized hot-spring water.
- Test water with **pH strips**. If pH is below 5, do not drink it. If between 5 and 6, you may neutralize it with a small amount of baking soda, then filter.
- Use a **water filter** with activated carbon to remove some metals and organic contaminants. A 0.1-micron filter removes bacteria and protozoa, but not dissolved heavy metals or acids.
- For best safety, **distill** water if possible. A simple still or boiling water and condensing steam is fuel-intensive but removes most dissolved solids. This is the only reliable way to make mineralized water safe.
- Add **electrolyte tablets** to your drinking water. You will lose salts through sweat and respiratory moisture.
- Do not collect water from warm or milky pools near vents. They may contain high levels of sulfur, arsenic, or other toxins.

### Food

- Bring high-calorie, high-fat, no-cook food as your mainstay: energy bars, nuts, chocolate, dried meat, cheese, seeds.
- Hot meals are morale. Freeze-dried meals are light and only need boiling water, but require fuel.
- Eat frequently. Altitude suppresses appetite; force yourself to eat small amounts every hour.
- Avoid alcohol. It worsens dehydration and altitude illness.
- Bring **extra food for 2–3 days** in case you are trapped by weather or injury.

### Stove and fuel

- Use a **liquid-fuel stove** (white gas) rather than canister gas, as canisters lose pressure in cold and are less reliable in wind.
- Carry a windscreen and a heat exchanger pot.
- Fuel enough for boiling at least 4–5 liters of water per day, plus extra for sterilizing or melting snow if needed.
- Do not use a stove inside a tent without ventilation. Carbon monoxide is odorless and deadly, and the tent may already be in an oxygen-poor spot.

## 6. Volcanic gas and thermal hazard protocols

This is the most unusual and deadly part of the Range. The air itself can kill you.

### Gas monitoring

- Carry a **4-gas monitor** that detects oxygen (O₂), hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), sulfur dioxide (SO₂), and carbon monoxide (CO), plus ideally carbon dioxide (CO₂). Some monitors include a CO₂ sensor.
- Wear it on the outside of your pack at breathing level.
- Check it before entering any gully, depression, or area with steam.
- If O₂ drops below 19.5%, or H₂S exceeds 10 ppm, or SO₂ exceeds 2 ppm, **retreat upwind and to higher ground**. Do not wait for symptoms.
- **Do not trust your nose.** H₂S deadens your sense of smell at high concentrations. You may think the smell has gone, but you are being poisoned.

### Respirator

- Carry a **half-face respirator** with acid-gas cartridges and P100 particulate filters. This protects against SO₂, H₂S, and acidic aerosols, but **not** against low oxygen or CO₂.
- Use it whenever you are within 50–100 meters of a vent or when the gas monitor alarms.
- Keep filters sealed in bags until use, as the damp air can degrade them.

### Thermal hazards

- In places the ground is still hot. Fresh lava benches may look solid but be thin crust over molten or very hot rock.
- Use an **infrared thermometer** or scan visually: if the surface is steaming, stained with yellow, orange, or white mineral deposits, or free of all vegetation, treat it as hot until proven otherwise.
- Probe the ground ahead with a trekking pole or throwing a small rock. If it makes a hollow sound or sizzles on contact with wet rock, do not step there.
- Steam vents may be hidden by fog. The air near them can be superheated. Never put your face or hands over a vent.
- If you must cross a thermally active area, move quickly but deliberately, keeping to unweathered rock and following the coolest route.

### Static electricity

Near vents, the air can carry a static charge. You may feel a faint pull on an upraised hand, see a blue glimmer along wet cliff edges, or smell ozone.

- If you feel hair standing on end, hear crackling, or see a glow, **crouch low immediately**, keep your feet together, and move away from the vent.
- Avoid raising metal trekking poles, ice axes, or other conductive objects near vent plumes.
- Remove metal jewelry and avoid wearing crampons in such areas.
- Do not take shelter under overhangs where water films and mineral stains create conductive paths.

## 7. Navigation and communication

### Navigation

There are no trails, no buildings, and the light is flat and deceptive.

- **GPS is likely unavailable** if the multiverse has no satellite network. Do not rely on it.
- Carry a **magnetic compass**, but be aware that basalt and other volcanic rocks can have strong local magnetic anomalies. Take bearings from a distance, use back bearings, and cross-check with terrain.
- A **barometric altimeter** is useful, but steam vents and pressure changes can affect it. Calibrate frequently if you have known elevation points.
- Use **dead reckoning and terrain association**. The landscape is jagged and distinctive, but fog can reduce visibility to meters.
- Mark your route with **removable flagging tape** or small cairns, but remove all markers on your return. The landscape is raw and should be left as you found it.
- Carry a **laminated map of your planned route** if you have any data, and a small notebook to sketch features.
- In fog, a **handheld compass with sighting mirror** and a **whistle** can help keep a group together.

### Communication

- There is no cell service and likely no satellite service.
- If your multiverse agency provides a **portal-linked communication device** or emergency recall beacon, carry it on your body, not in the pack.
- Consider a **personal locator beacon** only if it works in the target universe; otherwise, it is dead weight.
- A **VHF radio** with pre-arranged schedules to a base outside the portal may be the only way to call for help. Test range and battery life in similar terrain before entering.
- Leave a **detailed route plan and expected return time** with someone at the portal or your multiverse agency. If you do not return by a set time, they can send someone after you.

## 8. Daily movement rules

Move as if every surface is trying to hurt you, because it is.

- **Start slow.** The first hour is for warming up and assessing the ground.
- **Test every step** in thermally active or loose terrain. Use trekking poles to probe ahead.
- **Keep your pack weight centered** and avoid overbalancing on sharp, unstable talus.
- **Cross streams carefully.** The streams are young, straight, fast, and cold. They may have hidden depths. Unbuckle your pack sternum strap and waist belt before crossing; if you fall, you can shed the pack. Face upstream and use a pole.
- **Avoid traveling below cliffs or ledges with bird colonies.** Falling guano and dislodged rocks are real hazards.
- **Rest frequently, but not in hollows or near vents.** Choose rest spots on high, open rock with good visibility and fresh air.
- **Keep your gas monitor on at all times**, not just near obvious vents.
- **Hydrate and eat every hour**, even if you are not thirsty or hungry.
- **If you become confused, dizzy, or short of breath at rest, stop and assess.** It could be altitude, gas, or exhaustion. Do not push through.

## 9. Camp and night protocols

Night in the Unfinished Range is not truly dark — the dim, diffuse light persists — but the world becomes more dangerous.

- **Set up camp early**, at least two hours before you plan to stop, to allow for finding a safe site.
- **Do not cook or eat in the tent.** Food smells may attract the soft-winged owl-like predator. Store food in odor-proof bags, double-bagged, away from the tent.
- **Keep a headlamp with fresh batteries** on your person, and a backup light.
- **Use a red light** to preserve night vision and avoid attracting insects.
- **Keep your boots and essential gear inside the tent**, but in a dry bag to prevent condensation.
- **If you hear rockfall, do not rush outside.** Wait until it stops, then check the area with a light before exiting.
- **If you hear the owl-like predator’s low repeated note, stay quiet and keep your light steady.** Do not approach it. It is likely more curious than dangerous, but it is an unknown species.
- **In the morning, check your tent for punctures from sharp stones** and repair with tape before moving.

## 10. Emergency and self-rescue

Assume no one is coming quickly. You must be able to treat yourself and walk out.

- **If you are injured:** stop, secure the scene, treat the injury, and only then decide to move. A twisted ankle on sharp talus can become a death sentence if you continue.
- **If you suspect gas poisoning:** move upwind and to higher ground immediately. Remove contaminated clothing. If the victim is unconscious and breathing, drag them to fresh air. Do not enter a gas-filled hollow to rescue someone without a respirator and a plan — you will die too.
- **If you develop altitude illness:** descend. If you cannot descend, use dexamethasone and oxygen, but know that descent is the only real treatment.
- **If you fall into a cold or hot pool:** get out as fast as possible. For cold water, strip wet clothes, dry off, and get into a sleeping bag with a warm companion or hot water bottles. For hot or acidic water, flush with clean water if available, but do not delay in seeking medical help. Pack burns in dry sterile dressings.
- **If you are lost in fog:** stop, put on warm clothes, drink water, eat, and wait for visibility. Moving in whiteout on this terrain is a reliable way to break a leg or walk into a vent.

## 11. Ethics and decontamination

The Unfinished Range is not a place to leave human traces.

- **Pack out absolutely everything**, including human waste. The ground is too raw for catholes; carry a waste bag system and seal it well.
- **Do not disturb bacterial mats, fly larvae, bird colonies, or grazers.** The life here depends on a delicate chain of sulfur and iron. A single footprint in a warm spring can destroy a mat that took years to form.
- **Do not collect rocks, minerals, or biological samples** without a compelling scientific reason and the appropriate permits from your multiverse agency.
- **Before returning through the portal, decontaminate your boots and gear.** Scrub off mud, organic matter, and mineral deposits on a tarp, using a 10% bleach solution or quaternary ammonium if available. This prevents cross-universe contamination.
- **Seal all waste and contaminated materials** in designated bags and bring them back through the portal for disposal on your home world, if safely possible.

## Final note

The Unfinished Range will not welcome you. It will not notice you. It is too busy being born. Every cliff is still sharp, every vent is still breathing, every stream is still cutting its first path. You are an intruder in a place that has not yet decided what it will become.

Prepare as if you are walking through the inside of an engine: watch the heat, watch the gas, watch the falling metal, and keep your exit always in mind. If you do, you may return with a rare privilege — seeing a world in the act of creation. If you do not, the Range will not even pause to remember you.
