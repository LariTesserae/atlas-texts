# Preparation Brief: Visiting the Drowned Range

You are not preparing for a simple underwater excursion. You are preparing to enter a drowned mountain range that is also, in a very real sense, a machine: a cold, static, deafening, continental-scale system that has been running in equilibrium for millions of years. The water is near freezing, the pressure at the highest summits is immense, the sound never stops, and the emptiness between ridges is vast enough to swallow a lifetime.

Your body is the weakest part of this venture. Almost everything you bring must be designed to replace, protect, or insulate the fragile human inside.

---

## 1. Pressure and Life Support: You Are a Soft Creature in a Deep Place

The ridge you will stand on is roughly a thousand feet below the surface. That puts you under about **30 atmospheres of pressure** — roughly 450 pounds per square inch on every part of you. If you descend into the valleys between peaks, the pressure grows rapidly. The water is not air. Your lungs, sinuses, and gut are all designed for one atmosphere.

**Do not attempt this with ordinary scuba gear unless you are prepared for full saturation diving and many hours of decompression.** Even then, the cold and the sound will make everything harder and more dangerous.

The sane approach for an ordinary human is to use a **one-atmosphere hard suit** — an atmospheric diving suit (ADS) or a small one-person submersible that keeps its interior at surface pressure. This means:

- The suit or sub must be rated to at least **3,000 feet**, not just 1,000, because you will want margin if you slip off a ridge or want to descend into a valley even slightly.
- It must have redundant life support: CO₂ scrubbers, oxygen supply, temperature control, and a backup breathing system.
- If you use a hard suit, it must have fully articulated joints that can handle the cold without freezing solid. Many lubricants and seals become brittle at these temperatures.
- If you use a submersible, it should have at least one manipulator arm and a way to anchor to rock, because you will want to touch and collect things.

If you must make a short excursion in a flexible dry suit — for example, to enter a wreck or squeeze through a crack — it must be **heated**, fed by a surface-supplied or rebreather gas mix, and you must accept the decompression debt. At 1,000 feet, you are looking at helium-based breathing mixes to avoid oxygen toxicity and nitrogen narcosis. The cold will slow your body’s ability to off-gas inert gases. I cannot emphasize enough: **the hard suit is the sane path.**

Bring at least two independent CO₂ scrubbers, spare O₂ bottles, a full-face emergency mask, and an emergency bubble or ascent system if you are not in a hard suit.

## 2. Thermal Protection: The Cold Is a Physical Presence

The water is a few degrees above freezing at the bright upper reaches, and it drops toward zero in the deep gaps between peaks. This is not winter-lake cold. This is **geological cold** — the cold of water that has been deep for a long time and has no interest in warming you.

In this water, an unprotected human loses consciousness in minutes and dies shortly after. Hypothermia is not a distant threat; it is the default state of the environment.

Even inside a hard suit, you need:

- **Active heating**: electrically heated undergarments, recirculating warm-water tubes, or phase-change materials. Redundant heating loops are essential. If one fails, the other must keep you alive long enough to return to the habitat or surface.
- **Passive insulation**: closed-cell foam, aerogel, or other high-performance insulation around the life-support backpack and joints. The suit’s outer shell may be metal or composite, but the inside must be a cocoon.
- **Heated gloves and boots**. The rock is sharp and cold; touching it with unprotected skin or poorly insulated gloves will cause instant pain and then numbness. You need dexterity, but not at the cost of frostbite.
- **Battery management**. Cold batteries lose capacity quickly. Keep them in warmed compartments or use chemistries rated for extreme cold. Bring twice what you think you need.

Your suit’s external sensors, valves, and seals should all be rated for operation at 0°C and below. Many materials become brittle; many adhesives fail. Test everything in a cold chamber before you go.

## 3. Acoustic Protection: The Sound Will Try to Live in Your Body

This is the part most people underestimate. The Drowned Range has **no silence**. None. The sound is continuous, layered, and physical.

The layers are:

- The **roar of hydrothermal vents**: superheated mineral brine blasting into freezing water. This is loud enough to be physically painful up close. The water itself conducts the sound through the rock.
- The **mechanical drone of still-running geothermal engines**: deep, subsonic, rhythmic. It travels through the rock and into the water like a heartbeat. It never varies.
- The **songs of the leviathans**: enormous, bone-white filter feeders whose calls are more felt than heard. Their voices overlap into a standing chord that fills entire basins.

Together, this produces a noise that is not just a background hum. It is an acoustic architecture. Your jaw and chest will vibrate before you consciously hear it. Low-frequency sound can resonate with your lungs, your sinuses, and your gut. It can cause nausea, fatigue, disorientation, and a profound sense of dread.

You need:

- **Custom-molded earplugs** that attenuate broadband noise but still allow you to hear speech or alarms through a communication system.
- **Active noise-cancelling headphones or helmet inserts** tuned for low frequencies, because passive earplugs alone will not stop infrasound from entering through your skull.
- **Vibration isolation**: your suit’s internal padding, your boots, and your helmet should be designed to decouple you from the vibration conducted through the rock and water. If you stand on a ridge, the engine drone will come up through your feet. You need damping there.
- **A soundproof sleep pod or “silence capsule”** if you plan to stay more than a few hours. Total silence may be impossible, but you can reduce the noise to a level where your nervous system can rest. Without this, the constant roar will eventually erode your ability to think.

Avoid getting too close to active vents or running engines. The vents are literally deafening. The engines’ near field can cause resonance in your body. Use passive sonar to find them and then keep your distance.

## 4. Navigation and Mobility: The Void Between Ridges Is Not for Swimming

The scale of this place is continental. Ridges run for thousands of miles. Between one ridge and the next, there are miles of open water — empty, cold, and roaring. In that open water, there are almost no fish, no landmarks, and no visual reference points. You could swim for a lifetime and never touch either peak.

You must bring:

- **A submersible or a thruster pack** with enough range to cross open water if you ever leave a ridge. Do not attempt to swim between ridges. The currents are fixed, but they can be strong, and the distance is deceptive.
- **Inertial navigation and a Doppler velocity log**. GPS does not work underwater. The water is clear enough that near the summits you may be able to see far, but in the open water and below the light-line, you need instruments.
- **Acoustic transponders** placed along your route. The terrain is static — it never changes — so you can pre-map and place beacons. But if you lose your navigation fix, you will be lost in a void.
- **Laser or optical communication** if line of sight exists. The water is extremely clear, so a laser link can work over surprising distances. However, the continuous sound may interfere with acoustic modems; use coded, low-frequency acoustic signals if you must use sound.
- **A physical tether** to your habitat or support vessel if you are walking on the ridge. The rock is sharp, and a fall into deeper water could happen quickly.

If you walk on the ridge itself, you will find the rock is fresh and sharp — clean planes of dark basalt and glittering gabbro, broken by vertical fault scars with slick, glassy surfaces. Wear boots with deep tread and use crampons or spiked soles. The smooth fault planes are like polished ice, but harder and colder. Use an ice axe-like tool or a rock pick to steady yourself.

The fixed currents can be used as highways once you map them. They pour over passes and spiral down cliffs. They are predictable, but never trust them without testing. A current that has been stable for a million years is still a current.

## 5. Shelter and Power: You Cannot Live Off This Land

There is no vegetation. No kelp, no plankton blooms, nothing green. The base of the food web is chemosynthetic bacteria around the vents. You cannot eat anything here without risking toxicity, and there is almost nothing to eat anyway. Bring all your food. Bring all your water, or bring a desalinator rated for near-freezing seawater.

If you plan to stay for more than a few hours, you need a **habitat** — a pressure-resistant, heated, soundproofed module anchored to the rock. Anchoring is difficult because there is no sediment or mud. The basalt is young and hard. Use rock bolts, friction clamps, or gravity anchors. Avoid fault zones; the rock is still cooling and contracting, and you may hear sudden, sharp cracks. A falling slab would crush you.

Power is a challenge. Solar is nearly useless at 1,000 feet. A surface buoy with a cable is possible, but the cable must survive currents and depth. A small nuclear or radioisotope power system is ideal, but if you cannot bring that, use high-capacity batteries or fuel cells. The geothermal heat could theoretically be tapped near active vents, but the water is superheated and chemically aggressive; it is not worth the risk.

Desalination, air circulation, CO₂ scrubbing, and heating all require continuous power. Bring redundancies. If your power fails, you have only as long as your thermal mass and backup O₂ last.

## 6. Biological and Geological Hazards: The Machine Is Alive

The Drowned Range is not empty. It is, in places, abundant with life — but the life is strange, and some of it is hazardous.

- **Hydrothermal vents**: The superheated brine can be 350°C or more. It is loaded with hydrogen sulfide, heavy metals, and minerals. Even a brief suit breach near a vent can be fatal. Keep your distance. Carry gas sensors for H₂S and CO₂, and monitor temperature. If you see white or black smokers, you are already close enough.

- **Leviathans**: These are enormous, blind, singing filter feeders the size of small islands. They are probably not aggressive, but they are not aware of you. Their sheer size means their near-field currents can shove you off a ridge. Their songs are the loudest sound in the ocean. Do not approach one. Do not use active sonar near them. Do not attempt to touch or photograph them up close. They are part of the environment.

- **The tube worms and chemosynthetic animals**: They grow in straight rows along old pipeline trenches and over the dead machines. They are not green. They are grey, pale, papery. Some may have sharp mineral tubes. Treat them as you would any unknown organism: do not touch.

- **The still-running machines**: Some geothermal generators still operate. They are autonomous, have been running for millennia, and are now part of the thermal cycle of the mountains. They are not your friends. They may have sharp edges, hot surfaces, moving parts, and high-voltage or high-temperature components. They have become geological, but they are still machines. Treat them as active industrial hazards.

- **The rock itself**: Fresh basalt and gabbro are sharp enough to cut a suit. The fault scarps are smooth and glassy — slippery, cold, and fragile in places. The mountains are still cracking as they cool. Do not stand under overhanging rock. Do not anchor near obvious fault lines.

## 7. Psychological Preparation: The Place Will Not Notice You

This is the part no equipment list can solve. The Drowned Range is **vast, static, and indifferent**. It has no seasons, no storms, no change. The temperature gradient is fixed, the currents follow the same paths they have followed since the sea rose, and the sound is the same sound that has been there for millions of years.

You could leave and return after a thousand years and the same light would be falling on the same ridge in the same way.

That is a mind-breaking realization if you are not ready for it.

Prepare by:

- **Spending time in sensory deprivation and sensory overload, alternately.** The Drowned Range is both: empty and loud at the same time. Practice sitting in a room with a low continuous drone for hours. Practice being alone in a vast, featureless space.

- **Bringing a personal quiet space.** A soundproof sleep pod, a journal, music, a virtual window with images of Earth. You will need to retreat from the roar and the emptiness.

- **Setting small, achievable goals.** Do not try to map the whole range. Do not try to cross the open water on foot. Choose one ridge, one vent field, one dead machine. Witness it. Record it. Then leave.

- **Accepting your insignificance.** This place is not hostile; it is indifferent. It does not care if you live or die. Your job is to be a careful, respectful visitor — not a conqueror, not a savior. You cannot change it. You cannot fix it. You can only see it.

The light-line — the visible boundary between the bright upper water and the dark deep — will feel like crossing from one world into another. Above it, blue daylight. Below it, the song world, dark and roaring. Be ready for that transition. It is not just a change in light; it is a change in your own nervous system.

## 8. Emergency Protocols: Hope Is Not a Plan

Rehearse these before you go:

- **Suit breach at depth**: If your hard suit loses pressure at 30 atmospheres, you have seconds to react. Have an emergency ascent system, a rescue sub nearby, and a buddy. Never go alone into deep water.

- **Thermal failure**: If your heating loop dies, you have minutes to get back to the habitat or surface. Carry chemical heat packs and an emergency thermal blanket inside the suit.

- **Acoustic trauma**: If the sound suddenly spikes — for example, a leviathan calls directly overhead or a vent erupts — protect your ears and move away. Low-frequency sound can cause nausea and disorientation before you realize it.

- **Current drag**: If a fixed current catches you, do not fight it. Deploy a drogue or anchor, swim perpendicular to the current, and use your tether.

- **Getting lost in open water**: If you lose your navigation fix between ridges, stay put and transmit an emergency signal. The water is clear, but the ridges may be out of sight. Swimming in a random direction is a death sentence.

- **Vent gas exposure**: If your H₂S sensor alarms, move away perpendicular to the current. Hydrogen sulfide can kill at low concentrations and can be present even without visible smokers.

- **Leviathan close encounter**: If a leviathan passes near you, do not thrash. Stay still, reduce your power, and let its current move you if it must. It is blind and probably unaware of you, but it is the size of an island.

## 9. Packing List for an Ordinary Human Multiverse Traveler

Here is a quick checklist, assuming you have access to advanced but not magical technology:

### Life Support
- One-atmosphere hard suit or one-person submersible rated to 3,000+ feet
- Redundant CO₂ scrubbers and O₂ supply
- Emergency rebreather mask and bailout gas
- Full-face mask for any flexible-suit excursions
- Spare seals, O-rings, and tool kit

### Thermal
- Heated undergarment or recirculating warm-water suit
- Heated gloves and boots
- Phase-change cooling/warming packs
- Spare batteries in heated compartments
- Chemical heat packs for emergencies

### Acoustic
- Custom earplugs
- Active noise-cancelling helmet insert
- Vibration-damped boots and suit padding
- Soundproof sleep pod
- Noise dosimeter and low-frequency SPL meter

### Navigation & Communication
- Inertial navigation unit
- Doppler velocity log
- Acoustic transponders and beacons
- Laser or optical communication link
- Acoustic modem with coded low-frequency backup
- Physical tether and anchor points
- Sonar (passive first, active only if necessary)

### Tools & Safety
- Rock bolts, drill, and anchor equipment
- Crampons or spiked boots
- Rock pick or ice axe
- Cutting tool (line cutter, shears)
- H₂S, O₂, CO₂, CO, and temperature sensors
- First aid kit, including thermal and pressure-related supplies
- Emergency beacon and locator, both acoustic and radio for surface
- Flashlights and headlamps with red/low-intensity modes
- Camera or recording equipment if desired, with pressure housing

### Habitation & Supplies
- Pressure-resistant habitat module
- Power source: RTG, fuel cell, or large battery bank
- Desalinator rated for near-freezing water
- Freeze-dried food, high-calorie
- Water storage
- Sleep system, hygiene, waste management
- Clock, journal, artificial light with day/night cycle
- Virtual window or recorded images of Earth
- Personal items for sanity

## 10. Final Intuitive Note

Visiting this place will feel like being inside a drowned cathedral built by geology and abandoned industry, where the organ never stops playing. The light will be blue and clean on the high ridges, and black as a closed room below the light-line. The cold will slow your thoughts. The sound will become a part of your body, a vibration in your chest that you will carry with you long after you leave.

You will be a tiny, temporary, soft thing in a world that has achieved perfect equilibrium. It will not notice you. It will not remember you. That is not a reason to stay away; it is a reason to go carefully, with humility and very good equipment.

Understand that you are not going to the sea. You are going into the sea, into a drowned mountain range that is also a machine, a choir, and a silence that is pure roar. Prepare for all of it. And when you return, you will remember the silence we have here — the real silence, with rests and pauses — as a kind of luxury you never noticed before.
