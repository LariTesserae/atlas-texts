Before anything else, understand that this is not a campsite. It is a small, high, geologically active bowl with an archaeological-ceremonial core and at least seven subtle physical anomalies. You will be a guest in a place that is simultaneously fragile and hostile. Prepare for altitude, intense light, unstable ice, total sterility, and a silence that gets into your head.

---

## 1. Physical conditioning and altitude

The sky is a deep, lacquered blue and the air is thin. You will breathe harder even standing still. If your body is not acclimatized, the basin will feel like a place at the top of the world, because in practical terms it is.

- Spend at least **10–14 days** before arrival acclimatizing to similar altitude, ideally sleeping at 3,000–4,000 meters and doing day walks higher.
- Train for slow, steady uphill movement with a loaded pack. This is not about speed; it is about being able to keep moving while hypoxic.
- Learn the symptoms of acute mountain sickness, HAPE, and HACE. Bring **acetazolamide** if your doctor approves, and a **pulse oximeter**.
- If your resting oxygen saturation drops below about 80–85% and does not improve with rest, descend. Do not wait.
- The cold and altitude together will make simple tasks—tying boots, opening a pack, writing notes—surprisingly hard. Prepare for that.

---

## 2. Sun, light, and eye protection

The brightness here has a kind of physical pressure. It is not just bright; it is abrasive. The ground is covered in needle ice that reflects light upward, so you will burn in places you forgot existed.

- Wear **Category 4 glacier sunglasses with side shields** at all times.
- Carry **mountaineering goggles** as backup. The air holds ice dust and fine mineral grit, and wind can whip it into your eyes.
- Use **high-SPF sunscreen**, lip balm, and a full face covering. The tears on your cheeks will freeze almost immediately; goggles help prevent that.
- A balaclava or buff over your nose and mouth will pre-warm the air and protect your skin.
- Do not underestimate reflected light from the ice. The underside of your nose, your chin, and your earlobes are vulnerable.

---

## 3. Clothing and footwear

The cold is not the deep cold of winter; it is the daily cold of altitude. But it is persistent, and it will intensify the moment the sun leaves the basin.

### Clothing layers
- **Base layer:** merino wool or synthetic. No cotton.
- **Mid layer:** lightweight fleece or active insulation.
- **Insulated layer:** a hooded down or synthetic jacket.
- **Outer shell:** windproof and water-resistant. The basin is mostly dry, but meltwater and ice dust will find their way in.
- **Pants:** softshell or hardshell, with reinforced knees if possible.
- **Gaiters:** essential. The needle ice is sharp, and the grit under it is fine and abrasive.

### Footwear
- **Insulated mountaineering boots** with a stiff sole.
- They must be compatible with **microspikes or lightweight crampons**.
- Bring **two pairs of socks**, and keep one pair dry inside your pack.

### Gloves
- Thin liner gloves for fine work.
- Insulated gloves for general use.
- Thick mittens for waiting and early-morning periods.

Your hands will lose dexterity quickly in the cold. Keep spare gloves in an inner pocket so they are warm when you need them.

---

## 4. Shelter, water, and food

### Shelter
Do not camp inside the basin. The three low stone shelters are archaeological remains, not guest rooms. They are small, fragile, and part of the place’s meaning. Camp below the col or on durable rock outside the basin.

- Bring a **four-season tent or bivy sack**.
- Use a sleeping bag rated at least **-10°C / 14°F comfort**, preferably lower.
- Use **two sleeping pads**: a closed-cell foam pad and an insulated inflatable pad. The ground is ice and stone, and it will drain heat from your body.
- The basin floor is active. Do not leave your tent or gear on needle ice; it can melt, collapse, and refreeze around your equipment.

### Water
Water here is not a reliable presence; it is a habit. The seep may run for only an hour at midday. Hollows hold only spoonfuls. The needle ice can be melted, but it uses fuel and time.

- Carry in **all the water you need** for drinking and cooking.
- Treat the seep or melted ice as a supplement, not a primary source.
- **Filter or boil** all natural water. The glacial flour and mineral grit may upset your stomach even if the water is chemically clean.
- Use **insulated wide-mouth bottles**. Hydration bladders freeze.
- Do not drink from the cupmarks. They are not water sources.

### Food
Bring high-calorie, simple food that requires minimal preparation. Hot drinks help more than you expect. You will not find wood, so bring a **stove and enough fuel**.

---

## 5. Terrain, movement, and rockfall

Walk as if the ground is made of glass tubes, because in a sense it is. The needle ice can be firm one moment and collapse into wet mush the next.

- Use **trekking poles** to test the surface before putting full weight down.
- Move slowly. The basin is small, but speed is not your friend.
- The ground under the ice is sharp sand and glacial flour. It is unstable and abrasive.
- A spot that was solid in the morning may be slush by noon and ice by dusk.
- Do not leave gear directly on the needle ice for long. It can sink and freeze in place.

### Rockfall
The fluted walls are actively shedding stone. Freeze-thaw cycles pry flakes loose.

- Wear a **helmet** when near the walls, especially during thaw or after sunset when the rock contracts.
- Do not linger under the east wall when the sun first hits it.
- Listen for the sharp clack of falling stone. It takes a long time to fade.

### The fourth side
The fourth side opens onto a fall of blue air. It is not a gentle slope; it is a drop. Do not approach the edge casually, and if visibility is poor, stay well back.

## 6. Medical kit

In addition to standard first aid, carry:

- **Acetazolamide** for altitude prophylaxis.
- **Dexamethasone** for severe altitude illness, if prescribed.
- **Anti-nausea medication** and **pain relievers**.
- **Pulse oximeter**.
- **Blister care**: the grit will work into your boots; moleskin and leukotape are invaluable.
- **Antiseptic wipes and bandages** for cuts from ice or rock. The ice may look clean, but the ground is not sterile.
- **Eye drops and saline nasal spray**: the air is extremely dry.
- **Hand and foot warmers**.
- **Emergency bivy bag**.
- **Sunburn treatment**.
- Any personal medications.

---

## 7. Navigation and communication

The basin is small, but getting there may be complex. You are in the mountains, possibly above the tree line, with no vegetation to mark the route.

- Bring a **topographic map, compass, and GPS** if available.
- Do not build cairns or stack rocks. The place already has a low wall and shelters; adding your own marks is unnecessary and may be disruptive.
- Wind and ice will scatter any markers you leave.
- Bring a **satellite messenger or personal locator beacon** for emergency.
- Tell someone your exact route and expected return time.
- If you are traveling via multiverse jump, do not materialize inside the basin. Arrive outside the col and walk in. The sudden thermal and pressure change could disturb the ice and the site.

---

## 8. Recording and observation

You will want to document the place. The density of detail within a single pace is enormous. The seven cupmarks are the strangest part, but the whole basin is a slow machine of stone and water.

### Equipment
- **Notebook and pencil**, not a pen. Pens freeze.
- **Camera** with spare batteries kept in an inner pocket.
- **Audio recorder** with wind protection. The low note of the wind may be more felt than heard, but the drip and crackling are worth recording.
- **Non-contact infrared thermometer** to measure the warm cupmark without touching it.
- **Magnifying glass or loupe** to see the garnets, fossils, and manganese dendrites.
- **Time-lapse camera** if you want to watch the cupmarks or needle ice change. Use a tripod with broad feet or a clamp on stable rock, not on the ice.

### Observing the cupmarks
Observe at set times: before dawn, just after sunrise, midmorning, midday, afternoon, dusk.

- The first cup fills with condensation in the early morning.
- The second overflows from a crack that weeps water no one can find.
- The third gathers moisture from the air, even on dry days.
- The fourth is always dry, even when water runs across the slab.
- The fifth holds a coin of ice through the warmest part of the day.
- The sixth is warm to the touch.
- The seventh reflects the sky perfectly, but the reflected sky may not be the same as the one above.

Do not touch the water or the stone. Do not pour water into the dry cup to test it. Do not break the ice in the fifth cup. Do not try to alter the reflection in the seventh. If a difference seems to persist, note it, photograph it, and compare with direct sky. Trust your instruments, but also note your own impressions separately.

## 9. Psychological preparation

The silence is not peaceful. It is full, like a held breath. You will hear your own pulse, your own joints, the tick of the ice. This can become uncanny.

- The absence of green and animal life may make you feel as if you have stepped onto a clean bone. That is a normal reaction.
- Bring a small familiar object—a photograph, a stone from home—as an anchor.
- If you are alone, keep a spoken log. Talk aloud occasionally to break the silence.
- If you are with companions, agree on regular check-ins.

The dense detail can overwhelm. The ground within a pace contains a thousand things. Do not try to see everything. Choose small tasks: map one square meter, count the cupmarks’ shadows, watch one rivulet for ten minutes.

The central slab and its seven cupmarks may produce a feeling of being watched, or a compulsion to touch, drink, or speak. Set limits before you enter. If you feel an urge to do something you did not plan, step outside the low wall and sit down for five minutes. Most likely the urge is your mind trying to make sense of the silence and thin air. But if it becomes persistent, leave the basin.

---

## 10. Site etiquette and preservation

You are a visitor in a place with human marks but no living human culture to ask permission. The stone shelters, the cache of tools, the steps, the low wall, and the slab are all part of the site.

- Do not move stones, take flakes, or add your own carvings.
- Do not eat or leave crumbs. Pack out all waste, including human waste if possible.
- Do not introduce organic material. The place has no vegetation or animals; try not to be the first source of contamination.
- The low wall around the center may be a boundary or an invitation. If you cross it, step over it; do not walk on it.
- Consider spending your first visit entirely outside the wall, just watching.
- When you do approach the slab, move slowly, stay low, and do not place your pack or hands on it.
- Do not use the stone tools. They are not souvenirs, and they are not yours.

---

## 11. Multiverse-specific considerations

If your travel method allows, bring a small emergency beacon with a multiverse relay and a way to signal for extraction. Tell someone your coordinates and expected return time.

- Bring an **air composition tester** if you have one. The air should be breathable, but it is thin.
- A **local timepiece or universal clock** is useful because the seventh cupmark may show a different hour.
- Do not rely on a compass near the slab; test it from a distance and note any deflection.
- Do not bring active scanning equipment that might heat the ice or disturb the site.

---

## 12. A typical day

You wake before dawn in your camp below the col. The cold is sharp. You drink hot tea, eat something, and climb slowly to the basin. The needle ice is firm. You sit outside the low wall as the first light comes over the east wall. The ice tips begin to sparkle and crackle. You put on your goggles. You record the first cup filling with condensation.

Midday, you find shade if you can; there is little. The warm cup is warm; the fifth still holds ice. You drink water, eat, and rest. Afternoon, the ground softens, then refreezes in shadow. You leave before dusk to avoid the deepening cold. In your tent, you write notes, clean your gear, and sleep.

---

## 13. Emergencies

- If you feel altitude sickness, descend immediately.
- If weather turns, leave the basin; it is exposed.
- If rockfall begins, move away from the walls and cover your head.
- If you fall on needle ice, treat cuts carefully; the ice may be clean but the grit is not.
- If you see something in the seventh cupmark that is not a cloud or sky, note it and leave the central area. Do not reach into it. Trust your instinct.

---

Finally, understand that the basin is active, patient, and slow. It will not care if you are comfortable. It will not reward ambition. It may, if you are careful, allow you to witness the thin place where water, stone, and meaning move together. Go with humility, and leave the cups as you found them.
