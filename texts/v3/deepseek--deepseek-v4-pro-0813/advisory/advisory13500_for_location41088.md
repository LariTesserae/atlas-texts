Visiting **The Green Still** is not like visiting a jungle, a cave, or a greenhouse. It is like stepping inside a living lung: hot, wet, lit from within, dense with green life, and utterly still in time. You will feel the ground give slightly underfoot, as if the whole valley is one enormous body. The air will settle in your chest. The light will come from everywhere, so shadows will feel wrong. And the silence will be deeper than you expect, broken only by drips, creaks, and the slow, almost sub-audible breath of the place.

The most important thing to understand before you go is this: **nothing here changes.** A broken leaf stays broken. A crushed stem does not heal or rot. A footprint may remain for as long as the world endures. So your main task is not survival in the usual sense—it is **avoidance of permanent harm**. You are heavier, hotter, and more disruptive than anything else in that valley. You must prepare to be a guest so careful that you leave almost no evidence you were ever there.

Below is a practical, intuitive preparation guide for an ordinary human multiverse traveler.

---

## 1. First, decide if you should go at all

This is not a place for casual tourism. The Green Still is a landscape caught in the moment of its own becoming—raw, embryonic, and perfectly balanced between growth and decay at zero. It is also scattered with faint evidence of other minds: a low wall, grown steps, a threshold-shaped imprint half-absorbed by the living ground. The whole valley seems to be waiting for someone to return.

Before you pack, ask yourself: **Is my visit necessary?**  
If the answer is “I am curious,” consider sending a remote probe or camera instead.  
If the answer is “I must go myself,” then prepare as if you are entering a sacred, fragile, stillborn world.

---

## 2. The Prime Directive: Leave no permanent trace

Because nothing in The Green Still decays or erodes, any damage you cause is effectively eternal.

- **Do not cut, snap, or tear plants.** This includes yanking a vine aside, picking a leaf, or breaking a root. A broken leaf will stay broken forever.
- **Do not mark surfaces.** No carving, no paint, no scratch marks, no tying tags or ropes tightly around roots.
- **Do not spit, bleed, or shed hair/skin freely.** Your body is full of foreign organic material. Wear a full-body containment suit with hood, booties, and wrist/ankle seals.
- **Do not leave any waste.** No food scraps, no packaging, no toilet waste. You must pack out everything, including condensation from your breath if possible.
- **Do not disturb the water films.** The ground is filmed with water that moves without eroding anything. Walking will disturb it, but you can minimize this by moving slowly and avoiding repeated paths.
- **Do not move stones or peel back the plant mat.** Beneath it is raw, sharp-edged stone. Exposing it would be a permanent alteration, and it could be dangerous.

Train in **“zero-trace” movement** before you leave. Practice walking on springy, uneven surfaces without snapping twigs. Learn to part soft vegetation gently with rounded poles or gloved hands. If you cannot move without breaking things, you should not enter.

---

## 3. Physical preparation

The Green Still is hot, wet, and physically demanding. You will sweat, but your sweat will not evaporate well. The air is nearly viscous, and breathing is slow work.

### Heat acclimatization
- Spend at least **two weeks** before departure acclimating to heat and humidity.
- Use saunas, steam rooms, hot yoga, or exercise in a hot bathroom.
- Gradually increase exposure until you can function calmly in 35–40°C with very high humidity for at least an hour.
- Learn your own early signs of heat exhaustion: headache, nausea, confusion, heavy sweating that stops, cold clammy skin, rapid pulse.

### Fitness
- Cardio: You need good cardiovascular endurance. Hiking, stair-climbing, swimming, or cycling in heat helps.
- Balance and leg strength: The ground is a living mat that gives slightly underfoot. It is uneven, with root-hills, tuber-swollen rises, and basins. Train on bosu balls, uneven trails, and soft sand.
- Breath control: Practice slow, deep breathing. Techniques like Ujjayi breathing or pursed-lip breathing can help you cope with thick, moist air.
- Flexibility: You will be pushing through dense, soft vegetation. Squatting, crouching, crawling, and side-stepping are common. Stretch your hips, knees, and back.

## 4. Clothing and personal protective gear

Your clothing must balance three things: **containment** (not shedding), **heat management**, and **protection from sharp/abrasive plant parts**.

### Base layer
- Wear a moisture-wicking, anti-chafing base layer. Synthetic or merino wool, but remember wool is organic—if you shed, it becomes foreign matter. A synthetic base layer is better for containment.

### Outer suit
- Use a lightweight, breathable but tightly woven coverall with taped seams, integrated hood, and elastic cuffs at wrists and ankles.
- Booties or over-boots should be integrated or sealable to the suit.
- The suit should be easy to decontaminate after exit.
- If you have access to multiverse-standard biohazard encounter suits, use one with active climate control.

### Hands
- Wear nitrile or similar inner gloves to contain skin cells and sweat.
- Over them, wear thin but tough cut-resistant gloves. The plants may have rough stems, fine spines, or sticky sap. Gloves must be flexible enough to part vegetation gently.

### Feet
- High-top, soft-soled boots with good traction on wet, springy plant fiber.
- Non-marking soles are essential.
- Gaiters to keep debris out of your boots.
- Change socks often to prevent fungal growth. Bring thin liner socks and thicker outer socks.

### Face and eyes
- A full-face respirator is ideal, but it will be hot. At minimum, use a well-sealed mask with P100/OV cartridges.
- The air is full of pollen or spores that never settle. You do not want that in your lungs.
- Wear anti-fog goggles or a face shield.
- Amber or neutral-tinted lenses can help break the relentless green light and restore some visual contrast.

### Cooling
- Evaporative cooling will **not** work well because the air is nearly saturated. Do not rely on wet neck wraps.
- Use a **phase-change cooling vest** with packs that stay cool at 15–20°C.
- Wear cooling wrist wraps and a neck gaiter.
- If possible, use a battery-powered air supply with a heat exchanger to deliver cooler, dry air to your mask.
- Carry spare cooling packs in an insulated container, but remember: if they leak, that material could become permanent pollution.

## 5. Air, water, and food

### Air
The air in The Green Still is hot, wet, and thick with plant smells and spores. It may be oxygen-rich or oxygen-poor in pockets; you cannot assume it is safe.

- **Before removing your mask**, use a portable gas monitor to check oxygen, carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, and volatile organic compounds.
- Keep the respirator on for the entire visit unless you are certain the air is safe and you accept the risk of inhaling spores.
- If you have asthma or any reactive airway disease, consider not going. If you go, carry a bronchodilator and epinephrine.
- The air moves only through hollow plant stems and the occasional slow exhale of far galleries. Do not block those openings. Avoid sitting directly on them.

### Water
Water is everywhere—dripping from the canopy, filming the ground, pooling in leaf cups. But you should not assume it is safe to drink.

- Bring **all your own water** for trips of less than a day.
- If you must refill, collect condensation on a clean, reusable plastic sheet or tarp. Do **not** scoop from leaf cups or seams; you may disturb the rare fauna or alter microhabitats.
- Purify any collected water with a 0.1-micron filter and UV treatment. Sterilize the collection surface before use.
- Test water with a portable pH and contamination kit before drinking.

### Food
- Bring high-energy, low-residue food: gel packs, paste pouches, soft bars that do not crumble.
- Eat inside a small containment tent or hood to catch any crumbs or flakes.
- No cooking. The Green Still is a living body; open flame is unthinkable. Use flameless heaters if you must warm food.
- All packaging goes back with you. Do not leave even a twist-tie.

## 6. Navigation and timekeeping

Navigation in The Green Still is extremely difficult:

- The light comes from every direction, so shadows are rare. This ruins depth perception. You may not see a hole or a drop until you are on it.
- The green haze obscures the far side. Visual estimates of distance are unreliable.
- There are no paths, no open rock, no clear landmarks except the faint ruins and the northern ridge.

### Tools
- **Ranging laser or LIDAR**: Use it constantly to judge distance and detect drops.
- **Inertial navigation unit**: GPS may not work underground. A chest-mounted inertial mapper can record your route.
- **Retractable guideline**: Anchor a thin, low-tension line at your entry portal. Use it to find your way back. It must be soft-sheathed so it does not cut into plants.
- **Walking staff/probe**: Use a lightweight pole with a rounded end to test the ground ahead for hidden voids, water-filled seams, or weak spots in the mat.
- **Strong flashlight**: Even though the ambient light is bright, a low-angle flashlight beam will create shadows and reveal texture, holes, and edges. Use it often.

### Timekeeping
The valley is a time-pool. The same drop hangs forever; the same leaf never unfurls. Your sense of time will fail.

- Wear a vibration-alarm wristwatch and a backup timer.
- Set alarms for every 30 minutes. When the alarm sounds, stop, drink water, check your body and equipment, and note your position.
- Do not trust fatigue as a sign of passing time. Schedule rest by the clock.
- Keep a voice log or written log. The act of recording helps anchor you in sequential time.

## 7. Movement and camping

### Movement
- Move **slowly**. Never run, jump, or make sudden turns.
- Part vegetation with your hands or a rounded pole. Do not snap stems. If you must push through, turn sideways, breathe out, and ease through.
- Test every step. The ground is a continuous living mat, but it may thin over fissures or between fused trunks.
- Avoid steep root-hills unless absolutely necessary. If you must climb, use hands and feet, and check that your weight does not crush or tear the plant surface.
- Avoid touching the water channels. They are smooth and may be slippery.
- The far side of the central basin is half a day’s walk across under normal ground conditions. In this dense, springy, hot environment, plan for twice that time or more.

### Camping
Camping should be extremely limited. If you must rest or sleep:

- Use a **freestanding tent or bivy sack**; do not use stakes.
- Put a lightweight ground sheet under you to distribute pressure, but be aware that any prolonged pressure may leave a mark.
- Choose a spot already relatively open. The room-like hollow under the northern ridge is slightly cooler and has a faint air current, but it is also a distinct space—treat it with respect. Do not build anything.
- Sleep in short shifts. Always have one person awake to monitor environmental alarms.
- No candles, no flames, no smoking.

## 8. Health risks and medical kit

### Heat illness
This is your greatest physical threat. The heat is constant, humid, and breathless.

**Watch for:**
- Headache
- Nausea
- Dizziness or confusion
- Rapid weak pulse
- Hot skin that becomes dry or pale
- Cramps

**Immediate actions:**
- Stop moving.
- Move to a slightly cooler spot if possible (the hollow under the ridge is marginally cooler).
- Apply cooling packs to neck, armpits, and groin.
- Drink small sips of electrolyte solution.
- If confusion or vomiting occurs, begin evacuation immediately.

### Respiratory issues
The air is full of spores and plant volatiles. Symptoms may include:
- Wheezing, chest tightness, cough
- Itchy eyes, sneezing
- Difficulty breathing

Carry:
- Antihistamines
- Bronchodilator inhaler
- Epinephrine auto-injector
- Oxygen saturation monitor

If breathing becomes difficult, increase respirator filtration or switch to supplied air, and leave the valley.

### Skin and wound care
Constant moisture will macerate your skin. Fungal and bacterial infections are a risk.

- Change socks and gloves at every rest.
- Dry your feet whenever possible.
- Use antifungal powder.
- Any cut or puncture must be cleaned, disinfected, and covered with a sterile dressing. Even a small wound can become serious in hot, humid conditions.
- The raw stone beneath the mat is sharp-edged. If you feel the ground give way, do not panic; ease your weight back and find a safer route.

### Psychological stress
The Green Still exerts a subtle psychological pull:

- The green light is monotonous. After hours, you may feel as if you are dissolving into it.
- The silence is deep. Your own breathing and heartbeat will seem loud.
- The place is timeless. You may begin to feel that returning is not urgent.
- The faint ruins suggest waiting. You may feel unreasonably drawn to stay, to wait, or to search for whoever left them.

**Countermeasures:**
- Travel with at least one partner. Never go alone.
- Check in with each other on a fixed schedule.
- Use a small white or amber light inside your tent to break the green monotony.
- Listen to pre-recorded messages from home on headphones, if your mission allows.
- Set a firm pre-commitment: **“I will exit after X hours no matter what.”** Write it on your arm with ink that does not wash off.
- If you begin to feel euphoric, deeply passive, or convinced that you should stay longer than planned, that is a signal to leave immediately.

## 9. The ruins and the living things

### The ruins
There is a low wall of packed leaf-mold and hardened resin. There are three grown steps in a root-hill. There is a threshold-shaped imprint beneath a woody arch.

These are the only evidence of other minds.

- **Do not touch them.**
- Do not sit on the wall, walk on the steps, or stand in the threshold imprint.
- Photograph or measure them from a distance.
- They are not your destination. You are a visitor, not the one the valley is waiting for.

### The fauna
A pale millipede, a blind translucent frog, and a moth with wet paper wings are the only animals.

- Do not chase, catch, or feed them.
- The frog may be almost invisible; check leaf cups before you reach into one.
- If the moth circles you and disappears, do not follow it.
- Their presence is rare and seems accidental. Leave them exactly as they are.

## 10. Arrival checklist

When you first arrive at the portal or entrance:

1. **Stop.** Do not step out immediately. Observe from the threshold.
2. **Test the air** with gas detectors while still in your respirator.
3. **Check temperature, humidity, and air movement.** Assume heat stress risk from the first minute.
4. **Set up a base camp at the entry point.** This is where you keep spare water, cooling packs, medical supplies, and emergency extraction gear.
5. **Anchor a retractable guideline** and turn on your inertial mapper and radio beacon.
6. **Establish communication with outside support.** If using a portal relay, confirm it works before proceeding.
7. **Do a partner check:** suit seals, cooling systems, hydration, alarms, mental state.
8. **Set a turn-back time.** For a first visit, no more than **4–6 hours** total inside the Green Still.
9. **Move out slowly.** Probe the ground, avoid snapping stems, and stay within sight of your guideline.

---

## 11. Extraction and aftercare

When you leave, assume you are contaminated with spores, plant residues, and possibly unknown bio-aerosols.

- **Decontaminate outside the portal** in a containment chamber. Remove your suit inside, bag it, and shower with antimicrobial wash.
- **Do not bring plant material out** unless you have explicit permission and proper containment.
- **Quarantine for at least 48 hours.** Monitor for respiratory symptoms, fever, rashes, or delayed allergic reactions.
- **Clean all equipment.** Some spores may be incredibly persistent. Use disinfection appropriate for exotic bio-materials.
- **Inspect your body for tiny cuts** and treat them. The humid environment may have masked pain.

## 12. What it will actually feel like

You will step into a hot, green half-light that seems to come from the leaves themselves. The air will push against your chest, thick with the smell of crushed ferns, sweet sap, and warm stone. You will sweat immediately, and it will not cool you. The ground will give slightly underfoot, as if the world is made of living flesh. You will hear the drip of water—so regular it seems like one drop falling and re-falling—and the faint creak of a tendril adjusting its grip over centuries.

The lack of shadows will make you feel as if you are walking in a dream. Distances will lie to you. Your own hands will look wrong in the green light. You may feel claustrophobic and agoraphobic at the same time: closed in by root-hills and hanging curtains, yet aware of vast side-galleries opening out into green haze.

And beneath it all, there is the sense of waiting. The place is not empty. It is paused. The drop never falls. The leaf never unfurls. The ruins wait half-absorbed by the living ground.

Your job is not to fix it, not to explore it fully, and not to become part of its stillness. Your job is to move through it with the lightest possible touch, to record what you can, and to leave before you become a permanent mark on its surface.

---

**Final advice:**  
Go slow. Sweat. Breathe. Do not break anything. Do not stay too long. And when the valley exhales, do not mistake it for an invitation. It was breathing long before you arrived, and it will go on breathing—unchanged—long after you leave.
