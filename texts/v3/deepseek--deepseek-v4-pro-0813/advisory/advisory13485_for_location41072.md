**Preparation for the Resonant Basin**

You are not going to a normal wilderness, a normal ruin, or a normal city. You are going to a warm, dark, wet, sonically saturated living infrastructure. The place treats sound, moisture, and growth as convertible forces. Its surface is sharp, its air is thick, its light is dim, and it is never silent. For an ordinary human multiverse traveler, the goal is not to conquer or explore aggressively. The goal is to move through without being worn down by noise, humidity, spores, disorientation, or the subtle feeling that the city is listening.

If you have inner-ear, respiratory, cardiac, seizure, or severe anxiety conditions, treat this location as high risk. Get medical clearance before you go.

---

## 1. What It Will Feel Like

The first thing will be the sound. You will feel it in your chest, jaw, and the soles of your feet before your brain can separate it into sources. A low ground murmur, a high insect shriek, wind-driven organ tones from the towers, water dripping into tuned basins. It will feel like the air is pressing against you from the inside.

Then you will notice the warmth and wetness. The air will feel like a mouth. Your skin will bead with condensation even before you sweat. Your clothing will want to cling. The stone will be black, sharp, wet, and split into ridges. The light will be a permanent amber-gray dusk, with faint blue-green glow from moss and stone edges.

You will likely feel watched—not by eyes, but by resonance. That is not necessarily paranoia. The place may store or transmit something through sound. Treat that feeling as information, not proof of danger. Do not let it push you into panic or euphoria.

---

## 2. First Principles

Treat this place as an active system, not an abandoned one. The towers secrete resin that heals cracks. Some spires vibrate and condense their own rain. The sound may be functional. The vegetation is old and layered, but the rock edges are raw. It is simultaneously new and ancient.

Your main dangers are:

- **Noise fatigue and hearing damage** from constant layered sound and infrasound.
- **Humidity, heat, and wet stone** causing falls, blisters, fungal infection, and exhaustion.
- **Spore and microorganism exposure** in the thick air.
- **Disorientation** from dim light, hazy distance, lack of silence, and structures that blur at the edges.
- **Structural and biological hazards** if you enter or climb where you should not.
- **Psychological strain** from never having silence, darkness, or a clear horizon.

Be humble. Move slowly. Leave no trace.

## 3. Pre-Travel Training

Begin preparation 8–12 weeks before departure if possible.

### Physical
- Train in hot, humid conditions while wearing your intended clothing and mask.
- Hike carrying a loaded pack over uneven, wet rock. The ground there is ridged, fissured, and slick.
- Strengthen ankles, knees, and balance. You will spend hours on irregular stone.
- Practice walking in low light with a headlamp and reduced contrast.
- Do stair and ramp work—not because you should climb the towers, but because the terrain includes ramps, terraces, and low stairways.

### Auditory
- Practice sleeping with layered noise: a low rumble, a high drone, irregular drips. Keep it at safe volume, but learn how your body reacts to continuous sound.
- Wear earplugs for long periods while awake. Learn to function, eat, and navigate while partially deafened.
- Practice using electronic hearing protection and switching between modes without removing it.

### Respiratory
- Exercise while wearing a P100 or N95 mask so breathing does not feel alien.
- If you have seasonal allergies, asthma, or reactive airways, do a trial under medical guidance before committing to this trip.

### Mental
- Do a 48-hour drill with no true silence and no bright daylight. Use dim amber light, played soundscapes, and enforced schedule.
- Practice journaling under fatigue. You will need the habit to track time and mood.

## 4. Sound Protection

Sound is the most invasive feature of the basin. It is continuous, multi-layered, and includes both high-frequency shrieks and subsonic pressure.

### Hearing gear
- **Custom-molded musician’s earplugs** with exchangeable filters: 9, 15, and 25 dB. These reduce volume evenly, preserving some environmental awareness.
- **High-fidelity foam earplugs** as backups.
- **Over-ear passive earmuffs** for high-noise periods and sleep.
- **Electronic shooting/gunner earpro**: amplifies quiet sounds but compresses loud peaks. Useful for maintaining awareness while protecting against the high insect shriek and wind-driven tower tones.
- **A small decibel meter**. If sustained noise exceeds roughly 85 dBA, use protection. The insect drone and tower organ tones may be louder than they seem.

### Sleep
- Use foam earplugs plus passive earmuffs.
- In camp, a low-volume white-noise generator may help mask the high shriek, but do not let it mask every danger. Post a watch.
- If you use active noise-canceling earbuds for sleep, understand they are better against low drone than high insect noise. Combine with passive protection.

### Infrasound
The low vibration in the ground can cause nausea, headache, unease, blurred vision, or a feeling that your heartbeat is entraining to the pulse. You cannot always hear it.

- Use thick footwear and a sleeping pad to reduce ground transmission.
- If you feel chest pressure or rhythmic discomfort, move to a different position, ideally away from large fluted towers or open basins.
- Do not remain in a low-vibration node because you “get used to it.” Move off it.

### Rule
Do not add sound. No loud music, no whistles, no beeping devices. Speak quietly. The environment already uses sound as a medium. Your noise may disrupt the local system or attract attention.

## 5. Breathing and Eye Protection

The air is heavy with spores, water vapor, and possibly resin volatiles.

### Respiratory
- **Half-face respirator with P100 and organic vapor/acid gas cartridges** for moving near thick vegetation, inside any structure, or through spore haze.
- **N95 or FFP3 masks** for lower-exposure periods and as backups.
- Spare cartridges and masks in sealed dry bags.
- In high humidity, respirator valves and filters may condense. Carry something to dry and inspect them.
- Assume you have no immunity to local spores. Do not touch your face after touching wet vegetation or stone.

### Eye
- **Clear anti-fog safety glasses or goggles** as primary eye protection.
- **Amber or yellow lenses** improve contrast in dim, hazy amber light.
- Blue-green bioluminescence can make your eyes strain; yellow lenses may help contrast but will not restore full color.
- Carry anti-fog wipes. The humidity is relentless.
- If you encounter a dense spore cloud, switch to sealed goggles.
- Do not rub your eyes.

## 6. Clothing and Footwear

The climate is warm and humid, with sudden warm rain squalls. The stone is wet and sharp.

### Base and mid layers
- Synthetic or merino wool base layers. No cotton.
- Lightweight fleece or soft-shell mid layer.
- Quick-dry pants with abrasion resistance. Cargo pockets help.

### Outer layer
- Waterproof and breathable shell jacket and pants.
- Pit zips for ventilation.
- Hood large enough to fit over a hat and mask straps.

### Head and neck
- Wide-brim hat for drizzle and spore fall, or a waterproof hood.
- Neck gaiter or buff.
- Bug net hood for periods near still water, vegetation, or tower openings.

### Hands
- Water-resistant, cut-resistant gloves.
- Thin liner gloves for fine tasks.

### Feet
- High-ankle waterproof boots with deep lug soles and a puncture-resistant insole.
- Waterproof breathable socks plus liner socks.
- Extra dry socks in sealed dry bags.
- Gaiters to keep water and debris out.
- Lightweight camp shoes that can get wet.

### Knee protection
- Light knee pads if you expect to crouch, climb low ledges, or walk through tight rocky troughs.

## 7. Camp, Shelter, and Sleep

The ground is often bare wet stone. You cannot rely on tent stakes.

### Shelter
- A freestanding tent with a bathtub floor and full rainfly.
- A footprint or groundsheet.
- Use rocks, sandbags, or heavy dry bags to anchor the tent on stone.
- A tarp for a cook area or group shelter; use trekking poles or rock anchors.

### Site selection
- Choose high, well-drained bare rock away from water channels and mist-filled depressions.
- Do not camp directly beneath tower openings, under unstable vegetation, or under stalactite-like stone edges.
- Stay at least 150–200 meters from tower bases if possible. Towers shed condensation, resin, possibly debris, and produce intense sound in wind.
- Avoid camping near papery nest colonies or obvious game trails.

### Sleeping
- Insulated sleeping pad. The ground may be warm, but you still lose heat to wet stone.
- Light synthetic quilt or sleeping bag suitable for warm humid conditions.
- Pillow or stuff sack.
- Eye mask. The ambient dusk and bioluminescence can disrupt sleep even when you are exhausted.
- Earplugs and earmuffs as above.

### Hygiene
- Dry your feet and groin frequently.
- Use antifungal powder.
- Change out of wet socks and base layers before sleep.
- Pack out all human waste; do not contaminate the watercourses.

## 8. Water, Food, and Cooking

### Water
- Bring your own water for the first 2–3 days. Do not assume local water is safe.
- Purify any local water with a combination of:
  - coarse pre-filter,
  - activated carbon,
  - 0.1-micron or finer filter,
  - boiling for at least 1 minute,
  - UV purification as an additional step.
- Rain can be collected in a clean tarp or container, but treat it. The air is full of spores.
- Avoid water from tower-condensed fog or resin-sweating surfaces unless it is an emergency. Even then, treat it heavily and watch for reactions.
- Water channels are shallow but may contain biological or mineral contamination.

### Food
- Bring dense, high-calorie rations. You will burn energy due to heat, humidity, and uneven terrain.
- Use no-cook or low-cook meals to reduce fuel and noise.
- If you cook, use a canister stove on a stable rock surface. Do not place a hot stove on wet or cracked stone without protection.
- Store food in odor-proof bags and sealed containers. The local animals are not dense, but they are present.
- Do not eat local plants, fungi, animals, or resin. Assume they are bioactive or toxic to your biology.

## 9. Navigation and Lighting

The landscape is enormous, dim, and easy to misjudge.

### Navigation
- Bring a good magnetic compass, but be aware that the towers and possible local magnetic anomalies may cause deflection. Cross-check with landmarks.
- Do not assume GPS or satellite systems work across universes. If your multiversal navigation device works, still keep manual backups.
- Carry waterproof paper, pencils, and a map sleeve. Keep a running route log.
- Use towers as long-range landmarks. The upper parts may blur or vibrate, so take bearings on lower, stable sections.
- Distances are deceptive in dim haze. Use a laser rangefinder if possible.
- Mark your route with removable reflective stakes or flags. Remove them on your way back.

### Lighting
- Headlamp with red light, low white, and high white modes.
- Handheld flashlight with adjustable beam.
- Spare batteries or a reliable charging system. Do not rely on solar in this dim, hazy place.
- Use red light in camp to preserve night vision and reduce insect attraction.
- Use low white for route-finding; bright white only for emergencies or distance.
- Retroreflective tape on gear and camp markers.
- Chemlights for marking camp or emergencies.

### Visual aids
- Binoculars or monocular for distance.
- A camera or smartphone screen can help you view structures that look “too soft to focus on.” Looking through a screen may reduce eyestrain and let you study blurred upper towers without staring directly.
- If a structure seems to vibrate at the wrong frequency, do not stare at it naked-eye for long. Use the screen or break observation into short glances.

## 10. Medical and First Aid

Assume the local biome is alien to your immune system, and your own microbiome is alien to it. Avoid unnecessary contact.

### First aid kit
- Blister care: moleskin, gel pads, tape.
- Antifungal powder and cream.
- Antibacterial ointment and antiseptic wipes.
- Sterile saline for wound irrigation and eye washing.
- Eye wash cup.
- Liquid bandage and Steri-strips.
- Pressure bandages and a tourniquet if trained.
- Splint material.
- Painkillers, antihistamines, decongestants.
- Anti-nausea and motion-sickness medication for infrasound or balance disturbance.
- Antidiarrheal medication.
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics only if prescribed and appropriate.
- Epinephrine auto-injector for severe allergic reactions.
- Electrolyte packets.
- Thermal emergency blanket and emergency bivy.

### Special concerns
- **Wet stone:** sprains and falls are likely. Go slowly.
- **Humidity and heat:** heat exhaustion can sneak up even in dim light. Drink electrolytes and rest.
- **Spores:** if respiratory distress occurs, leave dense vegetation, keep mask on, take antihistamines if appropriate, and call for help.
- **Resin:** avoid contact. If you touch tower resin, do not use harsh solvents first. Try oil-based wipes or medical adhesive remover, then wash with clean water and mild soap. Do not apply to open wounds.
- **Ears:** if you develop pain, fullness, discharge, or sudden hearing loss, stop. Repeated exposure to high humidity and moisture can lead to ear infection.

## 11. Interaction with the Environment

The city is maintained, but not by visible human hands. Treat it as a machine or organism you do not understand.

- **Do not carve, chip, anchor, or paint the stone.**
- **Do not remove resin, plants, animals, or artifacts.**
- **Do not block water channels or basins.**
- **Do not enter circular openings, narrow slots, or doorless thresholds.** They are not doors for you, and the air inside is likely thicker with spores, sound, and hidden life.
- **Do not climb the towers, ramps, or bridges.** The structures are not designed for human safety, and the blurred upper sections may have physical properties you cannot predict.
- **Avoid standing beneath vibrating spires.** They produce their own rain and may focus sound or vibration.
- **Avoid loud noises.** If you need to signal, use light, hand signals, or short-range radio with earpieces.

### The soundscape
The sound is not background noise. It is a whole climate. Treat it as weather.

- Wind in the flutes will produce organ chords. When wind rises, the valley may roar.
- Water drops into tuned basins like drumbeats.
- Insect drone may seem to come from the air itself.
- The ground has its own seismic murmur.

Do not try to “answer” the sound with your own voice, whistling, or instruments. The place may be storing or transmitting something through resonance. Your noise could perturb it.

## 12. Arrival and First 72 Hours

### First hour
- Remain at the arrival point. Drop your pack but do not wander.
- Sit or stand on solid stone.
- Keep hearing protection in. Do not expose your ears to full volume immediately.
- Breathe slowly. Let your heart rate settle.
- Visually mark your exact arrival point with a removable flag or beacon.
- Drink water.

### First 24 hours
- Establish a base camp within sight of the arrival point.
- Do not enter any structure.
- Practice moving on the wet stone for short distances.
- Note how wind changes the sound.
- Check your gear for humidity damage.
- Sleep in shifts if in a group.

### First 72 hours
- Extend routes only within visual range of camp or a known landmark.
- Keep a strict schedule. Do not rely on daylight.
- Journal every few hours. Note time, location, sound intensity, weather, and your mood.
- Watch for footwear failure, chafing, foot rot, and mask discomfort.
- Reassess whether the group is adapting to the noise. Some people do not.

## 13. Psychological Survival

This place will wear on you.

- **There is no silence.** Even with earplugs, you may miss the idea of silence. That is normal.
- **The permanent dusk may distort time.** Use a mechanical watch and respond to elapsed time, not perceived time.
- **The blue-green light and sound may produce strange dreams.** Sleep with a guard if possible.
- **You may feel the city listening.** Do not treat that as an immediate threat, but do not seek to communicate with it. If you feel euphoria, dread, or a sudden desire to stay, tell your group. Use grounding exercises.
- **Auditory fatigue may make you irritable.** Do not make navigation decisions when exhausted or overwhelmed.
- **Isolation and scale may make you feel tiny.** That is accurate. Move with care.

### Watch for overexposure
If you or a teammate experience:
- ringing in the ears that does not stop,
- dizziness or nausea,
- visual shimmering,
- difficulty understanding speech even in quiet conditions,
- a feeling that the towers are breathing with you,
- sudden euphoria or dissociation,

then retreat to camp, use hearing protection, hydrate, and rest. Do not push deeper.

## 14. Emergency Planning

### Separation
- Predefine a rendezvous point. The base camp is best.
- If separated, do not wander toward blurred structures in mist. Stop, use compass and known tower bearings, and retrace your flagged route if possible.

### Injury
- Treat on site.
- Use signal mirror, strobe, or high-visibility panel. Sound may not carry usefully, so do not rely on shouting.
- Keep the injured person warm. The air is warm, but wet stone and rain can chill.

### Predator encounter
- Group together. Appear larger.
- Move slowly to open ground.
- Avoid high-pitched screams or beeps. These may stimulate a predator or alter the soundscape.
- Do not run on wet stone.

### Flooding
- Rain squalls can fill channels quickly.
- Move to higher bare rock.
- Do not shelter inside tower openings or under ledges that cannot drain.

### Structural anomaly
- If the ground shakes, if resin flows suddenly, if a bridge ripples like heat, if a tower’s blurred section seems to descend, leave the immediate area. Move laterally away from the structure, not directly toward another tower face.
