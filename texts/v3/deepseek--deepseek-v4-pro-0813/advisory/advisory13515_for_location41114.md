# Preparation Brief: The Hollow-Stem Valley

You should not think of this as a place you visit. Think of it as a body you are about to enter—a slow, wet, dim, loud, folded body with its own metabolism, its own timekeeping, and its own inconsistent physics. The valley will not care whether you survive. It may not even notice you except as a small foreign object in its throat. Preparation is therefore not about comfort; it is about being able to leave the way you came.

This guide assumes you are an ordinary unaugmented human with access to standard multiversal expedition equipment: a stable anchor beacon, a recall device or extraction line, an environmental recorder, and a support team. If you lack any of these, do not enter.

---

## 1. Preliminary Read: What You Are Walking Into

The valley is a cold, wet basin that behaves less like a landscape and more like a living machine. The ground is not solid; it is a slow liquid wearing a skin of peat and moss. The air is thick, milky, and full of fog droplets that may act as a colloidal computer. The water is everywhere—weeping, rising, condensing, running uphill in places. The light is dim and delayed. Sound is loud but unreliable. Space is folded along the old drainage channels. Time runs unevenly depending on whether you stand over dark moss or open water. Gravity is patchy; some ponds bulge upward, and some pebbles fall the wrong way.

There are almost no large animals. The density of life is in the invisible: spores, bacteria, fungal hyphae, algae, charged droplets, gas bubbles, and water chemistry. The whole ecosystem seems to be not a food web but a slow computation. The peat is a neural net of roots and metal-reducing bacteria. The streams are wires. The fog is a logic gate. The valley is running a program no one wrote.

You are a foreign signal entering that program. Your goal is to be small, brief, non-destructive, and reversible.

## 2. Core Hazards

Treat these as the main killers, in order of likely threat:

1. **Drowning/sinking in peat or water** — the ground can give way, and water may be deeper than it looks.
2. **Hypothermia** — the temperature is cave-mouth cool, and humidity is near saturation.
3. **Toxic or flammable gas** — methane, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide, sulfur compounds from peat and sulfur bacteria.
4. **Disorientation** — folded space, delayed light, wrong echoes, time dilation, and featureless fog will destroy your sense of direction.
5. **Infection or biological contamination** — every breath and touch is full of novel spores, bacteria, and micro-life.
6. **Psychological deterioration** — sensory ambiguity, constant roar, dimness, and the sense of being inside an active mind can cause panic, derealization, or obsession.
7. **Anomalous physics** — time pockets, spatial folds, gravity anomalies, and slow light can trap you or make rescue impossible.

## 3. Pre-Entry Team and Reconnaissance

Do not go alone. Minimum team: four people.

- **Base operator** — stays at the edge/airlock, maintains tether, monitors gas and time, communicates with outside.
- **Lead walker** — probes ground, chooses route, watches for anomalies.
- **Navigator** — reads instruments, places beacon pucks, tracks time and space.
- **Rear guard** — manages tether, watches behind, assists in retreat.

Do a remote reconnaissance before physical entry if possible. But do not trust drones or autonomous probes fully; the fog, charged droplets, and folded space may corrupt their signals. Use multiple modalities: infrared, radar, acoustic, chemical sensors, and visual light. If a probe enters and returns wrong—missing time, bent structure, or sending its own signal back in reverse—do not follow it.

If you can, monitor from the edge for at least one full daily cycle. Note the fog density, gas readings, water movement, sound patterns, and any occurrence of convex ponds or slow/fast ripples. Avoid entering during weekly heavy rain, monthly sulfur bacteria blooms, or the yearly migration of floating peat islands.

## 4. Physical Conditioning and Skills

You are not going for a walk. You are going into a cold, waterlogged, unstable, disorienting environment that will exhaust you quickly.

### Train before departure:

- **Walking on quaking bog** — practice with snowshoe-like platforms over soft ground. Learn to distribute your weight.
- **Self-rescue from peat or water** — practice rolling onto your back, spreading your arms, and swimming horizontally out of a sinkhole.
- **Rope work** — fixed lines, ascenders, pulley systems, and rescue hauling.
- **Cold water immersion** — conditioned response to cold shock; practice breathing control.
- **Respirator tolerance** — spend hours breathing through a full-face mask while working.
- **Navigation in whiteout/fog** — rely on compass, inertial unit, and tether, not landmarks.
- **First aid** — hypothermia, drowning, gas exposure, severe cuts, anaphylaxis.
- **Communication protocols** — rope tugs, radio codes, light signals.
- **Mental resilience** — meditation, panic management, sensory ambiguity training.

## 5. Clothing and Personal Protective Equipment

The valley will soak you, chill you, and try to fill your lungs with its microbiome. Dress as if for a cold, wet cave dive with a biological hazard overlay.

### Base layers
- Merino wool or polypropylene next to skin. No cotton.
- Vapor-barrier liner if you are prone to chilling.
- Thick insulating layer that retains warmth when wet.

### Outer layer
- A full **dry suit** with attached hood, waterproof zippers, and latex or neoprene cuffs. It must be breathable enough to prevent sweat pooling, but sealed enough to keep out water and micro-life.
- Reinforced knees and elbows for crawling over hummocks.

### Footwear
- Insulated waterproof boots or dry-suit boots.
- Wear **bog shoes** or **mudders** over them: large, snowshoe-like platforms that spread your weight over the floating peat.
- Keep the bog shoes easy to shed if you sink. Use quick-release bindings.

### Hands and face
- Waterproof insulated gloves with nitrile inner gloves for chemical protection.
- A full-face respirator with a P100/HEPA filter and acid-gas/organic vapor cartridges. The air is full of spores, sulfur gases, and unknown biological particles.
- Goggles if not using a full-face mask. Your eyes can be a route for infection or irritation.
- A hood or balaclava that seals against the mask.

### Visibility
- Bright colors or reflective strips on your pack and helmet so your team can see you in the fog.
- Do not rely on bright colors being visible beyond a few meters.

### Specialized items
- **Flotation aid** — an auto-inflating PFD or foam vest worn under or over the dry suit. If the ground gives way, you need immediate buoyancy.
- **Climbing harness** with chest and seat, multiple carabiners, and a locking attachment for the tether.
- **Helmet** with a headlamp and camera mount.
- **Two trekking poles** with large baskets for balance, plus a dedicated **probe pole** at least 2 meters long with a blunt tip for testing ground firmness.

## 6. Air, Water, Food, and Medical

### Air
- Wear your respirator at all times in the valley. Do not remove it to eat, drink, or wipe your face.
- Carry a **multi-gas monitor** for O₂, CH₄, H₂S, CO, and SO₂. Clip it to the outside of your pack with an audible alarm.
- If the monitor alarms, do not panic. Move upwind or perpendicular to the gas source, do not create sparks, and retreat along the tether.
- No open flames. No smoking. No stoves. Methane may be present in pockets. Use intrinsically safe electronics only.
- If you must eat or drink during a long sortie, use a drinking tube through the mask port and gel packs. Do not break the seal.

### Water
- Do not drink local water. It is brown with tannins, milky with sulfur bacteria, or clear but full of unknown chemistry.
- Carry all drinking water from outside. Minimum 3 liters per person per day, more if you are working hard.
- If you carry a purifier, it is a backup only. Do not trust it against unknown heavy metals, nanoparticles, or local biology.

### Food
- High-energy, low-residue, sealed food. No crumbs. No loose wrappers.
- Eat only at the edge base or inside a sealed shelter. If you must eat in the valley, use gel packs and a straw.
- No local plants or animals. Sundews and bladderworts are carnivorous but not food. The frogs, eels, and birds may be temporally or spatially anomalous; do not consume them.

### Medical
Carry a large expedition first-aid kit, including:
- Trauma shears, bandages, quick-clot, splints.
- Hypothermia treatment: heat packs, insulated blanket, dry base layers.
- Gas exposure treatment: oxygen canister if possible, bronchodilators.
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics and antifungals.
- Antihistamines, epinephrine auto-injector.
- Eye wash and saline.
- Oral rehydration salts.
- Quarantine bags for contaminated gear.
- A small medical manual or offline device.

## 7. Navigation, Timekeeping, and Space-Fold Management

The valley will try to lose you. Not maliciously, but because its space and time are not your space and time.

### Tether and anchor
- Run a **fixed tether** from a solid anchor at the edge into the valley. Use floating, brightly colored static rope or Dyneema. Never detach from it.
- The tether is your only reliable physical reference. If it goes slack uphill, taut sideways, or appears to pass through itself, stop and reel back.
- Use screw-in ground anchors or deadman plates; ordinary stakes will pull out of peat.
- The base operator maintains tension and monitors the amount of line paid out. If the line pays out faster than you are walking, you may be in a time or space anomaly.

### Beacon pucks
- Carry small **beacon pucks** that record local sensor data and emit a unique ID. Place one every 20–30 meters or at every change of terrain.
- They should be self-powered, waterproof, and intrinsically safe. Attach a short reflective wand or fiberglass pole so you can find them in fog.
- Do not rely on GPS. It may fail or give impossible readings. Use an **inertial navigation unit** as your primary electronic navigation.
- Draw your own grid as you go. Do not trust pre-existing maps; the terrain may not be topologically consistent.

### Timekeeping
- Carry at least two watches: one mechanical, one digital, synchronized to base time before entry.
- If they diverge by more than a few seconds per hour, you are near a time anomaly. Stop and retreat.
- Watch natural processes: if a water drop hangs for minutes, you are in slow time. If ripples cross a pond in a blink, you are in fast time. Avoid both.
- Do not make decisions based on how long something *feels*. Use your external clock and the base operator’s time checks.

### Detecting space folds
- If you see the same hill in two places, or a stream that crosses itself without meeting, you are in a spatial fold. Stop, mark the location, and retreat along the tether.
- Do not follow channels. They are old and meandering, and space is folded along them. Cross them perpendicular with the tether tight, if at all.
- If you see your own trail ahead of you, or a beacon puck that should be behind you, do not keep walking. You are looped.

### Detecting gravity anomalies
- Carry a small **accelerometer** or inertial measurement unit. If it beeps, stop.
- Test suspicious ground with a small object. If it falls upward or sideways, retreat.
- If a pond surface bulges upward, do not approach. The water is not obeying normal gravity.
- Do not throw objects for fun. They may follow curved paths or return to you.

## 8. Movement and Camp Protocol

Moving through this valley is not like hiking. It is more like moving through a soft, wet, living surface that may give way, reject you, or fold around you.

### Walking rules
- Move slowly. Test every step with the probe pole.
- If the ground quakes, water rises around your foot, or the moss feels like a waterbed, stop. Back up along your tether.
- Step on dense sedges, root masses, or the bases of stunted alder and willow when possible. Avoid bare mud and open water.
- Never walk in a straight line across open water. Use a packraft or a detour.
- If you sink: lie flat, spread your arms and legs, inflate your PFD, and swim horizontally out. Do not struggle vertical.

### Crossing water
- Use a small **packraft** for open water. Cross with the tether attached.
- Never wade into water deeper than your knees. The bottom may be liquid peat.
- If you must wade, use the probe pole to test each step, and keep your PFD inflated.

### Camp rules
- **Do not sleep inside the valley if you can avoid it.** Return to the edge base for rest.
- If you must camp, pitch a sealed one-person tent on a thick foam pad on top of a packraft or stable hummock. Use a vapor-barrier bivy inside.
- Sleep with your dry suit on and your respirator nearby. Do not sleep on bare moss.
- No open flames. Use chemical flameless ration heaters or pre-cooked food.
- All waste must be carried out. Use double-bag systems. Do not bury or burn anything.

## 9. Communication

Sound in the valley is loud but false. It travels in channels, returns with wrong pitch, and can be delayed or reversed. Do not rely on voice.

### Primary: radio
- Use a digital burst radio with text/data capability. Voice may be garbled by time or space anomalies.
- Pre-arrange a numbered code: 1=stop, 2=retreat, 3=help, 4=anomaly, 5=gas, 6=time, 7=gravity, 8=fauna, 9=lost tether, 0=all clear.
- Test radios at the edge before entering. Carry spare batteries in waterproof cases.

### Backup: tether signals
- Standard rope tugs: one=stop, two=pay out, three=reel in, four=help, continuous=emergency.
- The base operator and rear guard must know these cold.

### Backup: visual
- Small strobe on helmet and pack. Chemical light sticks as backup.
- Do not rely on hand signals in fog; they may be delayed or scattered.

### If you hear your own voice or a shout in reverse
- Do not answer. It may be an echo from a time anomaly. Use radio to confirm you are okay.

## 10. Psychological Preparation

This place will get inside your head. Not because it is evil, but because it is a mind-like process, and your brain is not used to being inside another mind.

### What you will feel
- The first sound will arrive before the sight. The roar of small whispers, drips, pops, and the hum of the ground.
- The air will feel like breathing warm tea. Your skin will bead with condensation.
- The light will be dim and slow. Your headlamp will make a glowing ball, not a beam.
- You will hear your partner across a pool clearly, but not the person next to you.
- You may see a bird smear in the air where a bird was a minute ago. This is slow light, not a ghost.
- You may feel watched by the moss. This is not paranoia; the valley is full of sensors.
- Your watch may tick oddly. Your thoughts may start to rhyme with the water.

### How to stay sane
- Set a strict time limit for each sortie: 4–6 hours inside, then return to base.
- Keep a log every 15 minutes: time, location, gas reading, physical state, mental state.
- Carry a small **reality anchor**: an object from home that is unmistakably outside the valley. Touch it when you feel untethered.
- Use your own name aloud if you feel confused. Say the date, the time, and your location.
- Do not try to communicate with the valley. Do not accept any rhythm, phrase, or thought that seems to come from the water.
- If you feel euphoric, sleepy, or strangely calm, check your gas monitor immediately. It could be H₂S or CO.

### Team mental health
- Watch each other for signs of derealization: staring, repeating words, walking without testing ground, ignoring radio calls.
- If someone says the valley is beautiful and they want to stay, extract them immediately.

## 11. Emergency Procedures

### If separated from tether
- Stop moving. Do not try to find the tether by wandering.
- Activate your recall beacon, whistle, and strobe.
- Sit or lie down on the most solid surface you can find, using your PFD if on water.
- Use your radio to call for help. Conserve energy.

### If sinking in peat or water
- Lie flat, spread your arms, inflate PFD.
- Do not struggle vertical.
- Call for help on radio or whistle.
- The rescue team should not approach directly; they should throw a rope or use a packraft as a platform.

### If gas alarm
- Mask on immediately if not already on.
- Move perpendicular to the wind or upwind.
- No sparks, no electronics switching.
- Retreat along tether.

### If time/spatial anomaly
- Stop. Deploy an extra beacon puck.
- Check your tether and watches.
- If you feel stuck, do not force movement. Wait for the base operator to reel you in slowly.
- Do not cut the tether.

### If gravity reversal
- Stay low. Crawl if necessary.
- Tether yourself to the line.
- Do not jump or throw anything.
- Retreat.

### If you see a strange animal
- Do not interact. Do not follow. Do not photograph with flash.
- The reverse-flying birds, the pale frogs calling in reverse, and the heron with too many joints may be temporally or spatially anomalous.
- Back away slowly along the tether.

### If you see a methane flare
- Do not approach. It is a pale blue flame from methane ignition.
- Keep all electronics off unless intrinsically safe.
- Move away upwind.

## 12. Decontamination and Quarantine

The valley is full of life. You do not want to bring it home.

### At the edge airlock
- Remove all outer gear in a sealed decontamination tent.
- Shower with antimicrobial soap and warm water.
- Irrigate eyes and nose with saline.
- Change into clean clothes stored outside the valley.
- Seal all used gear in leak-proof bags.

### After return
- Quarantine for at least 72 hours. Monitor for fever, respiratory symptoms, skin changes, or altered time perception.
- Report any persistent sounds, dreams of water, or a feeling that your watch is running slow.
- Do not open sealed gear bags until they can be thoroughly sterilized, vacuumed, and inspected.

## 13. What to Expect in the First Ten Minutes

You will step from the edge into a different physical order.

First, the ground will give. Not a collapse, but a slow, soft yield, like stepping onto a waterbed covered in moss. The air will be thick and cool, smelling of wet leaves, sulfur, and stone. You will hear the place before you see it: a roar made of whispers, drips, pops, and a low hum.

Your headlamp will not make a beam. It will make a soft glow in the fog. Shadows will be multiple and faint. You will hear a drip that sounds like a stone falling, but when you look, the water is still. You may see a bird-shaped smear in the air where no bird is.

Your watch may hesitate.

This is normal. Do not panic. Check your tether. Check your gas monitor. Check your buddy. Then take one slow step, and another, and remember: the valley is not a place you conquer. It is a place you visit briefly, carefully, and leave the way you came.
