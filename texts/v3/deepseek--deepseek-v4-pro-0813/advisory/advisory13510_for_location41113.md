**Preparation Guide for Visiting the Wet Mineral City**

*Before you arrive, the roar will reach you first. Put in hearing protection before the sound becomes painful. This is not a place where you stay dry, find quiet, or trust the ground. It is a hot, humid, deafening, self-remaking environment—more like the inside of a living body than a city. Your job is to be a temporary, sealed, low-impact observer who can get out safely.*

---

## 1. Environmental assumptions and risk summary

- **Air:** Warm, dense, humid, smelling of wet stone, hot metal, ozone, and animal musk. Assume it is breathable but not clean. Carry a wearable gas/particulate monitor. In enclosed or low-lying areas, oxygen may be low and CO₂ or methane may accumulate.
- **Water:** Everywhere. Warm, mineral-rich, probably saline or brackish, biologically active. Do not drink untreated water. Do not assume rain is safe; it may be mineral-laden or acidic.
- **Ground:** Low, wet, dynamic, often not solid. Canals become streets; streets become spillways. Structures form, fold, and dissolve. There is no guaranteed stable ground.
- **Noise:** Constant, stacked, deafening. You will feel it through your feet and chest.
- **Biology:** No green plants. Animals are abundant, some partially mechanical or sensor-bearing. Do not touch them. They are part of the city’s processes.
- **Heat:** Warm to hot and extremely humid. Heat index will be dangerous. Work/rest cycles are essential.

---

## 2. Physical and psychological preparation

### Heat acclimatization
Spend at least 7–14 days before departure training in hot, humid conditions—sauna, steam room, or a warm climate. Exercise in the heat to train your sweat response and hydration habits. You will sweat constantly, but in high humidity it will not evaporate well. Active cooling is more reliable than sweating.

### Water skills
Practice swimming in currents, treading water, self-rescue, and swift-water safety. You may have to cross canals, escape a suddenly emptying channel, or avoid a geyser. Wear a personal flotation device whenever near water.

### Balance and mobility
Train on wet, slick, uneven surfaces—algae-covered rocks, metal grating, shifting sand. Strengthen ankles, knees, and core. You will be walking on mineral scale, wet pavement, and surfaces that may move while you stand on them.

### Noise desensitization
You cannot acclimate to hearing damage, but you can practice functioning with earplugs and hand signals. Learn to rely on visual and tactile cues. The roar will be constant; protect your hearing from the first moment.

### Mental preparation
There is no green. The absence of plants can cause unexpected unease or grief. Bring images, sounds, or scents of forests and grasslands for your shelter. Prepare for sensory overload: constant roar, motion, heat, smell, and the knowledge that the city is alive and changing. Practice grounding techniques—focus on breath, feet, tasks.

### Accept wetness
You will never be dry. Skin maceration is a real risk. The goal is not dryness but controlled wetness and regular drying. Do not fight the water; work with it.

## 3. Clothing and personal protective equipment

- **Base layer:** Quick-dry synthetic or merino wool. No cotton. Bring at least three sets so you can rotate and dry them inside your shelter.
- **Outer layer:** Lightweight, abrasion-resistant, quick-dry pants and long-sleeve shirt. Pockets should have drain holes. Avoid heavy waterproof suits unless in heavy rain; they trap sweat and cause heat injury.
- **Rain shell:** Vented, breathable waterproof jacket/poncho for downpours. In warm rain, you may choose to get wet rather than overheat.
- **Footwear:** High-top wading boots or approach shoes with aggressive non-slip soles, drainage ports, and composite toe. Avoid steel shanks or toes—corrosion and heat. Neoprene or quick-dry socks; change socks at least twice daily. Gaiters keep debris out.
- **Gloves:** Cut-resistant, quick-dry, rubber-dipped palms for grip on wet mineral and metal. Carry spares; wet gloves cause blisters.
- **Headwear:** Wide-brim hat with chin strap, neck gaiter, and a hooded sun/rain shield. Protect from UV even through steam.
- **Eye protection:** Sealed or wraparound safety glasses with anti-fog. Carry clear and tinted lenses.
- **Respiratory protection:** At minimum an N95/P100 respirator for mineral dust and bioaerosols. A half-face respirator with multi-gas cartridges is better, especially near ozone or steam vents. Fit-test before departure.
- **Hearing protection:** Electronic earplugs or earmuffs that compress loud noise but allow speech, waterproof and sweat-resistant. Carry many foam earplugs as backup. Use a bone-conduction headset and throat mic for communication.
- **Life safety:** A slim, auto/manual inflatable PFD worn whenever near canals, docks, or low areas. A throw rope and a small knife or line cutter.
- **Skin protection:** Zinc oxide barrier cream for feet, groin, armpits; antifungal powder; antiseptic wipes. Use frequently.

## 4. Shelter, sleep, and power

Do not camp directly on the ground. It may flood, open, or be absorbed by the city. Use an elevated cot, a hammock with rainfly and bug net, or—best—a small inflatable raft or pontoon with a sealed bivy pod. Anchor with multiple quick-release lines; never tie to a single structure that may fold.

- **Shelter materials:** Waterproof, seam-sealed, marine-grade. Must tolerate constant humidity, salt, and mineral scale.
- **Inside the shelter:** Create a dry microclimate with a battery-powered dehumidifier or silica gel canisters. A small fan helps. Expect everything to remain slightly damp.
- **Sleeping bag:** Synthetic, quick-dry, warm-rated but with a vapor barrier if needed. Use a sleeping pad that can float.
- **Power:** Bring multiple sealed battery packs, a hand-crank generator, and possibly a fuel cell. Solar panels may be reduced by steam, rain, and fouling. Keep all electronics in IP68 waterproof cases with desiccant.
- **Lighting:** Waterproof headlamp, handheld light, and chemical light sticks. The city may be dark at night.

---

## 5. Water and food

- **Potable water:** Carry enough for the first 72 hours—3–4 liters per day minimum in heat, more if active.
- **Purification:** A portable desalinator/purifier is ideal because local water may be saline and mineral-heavy. Pre-filter and use a carbon filter to reduce scale. Distillation is the most thorough but energy-intensive.
- **Rainwater:** Do not rely on rain collected from city surfaces; it may be contaminated. If collecting rain, use a first-flush diverter and test.
- **Food:** Bring all food. High-energy, low-residue, sealed in waterproof packaging. Do not eat local organisms—they may be toxic, mineral-laden, or part of the city’s functioning. Eat only inside your shelter or over a catch bag to avoid crumbs.
- **Cooking:** No open flames near possible methane pockets. Use flameless ration heaters or electric cooking.

---

## 6. Navigation and communication

- **Navigation:** Do not expect GPS or magnetic compass to be reliable. Bring an inertial navigation unit if possible, plus a waterproof map on synthetic paper and a laser rangefinder.
- **Marking route:** Use retrievable, high-visibility tags or small beacon pucks. Do not rely on marks surviving—the city may absorb or overgrow them.
- **Communication:** Waterproof VHF/UHF radios with encryption, throat mics, and bone-conduction earpieces. Agree on hand and light signals. In the roar, voice may not carry more than a meter.
- **Emergency beacon:** Personal locator beacon, high-intensity strobe, signal mirror, dye markers, whistle, and a small air horn. Have an extraction plan with a pre-arranged pickup point and time window.
- **Animal behavior as warning:** Learn to read the animals. If grazers shift direction, a canal may empty. If flying things suddenly rise, there may be a gas release or geyser. They are your best early-warning system.

---

## 7. Environmental hazards and how to handle them

- **Heat and humidity:** Schedule work/rest cycles—e.g., 30 minutes work, 15 minutes shade/rest. Use cooling towels, portable fans, and electrolyte drinks. Watch for heat exhaustion and heatstroke.
- **Noise:** Wear hearing protection at all times above ground. Limit exposure. Sleep in a quiet pod with white noise or earplugs. Watch for tinnitus or muffled hearing.
- **Air quality:** Use a respirator when dust, steam, or ozone is visible or when monitors alarm. Avoid areas with strong electrical smell. In enclosed spaces, test air before entering.
- **Water hazards:** Sudden canal emptying, geysers, whirlpools, slippery edges, hidden drop-offs. Wear a PFD near water. Never wade into deep or moving water without a tether and exit plan. Watch for water flowing uphill or forming spheres; avoid walking under bridges or low areas where water may accumulate overhead.
- **Dynamic terrain:** Cracks close, walls fold, streets become staircases. Do not place hands or feet in cracks. Test surfaces before committing weight. Avoid narrow gaps, overhangs, or tall structures that may reorganize. Maintain three points of contact on climbs.
- **Animals:** Most are not aggressive, but may be large, startled, or have sharp carapaces or feelers. Give them space. Do not block their paths or feeding areas. Do not touch, tag, or collect them. Some are partially mechanical; treat them as unknown technology.
- **Biological contamination:** The water and surfaces are alive. Avoid contact with exposed skin where possible. Treat any cut or scrape immediately with antiseptic. Wash hands before eating. Monitor for infection.
- **Chemical/mineral hazards:** Salt and mineral scale abrade skin and equipment. Rinse gear with distilled water and dry. Avoid inhaling mineral dust. Do not touch wet mineral slurry; it may harden quickly and adhere.

---

## 8. Waste and contamination control

Pack out everything you bring, including human waste. Use WAG bags or a portable chemical toilet. Seal waste in double bags inside hard containers.

- Do not urinate or defecate into the water or on the ground—the city’s material cycles may incorporate it.
- Contain shed skin, hair, and nail clippings. Wear a hair cover and beard cover if possible. Use antimicrobial clothing and change in the shelter to collect fibers.
- If you must wash, use no-rinse cleanser and pack out wipes. Do not use soap in open water.
- Clean gear before departure to avoid removing local material or organisms. A soft brush and distilled water can remove mineral scale; pack the debris as waste.

---

## 9. Psychological and social rhythm

- Establish a daily routine: check gear, dry feet, eat, rest, observe. The city’s constant change can erode a sense of time; use a watch with alarms.
- Travel with at least one other person. In the roar, maintain visual contact and use radios.
- Take breaks in the shelter to escape noise and humidity. Use earplugs and an eye mask; listen to recordings of quiet nature if needed.
- The absence of green may cause subtle grief. Keep a small photo of a forest or field. Remind yourself this is a different kind of living system.
- The city’s scale and aliveness can feel sublime and overwhelming. Do not try to comprehend the whole city at once. Focus on small tasks, breathing, and the immediate environment.
- Be humble. You are a temporary guest in a self-remaking process. Move slowly, watch, and do not interfere.

---

## 10. Emergency procedures

- **Getting lost:** Stop, find a stable spot, use your beacon. Do not wander deeper. If structures shift, stay put and signal.
- **Swept by water:** Inflate PFD, float feet-first downstream, avoid strainers, swim to edge when possible. Use whistle/strobe.
- **Trapped by structure:** If a crack closes or a wall folds, do not force limbs. Use a pry bar or cutting tool if needed. Call for help on radio. Carry a personal locator beacon.
- **Heat illness:** Move to shade, remove excess clothing, cool with water/fan, sip electrolytes. If confused or unconscious, immediate extraction.
- **Inhalation/eye exposure:** Move to clean air, flush eyes with sterile saline, use oxygen if available. Bronchodilator for wheezing.
- **Wound:** Clean with antiseptic, cover, monitor for infection. In this environment, small wounds can become serious quickly.
- **Extraction:** Have a pre-arranged signal and pickup point. If the city changes the terrain, use your beacon and strobe to guide extraction. Do not delay.

---

You will not conquer this place. You will not even stay comfortable. But if you prepare well, you can visit it without becoming part of its cycles—and you may come away with the rare understanding of a world that is not a container for life but life itself, built and rebuilt in every moment.
