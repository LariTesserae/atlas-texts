# Preparation for Visiting the Sumpworks

Treat this as a confined-space industrial expedition into a hot, humid, geologically active engine room—not a sightseeing walk. The Sumpworks is not openly hostile, but it is profoundly indifferent to a human body. It will not notice you, and that is precisely what makes it dangerous. Your preparation should assume that no rescue is coming, that the heat is the primary killer, and that the machinery and rock will behave as if you are not there.

Below is a practical, deeply detailed preparation guide for an ordinary human multiverse traveler.

---

## 1. Understand What You Are Walking Into

The Sumpworks is a small, folded, subterranean city-machine inside fresh black basalt. The air is hot and heavy—near body temperature or higher, saturated with moisture. Surfaces are warm, often wet, sometimes scalding. The light is dim, orange-red, and scattered by steam. The space is extremely cramped in places, dense with pipes, catwalks, boilers, cables, hoppers, and turbines. Rain occurs every hour or two: a warm, dirty drizzle lasting about four minutes, followed by drainage down a central channel. There are no animals. The silence is thick, wet, and mechanical. The rock is young, sharp-edged, and still being cut.

You will sweat, but because the humidity is so high, sweat will not evaporate well. Your body will gain heat from the environment if the air is warmer than your skin. Exertion becomes dangerous quickly. The place is not just warm—it is a hot bath that you cannot see through and cannot lean against safely.

Your preparation must therefore center on **heat stress, sharp/mechanical hazards, respiratory irritants, low visibility, and disorientation**.

---

## 2. Medical and Physical Preparation

### 2.1 Health Screening
Before attempting this, be honest about your health. Do not go if you have:
- Heart disease, hypertension, or arrhythmia
- Asthma, COPD, or any respiratory condition
- Kidney disease or electrolyte imbalance
- Heat intolerance or previous heat injury
- Severe claustrophobia or panic disorder
- Poor balance or reduced mobility

The Sumpworks will worsen all of these.

### 2.2 Heat Acclimatization
If you have time, spend 7–10 days before the visit acclimatizing to hot, humid conditions. Exercise gently in a steam room, sauna, or hot yoga studio, but do not overdo it. This helps your body learn to sweat more efficiently and maintain core temperature. Even so, acclimatization only buys you a small margin.

### 2.3 Physical Fitness
You do not need to be an athlete, but you need:
- Good cardiovascular endurance for slow, careful movement in heat
- Upper-body strength for climbing the entry slit and possibly gripping rope or fixed lines
- Core stability and balance for walking on wet, uneven iron plates
- Flexibility to duck under low pipes and squeeze through narrow gaps

Practice walking on slick metal, climbing a fixed rope with gloves, and spending time in a hot, dark, noisy environment before going.

### 2.4 Medical Training
Take a basic first aid course covering:
- Heat exhaustion and heat stroke recognition
- Burn care
- Wound cleaning and dressing
- Steam inhalation injury
- Basic confined-space rescue

You are your own first responder. There is no ambulance in the Sumpworks.

## 3. Clothing and Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

Your clothing must balance two conflicting needs: protection from heat, steam, and sharp edges, versus not overheating in near-100% humidity. The solution is to use lightweight, fire-resistant, moisture-wicking layers with strategic ventilation, and to accept that you will be wet.

### 3.1 Base Layer
- Use a **lightweight merino wool or FR-rated synthetic** base layer. It will wick sweat and dry relatively quickly.
- Avoid pure cotton; it soaks up sweat, becomes heavy, and chafes.
- Avoid conventional polyester or nylon next to skin unless specifically FR-rated; they can melt if you brush against a hot pipe.

### 3.2 Outer Layer
- Wear a **long-sleeved shirt and full-length pants** made of fire-resistant cotton, FR cotton/nylon blend, or lightweight aramid fabric.
- Long sleeves and pants are essential, despite the heat, because the rock and metal are sharp and hot.
- Choose a fabric that is breathable but dense enough to resist snags and minor abrasions.
- Consider a one-piece FR coverall if you prefer, but make sure it has ventilation zips under the arms and on the legs.

### 3.3 Boots
This is critical. Wear:
- **Leather work boots with high heat-resistant soles** (rated for hot surfaces)
- **Steel toe and steel shank** to protect against sharp rock and falling debris
- **Slip-resistant, oil-resistant, heat-resistant outsole**—the floor will be wet and warm
- **High ankle support** for twisted footing on loose debris
- **Waterproof or at least water-resistant** upper, but be aware waterproof membranes trap sweat; bring spare socks

Do not wear rubber boots unless they are specifically heat-rated; they can melt or degrade. Leather is better.

### 3.4 Gloves
Bring two pairs:
- **Heavy heat-resistant leather or Kevlar gloves** for touching hot pipes, rock, or metal surfaces
- **Lighter, high-dexterity FR gloves** for climbing, using tools, or navigating

Never touch any surface with bare skin until you have tested it with the back of a gloved hand. Assume every pipe is hot.

### 3.5 Head and Face Protection
- **Hard hat with chin strap**—the ceiling may drip hot water, and there is potential for rock spalls or falling debris from upper catwalks.
- **Anti-fog safety glasses or goggles**—the humidity will fog cheap lenses immediately. Use double-pane, coated, or fan-assisted goggles if possible.
- **Face shield** (optional but useful) for close work near steam jets or when passing under dripping pipes.
- A **neck flap or cap** made of light FR fabric can keep warm drizzle off your neck.

### 3.6 Respirator and Air Quality
The air is hot, wet, and contains mineral dust, spores from moss, and possibly gases from furnaces or geothermal activity. Bring:
- A **half-face respirator with P100 particulate filters** as a minimum.
- If possible, carry **multi-gas cartridges** (organic vapor, acid gas) to handle unknown fumes.
- A **personal gas monitor** that measures O2, CO, H2S, and LEV (lower explosive limit). The Sumpworks has open furnaces, steam, and confined spaces—carbon monoxide and low oxygen are real risks.
- Check the gas monitor before removing your respirator. If O2 is below 19.5%, or if CO/H2S is elevated, leave immediately.

In such high heat and humidity, wearing a respirator is exhausting. Use it when necessary, but if the gas monitor shows clean air and you are in an area without visible dust or spores, you may switch to a lighter N95/P100 mask. Do not stay without respiratory protection if you see steam carrying mineral scale or moss spores.

### 3.7 Hearing Protection
The turbine hum, steam hiss, and occasional grinding are not extremely loud, but they are constant. Consider **electronic earplugs or earmuffs** that allow you to hear conversation and warning sounds while reducing background noise. Do not use solid foam earplugs that block all sound; you need to hear rock creaks, water shifts, and mechanical changes.

### 3.8 Knee and Elbow Pads
The floor is iron plates over rock, often sharp and hot. You may need to crouch, kneel, or squeeze through low passages. Lightweight flexible knee pads and elbow pads will prevent cuts and burns.

## 4. Hydration, Cooling, and Food

### 4.1 Water and Electrolytes
Despite the wet air, you will lose a lot of fluid through sweat. Because sweat does not evaporate, you may not notice how much you are losing. Plan for **1 liter of water per hour**, more if you are actively moving.

- Carry water in **insulated containers or a hydration bladder with an insulating sleeve** to keep it from becoming hot.
- Add **electrolyte tablets or powder** to at least half your water. You need sodium, potassium, and magnesium.
- Drink small amounts every 10–15 minutes, even if you do not feel thirsty.
- Do not drink local water. It may be briny, mineral-laden, or contaminated by machine oils. Treat all condensation, channel water, and pipe drips as non-potable.

### 4.2 Cooling Strategies
Sweat will not cool you effectively in near-100% humidity. You need external cooling.

- Bring a **phase-change cooling vest** charged before entry. It can provide 2–4 hours of relief.
- Carry **instant cold packs** or **small ice packs** to place on the neck, wrists, and groin during breaks.
- A battery-powered fan helps only if the air temperature is lower than your skin temperature. In the Sumpworks, the air may be equal to or hotter than your body, so a fan may actually add heat. Use it only near the cooler condenser banks or near the entry crack.
- A **cooling towel** will not work well in high humidity, but if you wet it with your own clean water and it is exposed to dry heat near the furnaces, it may provide brief evaporative cooling.
- Rest often in the coolest available spot, likely near the **condenser banks** where the brine pipes run, or near the entry slit where fresh surface air may spill down.

### 4.3 Food
Bring high-energy, easy-to-eat food that does not require cooking:
- Energy gels, chews, or bars
- Nuts or trail mix (in a sealed container)
- Electrolyte gummies
- Avoid large meals; digestion raises body temperature.

Eat small amounts regularly. Heat suppresses appetite, but you need fuel.

## 5. Navigation, Lighting, and Communication

### 5.1 Lighting
The Sumpworks is dim, orange-red, and full of steam. Your lighting must be powerful but not blinding.

- **Primary headlamp** with adjustable brightness and a wide beam. Use low or medium setting up close. High beams in steam will reflect back and create a whiteout.
- **Handheld flashlight** with a focused beam for distance. Choose one with a warm/amber LED if possible; warm light penetrates steam better than blue-white.
- **Spare batteries** in waterproof cases. Heat and humidity drain batteries faster.
- **Chemical light sticks** as emergency backup and for marking.
- If there is any chance of flammable gases, use **intrinsically safe lights** rated for hazardous environments. Do not bring a device that could spark.

Avoid relying solely on the chamber's own lamps or furnace glow. They are too dim and too scattered.

### 5.2 Navigation
There is no GPS, no sky, and no day/night. The layout is dense and confusing.

- Bring **high-visibility route markers**—plastic or metal tags that can be clipped to pipes or placed on the floor. Avoid adhesive tape; it will fail in heat and moisture.
- Carry a **reel of thin Kevlar or polyester cord** to lay a lifeline from the entry if you plan to explore beyond line of sight. This is especially important in the cramped lower sections.
- A compass may be unreliable due to the massive amount of iron and electrical equipment. Do not trust it absolutely.
- Take notes and draw a simple map as you go, using the central channel, turbine pit, and condenser banks as landmarks.
- Pay attention to the **rain cycle**. Time between rain events can help you gauge how long you have been inside.

### 5.3 Timekeeping
Bring a **waterproof watch with a vibration alarm**. You may not hear an audible alarm over the hiss and drip. Use it to:
- Track how long you have been inside
- Anticipate the next rain cycle
- Set a hard turnaround time

### 5.4 Communication
- If traveling with a buddy, use **two-way radios** with headsets, but understand that thick rock and metal may block signals. Keep line-of-sight or use relay.
- Agree on **hand signals** and **whistle codes** before entering.
- If alone, leave a detailed plan with someone outside, including check-in times. If you do not check in by a certain time, they should attempt to locate you.
- A personal locator beacon may not work underground, but if you have one, bring it anyway for the surface.

## 6. Safety Procedures and Hazard Management

### 6.1 Heat Illness
Know the signs:
- **Heat exhaustion**: heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea, cool clammy skin, rapid pulse.
- **Heat stroke**: confusion, hot dry skin (or sweating stops), body temperature above 104°F/40°C, loss of consciousness. This is a medical emergency.

If you feel unwell, **stop immediately**, move to the coolest area, hydrate with electrolytes, apply cold packs to neck/armpits/groin, and if symptoms do not improve within 10–15 minutes, leave the Sumpworks.

Set a strict time limit. For a first visit, do not stay underground longer than **2–3 hours** without a long rest at the surface. Even if you feel fine, the heat load accumulates.

### 6.2 Burns and Steam
- Assume every pipe, valve, and iron plate is hot. Test with the back of a gloved hand before touching.
- Never stand directly over or under a visible steam leak. Steam jets can be invisible and extremely hot.
- Watch for fog on pipes—it may indicate a high-temperature surface.
- If you are caught in the rain cycle, it is warm, not scalding, but it may carry mineral scale and oil. Protect your eyes and any open wounds.
- If burned, cool with **clean water from your own supply**, not local water. Cover with a sterile dressing. Do not apply ointments immediately.

### 6.3 Sharp Edges and Slips
- The rock and metal are sharp-edged. Move slowly and deliberately.
- Use a **walking stick or probe** made of fiberglass or wood to test floor plates, edges, and surfaces. It can also help you gauge the depth of puddles and the stability of debris.
- During and after the rain cycle, all surfaces become slick. Stay away from the central channel's banks—they are knife-sharp and may be slippery.
- Use three points of contact when climbing or descending.

### 6.4 Machinery
The Sumpworks is an active industrial site. Machines run regardless of your presence.

- **Do not touch any valve, lever, gauge, or control.**
- Avoid the turbine pit, hoppers, conveyor chains, and any moving part.
- Listen for automated hammers chipping scale—they can throw sharp debris. Wear eye protection.
- Do not stand under sagging cables or near open furnaces.
- Do not lean on pipes; they may be under pressure or extremely hot.
- If a machine begins to move or make a new sound, back away slowly.

### 6.5 Rock Stability
The rock is young and actively settling. You may hear deep, grinding creaks. That is the rock adjusting.

- Avoid the north end where drilling machines are cutting. That area has higher risk of rockfall, vibration, and unstable ground.
- Do not linger under fresh cracks or loose-looking spalls.
- Wear your hard hat at all times.
- If you hear a sudden, sharp crack or feel the floor shift, move cautiously toward the exit, watching for falling debris.

### 6.6 Air Quality
- Use your gas monitor continuously.
- If the air becomes difficult to breathe, or if you feel headache, dizziness, or nausea, leave immediately.
- Steam can displace oxygen, especially in low areas. The heat and humidity may mask the early signs of hypoxia.
- Avoid breathing directly from steam jets; they may carry dissolved minerals or gases.

### 6.7 Rain Cycle Protocol
The rain arrives every hour or two and lasts about four minutes. It is not dangerous by itself, but it changes conditions:

- When you hear the condenser banks begin to tremble, pause and find a stable spot away from the central channel.
- Cover electronics and any open bags.
- Lower your head and let the drizzle pass. It will reduce visibility and make surfaces slick.
- After the rain, watch your footing. The floor will be wet, and the channel will be draining.
- Use the rain cycle as a natural time marker. Do not try to move quickly during it.

## 7. Psychological Preparation

This may be the hardest part. The Sumpworks is quiet, but not peacefully quiet. It has the low hum of machines, the hiss of steam, the regular drip of water, and the occasional deep creak of rock. It is dark, hot, and close. There are no animals, no birds, no insects. You will feel like the only living thing in a giant lung.

- **Expect claustrophobia.** The path is narrow. You can touch both walls at once in places. Pipes hang low. The ceiling disappears in steam. If you are prone to claustrophobia, this place will trigger it.
- **Expect disorientation.** There is no day, no sky, no wind. The only cycle is the rain. Without a watch, you will lose track of time quickly.
- **Expect a sense of being swallowed.** The air is so wet and warm that your skin feels like just another damp surface. The silence swallows your voice. Your footsteps may be the loudest sound, which can make you feel both huge and tiny.
- **The machines will not acknowledge you.** This can be eerie. The city runs itself. You are not needed, not noticed, not relevant. This can lead to a strange feeling of unreality or panic.

To cope:
- Move slowly and breathe deliberately.
- Focus on small tasks: check the time, note the next rain cycle, take a sip of water, adjust your light.
- Use a buddy if at all possible. The presence of another person is the single best psychological anchor.
- Set small goals—reach a landmark, wait out one rain cycle, return to entry—rather than trying to "explore" all at once.
- If you feel panic rising, stop, put your hand on a cool (or cooler) surface, and breathe out slowly. Remind yourself that the exit is marked and you can leave at any time.
- Do not let the heat and the quiet convince you to stay longer than planned. That is how accidents happen.

## 8. Arrival Protocol: First Hour

When you first descend through the slit, do not immediately push into the chamber. Instead:

1. **Secure the entry.** Check the crack for stability. Set a rope if needed. Mark the entry with two bright lights or reflective tags that can be seen through steam.
2. **Put on all PPE.** Respirator ready, gas monitor on, hard hat, gloves.
3. **Check air and temperature.** Use your gas monitor. Assess the heat. If the air is above body temperature or O2 is low, limit your time drastically.
4. **Set a base just inside the entry.** Place your pack, cooling vest, and water in a stable, dry-ish spot. This is your refuge.
5. **Wait for one full rain cycle.** Observe where the water falls, where it drains, how the sound changes. This teaches you the rhythm and shows you safe places to stand.
6. **Plan your first short exploration.** Go only a few dozen strides from base, then return. Do not try to see everything at once.
7. **Set a turnaround time.** For a first visit, 2–3 hours maximum underground, including the observation rain cycle.

## 9. Rules of Conduct

The Sumpworks is not a ruin to be looted or a wilderness to be tamed. It is a working system. Treat it as you would a live engine room:

- **Do not touch valves, levers, gauges, or controls.**
- **Do not remove or damage any equipment, even if it looks old or abandoned.**
- **Do not disturb the vegetation.** The ferns, moss, and root-threads act as living seals. Pulling them could cause steam leaks or disrupt the city's self-repair.
- **Do not take souvenirs.** The pressure gauge in the chapel-like alcove, the spare valves, the mineral scale—all of it is part of the place.
- **Pack out all trash and waste.** There are no scavengers here. Anything you leave will stay.
- **Do not introduce open flames or flammable substances.**
- **Move as a guest, not an owner.** The city will not notice you, but your actions can still harm it—or you.

## 11. Final Intuition

Visiting the Sumpworks will feel less like exploring a city and more like being allowed into the chest cavity of something vast and waking. The heat will press on you. The steam will fill your ears. The sharp rock and iron will make every movement deliberate. The rain will come and go like a slow, metallic heartbeat. And through it all, the place will continue to build itself deeper, indifferent to your presence.

Your job is not to conquer it or understand it fully. Your job is to **return safely**, with your lungs intact, your skin unburned, and your mind clear enough to remember the strange, hot, dripping silence of a city that breathes without living.

Prepare for heat. Prepare for wet. Prepare for the dark. Then move slowly, listen to the rhythm, and leave before the place has a chance to take your heat tolerance to its limit.
