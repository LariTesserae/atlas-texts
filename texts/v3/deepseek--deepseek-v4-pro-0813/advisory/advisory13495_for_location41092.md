**Preparation for Below the Salt Pan: The Fourth Stator**

Because you are an ordinary human multiverse traveler, assume your body is the most fragile and the most contaminating thing in the room. You are a wet, warm, irregular, time-bound animal entering a dry, electrically alive, mechanically closed system that has already reached a fixed point. The goal is not to survive monsters; it is to enter, observe, avoid becoming part of the machine, and leave without breaking the loop.

---

## 1. Understand what visiting will be like

You will descend 87 rungs through salt and black anhydrite. The air will shift from dry to warmer, then blood-warm. The first thing you notice will be the sound: a soft, metallic 78-beat-per-minute pulse, a deep hum, and a thousand tiny dry clicks, all arriving before you can see the chamber. It never varies.

At the bottom, the air will pull at your lips and the inside of your nostrils. It will smell faintly of hot iron, ozone, and burnt borax. The light will be dim: small electric arcs in glass tubes, red tungsten filaments behind steel cages, blue static discharge near nests, and, every 3.7 seconds, a green bulb flashing on a control cabinet.

The floor will crunch or clack underfoot, carpeted with living and shed shells packed like shingles. The walls are raw anhydrite, chisel-pocked, pyrite glinting. Nothing is rounded. There are no water stains, no dripstone, no salt crusts. It looks newly cut and then stopped in time.

After ten or fifteen minutes, the deeper effect will start. The sound stops being noticeable. The green flash becomes a kind of metronome. The flywheel turns at exactly 78 rpm and does not tremble. The dust hangs in fixed spirals. The room has no weather, no day, no season, and no arrow of time. If you stay too long, you may begin to feel calm in a way that is not safe. That calm is the room trying to make you a component.

## 2. Medical and fitness prerequisites

Do not attempt this visit if you have:

- a cardiac pacemaker, implanted defibrillator, insulin pump, neurostimulator, or any active implanted electronic device; the electromagnetic fields, static discharge, and charged surfaces may interfere with it.
- significant cardiac arrhythmia or uncontrolled hypertension; the sustained low-frequency 78-bpm pulse and vibration can be physically unsettling.
- severe claustrophobia, panic disorder, or sensory-processing problems; the chamber is enclosed, monotonous, and loud.
- an acute respiratory condition; the air is dry and likely contains fine dust, ozone, and possibly other arc byproducts.
- open wounds, significant cuts, or recent surgery; the environment is sharp, dry, and biologically strange.

Before you go:

- Have a confined-space medical clearance.
- Drink water and take electrolytes in the 24 hours before entry.
- Moisturize your lips, nose, and hands with oil-based products, not water-based creams. The chamber is so dry that your lips and nasal passages will crack quickly.
- Cover any small abrasion with an occlusive dressing. Bleeding is not just a medical problem here; it introduces water into a system that has none.

## 3. Training

An ordinary human should not enter the Fourth Stator without practicing:

- climbing an 87-rung vertical shaft while wearing full gear;
- moving in a confined space with double hearing protection and a full-face respirator;
- electrical safety: arc-flash awareness, shock avoidance, and rescue with a non-conductive hook;
- being physically still in a hot, noisy, dim space for at least 30 minutes without touching anything;
- mental discipline: checking a timer, speaking to a partner, and resisting the urge to stare at the flywheel.

If you cannot do these in a safe simulation, do not go.

## 4. Clothing and PPE

Dress for a dry, hot, electrically live, mechanically dangerous room. Do not wear shorts, sandals, loose sleeves, dangling straps, or anything that can catch in moving machinery.

### Base layer

- Close-fitting, moisture-wicking long-sleeve shirt and long pants.
- No exposed skin below the collar.

### Outer layer

- Flame-resistant, anti-static coverall with high-visibility strips.
- No loose drawstrings, no open cuffs.
- If you are especially concerned about contamination, add a disposable anti-static Tyvek over-suit, but be aware it can cause overheating. For most first visits, the breathable flame-resistant coverall is safer.

### Head and face

- Hard hat with chin strap.
- Full-face respirator, ideally with P100 particulate filters and nuisance vapor/acid gas cartridges for ozone, nitrogen oxides, and fine mineral dust.
- If you do not use a full-face respirator, use tight-fitting safety goggles with indirect vents.
- Arc-flash hood or face shield available for close approach to the generator or bus-bars.

### Hearing

The room is loud enough that hearing protection is required. The stencil on the wall says so.

Use double protection:

- foam earplugs with a high noise reduction rating, inserted correctly;
- over-the-ear electronic earmuffs so you can still hear radio communication or alarms at a reduced level.

Do not remove earplugs once you are inside. The noise is so constant that you will stop noticing it, but it is still damaging.

### Hands

- Thin cut-resistant liner glove.
- Electrical-insulating rubber glove, especially if you will come near bus-bars, knife switches, or the generator.
- Leather or Kevlar over-glove for sharp shells, basalt edges, and hot surfaces.

Carry spare gloves. You may need to change if a glove becomes conductive, wet, or punctured.

### Feet

- Dielectric, puncture-resistant, heat-resistant boots.
- Composite toe preferred; steel toe can conduct electricity if contact is bad.
- Ankle-high lacing, fully covered by the coverall leg.
- Do not use boots with exposed metal eyelets or shanks.

## 5. Load-out: what to carry

### Air and gas monitoring

- Multigas detector for oxygen, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, and volatile organic compounds.
- If possible, an ozone sensor. The arcs and static discharge may produce ozone.
- Lower the gas monitor into the shaft or chamber before entry. Do not enter until you know the atmosphere is safe.

### Breathing

- Full-face or half-face elastomeric respirator with P100/OV/AG cartridges.
- Spare cartridges in sealed bags.
- An emergency escape air bottle if there is any chance of oxygen deficiency.
- A mouth/nose exhalation cover or moisture-capture filter if you want to minimize the moisture you add to the air. Your exhaled breath is a plume of warm, wet, salt-scented air. In a room with no water, that is contamination.

### Hydration and food

- 2–3 liters of electrolyte solution in a sealed bladder with a drink tube routed under your mask.
- No open cups or bottles. You cannot set them down, and spills would be harmful.
- High-calorie, low-crumb food: paste packs, gel packs, nut butter sachets. Do not bring crackers, energy bars that crumble, or anything that sheds.

### Skin and eye care

- Oil-based lip balm.
- Mineral-oil-based lotion for exposed skin.
- Sterile saline eye drops for dryness.
- Nasal gel or oil-based nasal moisturizer.
- Do not use water-based sunscreen or lotions inside the chamber.

### Lighting

- Intrinsically safe headlamp with a red mode and a white mode.
- Handheld intrinsically safe flashlight.
- Chemical light sticks as backup.
- Avoid bright white light at first. It will overwhelm your dark adaptation and may disturb the creatures.

### Communication

- Two-way radio with an earpiece that fits under your earmuffs.
- A throat microphone or bone-conduction microphone is better than a boom mic.
- A hard-wired communication reel as backup if radio is blocked by rock or machine noise.

### Timekeeping

- Two independent watches or timers.
- One timer left at the surface with a partner.
- Do not rely on the green 3.7-second flash as a clock. It is not for you.

### Egress and climbing

- Full-body climbing or confined-space harness.
- Vertical lifeline and rope access equipment.
- Two lanyards with locking carabiners.
- Ascenders/descenders suitable for the shaft.
- Do not trust the 87 rungs alone. One of them has a wire-plant shaped like an open hand growing on it.

### Medical

- Hemostatic dressings for cuts.
- Dry burn dressings.
- Antihistamine.
- Electrolyte tablets.
- Blister kit.
- A small pair of tweezers for shell splinters.

### Waste and contamination control

- Urine collection system if you are staying long.
- Sealable plastic bags for any waste.
- Lint-free wipes.
- A small dry brush for removing dust from clothing before you use water at the surface.

### Tools

- Non-contact voltage detector.
- Insulated probe.
- Non-sparking utility knife.
- Small mirror for looking around corners or into dark cabinets.
- Do not bring unnecessary tools. Every extra object is a potential dropped item, and dropped items do not come back.

## 6. Entry and descent

Before you descend:

1. Confirm the sound pattern: 78 bpm pulse, hum, mite clicks. If it varies, stutters, or stops, do not enter.
2. Watch or listen for the green flash every 3.7 seconds. If it is absent or irregular, leave.
3. Set a maximum visit time. For a first visit, 45 minutes inside the chamber is a sensible hard limit.
4. Tell someone on the surface exactly when you are going and when you must be back.
5. Lower a gas monitor on a line. Check oxygen and contaminants.
6. Install a fall-arrest line.

On the ladder:

- Move slowly and deliberately.
- Do not grab a rung without looking at it. The black wire-plants may be hair-thin and glossy, with tiny glassy beads at their tips. They orient to the electric field. They may be charged.
- Pause halfway to let your body adjust to the heat and noise.
- Keep your mouth closed or breathe through your nose behind the respirator.

---

## 7. Inside the chamber: movement and behavior

When you reach the bottom:

- Stand still for 60 seconds. Let your eyes adjust to the dim light. Find the exit in your memory and mark it with a bright non-conductive line or a small strobe.
- Do not step off the last rung until you have looked at the floor. It is covered in living and shed shells, packed like shingles. Some are alive.
- Move with a slow, wide stance. Step on bare basalt edges where possible. Avoid crushing shells.
- Stay at least 1.5 meters from the flywheel rim at all times. The flywheel is 12 feet across and turns at 78 rpm. Its rim speed is roughly 15 meters per second, enough to take fingers, clothing, or hair before you feel it.
- Do not reach into the plane of rotation.
- Do not walk under sagging bus-bars or low cable trays with your head up. The ceiling is a false one of cable trays and pipe runs.
- Use red light first. White light may disrupt the arc-moths or camber mites.
- If you need to touch a surface, test it first with a voltage detector, then with an insulated probe. Never touch metal, wire-plants, shells, or the generator with bare skin.

## 8. Rules of non-interference

The chamber is a closed loop. The machine and the animals are not separate. The brushes are grown from a wall-animal. The flywheel is balanced by balanced beetles. The whisker-eel’s touch on the bus-bar is part of the control circuit. Your only safe role is witness.

Therefore:

- Do not remove, relocate, or handle any animal, shell, plant, tool, key, helmet, or stone.
- Do not sit on the booth stools, the lockers, or the floor.
- Do not write on the clipboard. The top page says “Check brush wear.” Leave it.
- Do not take the brass key.
- Do not put down a coin, a bag, or any object to test the dust. The dust is already shaped around something missing. A new object will not dent it; the room has no place for new history.
- Do not block the whisker-eel’s touch on the bus-bar. If a touch is due, keep clear.
- Do not feed, water, or cool any creature.
- Do not apply water to any surface, even for first aid. Use dry dressings and leave the chamber.
- Do not clean brush wear, adjust relays, or touch oil. There is no maintenance task for you here.

## 9. Creature and machine interaction

### Camber mites

Lentil-sized, armored in copper-oxide plates. They live on the flywheel rim in long radial rows and move in synchronized waves. Do not touch the flywheel rim. Do not break their wave. Do not put your hand near the rotating rim. Their claws on steel sound like rain that never changes.

### Arc-moths

Pale, mineralized, phase-locked to the arc waveform. They form a fixed spiral around the arc tubes. Do not swat them. Do not shine bright light into the spiral. Do not breathe on them. They may be electrically live.

### Blind whisker-eels

They live in cable trays and move only every quarter-hour. They touch a bus-bar with one long whisker, closing a circuit, and a green bulb flashes. Treat them as live conductors. Do not touch the eel or the bus-bar. If you are near a relay or bus-bar at the wrong moment, you could become the circuit.

### Clapper-mites

They live between relay contacts and pour in and out as the relay opens and closes. Their mass movement creates a roar of tiny rattling. Do not put your fingers anywhere near relay gaps. They occupy the gap exactly as it opens, like water entering a lock. Your finger will not be accommodated.

### Balance-beetles

Fist-sized, with heavy mandibles of chilled iron. They cling at equal intervals on the axle. They are part of the rotating mass. If one is removed, the bearing warms and another beetle climbs from the floor to replace it. Do not touch them. Do not help them. If you accidentally dislodge one, move away from the bearing and watch for heat.

### Black wire-plants and lichen

Black wire-plants grow in gaps where rock meets a warm conductor. They may be charged. The pale blue-gray lichen on porcelain insulators is brittle and smells faintly of burnt borax. Do not touch or collect either. The lichen’s perfect circles are not decorative.

## 10. Psychological and temporal discipline

The room has no horizon, no sky, no water, and no arrow of time. The flywheel turns like a heart that cannot learn a new rhythm. This can do strange things to a human mind.

- Do not stare at the flywheel, the arc-moth spiral, or the green flash for more than a few seconds. These are hypnotic.
- Use a personal timer with a different rhythm, such as a 90-beat-per-minute vibration, to keep your body from entraining to the 78 bpm pulse.
- Check your watch. Do not count the green flashes. They are not seconds.
- Speak to your partner on the radio every few minutes. If you stop speaking, the partner should call you back. If there is no answer, they should begin retrieval.
- If you start to feel peaceful, or as if you belong there, or as if leaving would be too much effort, leave immediately. That is not a genuine insight. It is the room’s fixedness pressing on your mind.
- Do not sit, lean, or sleep. Do not let yourself become a still object in the dust.

The chamber has a way of making humans feel like they are already part of the machine. Resist that.

## 11. Emergency plan

### If the sound changes or stops

Leave immediately. The loop has been disturbed or is aware of you.

### If the green flash changes interval or stops

Leave immediately. The control circuit is not yours to repair.

### If you are shocked

Do not grab anything. If you are conscious and can move, step away with small steps, then ascend. If you cannot move, your partner must use a non-conductive rescue hook. Do not touch the person until they are clear of the circuit.

### If you are cut

Use dry hemostatic dressing. Apply pressure. Leave the chamber. Do not let blood drip on the floor. Blood is water, and this room has no water.

### If you drop something

Let it go. Do not chase it into the flywheel, the relays, or the cable trays. It is now a feature of the Fourth Stator.

### If you see a dust-shaped absence

Do not step into it. The dust is already shaped around something missing. Treat such spaces as occupied. Do not set a coin, a boot, or a hand in them.

## 12. Exit and decontamination

When your time is up, leave.

1. Signal your partner.
2. Move slowly back to the shaft.
3. Clip into the ladder line before climbing.
4. Ascend with care. The noise will fade, but the heat may linger.
5. At the surface, do not pour water on your boots or gear. Brush off dust and shells with a dry brush.
6. Sip electrolyte solution. Do not gulp plain water.
7. Check your skin for cuts, embedded shells, or small wire-plant fragments.
8. Wash with water only after you are well away from the shaft opening.
9. Write down your actual elapsed time. Compare it with the surface clock. If there is a discrepancy, report it to your multiversal authority before traveling elsewhere.

## Summary

For the Fourth Stator, prepare as if you are entering a dry, hot, electrically live engine room that is also a living animal and a closed loop. Wear full coverage, protect your ears and lungs, carry no more than you need, drink electrolytes, and use oil-based skin protection. Move like a careful visitor in a place that does not want to be disturbed. Do not touch the machine, the creatures, or the dust. Set a hard time limit and leave before you feel comfortable.

The chamber does not need you. It has reached a fixed point. The best visit is one that leaves no mark, no water, and no new rhythm. Go in, look, and get out before the flywheel teaches your heart to beat at 78 times a minute.
