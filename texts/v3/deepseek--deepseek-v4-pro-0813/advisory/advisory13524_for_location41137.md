**Preparing for the Choral Pocket: A Field Guide for the Ordinary Human Multiverse Traveler**

The Choral Pocket is not hostile in the way of predators or storms. It is hostile by indifference: cold, thin, desiccating, chemically strange, acoustically saturated, and so tightly packed that a single careless step can break something that has been still for millennia. You are an ordinary human—a warm, wet, noisy, time-bound creature—about to enter a place that has replaced time with a chord. Plan accordingly.

### What to Expect
You will feel the cold as a kind of theft. The air will pull moisture from your lips, your lungs, your eyes. The sound will not just be heard; it will live in your sternum, your teeth, the fine bones of your ears. The light will be gray and tired, as if the sun has given up. The forest of mineral growth will look dense enough to be a solid mass, and it will not move for you. You will lose your sense of time. You may feel an urge to stay, to become part of the equilibrium. That is not supernatural; it is sensory monotony. Recognize it and resist it.

---

### Before You Go

- **Acclimatize to altitude.** The plateau is high enough that unacclimatized exertion will produce headache, nausea, and poor judgment. Spend at least three to five days at progressively higher camps if possible. Consider acetazolamide after medical advice. If you have any history of high-altitude pulmonary or cerebral edema, do not go.
- **Train for the squeeze.** The entry slit is no wider than a person. Practice moving through narrow passages while wearing your pack, or better, practice pushing and pulling gear ahead of you. Claustrophobia is a real risk, not a weakness.
- **Dental and medical check.** The standing wave will vibrate your teeth and sternum. Loose fillings, middle-ear problems, or cardiac rhythm issues are reasons to stay outside. If you have any implant, consult a doctor about vibration exposure.
- **Psychological preparation.** Inside, there is no weather, no animal life, no change of light. The sound is unending. For some, this induces a dreamy detachment; for others, panic. Train yourself to recognize both. The Pocket does not care which you feel.
- **Do not go alone.** Minimum team of three: one outside as anchor and monitor, two inside as buddies. The outside person should have a radio, a clock, and the ability to haul on a guide line if needed.

---

### Clothing and Physical Protection

- **Layer for cold, dry air.** A few degrees above freezing, with no sun and moving cold air at the entrance, means you will lose heat steadily. Use a moisture-wicking base layer, thick insulation, and a windproof, abrasion-resistant outer shell. Avoid cotton.
- **Cover every inch of skin.** The mineralized stems are sharp and may be brittle. Wear a durable coverall, cut-resistant gloves over warm liners, high-top mountaineering boots with stiff soles, and gaiters. A helmet is mandatory—not for falling rock, but for the moment you slip and the forest catches you.
- **Protect your eyes.** Safety glasses or goggles, because a stem can whip back. The light is dim and gray; bring clear or lightly tinted lenses, not dark.
- **No dangling straps.** The vibration will make every loose strap rattle and every buckle click. Tape or secure all straps, laces, and equipment loops. Silence your gear as much as possible; the Pocket already has its own sound.

---

### Breathing and Air

- **Bring a multi-gas detector.** Before entering, sample the air through the slit. You need to know oxygen, flammable gas, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, and ideally ozone. The smell of ozone and acetylene is a warning, not a measurement. If oxygen is below 19.5% or flammable gas above 10% LEL, do not enter.
- **Use a respirator.** An air-purifying respirator with organic vapor/acid gas cartridges and P100 filters may not remove all the gas, especially ozone, but it will reduce irritation. Better is a supplied-air or self-contained system, but the bulk may be prohibitive. At minimum, carry an emergency escape respirator. Do not rely on a bandana.
- **Carry supplemental oxygen.** The altitude plus the enclosed pocket may push you into hypoxia. A small cylinder with a demand valve can be a lifeline. Even a few liters per minute by nasal cannula under your mask can keep you thinking clearly.
- **Manage moisture.** The air will steal water from your lips, throat, and lungs with every breath. Pre-hydrate. Use a moisture-exchange mask if possible. Bring lip balm, saline nasal spray, and eye drops. Do not drink alcohol or caffeine for 24 hours before.

---

### Hydration and Nutrition

- **Bring water in insulated containers.** The air is above freezing, but your water will still chill. Use a hydration bladder with a bite valve, but be careful: any spill is a foreign event. Sip frequently, but do not let the tube drip.
- **Eat before entry.** Do not eat inside. Crumbs are an insult. If you must have energy, use a gel packet you can consume without removing your mask, and pack out the empty wrapper.
- **Plan for waste.** There is no place to relieve yourself inside that would not violate the equilibrium. Go before entry. If you must, use a sealable container or adult diaper. Pack out everything, including urine. The Pocket has no water; do not introduce any.

---

### Hearing and Vibration

- **Wear hearing protection.** The sound is louder than a factory floor. Earplugs, preferably high-NRR foam plugs, under earmuffs. However, the vibration will bypass earplugs through your bones. You will still feel the chord in your chest and teeth.
- **Limit exposure.** Prolonged vibration can cause fatigue, nausea, and vibroacoustic syndrome. Start with short visits—thirty to forty-five minutes—then exit to the relative quiet outside. Do not try to “get used to it.”
- **Monitor yourself and your team.** If someone develops a headache, heart palpitations, dizziness, or ringing in the ears, leave immediately. The sound does not stop; it will not give you a break.

---

### Navigation, Communication, and Time

- **Use a guide line.** Run a thin, slick, brightly colored cord from outside the slit to your deepest point. Tie it to a solid anchor outside. Inside the dense growth, you may lose sight of the entrance within a few meters. The guide line is your way back.
- **Communication.** Voice will be swallowed. Use two-way radios with noise-canceling headsets or bone-conduction microphones. Test them before entering. Agree on hand signals: one tug means “okay,” two means “stop,” three means “emergency, pull me out.”
- **Timekeeping.** The Pocket destroys your sense of time. Use a mechanical wristwatch and a separate countdown timer on a lanyard. Set alarms. The vibration may stop one device; carry two.
- **Set a return beacon outside.** If you use multiversal travel, calibrate your return device in open air, not in the slit. The mineral mass and standing wave may interfere with signals. Make sure you can leave from outside if something goes wrong.

---

### Tools and Safety Equipment

- **Lighting.** The twilight is enough to see shapes, but not detail. Bring an intrinsically safe LED headlamp and a handheld flashlight. Avoid open flames and anything that could spark; the gas may be flammable. Red light preserves night vision but washes out the already muted colors; use a high-CRI white light for detail.
- **Cutting tool.** A sharp knife or shears for emergency entanglement. Use only if you are trapped. Breaking a stem permanently changes the Pocket’s harmony; do not do it casually.
- **First aid.** Bandages, antiseptic, eye wash, thermal blanket, bivy sack, and any personal medications. A small trauma kit for lacerations—the stems are sharp.
- **Rope and harness.** A lightweight climbing harness and a short rope for safety in the narrow slit. You may not need to rappel, but the slit is an awkward squeeze; a harness can prevent a fall or allow extraction.
- **Camera and notebook.** Document from a distance. Use a solid-state camera; vibration may damage moving parts. Write notes outside, not inside—your hands will be cold and the sound will make concentration hard.

---

### Approach and Entry

- **Plan your ascent.** The plateau is remote, cold, and cloud-roofed. Use GPS and a map. Travel in daylight, but expect gray light. The slit may be hard to find; mark its coordinates and take bearings.
- **Enter slowly.** The slit is no wider than a person. Remove your pack and push it ahead or drag it on a rope. Go sideways if needed. The cold air pouring out will feel like a wind; your goggles may fog. Pause just inside to let your senses adjust—though they never will fully.
- **Do not rush.** The Pocket has been there for a long time. It will not change while you enter.

---

### Inside the Pocket: Rules of Conduct

- **Move as if in a museum made of glass.** Every stem, frond, and blade is part of a single equilibrium. Do not touch anything with bare skin. Do not lean, do not kneel, do not rest your backpack against the growth. If there is no space to pass, turn back.
- **Watch your footing.** The floor is mineral pavement, not soil. It may be uneven, and the growth hides holes or cables. Use a trekking pole or probe to test each step.
- **Do not interfere with the gas stream.** The copper pipe exhaling gas is not a leak; it is breath. Do not try to close it, do not put your face near it, do not take a sample by hand. Observe only.
- **Do not attempt to read the pressure gauge with your fingers.** The old machinery is part of the forest. Touching it may damage a surface that has not been touched in centuries. Use a light to look, not to touch.
- **Expect the sound to feel like pressure.** It is not just in your ears; it is in your chest, your jaw, your sinuses. It may make your own voice seem thin and irrelevant. Communicate by hand signals or radio.
- **Expect the cold to feel like theft.** The air will pull moisture from your lips and lungs. Your breath will fog and vanish. Your skin will dry and tighten. Drink, but do not spill.
- **Expect time to slip.** After ten minutes, you may feel you have been there for hours. After an hour, you may feel no time has passed. Trust your timers, not your feelings.
- **If you accidentally break something, stop.** Do not try to fix it. Note the location, take a photograph if possible, and leave the area. The Pocket will settle into a new equilibrium, but you have changed it forever. Do not make it worse by thrashing.
- **Do not take souvenirs.** No stem, no crystal, no flake of rust. The Pocket is not a resource. It is a place that has achieved a state. Taking a part of it is a kind of amputation.
- **Leave no trace.** Pack out everything you brought, including hair, skin flakes, and moisture. If you bleed, contain it. If you cough, do it into a sealed bag or your clothing. Your body is a foreign object here; minimize its leakage.

---

### Exit and Post-Visit

- **Reverse your path exactly.** Follow the guide line. Check that you have all your gear. Do not leave anything tied to a stem or wedged in a crack.
- **Exit slowly.** The sound will fade only when you pass through the slit. The sudden quiet may make you dizzy. Sit down outside, drink water, and let your ears re-tune.
- **Check each other.** Look for signs of hypothermia, dehydration, altitude sickness, or respiratory irritation. If anyone is coughing, short of breath, or confused, descend immediately.
- **Clean your gear outside.** Do not bring mineral dust into your camp. The dust may be chemically active. Shake out clothing away from water sources.
- **Monitor yourself for 24 hours.** Ozone can cause delayed lung irritation. Vibration can leave your hands or chest feeling strange. Altitude sickness can worsen after exertion. Rest, hydrate, and seek medical attention if symptoms persist.
- **Reflect.** The Choral Pocket will remain as it was, except for any break you made. You have been a brief, warm, noisy interruption in a place where nothing changes. That is a heavy thing. Carry it carefully.

---

### Contingencies

- **Entrapment:** If you are stuck in the slit or the growth, do not struggle. Call for help by radio or tug rope. Have the outside person pull the guide line or use a harness to extract you. Cutting growth is a last resort.
- **Gas exposure:** If you smell gas strongly or feel dizzy, leave immediately. Use emergency oxygen. Do not re-enter until the air has been re-tested.
- **Injury:** A cut from a mineralized stem may be clean but deep. Clean it with antiseptic, close it, and evacuate. The dryness may make bleeding less obvious; check yourself carefully.
- **Altitude illness:** Headache, nausea, confusion, or ataxia means descend. Do not wait. The plateau is not a place to tough it out.
- **Lost:** If you lose the guide line, stop. Use your light to look for landmarks. Do not wander. Call for help. The pocket is only thirty meters long; if you stay calm, you will find the entrance or your buddy.
- **Equipment failure:** Carry spares for critical items: batteries, light, radio, water. If your respirator fails, exit immediately. The air is not safe to breathe for long.

---

### Final Intuition

The Choral Pocket is not a place to conquer. It is a place to witness. It is a single note, held for perhaps thousands of years, in a room too small to be a cathedral. You are an ordinary human—a creature of water, time, and noise. You will enter, you will be tolerated, and if you are careful, you will leave only footprints that are not there. Prepare as if you are visiting a sleeping organism that does not dream of you.

**Pack warm. Pack dry. Pack silent. Pack small. And set a timer.**
