**Briefing: Preparing to Visit the Growing Mountain Chamber**

You are not going to a ruin or a natural cave; you are going to a living room inside a machine that is building a mountain. The first thing to understand is that this place is **under construction and alive in its own way**. Every surface may be a component, every pool a basin of coolant, every hum a note in the mountain’s heartbeat. Preparation is not just gear; it is an attitude of careful, temporary presence.

---

## 0. Pre-visit observation

If you can, arrive at a stable vantage point outside the chamber and observe for at least **one full fog cycle** — thickening and thinning of the pearl-colored mist, the rise and fall of the pools, the ebb and surge of the animal chorus. Note where the stone seems softest, where the monoliths hum loudest, and which crevices the ribbon predators use. Learn the rhythm before you step inside. The mountain will not pause for you.

---

## 1. General hazard assessment

The chamber combines:

- **Thin air** at high elevation (hypoxia risk)
- **High heat and radiant warmth** from fever-hot rock and warm mineral water, plus near-total humidity
- **Chemically active water and fog** — copper salts, dissolved minerals, possible acids or alkalis, nano-particulate mineral mist
- **Unstable, growing terrain** — soft new stone, weeping pores, hidden voids, surfaces that heal and shift
- **A loud, disorienting soundscape** — constant hiss, metallic chime of midges, bell-tone frogs, low-frequency hum
- **Non-standard physical behavior** — water running slightly uphill, light forming in the air in front of surfaces, sound felt inside your sternum
- **Active biological community** — glass midges, glowing skaters, translucent frogs, stone-colored lizards, ribbon predators, unknown pale shapes in the cloud sea
- **Unknown local technology** — monoliths, black box, fused walls, sockets, half-staircase — all likely active parts of the mountain-building system

Treat this as a **high-altitude hot spring, active industrial site, and alien garden** all in one. You are the most fragile element present.

---

## 2. Physical and medical preparation

- **Altitude acclimatization:** Spend 3–5 days at moderate altitude before visiting, or carry supplemental oxygen. The air is thin enough that any exertion will feel heavy. Know the signs of acute mountain sickness, high-altitude pulmonary edema, and high-altitude cerebral edema. A pulse oximeter is mandatory; if your SpO₂ drops below 85% or you feel confused or get a severe headache, use oxygen or descend immediately.
- **Heat tolerance:** The rock is internally warm, like a fever, and the air is saturated. Sweat will not evaporate, so your body’s normal cooling will fail. Train in hot, humid environments. Prepare for possible heat exhaustion; carry phase-change cooling packs.
- **Cardiovascular fitness:** Walking on a soft, yielding surface in high heat and thin air is exhausting. You should be able to move slowly and steadily without gasping. If you must exert, use oxygen.
- **Medications:** Bring antihistamines (for possible reactions to mineral dust or organisms), anti-nausea medication (for motion sickness from gravity anomalies and infrasound), and basic pain relievers. Include a first-aid kit with burn gel, chemical splash wash, and eye drops.

---

## 3. Clothing and personal protective equipment

Your outfit should be **waterproof, chemical-resistant, heat-tolerant, and breathable enough for high humidity**.

- **Baselayer:** Moisture-wicking synthetic (no cotton) against the skin.
- **Midlayer:** Light fleece or softshell for the cooler air above the warm rock. You may strip down if you overheat.
- **Outer layer:** Sealed-seam waterproof/chemical-resistant jacket and pants. Cuffs must seal tightly to keep out mineral water, midges, and fog.
- **Head:** A climbing helmet with a detachable head net. The helmet protects against unexpected stone spalls or bumps in low visibility. The head net keeps glass midges from your eyes and mouth.
- **Face and eyes:** A full-face respirator with P100 particulate filters and acid-gas/organic-vapor cartridges. The mineral fog may be harmful if inhaled; the water may release fumes. Use anti-fog inserts or a powered respirator to reduce fogging.
- **Hands:** Nitrile gloves over thin liner gloves. Add heat-resistant over-gloves if you must touch warm rock.
- **Feet:** Insulated, waterproof boots with thick, heat-resistant soles (rated to at least 100°C) and aggressive tread. The stone is slippery when wet and can be hot. Gaiters will keep water and organisms out.
- **Hearing:** Use high-fidelity earplugs or electronic ear muffs that reduce loud noise but allow conversation. The constant chorus can be overwhelming. Be alert to low-frequency hum; it can cause chest vibrations and anxiety. If your sternum resonates, shift posture or move slightly — it is environmental, not internal.
- **Visibility:** Wear a high-visibility vest or reflective tape. If visibility drops in fog, your partner must see you. Avoid bright flashing lights unless necessary; they may disturb fauna or local systems.

---

## 4. Air and thermal management

- **Oxygen:** Carry a small supplemental oxygen cylinder with a regulator and nasal cannula or mask. Use it during exertion or if you feel lightheaded. The thin, humid air will feel like breathing through a warm, wet cloth.
- **Air monitoring:** Bring a portable multi-gas detector for O₂, CO₂, H₂S, SO₂, and volatile organics, plus a particulate counter. If any gas exceeds safe limits, don the respirator and retreat toward the lip or exit.
- **Cooling:** Because evaporative cooling is ineffective, carry phase-change cooling packs (e.g., frozen gel packs) for your neck, wrists, and groin. A small battery-powered fan on your pack straps can help move air across your face.
- **Hydration:** Drink frequently, even if you don’t feel thirsty. Use electrolyte replacement tabs in your own clean water. **Never drink local water** — it is mineral-rich, possibly hot, acidic, or alkaline, and may carry unusual chemistry.
- **Check ground temperature:** Use an infrared thermometer or the back of a gloved hand before sitting, kneeling, or touching rock. The stone may be only fever-warm in one spot and dangerously hot in another.

---

## 5. Movement and terrain safety

The floor is not solid ground; it is newly laid stone, soft as warm wax in places, and **it heals over footprints within hours**. This means:

- **Probe before stepping:** Use a fiberglass or wooden walking stick to tap the ground ahead. Look for thin crust over hot water or hidden voids. The stone may look solid but be only a few centimeters thick.
- **Walk flat-footed and deliberately:** Avoid pivoting or scraping. This preserves the surface and reduces the chance of breaking through.
- **Do not stand in one spot for more than a few minutes.** The soft stone may slowly deform under your weight, and you could become lightly adhered as minerals deposit. Shift your weight frequently.
- **Use weight-distributing attachments** on your boots if the ground is especially soft — lightweight plastic snowshoe-style bases or wide crampons.
- **Sitting or resting:** Do not sit directly on the rock unless it feels solid and stable. Use a folding camp stool or a rigid foam pad with a large surface area. Place it on a stable-looking outcrop, not on the soft, weeping floor or moss mats. Move every few minutes.
- **Stay away from the lip.** The edge over the cloud sea may be new, brittle, and undercut. If you want to look over, approach on your belly with a safety tether anchored to a heavy object at least 5 meters back. Better yet, use binoculars from a safe distance.
- **Crevices and overhangs:** Ribbon predators drop from crevices. Do not put your hands or face near dark openings. Watch for shadows above.

---

## 6. Water and food

- **Bring all your own water.** The local water is a cocktail of dissolved minerals (copper, salts, etc.) and may be scalding or chemically reactive. Even if it looks clear, do not drink it, cook with it, or wash wounds with it.
- **Food:** High-energy, low-residue bars and gels. You may not want to eat much in the heat, but keep your energy up. Pack out all trash; anything left will become embedded in new stone or eaten by fauna.
- **Do not consume local organisms.** The frogs, midges, skaters, or plants may concentrate minerals or toxins. Even touching them could cause irritation; observe only.

---

## 7. Biological interactions

- **Glass midges:** Attracted to moisture and carbon dioxide. Your breath will draw them. Keep your respirator on or use a head net. They are probably harmless but could clog filters; use a pre-filter.
- **Translucent frogs:** Their bell-tone chorus is beautiful but can be very loud. Do not handle them; their skin may secrete irritants. Use a zoom lens if you need a closer look.
- **Stone-colored lizards:** Nearly invisible until they move. Watch where you place your hands and feet.
- **Ribbon predators:** Fast and agile. They are unlikely to attack a human, but a bite or sting is possible. Avoid dark crevices and sudden movements near their hunting spots.
- **Pale shapes in the cloud sea:** Unknown. Do not shine lights into the fog or lean over the edge. They may be large, slow, and potentially curious. Observe from a distance; do not disturb.
- **Silver moss and white tendrils:** Fragile. Avoid stepping on them. They grow only where heat and water cross; they may be part of the mountain’s biology.

---

## 8. Interacting with local technology

The monoliths, black box, socket wall, and staircase are **not ruins**. They are active components of the mountain-building machine. Treat them like a high-voltage substation or a quantum computer:

- **Do not touch, lean on, or sit on any built structure.**
- **Do not insert anything into the circular sockets.**
- **Do not try to read the shifting marks aloud or trace them.** They may be instructions, but they are not for you.
- **Observe from at least 2–3 meters.** Use binoculars or a camera with zoom.
- **If a monolith’s hum changes or the black box’s pulse quickens as you approach, retreat slowly.** You may be interfering with a regulator.
- **Do not sample the stone, water, or organisms** unless you have specific containment and permission. The mountain is growing; taking a piece is like removing a brick from an active wall.
- **Electronics:** The hum and magnetic fields may disrupt compasses, GPS, and flash memory. Carry mechanical backups (analog watch, magnetic compass — though it may be unreliable) and store critical data on shielded, non-electronic media. Use a Faraday bag for spare electronics. Test everything after exposure.

---

## 9. Navigation and communication

The chamber is only fifty strides by thirty, but fog can reduce visibility to a few meters. The terrain may also shift slightly as stone grows.

- **Set up a high-visibility, freestanding beacon** at your entry/exit point: a tall LED pole with a wide base, or a small strobe. Use low-heat, low-intensity light to avoid attracting fauna.
- **Run a lightweight guide line** from the entrance to your main observation point. Anchor it to heavy weights or spare water containers, not pitons (the stone is too soft and will heal over). Reel it in when you leave.
- **Buddy system:** Always travel with at least one partner. Keep line of sight or a tether between you. Agree on hand signals and whistle codes; the place is loud, and voice may not carry.
- **Backup marking:** Use small, weighted, biodegradable flagging tape or glow sticks on the ground to mark your path if you move beyond the line. Keep them minimal and retrieve them — anything left becomes part of the fossil record.

## 10. Psychological and perceptual preparation

This place will feel **alive, loud, and not built for humans**. Expect:

- **Sensory overload:** The constant hiss of weeping rock, the metallic chime of midges, the bell-calls of frogs, the low hum of monoliths, and the distant grinding of stone can be overwhelming. Take slow, deliberate breaths. Use earplugs if needed, but stay alert to changes.
- **Feelings of being watched:** The phrase “presences occur in gaps” suggests the chamber is not empty even when it looks empty. You may feel observed or as if the space is aware of you. This is a common reaction in places with strong ambient hum and unfamiliar physics. Do not panic; move calmly and slowly.
- **Disorientation from non-standard physics:** Water flowing slightly uphill, light appearing in front of surfaces, and vibrations starting in your sternum can cause nausea or vertigo. Use anti-nausea medication as a preventive. Focus on fixed landmarks (the three monoliths, the back wall, the staircase) and avoid staring too long at the glowing air.
- **Temporal distortion:** The stone grows fast enough to be visible; a spire can lengthen by a finger’s width in an hour. You may feel that time is moving strangely. Keep an analog watch to anchor yourself to external time.
- **Emotional impact:** The place is beautiful and raw, like being inside a developing embryo. You may feel awe, protectiveness, or an urge to touch things. Resist the urge. You are a guest.

## 12. Emergency and exit protocols

- **Heat exhaustion/stroke:** Move to a shadier or breezier spot (near the lip but not close to the edge). Apply cooling packs to neck, armpits, groin. Drink electrolytes. If confusion or fainting, evacuate.
- **Chemical splash:** Flush eyes/skin with clean water from your own supply for at least 15 minutes. Do not use local water. Apply burn dressing if needed.
- **Foot entanglement / sinking:** If your foot breaks through and becomes stuck in soft stone or hot water, lie flat to distribute weight. Use your pole or partner to pull you out with a rope. Do not struggle upright.
- **Injury:** Stabilize, splint, and call for help if communications work. If not, you must self-rescue. The chamber is small, so you can probably crawl to the exit if a leg is injured. Keep a compact emergency bivy sack in case you must wait.
- **Sudden fog/whiteout:** Stop moving, crouch low, and use your guide line or beacon to orient. Wait until visibility improves; the fog cycles, so it will pull back.
- **Unusual machine activity:** If the hum rises, the ground shakes, or the black box pulses faster, retreat to your entry point immediately. Do not wait to see what happens.
- **After exit:** Remove outer layers and bag them for decontamination. Check skin for rashes or mineral deposits. Monitor for delayed symptoms (respiratory irritation, metallic taste, unusual dreams or sensations) for 48 hours. Seek a physician familiar with multiverse travel if any persist.

---

## Final note

This chamber is a **vestibule in an embryonic mountain**, not a finished landscape. It is loud, wet, warm, and unfinished. Go there with the humility of a visitor to someone else’s construction site. Do not try to leave a mark; the stone will remember you anyway, turning any footprint into a fossil within a day. The best preparation is to understand that you are temporary, soft, and small — and that the mountain will continue growing whether you are there or not. Your job is to observe, learn, and leave without interfering.
