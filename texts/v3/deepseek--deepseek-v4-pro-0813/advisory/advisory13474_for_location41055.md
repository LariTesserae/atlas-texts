# Preparation Briefing: The High Basin

**Before you go, understand what you are walking into.** The high basin is not a wilderness in the ordinary sense. It is a place that has reached a single complete state and held it. Erosion equals deposition. Condensation equals evaporation. Growth equals decay. The same woman walks down the same stair with the same jar at the same interval, and the jar never empties. It is a landscape set down carefully and left, like an object made to be seen and not disturbed.

You, an ordinary human with a heartbeat, a microbiome, and a metabolism, are a moving anomaly in a still picture. You will feel it by the second hour. By the third, you will understand it. Your preparation is therefore not just about staying warm and fed. It is about being small, clean, quiet, and temporary.

---

## 1. Physical Preparation

The air is cool and thin enough to make the lungs work a little. That suggests altitude somewhere in the range where altitude sickness is possible, especially with exertion.

- **Acclimatize beforehand if you can.** Spend two or three days at a similar altitude before entering. If your multiverse travel doesn’t allow that, then plan to move at half speed for the first few hours. Do not go straight for the long central stair.
- **Cardiovascular fitness matters.** You don’t need to be an athlete, but you need steady endurance. The terrain is uneven, wet, and sharp. Practice hiking on broken rock and slippery boardwalks before you go.
- **Know the signs of altitude illness.** Headache, nausea, dizziness, unusual fatigue, shortness of breath at rest. If these worsen, leave. There is no local medical service, and you do not want to be a casualty in a place where nothing decays and no one changes.
- **Balance and ankle strength.** The basalt is split into fins, blades, pillars, and terraces. The edges are still clean enough to cut skin. A fall can be serious. Practice walking on rough ground with a light pack.

## 2. Clothing and Footwear

The environment is cool, damp, and shadowless. There is no wind, but the stone weeps and the air is full of fine moisture. Hypothermia is a real risk even without wind or rain.

- **Boots are your most important item.** Choose a high-ankle boot with a stiff shank and a sticky rubber sole. The basalt will chew up soft soles quickly. Break them in well before arrival. Consider approach shoes with a climbing-rated rubber edge for grip, but only if your ankles are strong.
- **Gaiters.** The damp and moss will soak your lower legs. Gaiters keep water out of your boots.
- **Gloves.** You will instinctively put a hand down on rock. The rock is sharp. Bring full-finger leather gloves for scrambling and thin liner gloves for warmth.
- **Layers.** A moisture-wicking base layer, a warm mid layer like fleece or light down, and a waterproof/breathable shell. The rain is fine and intermittent, more dewfall than weather, but constant damp will eventually wet out any membrane. Bring a spare dry base layer in a dry bag.
- **Colors and noise.** Muted tones—stone gray, moss green, dark brown. Nothing bright, nothing crinkly, nothing that snags or flaps. You are trying to be less of an intruder.
- **Headwear.** A warm beanie or hood. The air is cool. A buff or face covering can help with the damp.
- **Eye protection.** The light is ordinary and almost shadowless. There is no glare, but the lack of shadow flattens depth perception. Yellow or rose-tinted low-light glasses can improve contrast. Dark sunglasses are unnecessary.
- **Sun protection.** Even overcast, high-altitude UV can burn. Prefer physical barriers—long sleeves, hat, buff—over sunscreen, because sunscreen can wash into the water system and leave a lasting chemical trace.

## 3. Food, Water, and Waste

This is where the frozen equilibrium changes your usual outdoor habits. In a place where nothing grows or decays, your waste will not disappear. Your consumption will be a permanent subtraction.

### Water

- **Carry all the water you need.** Two to three liters for a day trip, more if overnight. The local water is everywhere and it looks pure, but it is part of a closed loop. If you drink from a pool, that water may never be replaced. Do not take even a liter.
- **Do not wash your hands or face in the channels or pools.** Use a small bottle of water and a cloth, and pack out the cloth.
- **Do not add anything to the water.** No soap, no toothpaste, no sunscreen, no insect repellent. Even a drop of DEET could sit in a pool forever.

### Food

- **High-calorie, low-fragrance, low-crumb food.** Nuts, dried fruit, chocolate, energy bars, hard cheese, flatbread. Avoid crumbly foods. If you drop a crumb, pick it up immediately.
- **No chewing gum, no loose tea, no soup powders that can spill.**
- **If you must cook**, use a contained canister stove on a flat bare rock, far from moss and water. Do not use local wood; there is no deadwood, and cutting live wood would be a permanent wound. A thermos of hot water prepared outside the basin is better. Remember that the thin air affects stove performance.
- **Pack out all food packaging.** Everything you bring in comes out.

### Waste

This is the hardest discipline, but the most important.

- **Solid waste:** Use a wag bag system or double-bag with absorbent powder. Pack it out. Do not bury it. In this place, buried waste would sit unchanged for an unknown span.
- **Urine:** Use a sealable urine bottle. Do not urinate on moss, into pools, or onto the rock. Your urine is full of salts, hormones, and nitrogen that the basin’s equilibrium has no room for.
- **Menstrual products:** Pack out everything. Bring extra sealable bags.
- **Tissues, bandages, hair, skin flakes:** Pack out what you can. Do not blow your nose onto the ground. Use a handkerchief and seal it.
- **Spit:** Avoid spitting. If you must, spit into a sealable container or swallow.
- **Trash:** Carry a dedicated trash dry bag. Inspect your rest stops before leaving. Leave the ground exactly as you found it.

## 4. Navigation and Movement

The overcast sky gives no sun-path, no glare, no long shadows. The fog stands in the hollows rather than drifting. Visibility can drop suddenly.

- **Bring a GPS if you have one**, but do not rely on it alone. Basalt can be magnetically noisy, so a compass may be unreliable. Use terrain association: the terraces, the central stair, the pools.
- **Mark your entry point** on your GPS or map. You need to know how to get back.
- **Do not build cairns, stack stones, or leave flagging tape.** The place is a finished object. Do not add to it.
- **Walk only on bare rock or established paths.** The moss is several inches deep and will not recover from your boot prints. Treat every green pocket as a fragile skin over knife-edged stone.
- **Use trekking poles with rubber tips.** Metal tips can scratch the rock. Rubber also gives better grip on wet stone.
- **Probe the ground ahead of you in fog or on moss.** Shadowless light hides depth. A fissure can look like a dark line on the ground.
- **Test handholds before trusting them.** The rock may be slick with invisible condensation even when it looks dry.
- **Move slowly.** The air is thin. The quiet amplifies your own noise. Slow movement also reduces the chance of a fall.

### If You Explore Fissures or Bridges

- The town has rope and fiber bridges spanning deeper cracks. They are preserved by the equilibrium, but you are not. Test them gently before putting full weight on them.
- Do not add your own anchors to their structures. If you need to rope up, do so on natural rock, and realize that placing gear may scar the stone. Better to stay on terraces and stairs.

## 5. First Aid and Emergency Gear

The rock edges are sharp enough to cut skin. There is no scree, so a fall is likely onto something hard and angular.

- **Laceration kit:** wound closure strips, antiseptic wipes, gauze, pressure bandage, medical tape.
- **Blister care:** moleskin, second-skin pads.
- **Altitude medication:** acetazolamide if you have used it before; anti-inflammatory; antihistamine; anti-diarrheal.
- **Splint and triangle bandage.**
- **Emergency blanket and small bivy bag**, even on a day trip. If you are injured and cannot move, you will need warmth on the cold stone.
- **Whistle.** Sound carries in the quiet. A whistle is better than shouting.
- **Your multiverse return device**, carried on your body, not in your pack. If you fall into a fissure, you need it one-handed. Check its charge before entering. Set a dead-man switch or timer if possible.

## 6. Interaction with the Place and Its Inhabitants

The town is urban in structure but sparse. Buildings are dry-stone and timber, roofed with turf and moss. People are present but locked in small, fixed cycles. Animals are abundant but spaced out. Everything is in equilibrium.

### Animals

- **Do not feed, touch, or chase them.** They are not skittish, but they are not tame. They are part of the stasis.
- **If an animal blocks your path, wait or go around.** Do not pick up frogs or fish. Their stillness may make them seem like statues; they are alive in a way you don’t understand.
- **Do not imitate their stillness** for too long. It can pull you toward the basin’s rhythm.

### People

- **Assume the inhabitants are real people in a paused state.** They may not perceive you. They may perceive you but be unable to break their loop. Either way, do not interrupt.
- **Do not stand in the path of the woman with the jar.** If you meet her on the stair, step aside and let her pass. Do not try to help her carry it. The jar never empties; your help is not needed.
- **Do not speak to them unless they clearly acknowledge you.** If they do, respond quietly and briefly, then move on. Do not ask questions that might demand a change from them.
- **Do not enter buildings, especially shrines and storehouses.** They are not abandoned. They are paused.
- **Do not take photographs of people without clear permission.** If they are in a loop, they cannot give permission. Consider not photographing them at all.
- **Do not touch offerings, tools, jars, cloth, or food.** Even moving a small object could be a permanent change.
- **Do not try to break the loop.** You may feel pity or curiosity. You do not know if the loop is a prison or a preservation. Intervening could harm them or trap you. Observe, do not alter.

### The Landscape

- **Do not take souvenirs.** No pebbles, no moss, no water, no feathers. Every object is part of the balance.
- **Do not carve or scratch the stone.** Your name would never weather away.
- **Do not try to repair anything.** If you see a cracked channel or a fallen stone, leave it. Your repair would be an intervention in a place where nothing is being repaired because nothing is broken.
- **If you accidentally cause a change**—snap a twig, step on moss, knock a stone—do not try to fix it. Note the location, leave the basin, and report it to whatever authority manages access. Your repair might make it worse.

## 7. Psychological Preparation

The first hour will feel peaceful. The second will feel uncanny. By the third, you will understand that you are a moving thing in a still picture. This can trigger anxiety, dissociation, or a strange desire to become still yourself.

- **Set a timer for every 30 minutes.** When it goes off, write one sentence in a small notebook: time, location, physical state. This anchors your sense of passing time.
- **Speak aloud if you feel unreal.** Say your name, the time, and your exit plan.
- **Do not follow the same loop twice.** Vary your path, vary your pace, do not return to the same spot more than necessary. If you find yourself repeating an action without deciding to, activate your return immediately.
- **If you start to feel that it doesn’t matter whether you leave, leave immediately.** That is the basin’s pull.
- **Sleep is difficult.** The light does not progress; it is never dark. If you stay overnight, use a blackout tent or a heavy sleep mask. Keep a fixed sleep schedule by your watch. Do not nap on the moss.
- **The constant drip** may become soothing or maddening. Bring earplugs for sleep, but during the day keep your ears uncovered to hear water, birds, or your own breathing.
- **Expect sensory monotony.** No wind, no insect drone, no mechanical noise. Your own heartbeat will become noticeable. This is normal. Do not let it frighten you.

## 8. Temporal Hazard Management

The basin is a frozen equilibrium. You are not frozen. That difference may not be stable.

- **Limit your first visit.** I would not stay more than three hours alone. Three hours is enough to understand the place. Six hours is enough to be changed by it.
- **If you plan an overnight**, do so only with a companion, a dead-man switch, and a pre-set extraction.
- **Watch for signs of being drawn in:** finding yourself in the same posture twice, losing track of time despite your timer, feeling that your own movements are becoming ritualized. If any of these occur, leave at once.
- **Do not try to test the stasis.** Do not sit still for an hour to see what happens. Do not follow the woman to see her loop complete. Your curiosity is not worth the risk.
- **Your return device is your only guaranteed exit.** Protect it, know how to activate it quickly, and set it to return automatically if you are incapacitated.

## Final Word

Pack as if a desert rat and a clean-room technician had a baby. Travel light, go quiet, go briefly. The basin is a cathedral with no ending. You are not there to survive it, fix it, or be changed by it. You are there to see it and leave it exactly as it was.

When you return, you may find ordinary places strange for a while—shadows moving, leaves falling, dust gathering. That is normal. Write down what you saw while it is fresh, because the memory will feel like a dream. And do not bring back even a drop of water in your boots. Clean everything before you go home.
