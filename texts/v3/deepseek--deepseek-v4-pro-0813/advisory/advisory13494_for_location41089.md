# Preparing for the City of Water and Stone

You are an ordinary human multiverse traveler. You have no gills, no stone-adapted skin, no supernatural endurance. You will be entering a high, cold, permanently wet, mineral-built city that has no human services, no rescue infrastructure, no dry ground, and no forgiveness. The place is not actively hostile—it is indifferent—but it will kill you through cold, disorientation, a bad fall, or simple exhaustion if you are not prepared.

The following is a practical and intuitive guide for how to prepare.

---

## 1. Understand What You Are Walking Into

Before you pack anything, understand the nature of the place.

The city is built by water and microbial films over immense time. It is not a ruin. It is alive in a slow, non-human way. The terraces, domes, walls, and channels are still growing, still being weathered, still shifting. The air is so saturated that breathing feels like drinking. The ground is mostly stone, water, or wet living film. There is no soil, no trees, no firewood, no large animals, no shelter, no marked trail, no other people.

The weather is locally generated. Clouds form on the plateau itself. Rain can fall on one district while another stands in pale thin sun. Fog rises in the afternoon. Night brings rime. The temperature is cool enough to see your breath well past morning. The silence is deep, broken only by water sounds, the rare soft tick of a bird, and the occasional spall of mineral crust.

This is a place where human timescales are like a single drop passing through one pool. You are not going to conquer it, map it fully, or “do” it in a weekend. You are going to pass through carefully, leave no trace, and return.

## 2. Physical and Mental Conditioning

### Physical preparation

- **Altitude acclimatization**: The plateau is high enough to generate its own weather. Assume it is at least 3,000–4,000 meters above the lower country. Spend several days at a similar altitude before entering, if possible. Ascend gradually. Know the symptoms of acute mountain sickness, HAPE, and HACE.
- **Cardiovascular fitness**: You will be walking on uneven, wet, narrow, and sometimes unstable stone for hours. Your legs and ankles will be tested constantly. Build endurance with loaded pack hikes on rough, rocky, wet terrain.
- **Balance and ankle strength**: The streets are narrow slots, the rims are handspan-wide walls, and the ground is slick. Practice walking on narrow beams, wet rock, and uneven surfaces with a weighted pack.
- **Cold tolerance**: You will be damp and cool for much of the trip. Get used to being slightly cold and damp without panicking. But also learn when to stop and warm up.
- **Foot care conditioning**: Your feet will be wet constantly. Train by hiking in wet boots or shoes to know your hot spots. Toughen your skin, and learn blister prevention.

### Mental preparation

- **Sensory monotony**: The landscape repeats—terraces, pools, domes, walls, water. The sky is often a white-gray lid. You may experience “terrace blindness,” where every place looks the same. Prepare for this by setting small, checkable goals and keeping a journal or voice log.
- **Silence and isolation**: There is no hum, no voice, no engine. The loudest sounds are water. The silence can feel heavy, almost sacred. Some travelers find it deeply peaceful; others find it unsettling. Accept both.
- **Indifference**: The city does not care if you live or die. It will let you drink, walk, fall, or freeze. Do not expect comfort or meaning to be handed to you. You are the one who must bring your own care.
- **Scale and existential smallness**: From a high dome, the terraces stretch away like the cells of a lung. You could walk for days and never leave the built land. This can be awe-inspiring or crushing. Prepare to feel small.

## 3. Clothing and Personal Wear

Your clothing system must keep you warm when wet, dry in rain, protected from wind, and agile on slick stone. Avoid cotton entirely. Choose synthetic or wool.

### Layers

- **Base layer**: Merino wool or synthetic, long-sleeve top and bottoms. Bring two sets: one for moving, one dedicated for sleeping only.
- **Mid layer**: A lightweight fleece or active insulation jacket. A second, warmer fleece or thin synthetic puffy for stops.
- **Outer shell**: A fully waterproof, breathable 3-layer jacket and pants. Hood must fit over a beanie and cinch down. Seams taped. Will be worn daily.
- **Insulation for rest stops/night**: Synthetic insulated jacket or vest. Down is lighter but loses warmth when wet; synthetic is safer in this humidity.
- **Wind shirt**: Optional but useful for dry, windy moments when a full shell is too much.

### Footwear

- **Primary footwear**: Waterproof-breathable hiking boots or approach shoes with aggressive, sticky rubber soles. Non-marking is important to avoid scarring the stone. Boots should have a rand or toe protection for rock contact. If water gets in, they must still be wearable.
- **Camp shoes**: Lightweight waterproof sandals, down booties, or dry socks with waterproof over-boots. You need to get your feet out of wet boots at night.
- **Gaiters**: High waterproof gaiters to keep water, debris, and fine mineral grit out of your boots. The city is all wet stone and shallow water.
- **Waterproof socks**: Consider a pair of waterproof-breathable socks as a backup or to wear inside boots if persistent leaks occur.

### Socks

- Bring 4–6 pairs of merino or synthetic hiking socks.
- Bring 1–2 pairs of liner socks if you use them.
- Bring 1–2 pairs of thick, dry sleep socks.
- Rotate socks during the day if possible. Dry wet socks in your sleeping bag at night.

### Handwear

- **Liner gloves**: Thin, grippy, quick-drying. Use for dexterity.
- **Waterproof insulated gloves**: For cold, wet conditions.
- **Grippy work gloves**: Rubber-palmed gloves for gripping wet rock, handling gear, and probing terrain.

### Head and neck

- **Beanie**: Wool or synthetic.
- **Sun hat**: Wide-brimmed or cap with a neck flap. The sun is thin but UV at altitude is real.
- **Waterproof hat or hood**: Your shell hood may be enough.
- **Neck gaiter or buff**: For warmth, sun protection, and wiping moisture.
- **Sunglasses**: Polarized, high UV protection. The white-gray sky and wet stone can cause glare. Flat light can affect depth perception; polarized lenses help.
- **Clear goggles**: For wind, fog, or rain in exposed areas. Also protects eyes from drifting mineral dust or spall fragments.

### Other clothing notes

- The air is too humid for large cotton towels; bring a small synthetic pack towel.
- All clothing should be quick-drying and able to be dried inside a sleeping bag or in a tent vestibule.
- Bring a set of dry sleeping clothes that never get used outside the tent.

## 4. Shelter and Sleeping

There is no bare ground. You will camp on stone terraces, mineral crust, or narrow ledges. No soil means no stakes. No trees means no hammock.

### Shelter options

- **Freestanding tent**: A one- or two-person, four-season, freestanding tent is best. It must be able to pitch on solid rock without stakes. Use rocks or sandbags as anchors. A bathtub floor is essential because water may sheet across the ground.
- **Bivy sack**: A waterproof, breathable bivy sack can work as an emergency or minimalist shelter, but in prolonged rain it becomes claustrophobic and wet. Use a small tarp over it if possible.
- **Tarp**: A lightweight tarp can be rigged with trekking poles and rocks as a cooking or rest shelter. It can also catch rainwater.

### Sleeping system

- **Sleeping bag**: Rated to at least -5°C or lower. Night temperatures drop, and rime forms on the rims. Choose synthetic insulation if possible, or use a waterproof-breathable shell around down.
- **Sleeping pad**: A high R-value inflatable pad (R-value 4 or higher). Put a closed-cell foam pad underneath to protect from punctures and add insulation from cold stone.
- **Pillow**: Inflatable or stuff sack filled with clothing.
- **Vapor barrier liner**: Optional but useful to keep sweat and moisture out of your sleeping bag.

### Moisture management in shelter

- The air is saturated. Expect condensation inside the tent every night.
- Ventilate as much as possible, even in rain.
- Keep wet gear in the vestibule, not inside the sleeping area.
- Use a small towel to wipe condensation.
- Bring a small sponge or pack towel for this purpose.
- At night, keep electronics, batteries, and water filters inside your sleeping bag to prevent freezing and moisture damage.

## 5. Food, Water, and Cooking

### Food

- There is no food to forage. Vegetation is sparse—only moss, thin grasses, lichens, and low green cushions. Animals are rare and should not be eaten. Bring all your food.
- Bring high-calorie, lightweight, easy-to-prepare meals: dehydrated backpacking meals, instant oats, nuts, chocolate, dried fruit, energy bars, nut butters, cheese, cured meats, and electrolyte powders.
- Plan for at least two extra days of food beyond your expected itinerary, in case you are trapped by fog, rain, or injury.
- Store food in waterproof bags or bear canisters (even though no large animals, it keeps things dry and organized).
- Keep food smells contained; while wildlife is minimal, you do not want to attract anything and you do not want to drop crumbs into pools.

### Water

- Water is everywhere, but it is mineral-charged. Some springs may be drinkable, but high calcium carbonate and other dissolved minerals can cause digestive upset if consumed in large quantities.
- **Do not assume any water is safe without treatment.** Bring a water filter or purifier that removes bacteria and protozoa. A pre-filter is helpful because the water can be milky with carbonate or green with living film.
- A standard filter will not remove dissolved minerals. For long stays, consider collecting rainwater from your tarp or tent fly using a clean container. Rainwater is likely softer than spring water.
- Boil water for at least one minute (or three minutes at altitude) to kill pathogens. Boiling does not remove minerals but makes it microbiologically safe.
- Bring electrolyte tablets or powder to replace salts lost through sweat and respiration.
- Carry at least 2–3 liters of water capacity per person. Drink frequently, even if the air feels wet. High altitude and exertion cause dehydration.
- The air is so saturated that breathing feels like drinking, but you still need liquid water. Do not confuse humid air with hydration.

### Cooking

- Use a stove: canister gas or white gas. White gas performs better in cold, but canister stoves with a pressure regulator can work. Bring enough fuel for the trip plus extra.
- No open fires. There is no wood, and fires on living microbial mats or mineral crust would be destructive and dangerous.
- A wind screen is essential; the plateau can be windy.
- A long-handled spoon, insulated mug, and a pot with a lid are enough.
- Clean cookware away from pools and channels. Use a small amount of biodegradable soap and pack out greywater, or wipe clean with a reusable cloth and pack out food residue.

## 6. Navigation and Communication

### Navigation

- The city is vast, repetitive, and often foggy. Do not expect visible landmarks to remain consistent. Channels can shift after a storm. Pools can appear or vanish. Rims can collapse.
- Bring a GPS with waypoint marking and trackback. Pre-load any available satellite or terrain data, but do not rely on it alone. The high humidity and thick cloud may reduce signal quality.
- Bring a physical map and compass, and know how to use them. If no map exists, create your own sketch map as you travel.
- Bring an altimeter watch. Elevation changes may help you orient.
- Bring a pedometer or distance tracker.
- In whiteout or thick fog, stop or move very slowly. Use a trekking pole to probe the ground. Follow a compass bearing to a known waypoint. Do not wander.
- Mark your camp with a high-visibility flag or light. Use GPS waypoints to find your way back.
- Do not use permanent markers, paint, or cairns. Leave no trace.

### Communication and emergency beacon

- Bring a satellite messenger or personal locator beacon (PLB) with two-way messaging if possible.
- Keep it in a waterproof case, and test it before entering.
- If you have a multiverse recall device or portal trigger, ensure it is waterproof, shock-resistant, and works in high humidity. Pre-program an emergency return coordinate.
- Carry spare batteries and a solar charger, but note that the sun is often thin and white. A hand-crank charger may be more reliable.
- A signal booster for your beacon may be necessary if the city is in a deep plateau surrounded by cloud.
- Tell someone outside your route and expected return time.

## 7. Safety and Hazards

The city has specific hazards. Prepare for each.

### Hidden shafts and unstable ground

- There are hidden shafts, deep pools, and channels that may be obscured by thin mineral crust or living film. The ground can look solid but be hollow.
- Always probe new ground with a trekking pole before committing your weight.
- Avoid stepping on edges of terraces, rims, or domes if they look thin or cracked.
- In fog, move slowly and test every step.
- Consider carrying a lightweight rope (20–30 meters of 6 mm cord) and a few slings/carabiners for handlines on exposed narrow walls or for self-rescue if you fall through a crust.

### Slippery surfaces

- Wet stone, wet moss, and living films are extremely slippery.
- Use non-slip, sticky rubber soles.
- Step on rough, unglazed stone where possible. Avoid green or black moss patches.
- Use trekking poles with rubber tips for extra points of contact.
- Keep your center of gravity low on slopes or narrow walls.

### Hypothermia

- The combination of cold, wet, wind, and humidity is dangerous.
- Stay dry where possible. Change out of wet base layers when you stop moving.
- Use your synthetic insulation even if damp.
- Eat high-energy food and drink warm fluids.
- Know the early signs: shivering, confusion, clumsiness. Stop and warm up immediately if they appear.
- Carry an emergency bivy sack and chemical heat pads.

### Altitude sickness

- The plateau is high. Watch for headache, nausea, dizziness, fatigue.
- Do not ascend further if symptoms appear. Rest, hydrate, and descend if symptoms worsen.
- Carry altitude medication if prescribed by a doctor.
- Spend time acclimatizing before entering.

### Disorientation

- The city is a labyrinth of similar terraces and fog.
- Use GPS, compass, and waypoints.
- If lost, stay put. Find a safe, stable spot and wait for fog to clear or for rescue.
- Do not keep walking in circles. It wastes energy and increases risk.

### Mineral water and waterborne illness

- Treat all water.
- Avoid drinking from milky or green pools.
- Do not swim or wade into pools unless absolutely necessary. The water may be deeper than it looks, very cold, and full of fragile life.

### Rockfall and spalling

- Older mineral crusts can spall off walls, especially after a freeze-thaw cycle.
- Avoid lingering directly under overhanging domes or ledges.
- A lightweight climbing helmet is optional but useful in districts with tall, crumbling towers.

### No rescue

- There is no one to help you. You are your own first responder.
- Carry a first aid kit and know how to use it.
- A serious injury may be unrecoverable if you cannot self-extract. This is the risk of visiting.

## 8. First Aid and Medical Kit

Bring a comprehensive first aid kit, including:

- Blister treatment: moleskin, tape, Compeed, antiseptic, needle.
- Foot powder or antifungal cream for constant wetness.
- Bandages, gauze, medical tape, antiseptic wipes.
- Antibiotic ointment.
- Pain relievers (ibuprofen, acetaminophen).
- Anti-diarrheal medication.
- Antihistamines for unexpected reactions.
- Rehydration salts.
- Altitude sickness medication (acetazolamide, dexamethasone, nifedipine) if prescribed and appropriate.
- Splint, elastic bandage, triangular bandage.
- Tweezers, scissors, safety pins, thermometer.
- Emergency blanket or bivy sack.
- Heat packs.
- Personal medications.

Know basic first aid: wound care, splinting, hypothermia treatment, altitude illness recognition, and self-rescue techniques.

## 10. Waste Management and Leave No Trace

The city is a living, growing entity. You must not damage it or leave anything behind.

- **Pack out everything**: all food wrappers, toilet paper, hygiene products, batteries, etc.
- **Solid human waste**: Use WAG bags or a portable toilet system. Pack out all solid waste. There is no soil for cat holes.
- **Urine**: If possible, use a pee bottle and pack it out, especially in terraced areas where urine could alter the mineral chemistry. If you must urinate, do so on bare mineral rock away from pools and channels, and dilute with water. Better to pack it out.
- **Do not wash** dishes, clothes, or yourself directly in pools or channels. Use a basin, pack out greywater, or scatter wash water on barren stone far from water.
- **Do not step on living microbial mats** if you can avoid them. They are the builders of the city. Walk on established mineral rims and bare stone.
- **Do not break rims, chip stone, or collect specimens.**
- **Do not introduce non-native seeds or microbes.** Clean your boots and gear before entering.
- **Observe wildlife from a distance.** The slate-colored bird that walks underwater is rare. Do not chase, feed, or disturb it. Its call is a soft wet tick; if you hear it, you are lucky.
- **Leave no trace** means the city should look exactly as if you were never there. That is the highest respect you can show.

## 11. Travel Strategy and Daily Rhythm

### Entry and exit

- If using a multiverse portal, set your arrival point on a stable, relatively flat mineral surface—not inside a pool, on a narrow rim, or under a dripping dome.
- Pre-program an exit coordinate and test your recall beacon before entering.
- If arriving on foot from the lower country, approach from the edge of the plateau where terraces are less dense.
- Do not enter the deepest, most tower-crowded districts unless you have solid navigation and experience.
- Know your exit route. Do not assume you can follow water downhill; the terraces may step into a sea of cloud and become impassable.

### Daily rhythm

- **Morning**: You will wake to rime on the tent and visible breath. Shake ice off the fly before packing. Eat a hot breakfast. Do not start too early if fog is thick. Wait for visibility to improve if needed.
- **Midday**: The sun may be thin and white, and the pools may shine. Mist may rise in the afternoon. This is often the best visibility window. Use it to travel.
- **Afternoon**: Fog banks may fill the streets. Rain can fall on one district while another is in sun. Stop by mid-afternoon to set up camp before the evening rains.
- **Evening**: Rain often settles back down. Cook dinner under a tarp or tent vestibule. Dry gear as much as possible.
- **Night**: Temperature drops; rime forms. Sleep with water filter, batteries, and electronics in your sleeping bag. The city glitters faintly under the moon.

### Movement rules

- Move slowly. The ground is uneven, slick, and fragile. Expect 1–2 km/h or less.
- Use trekking poles to probe and balance.
- In fog, use a compass bearing and GPS waypoints. Do not rely on memory.
- Do not cross narrow rims or ledges unless necessary. If you must, use a handline and move one at a time.
- Avoid stepping in pools. Use natural bridges or walk around.
- If a storm suddenly hits, find a stable spot and wait it out. Do not keep moving blindly.

## 12. Psychological Preparedness and What to Expect

### Sensory experience

- **Smell**: Wet limestone, cold stone, rain-soaked dust, the inside of a cave, the edge of a cold spring. It is clean but mineral, earthless.
- **Sound**: Water dripping, gurgling, hissing. Wind moving through domes. The occasional click of a pebble shifting. The rare, soft wet tick of the bird. Your own breath and footsteps. The silence is not empty; it is the silence of slow water inside stone.
- **Sight**: White-gray sky. Honeycombed terraces of stone and water reflecting pale light. Fog bands separating streets. Stone colors ranging from pale yellow to gray to cream, stained by iron and manganese. Very little green—only seams of moss, thin grass, lichen. The city stretches to a sea of cloud.
- **Touch**: Cold, wet, rough stone. Rime on rims in the morning. Thin sheets of water over mineral lips. Humid air that clings to your skin.
- **Taste**: Mineral in the air, like licking a wet stone. If you drink the water, it may taste hard, slightly alkaline, cave-like.

### Emotional landscape

You may feel:

- **Awe** at the scale and the slow agency of water and life.
- **Loneliness** or **smallness** in the absence of human presence.
- **Peace** in the silence and repetition.
- **Unease** in the narrow dark slots and the indifferent growth.
- **Frustration** at the slow progress and constant damp.
- **Wonder** at the tiny food chain: films, amphipods, bird.

All of these are normal. Do not fight them. The city is not there to comfort or challenge you; it is simply continuing. Your task is to move through it with care.

## 13. Special Multiverse Traveler Considerations

If you are traveling between worlds, add these:

- **Waterproof your portal device**: High humidity can short-circuit electronics. Use a waterproof case with desiccant.
- **Test your recall function**: Make sure your return mechanism works in saturated air and at altitude.
- **Pre-program an emergency extraction**: If you are injured or lost, you need a way to leave immediately. A “dead man’s switch” that triggers if you are unresponsive is a good idea.
- **Carry a physical map and compass**: Multiverse GPS may not work. Do not rely on dimensional beacons alone.
- **Bring a signal booster**: The local weather and mineral stone may interfere with signals.
- **Plan for language and communication**: There are no people, so no local language. Your own language is fine.
- **Consider a local guide?** There are no local humans, but if your multiverse network has records or AI, study them before entering.
- **Quarantine and decontamination**: You may be carrying microbes from other worlds. Clean your gear before and after to avoid contaminating the microbial mats or bringing things home.

## 15. Final Word

Prepare as if you are going to a high, cold, wet, living labyrinth where no one will come to save you. Bring more than you think you need. Move slowly. Respect the stone and the water and the living films. Do not try to change the city, and do not leave anything behind.

The city will let you drink from its springs and walk its narrow walls. It will also let you fall or freeze. It is indifferent, but it is not malicious. It is simply continuing—drip by drip, layer by layer, pool by pool—at the edge of the sky.

If you are careful, you will return with the taste of wet limestone in your memory, the sound of water in your ears, and the knowledge that you have walked through a city built by no one, still building itself, without any need for you at all.

That is the best you can hope for.
