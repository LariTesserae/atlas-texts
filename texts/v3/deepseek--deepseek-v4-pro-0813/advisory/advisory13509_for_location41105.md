**Preparing for the Half-Drowned Industrial Tidal City**

If you are an ordinary human multiverse traveler, do not treat this as a simple ruin or a sightseeing stop. Treat it as a hot, wet, tidally active, chemically suspect coastal environment where the main dangers are not monsters but heat, contaminated water, sharp shells, unstable structures, and the slow exhaustion of constant moisture. You will not stay dry. You will be hot. You will need to move slowly, plan around the tide, and accept that the city is alive in ways that are unfamiliar.

---

### 1. Understand the place before you arrive

This is a subsided basin of reclaimed tidal flats. The sea has not gone away; it has been reorganized into canals, storm drains, and flooded streets. The water table is so high that any depression in the concrete becomes a pond within an hour. The air is hot and wet enough to condense on every surface. Heat comes from the sun through permanent haze, but also from below—warm groundwater, old engine heat, and steam pipes. There is no green anywhere. Life begins as bacterial mats and becomes a dense animal biomass at the waterline: mussels, barnacles, tubeworms, brine shrimp, crabs. The city is quiet, dim, and half-drowned.

Prepare for a place where the waterline is the real boundary. Everything important happens there.

---

### 2. Physical and medical preparation

**Heat and humidity.**  
Acclimate yourself before arrival. Spend at least a week in a hot, humid place. Exercise gently in those conditions. The heat here feels less like sun on skin and more like a fever that has settled into the stone, iron, and water. Physical exertion in this environment is exhausting. Plan for shorter work periods and frequent rest.

**Foot conditioning.**  
Your feet will be wet almost constantly. That is unavoidable. Train by walking in wet shoes or boots for long periods. Learn how your skin reacts. Trim nails, treat blisters early, and practice foot care. Trench foot and maceration are real risks.

**Vaccinations and medical prep.**  
At minimum, tetanus is essential. Consider hepatitis A and B, typhoid, and leptospirosis after consulting a travel or expedition physician. The water is warm, brackish, and full of organic material. It is not safe to drink or to enter open wounds.

Bring a comprehensive medical kit:
- Wound irrigation syringe
- Antiseptic solution
- Antibiotic ointment
- Sterile dressings and gauze
- Wound closure strips
- Blister pads and moleskin
- Antifungal powder and cream
- Zinc oxide barrier cream
- Oral rehydration salts
- Cooling towel or instant cold packs
- Thermometer
- Eye wash or sterile saline

**Heat illness.**  
Know the signs of heat exhaustion and heat stroke. In this environment, you may not feel thirsty until you are already behind on fluids. Drink constantly. Use electrolytes. Rest during the hottest part of the day. If you stop sweating, feel confused, or develop a throbbing headache, stop and cool down immediately.

### 3. Clothing and personal protective equipment

**Base layers.**  
Use synthetic quick-drying fabric, not cotton. Cotton will become heavy, cold, and abrasive. In this humidity nothing truly dries, but synthetic materials will not hold as much water against your skin. Bring several sets sealed in dry bags.

**Outer layer.**  
A lightweight waterproof/breathable rain shell with large pit zips is best. Avoid heavy rubber coats; they trap heat. A poncho can work but may snag on metal and limit movement. The afternoon rain is hot and heavy, but it passes. You need protection that does not overheat you.

**Head and face.**  
Wear a wide-brim hat or cap with a neck flap. The light is dim, but the heat is pervasive. A buff or neck gaiter can wipe sweat and keep salt off your face.

**Eye protection.**  
Wraparound safety glasses or goggles. The haze, salt, and steam will irritate your eyes. Clear lenses are fine; the light is already dim.

**Gloves.**  
Rubber-dipped cut-resistant gloves are ideal. They protect against sharp barnacles, mussels, rusty metal, and rough concrete.

**Footwear.**  
This is the most important decision. Bring two types:
- Sturdy high wading boots or neoprene boots with puncture-resistant soles for deeper water and sharp debris. The canal walls and pilings are shaggy with sharp shells.
- Quick-draining amphibious shoes for walking on wet pavement and shallow areas.

Avoid ordinary waterproof hiking boots. If water goes over the top, they fill and become heavy. If you must wade, choose footwear that drains. Bring multiple pairs of synthetic or merino socks and change them whenever you rest. Use antifungal powder.

**Flotation.**  
Wear a low-profile inflatable life vest or PFD. The city is partly below the high-tide line. Flooded streets, canals, and hidden depressions can be deeper than they look. If you fall into water, waterlogged gear can pull you down. Wear flotation whenever crossing water above knee height.

**Respiratory protection.**  
At minimum, carry N95 or P100 particulate masks. The haze is thick, and there may be industrial dust, rust particles, and biological aerosols. If you go near refinery pipes, warm seeps, or outfall areas, use a mask with organic vapor and acid gas cartridges. Humidity will degrade filters, so bring spares in dry bags.

---

### 4. Shelter and sleeping

Do not camp on ground floors or in basements. They are flooded or will flood. Choose upper floors, intact catwalks, elevated platforms, or the tops of sturdy structures.

A hammock with a mosquito net and tarp is often better than a tent. The ground is wet and possibly contaminated. If you use a tent, use a footprint and expect condensation. Every surface weeps.

Bring a sleeping pad for insulation and comfort. An inflatable pad can double as flotation. A light synthetic blanket or silk liner is usually enough in the heat. Avoid down insulation, which loses its loft when wet.

Secure everything against tide and rain. Tie down loose gear. Mark the high-tide line with reflective tape or a small light. The tide rises twice a day through the storm drains and lifts the water in the streets six inches. That may sound small, but it can isolate a campsite or hide a hazard.

---

### 5. Water and food

**Do not drink the local water.**  
It is brackish, warm, and contaminated with industrial leachate, salt, and biological material. Do not drink from puddles, canals, or taps. If you must use local water, you need a desalinator or distillation system plus chemical filtration. Ordinary filters and tablets do not remove salt or heavy metals. Boiling will not remove dissolved metals.

Carry more water than you think. Heat and humidity cause constant sweating. Plan for 4–6 liters per day minimum. Bring electrolyte replacement.

**Food.**  
Bring all your food. Do not forage. There is no vegetation. The mussels, crabs, and brine shrimp are abundant but may bioaccumulate heavy metals and industrial toxins. Do not eat them. Use high-energy, non-perishable foods sealed in waterproof containers.

If you cook, use a stove on a stable, non-combustible surface. Avoid open flames near old refinery areas, visible sheens on water, or enclosed spaces where gas may collect.

### 6. Tools, navigation, and communication

- **Wading staff or trekking pole:** Essential for probing depth, hidden holes, and stability. A long, sturdy stick can save you from stepping into a submerged manhole or a deep pothole.
- **Lighting:** Waterproof headlamp and handheld flashlight with red mode. Days are dim; inside buildings is dark. Bring spare batteries in dry bags.
- **Navigation:** GPS may work, but haze and old electrical infrastructure might interfere. Carry a compass and paper maps. Mark your route with bright flagging tape. Chalk will not hold on wet surfaces.
- **Dry bags:** Multiple roll-top bags for electronics, food, medical supplies, and spare clothes. They also provide flotation.
- **Rope or webbing:** 20–30 feet of lightweight cord for securing gear, ascending or descending, or emergency use.
- **Cutting tool:** A corrosion-resistant knife or multi-tool.
- **Whistle and signal mirror:** The city is quiet; sound does not carry far, but a whistle may help in an emergency. The haze may hinder visual signals.
- **Communication:** Satellite phone or personal locator beacon in a waterproof case. Pre-arrange check-in times. Do not rely on local infrastructure.

### 7. Safety protocols and hazards

**Structural hazards.**  
Many structures are rusted, collapsed, or undermined by water. Test before you put weight on anything. Avoid catwalks with visible rust-through, sagging, or missing sections. Do not enter tanks, pipes, or flooded basements. Confined spaces can contain toxic gases, low oxygen, or deep water.

**Tidal hazards.**  
Know the tide schedule. The tide rises through storm drains and lifts water in the streets six inches. It reverses canal flow. Six inches may not sound dangerous, but it can hide a two-foot hole, float debris, and isolate you. Do not cross a canal or flooded street when the tide is rising unless you know the route very well.

**Rain.**  
Afternoon rain is heavy, hot, and arrives from the sea. Visibility drops, steam rises, and streets become shallow hot rivers for about an hour. Seek elevated shelter before the rain starts. After rain, surfaces are slick and coated with a fresh ring of salt.

**Electrical hazards.**  
Some stacks still have red lights on remnant power. Avoid downed wires, substations, and electrical equipment. Water conducts electricity.

**Chemical hazards.**  
Refinery tanks, pipes, and seeps may leak hydrocarbons, acids, or other industrial chemicals. Look for sheens on water, strong odors, or discolored patches. Do not touch or wade through suspicious water. If you smell rotten eggs or gas, move away, especially near warm seeps and outfalls.

**Biological hazards.**  
The water is full of life. Most of it is not aggressive, but shells are sharp and can cause cuts that become infected in warm water. The bacterial mats—sulfur-yellow, rust-red, white—may be toxic. Do not touch them or disturb them. Avoid inhaling mist near warm seeps or outfall pipes. Hydrogen sulfide may be present.

**Heat.**  
The heat is the main presence. It comes from the sun through permanent cloud and from below. Puddles steam. Heat exhaustion can come on quickly. Rest in shade, drink constantly, and cool yourself with clean water if available. Do not attempt hard physical labor during the hottest part of the day.

**Drowning.**  
The water table is so close that any depression becomes a pond within an hour. Flooded basements, sunken rail yards, and dead canal basins can be deceptively deep. Wear a PFD when wading or crossing water. Do not swim in canals. Currents may reverse with the tide, and submerged hazards are numerous.

### 8. Mental preparation and etiquette

The quiet is real. The city is quiet not because it is dead but because everything alive here moves in small, voiceless ways. You may hear only dripping water, the snap of mussels like rain on a distant roof, the hiss of steam, and the low throb of a pump station. This can be calming or unnerving. Prepare for the absence of human voices and mechanical noise.

The light is dim and brown-gray. Colors go to lead, rust, black water, gray concrete, and white salt crust. This can feel monotonous and disorienting. Bring small objects of color if that helps morale.

The lack of green may affect you more than expected. There are no plants, no weeds, no grass. Life exists as bacterial mats and dense animal aggregations at the waterline. The landscape is mineral, wet, and alive in unfamiliar ways.

Time moves differently here. The city is in motion at the speed of rust, shell growth, and tide. You will need to stand still for a while to notice it. Practice patience. Watch the crab mats move up and down with the tide. Observe the new white salt band each morning.

You are a visitor in a living ecosystem. Minimize your impact. Do not crush mussels or crabs unnecessarily. Do not leave waste. Do not introduce seeds, soil, or organisms from other worlds. Pack out everything. Treat the bacterial mats and shell columns as the city's new inhabitants; they are not scenery.

The emptiness between things is the city's true architecture. It may feel lonely or agoraphobic. If traveling solo, consider bringing a companion or at least a communication link to break the isolation.

### 9. A sample day in the city

Wake before first light. The haze will not brighten much; use a red headlamp.

Check the tide and weather. If low tide is early, move during the morning window.

Eat a high-energy meal and drink electrolytes.

Travel along elevated routes or shallow streets. Use the wading staff. Watch for submerged hazards.

By midday, the heat and dim light will be strongest. Rest on an upper floor or shaded platform. Change socks, dry feet, apply antifungal and barrier cream.

Afternoon rain will arrive from the sea. Seek shelter before it hits. Wait out the rain; watch steam rise.

After rain, the streets are wet and slick. Salt crust forms. The waterline moves. If low tide allows, continue; otherwise stay on high ground.

As dusk comes, the light goes almost dark except for red lights on the tallest stacks. Return to your elevated camp before dark.

Sleep secured, with gear tied down and a light marking the high-tide line.

### 10. Final checklist

- Travel documents and multiverse return beacon, waterproofed
- Tide tables, maps, compass, GPS
- PFD, wading staff, rope, knife, multi-tool
- Footwear: wading boots + quick-drain shoes
- Clothing: synthetic base layers, rain shell, hat, gloves, socks
- PPE: respirator with spare filters, safety glasses
- Shelter: hammock, tarp, bug net, sleeping pad, light blanket
- Water: 4–6 L/day, electrolytes, desalinator if necessary
- Food: high-energy, sealed
- Medical: comprehensive kit, heat illness, foot care, wound care
- Communication: satellite phone/beacon, whistle, signal mirror
- Repair: duct tape, zip ties, cord, waterproof patches

---

The place is not supernatural. The strangeness is only in the ratios: hot, wet, low, half-drowned, animals instead of vegetation, humans rare. If you prepare for a hot, humid, tidally active, chemically contaminated industrial coast, you can move through it safely. Respect the tide, the heat, and the quiet. The city will not try to hurt you, but it will not help you either. You are responsible for your own water, your own footing, and your own exit.
