**Preparation Brief for the Bell, the Drowned Range**  
*For an ordinary human multiverse traveler*

Before you go, stop thinking of the Bell as a cave, a shrine, a campsite, or a geological curiosity. It is all of those, but mostly it is an unfinished place. The mountain is still rising. The sea has not finished arriving. The rock has not learned to be stone. For an ordinary human body—one that bruises, bleeds, feels cold, needs sleep, and trusts solid ground—the Bell is less a destination than a live machine of water, stone, and air. You prepare for it less like a tourist and more like someone entering a slow industrial accident.

Here is what I would tell you to do, bring, learn, and expect.

---

## 1. Understand what you are actually walking into

The Bell is a stack of black basalt rising out of a new, cold sea. There is no shore. The rock is sharp, glassy, and unweathered. Waves hit it with the force of something still arguing with the world. Inside the stack, hidden behind a curtain of spray near the summit, there is a small room of air—no bigger than a shepherd’s hut—with a shattered basalt floor, a pool connected to the sea, and a narrow opening in the roof.

The room is alive in a mechanical way:

- The pool breathes with every swell.
- The air pressure changes when large waves seal the lower entrance.
- A hidden vent near the top whistles when the pressure shifts.
- The rock grinds, ticks, and hums as the mountain rises.
- The floor is not sand; it is angular shards, sharp as broken obsidian.
- The water outside is layered—cold fresh meltwater over warmer brine—and the lower water is so clear that depth perception fails.
- The rock can hold heat in deep cracks.
- The noise never stops. It only changes shape.

You are not going to a stable shelter. You are going to an air bubble inside a process.

---

## 2. Physical and mental readiness

Ordinary humans need to be honest with themselves before going here.

### Physical  
- You should be fit enough to climb, crawl, brace against waves, and carry a heavy pack over sharp, unstable ground.  
- Grip strength matters more than endurance. You will be holding onto edges, hauling ropes, and steadying yourself against the movement of the rock.  
- Cold tolerance matters. The water is described as “cold and clear as winter glass.” The air is cool and thin. If you fall in, you have minutes before hypothermia begins. Train yourself in cold-water immersion or at least understand the signs of shock.  
- You must be able to equalize your ears. The pressure in the room changes constantly. If you have a cold, sinus infection, or ear problem, do not go. Serious barotrauma is possible just sitting inside the room.  
- Balance and ankle strength are critical. The floor is loose shards over basalt. Your footing will shift constantly.

### Mental  
The place will not comfort you. The noise never stops. The walls can crack while you watch. The floor may shift. The light comes through spray and looks as if you are already underwater. You may not sleep well. You may feel a deep, strange pull—awe, unease, or the sense that the mountain is still deciding whether it will let you stay.

Prepare for:

- Sleep deprivation. Plan for short rest in shifts.  
- A constant, percussive sound that fills the skull. It is not gentle white noise. It is deep bass, hissing foam, falling pebbles, and the grind of rock growing.  
- Disorientation. The rock hum can make your teeth ache. The layered water in the pool can make you misjudge distance. The pressure changes can make your head feel full.  
- The feeling of being watched, not by a spirit, but by something unfinished.

You should not go alone. Three people is ideal: one to watch the water and cracks, one to manage ropes and gear, one to rest or keep notes. If you must go alone, you are taking a much larger risk.

---

## 3. Skills you should already have

Do not learn these on arrival. The Bell will not give you time.

- **Vertical rope work**: ascending, descending, changeovers, belaying on marginal anchors. The stack has no path, no trail. If you enter through the roof rent, you will rappel. If you need to leave quickly, you will ascend.  
- **Cold-water survival**: how to survive if you fall into the sea, how to get out of the water fast, how to prevent afterdrop.  
- **Cave safety**: especially understanding air bells, sumps, and pressure changes. The pool is a drowned throat to the sea. It is an overhead environment. Do not enter it unless you are a trained cave diver—and even then, think three times.  
- **Wilderness trauma care**: severe cuts, crush injuries, hypothermia, drowning, barotrauma.  
- **Basic rock assessment**: recognizing fresh cracks, loose blocks, rockfall runout zones, and unstable ledges. The mountain is growing; a ledge that was solid an hour ago may be gone by morning.  
- **Gas awareness**: knowing the smell of sulfur, the symptoms of low oxygen, and how to use a portable gas monitor.  
- **Radio and hand-signal communication**: the noise will drown voices. You need a system.

## 4. Gear: what to bring

Your packing principle: treat the Bell as a combination of a sea cave, an active rockfall site, a cold-water emergency, and a high-angle climbing objective. Then add redundancy.

### Clothing and personal protection

- **Base layers**: merino or synthetic, multiple layers. Do not rely on cotton.  
- **Dry suit**: full dry suit with hood, gloves, and boots if there is any chance of water contact. The water is extremely cold. Even if you plan to stay inside the room, spray and surges can soak you. A dry suit is not optional if you approach by sea.  
- **Cut-resistant gloves**: the basalt shards are sharp as broken glass. Your hands will be cut constantly without protection. Bring several pairs.  
- **Knee and elbow pads**: the floor is shattered basalt and sharp sand. Kneeling without pads is like kneeling on broken obsidian.  
- **Helmet**: caving or mountaineering helmet with a strong chin strap. Rockfall is a real, constant possibility. A falling pebble can kill.  
- **Eye protection**: sealed goggles or wraparound safety glasses. Spray and rock chips will be in the air.  
- **Hearing protection**: electronic ear muffs that reduce loud impulses but allow speech, plus foam earplugs as backup. But you must still hear the vent whistle and the sound of rock cracking. Practice with your ear pro so you know what the warning sounds like through them.  
- **Footwear**: stiff-soled boots with ankle support. If you enter via water, dry suit boots integrated with the suit. The ground is uneven and sharp; do not wear soft flexible shoes.  

### Climbing and access

- **Rope**: at least 60 meters of static rope for rappelling and hauling, plus a dynamic rope for climbing if needed. Dry-treated rope is best because it will get wet.  
- **Harness**: full-body or sit harness with gear loops.  
- **Ascenders and descenders**: mechanical ascenders, a reliable descender, prusik cords, slings, carabiners, quickdraws.  
- **Anchors**: removable bolts or slingable horns. The rock is brittle and fractured. Never trust a single anchor. Use redundancy.  
- **Edge protection**: rope protectors, because the basalt edges are sharp and will cut a rope under tension.  
- **Headlamp**: with spare batteries. The light inside is dim, blue-gray, and soft. At night or in storm, it will be black.  

### Shelter and sleep

- **Bivy sack**: waterproof and breathable.  
- **Sleeping bag**: rated for below freezing, synthetic rather than down, because it will be damp from spray.  
- **Sleeping pad**: two closed-cell foam pads. You need the second because the floor is sharp enough to puncture an inflatable pad and bruise your bones through a single foam pad.  
- **Small tarp**: to deflect spray and small falling debris. Tie it to gear loops or around rock horns; do not use tent stakes.  
- **No tent**: a tent would be shredded or blown away. Bivy is better.

### Water and food

- **Drinking water**: bring all of it. Do not expect to find fresh water inside the Bell. The pool is salt or brackish, connected to the sea. The outside water has fresh meltwater floating over brine, but collecting it from a heaving sea is unreliable and dangerous. Bring at least 4 liters per person per day, plus extra.  
- **Food**: high-energy, no-cook or minimal-cook meals. You will burn energy staying warm and alert.  
- **Stove**: small liquid fuel or canister stove with a windshield. Use only after you confirm the roof vent is clear and air quality is okay. Bring a carbon monoxide detector.  
- **Water purification**: if you absolutely must use outside fresh water, carry a filter and purification tablets, but do not rely on it.

### Medical and safety

- **Trauma kit**: pressure dressings, tourniquet, wound closure strips, antiseptic, antibiotics, painkillers, burn gel.  
- **Splint**: SAM splint or similar.  
- **Thermal blanket or bivy sack**: for emergency hypothermia treatment.  
- **Barotrauma supplies**: decongestants may help some people, but they are not a fix. If you cannot equalize, leave.  
- **Seasickness medication**: the sea is rough and the room moves with swell.  
- **Gas monitor**: portable multi-gas detector for O₂, H₂S, SO₂, CO. The air can carry sulfur smells from unseen tubes. Low oxygen is a possibility in a poorly ventilated rock cavity.  
- **Personal locator beacon / multiverse return device**: with redundant power. Do not go without a way to call for extraction.  
- **Whistle, signal mirror, strobe**.

### Tools and repair

- Multi-tool, knife, small saw, duct tape, zip ties, cordage.  
- Spare headlamp batteries and a power bank.  
- Waterproof notebook and pencil.  
- Camera or audio recorder if you want to document.  
- Crack monitors: small pieces of tape or brittle material you can place across new cracks to see if they widen. This is simple but can save your life.

## 5. Arrival: how to get in without dying

The Bell has no shore, no path, and no obvious entrance except two ways: the drowned throat in the rock and the rent in the roof.

### If you can choose your insertion point  
Ask to be placed directly on the high ledge inside the room, above the reach of all but the biggest swells. That is the safest option. Do not arrive in the water. Do not arrive on the lower ledge.

### If you must approach by sea  
Anchor a stable boat far enough away that waves cannot throw it against the stack. Use a small tender or packraft to get near the base. But there is no shore—the stack rises directly from the sea. You will need to climb from the tender onto wet, sharp, unstable rock. Wear a dry suit, PFD, helmet, and gloves. Use a fixed line. Watch the water for at least thirty minutes before attempting any landing. Large waves can seal the lower entrance and create surge that will slam you against the rock.

### The drowned throat  
Do not enter through the drowned throat. It is an overhead environment with wave surge, pressure changes, and no clear exit. For an ordinary human, it is a death trap unless you are a fully equipped cave diver with redundant gas, line, and the right training. Even then, it is a bad idea. The throat connects to the pool inside, but the pressure changes when large waves seal it can cause barotrauma and disorientation. Stay out.

### The roof rent  
If you can arrive from above—by aircraft, drone, or climbing the stack—you can rappel through the rent near the summit. Use double anchors, edge protection, and a haul line for your gear. The lip of the rent is wet with spray and may be loose. The spray curtain hides the room from outside. Rappel slowly; the rock may be brittle. Once inside, do not stand directly under the rent; falling spray and small stones come through it.

## 6. First actions inside the Bell

The moment you arrive, your first instinct may be to look around in wonder. Suppress that long enough to do these things.

1. **Clip into an anchor.** Immediately secure yourself to something high on the ledge. The room moves; the floor shifts; a surge can knock you off balance.  
2. **Put on helmet, eye, and hearing protection.** Do not take them off inside.  
3. **Watch the pool for a full swell cycle.** Learn its rhythm. Note how high the water rises, how often the big surges come, and how the air pressure changes.  
4. **Identify a safe zone.** Choose a spot above the splash zone and away from falling-rock trajectories. The highest ledge, out of reach of all but the biggest swells, is where previous travelers stacked their stones and made their hearth. But that does not mean it is safe from rockfall.  
5. **Check the roof vent.** Make sure it is open. The high whistle means the vent is open. If it is blocked or silent when it should not be, pressure may build.  
6. **Test the air.** Use a gas monitor. If you smell sulfur or feel lightheaded, do not stay.  
7. **Keep gear in waterproof bags, tied off.** The pool can surge up and throw spray; gear can be washed into the water or buried in sharp sand.  
8. **Do not disturb the artifacts.** The seven stacked basalt blocks, the blackened hearth, the flint scraper, the bone needle, the braided sinew, the carved ledger of water rises and falls, the pictograph of descending lines—these are not decorations. They are a record of people who came before. Do not move them, lean on them, or add your own marks. Treat them as an archaeological site.

## 7. Living in the Bell: day-to-day survival

### The floor  
The floor is a shelf of shattered basalt and black sand, but the sand is mostly angular shards. It is sharp enough to cut through fabric. Never kneel, sit, or lie down without a thick pad. Every surge moves the shards, creating new patterns that are erased before they become landforms. The ground under you is not stable.

### The pool  
The pool is the room’s heart and its danger. It connects through a drowned throat to the sea. Every swell makes the water rise—sometimes gently, sometimes with a slap that throws spray against the ceiling. When a large wave seals the lower entrance, the air pressure changes. Your ears will pop. The vent near the top will whistle.

- Do not swim, wade, or reach into the pool.  
- Do not sleep on the lowest ledge.  
- If you need water from the pool, use a tethered container from above.  
- If you must approach the pool edge, use a rope and a buddy.  
- Watch for the glimmering skin in the water—the boundary between fresh meltwater and heavier brine. It is a surface within a surface. The lower water is too clear and makes things appear closer than they are. Do not trust your eyes if you reach below the surface.

### Pressure and air  
The room is a pressure vessel. When a large wave seals the lower entrance, the air compresses. Your ears will pop. Equalize early and often. If you feel pressure building and cannot clear it, move near the vent and stay calm. If the vent is blocked, pressure could become dangerous. Know the high whistle that means the vent is open. If it stops, pay attention.

### Fire and stove  
Do not light a fire unless the roof vent is clear. Even a small stove can use up oxygen and fill the room with carbon monoxide. Use a stove with a windscreen and a CO detector. If the air smells wrong, do not use it.

### Rock movement  
The stack is growing. The mountains are still ascending. A new fissure may split the wall by evening. A slab may fall into the pool with a boom that shakes the bones. Watch for fresh cracks. Mark them with tape or small brittle indicators. If they widen, leave. Do not stand under overhangs or near the rent in the roof. Do not put your hand into deep cracks; they can hold heat and may close suddenly.

### Noise  
The noise never stops. It is not background. It fills the skull. There is the deep bass of water hammering rock, the hiss of retreating foam, the constant rain of small pebbles from the walls, and beneath it all a grinding, ticking sound—the mountain rising, cracking, making room for itself. You will hear it even in your sleep, if you sleep. Use hearing protection for rest, but you must still be able to hear the vent whistle and the sound of a large crack forming. Sleep in shifts.

### Life and biology  
Life here is sparse. Above the waterline, pale lichens flake from the stone. A green-black film of cyanobacteria darkens the wall where spray wets it. A ring of yellow-green scum marks the last high surge. In the pool, blind amphipods and a colorless flatworm live in the sediment. Do not wash with soap, do not collect them, do not disturb the biological film. Treat the place as a fragile, unfinished ecosystem.

### Sanitation  
There is no soil, no latrine, no place to bury waste. Pack out everything. Use wag bags or similar for human waste. Do not throw anything into the pool or the sea. Leave no trace.

## 8. What the experience will actually feel like

You will arrive, and the first thing you notice is the sound. Not as a background, but as a physical pressure on the ears and chest. A deep boom of water against rock, a hiss of foam, a clatter of pebbles. The air is cool, thin, and smells of crushed stone, salt, and something faintly electrical, as if the rock has not cooled all the way.

Light comes through the roof opening as if through a pane of water—gray-blue, soft, bright enough to read by but colored by spray. The floor crunches underfoot like black glass. The pool rises and falls, slaps the ceiling, retreats. Your ears pop. The vent whistles. The rock hums.

You will likely feel the place as unfinished, not hostile but indifferent. The walls are sharp. The noise is relentless. The floor shifts with each surge. You will learn the rhythm of the water, the compression just before the big surges, the high whistle that means the vent is open. You will not sleep on the lowest ledge. You will not light a fire unless the roof vent is clear.

At some point, you will see the seven stacked stones, the hearth, the ledger marks scratched into the wall, the pictograph of two descending lines like rain or a person dissolving into sea. You will realize that people have been here before, and that they, too, learned to listen. They left no shelter, only the record of water rises and falls. The place remembers them.

Then, perhaps in the evening, a new crack will open. A slab will fall into the pool with a boom that shakes your ribs. The mountain will remind you that it is still being born.

## 9. Exit and emergency

- **Leave during calm seas.** Watch the weather and swell.  
- **Pack out everything.** The Bell is not a dump.  
- **Have a pre-rigged exit line** if you came by rope.  
- **Know your emergency extraction.** If the vent blocks, gas smell increases, rockfall intensifies, or you cannot equalize, leave immediately. Use your beacon or multiverse return device.  
- **Do not wait for conditions to become perfect.** Leave while you can.

---

## 10. Final checklist for the Bell

- Full dry suit, hood, gloves, boots  
- Cut-resistant gloves, knee and elbow pads  
- Helmet, sealed eye protection, hearing protection  
- Rope, harness, ascenders, descenders, redundant anchors  
- Bivy sack, sleeping bag, two closed-cell foam pads  
- All drinking water, high-energy food, stove, CO detector  
- Trauma kit, gas monitor, personal locator beacon  
- Radio, whistle, headlamp, spare batteries  
- Waterproof bags, notebook, crack monitoring tape  
- A team of at least three people  
- A pre-rigged exit route  
- Respect for the place and its previous visitors

---

The Bell is not a place you conquer. It is a place you learn to stay inside for a little while, listening to the sea and the rock learning to be. Prepare for it as if you are going to sit inside the heartbeat of a world that has not yet decided what it will become.
