# Preparation for the Green Shaft

## What You Are Entering

The Green Shaft is not a pond, not an aquarium, and not a flooded ruin. It is a vertical, artificially maintained, plant-saturated water column inside a high-altitude tower. It is small enough to touch both walls, but more than a hundred meters deep. It is warm as blood, lit like a laboratory, and entirely without animals. Every surface, every cable, every pipe has been colonized by plants. There is no soil, no weather, no fast time. The only sounds are the subsonic hum of a pump, the tick of cooling metal, and the hiss of bubbles escaping from leaves.

As an ordinary human multiverse traveler, you are the most wrong thing this place has ever contained. You are an animal, you are fast, you are loud, you shed skin and exhale bubbles and radiate body heat. You do not belong here. Preparing to visit the Green Shaft means preparing to be a temporary, gentle intrusion into a closed loop that has no room for you.

You will need to think of this not as tourism, but as a technical, overhead, high-altitude, warm-water, confined-space, botanical dive. It combines the hazards of deep cave diving, high-angle rope access, an industrial plant room, and a sterile laboratory. Under- prepare, and the Green Shaft will tangle you, overheat you, crush you, or simply make you so claustrophobic and disoriented that you lose yourself in the green.

## Core Hazards

### 1. Depth and Pressure
More than a hundred meters of vertical water means you will experience pressures of roughly eleven bar at the bottom. That is well beyond recreational diving limits. You need technical dive training with mixed gases — trimix or heliox — to manage nitrogen narcosis, oxygen toxicity, and gas density. At that depth, ordinary air becomes thick, hard to breathe, and potentially lethal.

Because the tower is high on a ridge, the surface itself is at altitude. Thin outside air means reduced atmospheric pressure, which changes how your body absorbs and releases inert gas. You must use altitude-adjusted dive tables or a dive computer set for the actual surface pressure. Do not fly, jump between realities, or ascend further immediately after surfacing. Plan for a long surface interval at the tower’s altitude before any significant pressure change.

### 2. Heat, Not Cold
The water is warm as blood near the surface and warmer still at depth, heated by pumps and grow-lights. This means hypothermia is not your main risk. Instead, you face hyperthermia, dehydration, and exhaustion. A thick wetsuit or drysuit can become a sauna. You need a thin, abrasion-resistant exposure suit that allows you to shed heat, or a breathable drysuit with active cooling. Drink water before the dive. Do not assume warm water is comfortable; it can silently cook you while you work.

### 3. Confinement and Entanglement
The shaft is six paces by seven — no wider than a small room — and it is packed with vegetation. There is no open water. Every cubic meter holds a leaf, a root, a stem. Roots hang like beards from open elevator doors. Vines coil around rails. Free-floating plants drift at the depth of brightest side-light. The elevator car hangs between the seventh and eighth floors, its ceiling split by a fern. The counterweight is motionless on its cables. The pump draws water from the bottom and returns it through jets near the top.

This is an overhead environment, even though the surface is directly above. If you panic and thrash, you will become entangled. If you lose your line, you may not find your way up through the root curtains. Every piece of gear must be streamlined. Nothing dangles. You carry at least two cutting tools — shears and a blunt-tip knife — placed where you can reach them with either hand. You practice entanglement drills until they are automatic.

### 4. Machinery
The machinery is still running. The pump hum is felt in your teeth more than heard. Water is drawn from the bottom, filtered, warmed, oxygenated, and returned through jets near the top. The jets create a faint push that holds all the leaves leaning one way. There may be suction at the bottom intake, hidden by plants. Pipes run along the walls, some wrapped so tightly by roots it is hard to tell hose from root. A vine has grown inside a pressure gauge.

Treat the machinery as live industrial equipment. Avoid the intake, avoid the jets, avoid the counterweight and cables, avoid the elevator car. The water is conductive. Any electrical panel, pump housing, or exposed wire is a hazard. Do not touch anything that looks mechanical unless absolutely necessary. The Green Shaft is not a natural ecosystem; it is a machine for growing plants, and the machine does not know or care that you are inside it.

### 5. Altitude and Outside Air
Outside the tower, the air is thin and cold. Inside the top of the shaft, the air is conditioned — warm, humid, smelling of ozone and wet leaves. The contrast is disorienting. If you arrive by climbing the ridge, acclimatize first. If you step from the thin outside air into the warm, humid shaft, you may feel faint. If you exit after a long dive and step into the cold thin air, you risk cold shock, hypoxia, and decompression complications. Have warm clothing, supplemental oxygen, and a place to rest at the top.

The air at the top may also contain elevated ozone from the machinery. Ozone irritates lungs. Monitor the air before removing your mask. Do not assume the humid green smell is safe.

### 6. No Railings
Each floor opens directly into the water. There is no glass, no railing, no barrier. From the landings, the shaft looks like an aquarium, but the water presses directly against the air at the surface. If you stand on a landing, you are standing at the edge of a hundred-meter vertical drop into warm green water. The duckweed mat looks solid, like a green floor. It is not. One step and you are in.

Never approach an opening without a harness, a tether, and edge protection. If you are entering from a landing, use a controlled platform or ladder, not a jump. If you are working at the top, secure yourself to a structural anchor. The building has no railing to catch you.

## Required Skills and Training

An ordinary human multiverse traveler should not attempt the Green Shaft without the following:

- **Technical deep diving certification** — trimix or heliox, decompression procedures, and experience beyond 100 m.
- **Overhead environment training** — full cave or wreck penetration. The shaft is a vertical overhead environment with entanglement, no direct visual ascent, and potential zero visibility.
- **Altitude diving training** — understanding of altitude-adjusted decompression and gas planning.
- **Confined space and rope access** — vertical entry and exit, fall protection, winch operation, and rescue.
- **Plant-aware movement** — practice moving through dense aquatic vegetation without destroying it. Conventional fin kicks are unacceptable. Learn pull-and-glide, hand sculling, and neutral buoyancy so precise you can hang motionless among roots.
- **Emergency medical** — oxygen administration, first aid for barotrauma, decompression sickness, oxygen toxicity, heat illness, immersion pulmonary edema, and entanglement injuries.
- **Line handling and navigation** — cave line, reels, tie-offs, lost-line drills, and communication by line signals.
- **Psychological resilience** — sensory deprivation, confinement tolerance, panic management, and the ability to move at a speed that feels unnatural.

Do not attempt this alone. Minimum team: two divers, one surface tender, one standby diver. Everyone must understand the environment and the plan.

## Equipment List

The Green Shaft is warm, dense, vertical, and artificial. Your gear must be minimal, streamlined, and redundant.

### Dive Gear
- **Closed-circuit rebreather** preferred. Open circuit releases bubbles that disturb the water, damage delicate plant structures, and reduce visibility. A rebreather also gives you longer gas duration for slow, careful progress. It must be rated for the depth and configured for altitude use.
- **Full-face mask with voice communications** if possible. It protects your face from roots and lets you talk to your buddy and surface tender. A standard mask is a backup, not a primary.
- **Abrasion-resistant exposure suit** — thin neoprene or breathable drysuit with full-body coverage, including gloves, hood, and boots. The water is warm but roots, steel edges, and synthetic mesh can cut.
- **Dive helmet or hard liner** — protect your head from falling debris, the elevator car, and accidental contact with steel.
- **Cutting tools** — shears and a blunt-tip knife, both accessible to either hand. A small serrated line cutter for synthetic rope. Do not carry a large knife; it will snag.
- **Lights** — a primary canister light and two backups. The shaft is lit like a laboratory, but the green water and dense vegetation swallow light. You need light to see your instruments, your line, and potential entanglements.
- **Guideline reel and cave line** — you will run a line from the surface to your deepest point. Tie off to structural points, not plants. The line is your way home.
- **Redundant buoyancy** — a wing and drysuit or dual bladders. At depth, buoyancy changes with gas compression. You must be able to hold a stop without kicking.
- **Dive computer with altitude and trimix capability** — and a backup depth gauge and timer.
- **Backup gas** — bailout cylinders staged or carried, sufficient for your decompression plan and an emergency.
- **A small lift bag or dSMB** — for emergency signaling if you surface away from the entry point.

### Access and Safety
- **Full-body harness and rope access gear** — for entering from the top or a landing.
- **Fall protection lanyards and edge rollers** — to protect the concrete edge and the rope.
- **Tripod or anchor system** — to lower divers and equipment.
- **Winch or hauling system** — for evacuation.
- **Surface communications panel** — linked to the divers.
- **Oxygen and first aid kit** — including treatment for decompression sickness and heat illness.
- **Air monitor** — O2, CO2, ozone, temperature, humidity at the top.
- **Water testing kit** — pH, salinity, temperature, and dissolved oxygen, if you need to document conditions.
- **Decontamination supplies** — disinfectant, rinse water, brushes, and sealed containers for waste.
- **High-altitude outdoor gear** — for the approach and exit outside the tower.

### Decontamination and Containment
The Green Shaft has no animals, no soil, and an unusually clean plant community. You are a walking ecosystem. Before entering, wash every piece of gear with a mild disinfectant that will not harm plants. Dry it completely. Check for seeds, insects, and soil in cuffs, pockets, and seams. Do not bring food. Do not wear anything with loose fibers.

Your breath, skin oils, and hair are contamination. A full-face mask and exposure suit reduce but do not eliminate your shed. Use a drysuit with dry gloves and a hood, or at minimum a wetsuit that covers all skin. Do not enter with open wounds. If you must urinate during a long dive, use a closed collection system. No spitting, no vomiting, no bleeding into the water.

After the dive, rinse all gear in a decontamination area before it leaves the tower. Collect all waste. Treat everything that touched the water as potentially carrying a new microbial community back to your home reality.

## Entry and Exit Strategy

### Before You Enter
Acclimatize to the altitude. Check the outside weather. Check the air at the top of the shaft. Secure the area. Set up a surface station with gas, communications, and rescue equipment. Mark the entry point clearly. Brief the entire team on the plan, including maximum depth, planned decompression, and emergency procedures.

Do not trust the duckweed mat. It looks solid, but it is only a floating layer of plants. Your entry should be controlled — either from the skylight above, using a rope and harness, or from a landing with a properly protected edge. If you must break through the duckweed, part it gently with your hands or a soft frame. Do not cut it unless absolutely necessary. The mat will close over your head as you descend.

### The Descent
Run a guideline from the surface. Attach it to a structural point — a rail, a steel angle, a pipe bracket — not to a plant. Descend slowly, following the line. Do not kick with fins. Conventional fins will shred the vegetation. Use pull-and-glide: grip the line or a wall feature, pull yourself down a few centimeters, and pause. Let the plants close behind you. Move at the speed of a leaf unfurling.

Stay clear of the elevator car and counterweight. They are overhead hazards and entanglement points. The pump intake at the bottom may be hidden by roots and leaves. Stop your descent well above it. The water at the bottom is warmest and darkest; pressure is at its highest. Do not touch the bottom unless your plan requires it.

Watch your instruments constantly. The green light and the absence of animals can make depth feel meaningless. You may feel as if you are not moving at all. Your depth gauge is the only thing that tells you how far you are from the surface.

### The Ascent
Ascend along the line. Do not free-swim through the root curtains. You may have to stop frequently to untangle yourself or your line. Be patient. Decompression stops are mandatory. The warm water may make the stops feel endless. Do not skip them. The line is your path. If you lose the line, stop, hold position, and deploy your backup reel. In a shaft this narrow, the walls are always close, but the vegetation can disorient you completely. Do not thrash.

When you reach the surface, exit through the same controlled point. The duckweed will part around you. Have the surface tender help you out. Do not stand on the edge without a tether. Remove gear carefully, avoiding plant debris. Begin decontamination immediately.

## Ecological and Ethical Protocol

The Green Shaft is a closed loop. The plants are the only multicellular life. They are garden and forest and filter at once. The water is cleaned by them. The machinery is colonized by them. The place is not dead, but it is moving at a pace that makes you feel too fast.

Your presence is a contamination event. You must minimize every impact:

- **Introduce nothing.** No animals, no microbes beyond what you cannot avoid, no soil, no food, no chemicals.
- **Remove nothing.** Do not take plant samples, water samples, or souvenirs unless you have explicit permission. The Green Shaft is not a natural place to be collected from.
- **Do not cut plants.** If you become entangled, cut only synthetic line, mesh, or your own gear. The plants are not the enemy. They are the inhabitants.
- **Do not touch machinery.** The pressure gauge with the vine inside is not a photo opportunity. The vine has made it a tiny greenhouse. Leave it alone.
- **Move gently.** No fins, no sudden movements, no bright lights unless needed. Your bubbles, if you use open circuit, are already a disturbance. A rebreather is better.
- **Collect all waste.** Everything that comes into the shaft with you leaves with you.

If you cannot follow these rules, do not visit.

## Psychological Preparation

The Green Shaft will feel wrong to you. It is silent in a way that is not peaceful. There are no birds, no frogs, no fish. The water is empty of everything but plants and fine suspended dust. The plants do not sway because something has passed; they sway only when the slow circulation shifts. Some root masses have grown into shapes that suggest animals — a curve like a fish, a coil like a snake — but they are only roots. The stillness is total.

You will feel too fast. Your heartbeat, your breathing, your blinking will seem violent. The place moves at the speed of concrete settling. A bubble rises through the root curtain and vanishes without breaking the surface. The duckweed drifts a handspan in an hour. A vine extends a finger-width overnight. You are a storm that lasts a few hours and then leaves.

This can be deeply unsettling. Some people panic. Some become hypnotized. Some feel an overwhelming grief at the voicelessness of the place. Prepare yourself by practicing slow movement in confined water. Sit in a dark, quiet room and listen to your own breathing until it stops feeling loud. Meditate on what it means to be the only animal in a garden with no birdsong.

If you feel panic rising, stop moving. Hold the line. Close your eyes. Breathe slowly. The water is warm and supports you. The line leads home. Do not thrash. Do not try to swim upward without the line. Wait until your heart slows. Then move again, a few centimeters at a time.

Bring a buddy not just for safety, but for shared reality. After you exit, talk about what you saw. The Green Shaft can leave a strange residue in the mind — the feeling of having visited a place that is alive but not awake, a machine that forgot it was a building, a green slot at the top of the world with no room for you.

## Contingency Plans

**Entanglement:** Stop. Breathe. Signal your buddy. Untangle yourself slowly, pulling one loop at a time. If you cannot free yourself, use your cutting tools on synthetic line or mesh, not on plants. If you are truly stuck, the surface tender can lower a line or send a standby diver. Do not panic.

**Lost line:** Stop. Control buoyancy. Deploy your backup reel. Look for your buddy’s light. If you cannot find the line, ascend slowly along the wall, using it as a guide. The shaft is narrow; the wall is always within reach. But the vegetation may hide the surface. Use your depth gauge and proceed with extreme care.

**Gas emergency:** Go to your buddy, share gas, and begin the ascent along the line. If you are on a rebreather, bail out to open circuit. Follow your decompression plan as closely as possible, but do not skip critical stops unless the alternative is drowning.

**Heat illness:** If you feel faint, nauseated, or confused, signal your buddy and begin a slow, controlled ascent. The water is warm, but the effort of moving through dense vegetation can overheat you. At the surface, remove gear, rest, and hydrate.

**Equipment failure:** If a light fails, use a backup. If your mask floods, clear it. If your drysuit leaks, the warm water is not immediately dangerous, but you may lose buoyancy. Ascend.

**Machinery failure:** If the pump stops, the current will change. The water may begin to cool or stratify. The leaves may drift. This is not your emergency unless you are near the intake or jets. Monitor the situation and abort if conditions become unstable.

**Medical emergency:** Have oxygen ready at the surface. Administer first aid. Evacuate to a qualified medical facility as soon as possible. Remember that you are at altitude; decompression sickness may be more likely and more severe.

## Final Briefing

The Green Shaft is high in a tower on a high ridge, filled with water that should not be there, growing plants that should not be this clean, and staying so quiet that you can almost hear the cells dividing. It is a miniature abyss inside a cabinet, a tropical ocean in a high cold place, a landscape of concrete and steel and water and green.

An ordinary human multiverse traveler can visit it, but only with humility, training, and immense care. You must be a diver, a climber, an ecologist, and a ghost. You must move at the speed of the machines that maintain the place — which is to say, almost not at all. You must leave no trace.

When you surface through the duckweed mat, you will be changed. You will have been the only animal in a world of plants. You will carry the memory of that voiceless green silence for a long time. That is the price of entry. Prepare for it.
