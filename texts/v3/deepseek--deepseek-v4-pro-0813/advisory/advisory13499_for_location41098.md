**Preparation for the High Mire**

Pack for a place that will never dry, never sharpen, never give you a firm answer. This is not a hike through a forest; it is a slow immersion into a waterlogged, living dome. You are not walking on ground. You are walking on a thick, wet mat of dead and living plants. The main threats are moisture, disorientation, soft ground, fungal and bacterial load, and the psychological weight of endless green twilight.

---

## I. Understand what you are walking on

The high mire is not a swamp in a basin. It is the basin, the mountain, and the weather. The ground underfoot gives slightly, then springs back. It is made of peat, moss, root, leaf-mold, and water. There is no bare ground. What looks like soil is living tissue.

This means:

- Every step is soft and slightly unstable.
- The ground can hide water-filled hollows, old root shafts, and deep pockets.
- The terrain is slow and exhausting.
- There are no dry spots, no solid rock to rest on.

Expect to move slowly. A fit person may cover only one or two kilometers in an hour in the thickest parts. Plan for that.

---

## II. Clothing and skin care

Assume nothing you wear will stay dry. The air is warm and saturated. Rain does not so much fall as form. Mist condenses on every surface. Sweat does not evaporate well.

### Base layers
- Synthetic or merino wool, not cotton.
- Long sleeves and long pants, tight at cuffs.
- Treat outer clothing with permethrin to deter insects.

### Outer layers
- A waterproof-breathable rain jacket and pants.
- In 100% humidity, breathability is limited, so choose garments with large pit zips and mechanical ventilation.
- A wide-brim hat or hood is useful against constant drips.
- Light gloves protect your hands from sharp stems, thorns, and rough bark.

### Foot care is the highest priority
Constant wetness causes maceration, blisters, and fungal infections. Bring:

- Toe-sock liners or thin synthetic liners.
- Wool or synthetic outer socks.
- Multiple pairs in waterproof dry bags.
- Neoprene socks if you choose to accept wet feet.
- Gaiters to keep debris out.

Stop at least once a day to wash, dry, and inspect your feet. Apply antifungal powder or cream. Use barrier cream on areas prone to chafing. Carry a foot repair kit: blister pads, tape, antiseptic, and spare insoles.

---

## III. Footwear

Your feet will be wet regardless. Choose well-draining trail shoes or lightweight boots with aggressive tread, paired with neoprene socks. Shoes that drain will not hold water. Rubber boots may seem logical, but over long distances they cause blisters and overheating. They also have poor traction on roots.

For very soft areas, consider lightweight bog shoes or small snowshoes to spread your weight. Always carry a spare pair of insoles.

---

## IV. Navigation and light

The light is dim, greenish, and sourceless. There is no sun, no dawn, no dusk. Fog can reduce visibility to a few meters. The dense, absorbent quiet swallows sound, so you cannot rely on hearing distant landmarks.

Bring:

- A compass.
- GPS with extra batteries.
- Altimeter.
- Physical maps if you have them.
- A whistle and signal mirror.

The standing stones are the only reliable human landmarks. Record them as waypoints. In thick fog, keep companions within sight. Use whistle signals and bright clothing. Do not rely on solar chargers; the sky is a permanent low membrane. Bring extra batteries or a hand-crank charger.

A headlamp is essential. Use a red or low-white mode. Bright light reflects back in mist and ruins your night vision.

---

## V. Water and food

Water is everywhere but not safe. It is tannic, full of peat particles, microbes, and possibly unfamiliar pathogens.

### Water
- Pre-filter through a cloth or fine screen to remove particles.
- Then boil, or use a reliable filter/purifier.
- Chemical treatments may be less effective in tannic water. If in doubt, boil.
- Drink electrolytes. The high humidity hides sweat loss, and you may not realize how much fluid you are losing.

### Food
Bring all food. Do not rely on local plants or animals. The biochemistry may be unfamiliar, and you should avoid damaging the ecosystem.

Use a liquid-fuel stove for hot meals and water boiling. There is no dry wood, and open flames are dangerous near marsh gas pockets.

---

## VI. Shelter

A hammock with a bug net and large tarp is usually the best shelter. It keeps you off the wet, springy ground and reduces your impact. But many trees are hollow, leaning, or shallow-rooted. Test carefully before attaching anything. Use wide tree straps to avoid damaging bark.

If you must use a tent, choose a freestanding one with a bathtub floor. The ground may be too soft for normal stakes. Carry long sand/snow stakes or tie to roots. Everything will be damp, so store your sleeping insulation in dry bags. A synthetic sleeping bag or quilt is essential; down loses warmth when wet.

A bivy sack is a good backup.

---

## VII. Medical and hygiene

The air is full of spores, mold, and insects. The water carries biological risk. Your immune system is likely naive to this world.

### Respiratory protection
Bring a P100 or N95 respirator for long exposure, especially when disturbing moss or peat. Some spores may cause allergic or inflammatory reactions.

### Insects and leeches
Use insect repellent and a head net. Check for leeches regularly. Wear leech socks or tuck pants into socks. Assume any amphibian or insect may be toxic or carry parasites; do not handle them.

### Medical kit
Bring a broad medical kit:

- Antifungals
- Antibacterial ointment
- Antihistamines
- Anti-inflammatory
- Oral rehydration salts
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics
- Wound closure strips
- Pressure bandage
- SAM splint
- Painkillers
- Any personal medications

Assume you will be wet for days. Skin infections, foot rot, and respiratory irritation are the most likely medical issues.

---

## VIII. Safety on the mire

The ground itself is the main danger.

- Always probe with a pole before stepping onto dark or hollow-sounding ground.
- The peat can hide water-filled hollows, old root shafts, and vertical shafts where ferns hang.
- If you break through, do not struggle. Spread your arms and legs, float or swim, and roll onto firmer ground.
- Avoid enclosed hollows and depressions where marsh gas may accumulate. If you feel dizzy or nauseous, move away and do not light anything.
- Do not enter hollow trees or vertical shafts. They may be low in oxygen and structurally unstable.
- Do not camp beneath leaning trunks or large dead branches. The forest is old, and things collapse.

There is no fire culture here. Lightning smolders briefly and goes out. Do not attempt to build a fire.

---

## IX. Waste and leave no trace

This is a fragile, slow-growing ecosystem. The peat grows a fraction of an inch in a lifetime. Your footprints may linger for years.

- Pack out everything you bring in, including solid waste.
- The peat cannot break down human waste quickly, and digging would damage the mat.
- Use wag bags and carry them out.
- Avoid using soap or toothpaste near water; every drop drains into the mire.
- Urinate in a bottle if possible.
- Do not cut plants, break branches, or create new trails.
- If you find the old stone paths or slabs, use them.
- Do not disturb the standing stones. They may be the only geometry left by previous travelers.

---

## X. Psychological preparation

The mire is quiet in a dense, absorbent way. There is no sun, no horizon, no dawn or dusk to structure time. Fog can close in until the world is a ten-foot room. The constant drips, the slow creaks, the sigh of settling ground can become hypnotic or unsettling.

Bring a watch and keep a routine. Set small daily goals: reach a certain stone, a GPS waypoint, a change in terrain. Travel with a partner if you can. The mire swallows sound, so separation is dangerous.

Accept that you will be wet. Accept that progress will be slow. The place does not care about your schedule. The quiet is not emptiness; it is fullness. There is so much life packed into every inch that it has learned to be quiet.

---

## XI. Minimal equipment list

- Waterproof pack liner and multiple dry bags
- Hammock, bug net, large tarp, tree straps, long stakes
- Synthetic sleeping bag or quilt, insulated pad
- Water filter or purifier, water bottles or bladder
- Liquid-fuel stove, fuel, pot, spoon, mug, lighter
- High-energy food for duration plus 30%
- Electrolyte tablets
- Clothing: synthetic base layers, midlayer, rain gear, wide-brim hat, gloves, neck gaiter
- Footwear: trail shoes or boots, neoprene socks, liner socks, outer socks, gaiters, spare insoles
- Trekking poles with wide baskets, probe pole
- Headlamp, flashlight, extra batteries, hand-crank charger
- Compass, GPS, altimeter, maps, whistle, signal mirror
- P100 or N95 respirator, eye protection
- Insect repellent, permethrin, head net
- Comprehensive first aid kit
- Wag bags, toilet paper, hand sanitizer, pee bottle
- Repair kit, duct tape, knife or multi-tool, paracord
- Waterproof notebook and pencil
- Emergency beacon
- Waterproof hard case with desiccant for any multiverse traversal device or communicator

---

## XII. Final intuitive note

You are not walking on ground. You are walking on the body of the mire. The plants have built a mountain in reverse, and the water moves through it like blood. Your preparation should be less about conquering and more about learning to move without leaving a scar.

Pack for wetness, dimness, and slowness. Then, when you stand still and hear the million small sounds—the tick of water on a broad leaf, the faint rasp of insects, the creak of a leaning trunk, the low gurgle under the ground—you will understand why the silence is not absence. It is density. The high mire does not need to be loud. It is too old and too large for that.
