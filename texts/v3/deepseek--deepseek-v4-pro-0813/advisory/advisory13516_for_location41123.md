# Preparing to Visit The Drowned Lattice

The Drowned Lattice is not a casual destination. It is a fully submerged, near-freezing, near-lightless, hand-built landscape of stone terraces, sunken fields, kelp lattices, and ancient walls. There are no animals, no air pockets, and visibility is often measured in arm-lengths. The water is cold enough to kill you quickly without insulation. The structures are ancient and fragile. The silence is profound. Preparing for this place requires treating it as a combination of technical cold-water diving, cave diving, underwater archaeology, and long-duration isolation.

Below is a comprehensive preparation guide for an ordinary human multiverse traveler — someone with no superhuman abilities, only planning, training, and appropriate technology.

---

## 1. Understand the Environment First

Before packing a single piece of gear, understand what you are walking — or rather, sinking — into:

- **Medium:** All-liquid, dense, slow. You are not in air.
- **Temperature:** Near-freezing, the same temperature as the stone. Probably 0–4°C.
- **Light:** Faint blue-gray from above, but effectively darkness with texture. Visibility is extremely low.
- **Terrain:** Paved seafloor, steps, walls, slumped terraces, sunken rectangular fields, channels, fallen columns.
- **Vegetation:** Lush but colorless by surface standards — old leather, wet ash, lead, deep bluish purple. Dense moss, kelp-like fronds taller than houses, trellises, hanging hair-like filaments.
- **Animal life:** None. No fish, no crabs, no worms, no shrimp. The water is too cold, too dark, too chemically slow for animals. You will be the only moving animal.
- **Structures:** Hand-worked stone, split wood, twisted kelp-fiber cord. No metal. Ancient, worn smooth, but still standing.
- **Time:** Currents are weak. Processes happen on century-long clocks. The place is slow, patient, complete.

The key hazards are:

- Hypothermia
- Drowning
- Disorientation
- Entanglement in dense vegetation
- Structural collapse or shifting stone
- Anoxic or toxic water conditions
- Psychological stress from sensory deprivation and isolation

---

## 2. Physical and Medical Preparation

An ordinary human body is not adapted for this environment. You must train and condition yourself well in advance.

- **Get a dive medical clearance.** Your cardiovascular health, lung function, and equalization must be excellent.
- **Cold-water conditioning.** Gradual, supervised exposure to cold water will help you understand your limits, but never make you immune.
- **Cardio and strength training.** You will swim in bulky gear, carry cylinders, and possibly walk on uneven stone. Core strength and leg endurance are critical.
- **Flexibility.** You need to reach valves, deploy lines, and cut entanglement in tight spaces.
- **Equalization practice.** The pressure will change as you move through the water column. You must be able to equalize easily, repeatedly, and without thinking.
- **Panic resistance training.** If you are prone to claustrophobia or panic in dark, confined water, this destination is not for you. Practice in controlled, zero-visibility overhead environments.

---

## 3. Technical Diving and Overhead-Environment Training

The Drowned Lattice behaves like a cave or wreck penetration: overhead, dark, silty, and disorienting. You need formal training and certification in:

- **Advanced nitrox and trimix diving** — because depth is uncertain and gas management must be precise.
- **Cave diving** — for zero-visibility line work, entanglement management, and emergency procedures.
- **Wreck penetration** — for navigating man-made structures, low ceilings, and tight openings.
- **Underwater navigation** — using guidelines, compass, acoustic pingers, and memory.
- **Rescue and first aid** — including hypothermia treatment, near-drowning response, and emergency decompression management.

Do not attempt this with only basic open-water scuba certification. That would be suicidal.

---

## 4. Life Support and Breathing

The water is cold, dark, and likely low in oxygen. You need a self-contained breathing system that minimizes disturbance and maximizes reliability.

- **Use a closed-circuit rebreather (CCR).** It recycles breathing gas, produces few or no bubbles, reduces heat loss, and extends bottom time. Bubbles from open-circuit scuba would be loud, disturbing, and would stir up silt.
- **Carry redundant bailout cylinders.** Have enough gas for your maximum planned depth, plus decompression stops. Because the site depth may be unknown, plan conservatively.
- **Full-face mask with voice communications.** This allows you to talk to your buddy, hear your own breathing, and protect your face from cold water and debris.
- **Hard helmet.** The stone lintels, leaning trellises, and shifting gates can strike your head. Protect it.
- **Dive computer (primary and backup).** Track depth, time, and decompression obligations. Carry a backup in case of failure.

---

## 5. Thermal Protection

The water is near-freezing. Even brief skin exposure can cause pain, loss of dexterity, and rapid hypothermia.

- **Drysuit with thick undergarments.** A well-sealed drysuit is mandatory. Use multiple layers of thermal undergarments designed for cold water.
- **Actively heated suit if possible.** Electric heating elements in the chest, back, and gloves can extend safe exposure time.
- **Dry gloves.** Your hands are your primary tools. Keep them warm and dry.
- **Hard-soled boots.** The pavement is uneven, and you may need to walk. Soft dive boots will not protect your feet.
- **Hood and face seal.** Most heat is lost through the head. A thick hood is non-negotiable.
- **Chemical heat packs.** For hands, feet, and core. Bring spares.

Plan limited exposure times. Even the best suit will eventually let cold seep in. Know the signs of hypothermia: shivering, loss of dexterity, confusion, lethargy. End the dive before those appear.

---

## 6. Lighting and Visibility

The Drowned Lattice is dark, and the water is full of silt, spores, and leaf fragments. Visibility may be measured in arm-lengths. Light can be more of a curse than a help if used wrong.

- **Primary canister light.** Powerful, reliable, long burn time. Use a wide, low beam to reduce backscatter in turbid water.
- **Backup lights.** At least two. One on your body, one on your helmet. They must be reachable without looking.
- **Chemical lightsticks and small strobes.** For marking your line, your buddy, and your exit.
- **Handheld sonar or acoustic imaging.** In near-zero visibility, sonar can “see” walls, terraces, and large structures through the murk. This is far more useful than visible light alone.
- **Avoid bright, narrow beams** that reflect off silt and create a white-out. Use your light like a brush, not a spotlight.

Accept that you cannot see far. Your sense of the place will come through touch, line, and sonar more than through eyesight.

---

## 7. Navigation

The Drowned Lattice is a maze of walls, terraces, fields, channels, and kelp rows. It is easy to become disoriented and lost.

- **Primary guideline reel.** Run a continuous line from your entry point. Tie off only to solid, non-cultural anchors if absolutely necessary. Prefer weighted stakes or removable markers.
- **Multiple reels and line markers.** Use cookies and arrows to mark intersections. You must be able to find your way back in zero visibility.
- **Acoustic pinger/transponder.** A surface or habitat unit can guide you home even if the line breaks.
- **Compass.** Useful as a backup, but in the dark and tight spaces it is easy to lose track. Line is your lifeline.
- **Buddy line.** If visibility drops to nothing, hold a short tether between you and your buddy.

Practice line deployment and retrieval until it is muscle memory. In the dark, you must be able to tie knots, deploy reels, and cut line by feel alone.

---

## 8. Mobility and Entanglement Protection

The vegetation is dense and tangled. Broad kelp fronds, trellises, and hanging hair-like filaments can snag fins, hoses, and limbs. Move slowly and deliberately.

- **Streamlined gear configuration.** No dangling hoses, gauges, or accessories. Everything must be tucked away.
- **Short, stiff fins or none.** Some pavement may be easier to walk than swim. But walking can damage fragile surfaces and stir silt. Maintain neutral buoyancy and use gentle frog kicks.
- **Multiple cutting tools.** Carry shears and a line cutter accessible to either hand. In an entanglement, you may not be able to reach the tool on your right side.
- **Practice entanglement drills.** If caught: stop, breathe, locate the snag, and cut only what is necessary. Do not thrash.
- **Avoid the densest vegetation.** Unless absolutely necessary, do not enter areas where the kelp and trellises have grown into a single continuous tissue.

---

## 9. Water and Environmental Testing

The water may be anoxic, low in oxygen, or contain hydrogen sulfide, methane, or dissolved minerals. The absence of animals suggests the water is chemically inhospitable to animal life.

- **Test the water before entry.** Use portable sensors or sample bottles for O₂, H₂S, CO₂, pH, and temperature.
- **Wear a sealed suit.** Do not allow skin contact if the water is toxic or anoxic. Even if it looks clear, it may contain irritants.
- **Do not drink or taste the water.** The “faint mineral sweetness” is not for you.
- **Rinse all gear after exposure.** The water may be corrosive or biologically active.
- **Watch for structural hazards.** Steps are dished, walls may slump, slab gates can shift without warning. Avoid swimming under or through gate slots. Do not lean on walls or columns.

---

## 10. Emergency Planning

An ordinary human in this environment has a very small margin for error. Prepare for the worst.

- **Never go alone.** Minimum team of three: two divers in the water, one support at base/habitat.
- **Redundant gas, lights, cutting tools.** If any critical system fails, you must have a backup.
- **Emergency ascent plan.** If the surface is far above, you need staged decompression. Carry appropriate gases and plan for delays.
- **Emergency lift bags.** To raise yourself or a casualty if needed.
- **Spare mask, spare dive computer.** A lost or flooded mask in zero visibility is a crisis.
- **Hypothermia treatment.** Space blankets, heat packs, and a dry, heated shelter.
- **If separated from the line:** Stop, deploy a safety light, slow your breathing, and wait. Use acoustic signals. Do not swim blindly.
- **Consider a portable underwater shelter.** A small heated tent or habitat can be deployed on a flat terrace for decompression or emergency.

---

## 11. Psychological Preparation

The Drowned Lattice is profoundly alien and ancient. The darkness is thick, the silence heavy. You are the only moving animal in a vast underwater garden. That can be awe-inspiring or terrifying.

- **Train in sensory deprivation.** Spend time in dark, quiet water under supervision. Learn to trust your gear and your buddy.
- **Use communication constantly.** Hearing another human voice is grounding.
- **Set small objectives.** “Follow the wall to the second step, note the carved ripples, return.” Do not try to explore too much at once.
- **Accept the pace.** The place operates on a timescale far longer than yours. Moving slowly is natural here.
- **Be prepared for the glow.** The moss gives off a faint cold light when disturbed. You may leave a trail of green-gray light behind you. It is beautiful but marks your passage. Let it remind you to move gently.
- **The absence of animals may feel wrong.** You may feel watched, as if the garden itself is aware. It is not. But the feeling is real. Breathe through it.

---

## 12. Ethical and Preservation Protocols

The Drowned Lattice is a hand-built landscape of immense age. Treat it as a sacred site.

- **Do not remove stones, wood, cord, or plant material.**
- **Do not carve, anchor, or tie lines to original structures** unless absolutely necessary for safety. Use temporary, non-invasive markers.
- **Minimize contact with moss and kelp.** Avoid cutting plants except to free yourself from entanglement.
- **Document with photography and sonar.** Avoid bright lights or heat that could damage vegetation.
- **Pack out all waste.** The cold water preserves organic matter for a long time. Do not add to it.
- **Leave no trace.** The place has been underwater so long it has settled into its final form. Do not disturb that completion.

## 14. First Arrival: What to Expect

If your portal or entry places you directly in the water, the cold will hit you immediately, even through the suit. The darkness will feel absolute until your eyes adjust or your lights come on. The first thing you will likely see is not natural rock but a straight edge: a wall, a step, pavement. The shock of that — knowing it is all built — is one of the most important things to prepare for mentally.

Move slowly. Check your buoyancy. Look for your line. Listen for the low creak of kelp and the faint murmur of water. You are not in danger from animals, but you are in a place that will not notice you.

Respect it. Move with care. Leave no trace.

---

The Drowned Lattice is not a place for adrenaline or conquest. It is a place for patience, control, and reverence. An ordinary human can visit it safely — but only if they prepare as if their life depends on every detail, because it does.
