Treat this place as a confined, hot, toxic, electrically active, living machine rather than a landscape. You are a soft, water-filled, oxygen-breathing, surface-evolved mammal. Nothing about you is adapted for this place. Your preparation must create a small, movable Earth-like bubble around your body, and even then you must behave like a cautious guest inside a running engine.

---

### 1. Understand what you are entering

The site is a gas-blown blister in fresh volcanic glass high on a volcano. Inside:

- **No breathable air.** The atmosphere is sulfur dioxide, carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, and volatilized lead/arsenic. One unprotected breath would be lethal.
- **No water.** It is bone-dry. Your moisture, breath, sweat, or spilled water could cause acid formation or chemical shock.
- **Heat from everywhere.** Floor plates are warm as a griddle; near the main vent molten sulfur runs at about 115°C or more. Rock away from the vent can still blister skin.
- **Sharp glass everywhere.** The walls and floor are black volcanic glass with conchoidal fractures like obsidian. A fall could shred an ordinary suit.
- **Near-total darkness.** The only steady light is a dull red glow from cracks in the floor; blue-white static sparks give brief flashes.
- **Enormous noise.** A gas vent roars like a jet engine, with a pulse every minute or two. Smaller vents whistle, buzz, and flutter. Static crackles constantly.
- **Crowded with life.** The walls, floor, and even roof are covered by tube-dwelling animals, blind arthropods, microbial films, and mineral-secreting organisms. Almost no bare rock exists.
- **Slow, mechanical time.** The world does not hurry. Floor plates shift, gas pulses, and animals extend or retract over minutes. It feels like a factory built by geology and evolution.

---

### 2. Remote reconnaissance before you go

Do not enter blind. Send a tethered robot, snake camera, or drone first.

- Map the cavity’s shape, floor stability, hot spots, and static discharge points.
- Measure gas composition, temperature, and gas pulse rhythm.
- Identify the safest entry crack and a backup exit.
- Learn the rhythm of the floor plates clacking. You want to enter when the floor is as stable as possible.
- Use a fiber-optic or hardwire telemetry link. Radio may be useless inside due to rock shielding and electrical noise.

If you cannot send a robot, at least do a prolonged external watch from the crack. The faint yellow fume and shimmer of heat are clues, but they tell you little about the interior.

---

### 3. Physical and mental preparation

You do not need to be an athlete, but you do need to tolerate:

- Heat in a bulky suit.
- Cramped, hunched posture.
- Extreme noise.
- Darkness interrupted by sudden bright sparks.
- Claustrophobia.
- Slow, alien life rhythms that may feel unsettling.

Train in a hot, dark, noisy simulator. Practice moving in your suit on sharp, uneven surfaces. Learn to read a thermal camera display. Practice emergency exit drills until they are automatic.

Mentally, prepare for the feeling that you are inside a living machine. The roar has a pulse. The floor shifts like machinery. The walls are not rock but a dense mat of living tubes and mineral growths. This is not a cave; it is an unborn world still accreting.

---

### 4. Suit and life-support requirements

A standard pressure suit or hazmat suit is not enough. You need a specialized volcanic chemosynthetic environment suit.

**Outer layer**
- Cut-resistant, heat-reflective, chemically inert to sulfur gases and acids.
- Aluminized glass-fiber or ceramic fabric works.
- Must be electrically conductive or static-dissipative to prevent charge buildup near discharge points.
- Sacrificial outer gloves, knees, and elbows because the glass will abrade everything.

**Middle insulation**
- Multi-layer aerogel or ceramic insulation to slow heat soak.
- The suit must handle sustained contact with surfaces up to around 150–200°C near the vent, plus radiant heat.

**Inner cooling garment**
- Liquid cooling loops with a heat exchanger or phase-change packs.
- Your own metabolic heat plus external heat will build up fast. This is your main limit on stay time.
- Monitor core temperature continuously. If you gain more than 1°C, begin exit.

**Boots**
- Thick, heat-rated soles, cut-resistant, with good traction on glass.
- Wide, snowshoe-like sole extensions can spread your weight and reduce crushing of the living floor crust.
- Avoid aggressive treads that would tear microbial mats.

**Helmet and visor**
- Full face, impact-resistant, with heat-reflective coating.
- Use replaceable sacrificial visor covers; sulfur droplets and crystals will etch them.
- Carry a small dry brush to clear dust without scratching.

**Breathing system**
- Closed-circuit rebreather with positive pressure. Do not filter ambient gas.
- Provide oxygen and remove CO₂. Monitor O₂, CO₂, suit pressure, and temperature.
- The suit may need to maintain sea-level internal pressure because the ambient pressure is low at high altitude.
- Minimum duration should be at least twice your planned stay. Have a small emergency oxygen bottle.

**Hydration and waste**
- Inside the suit, a bite valve and water bladder are fine. But no water may leak to the exterior.
- Sweat must be contained and wicked away by the suit. Do not use evaporative cooling that vents moisture.
- No eating inside. No spitting. No exposed skin or hair.

**Communication**
- Hardwire tether to the outside is best. Radio may fail due to rock and static.
- Use a noise-canceling microphone and bone-conduction earpiece.
- Have a backup acoustic signal, like a metal striker, in case electronics fail.

**Electronics**
- Shield against static discharge and heat.
- Use fiber optics instead of exposed wires where possible.
- Keep batteries in insulated pouches; high heat will kill them quickly.
- All tools should be non-sparking and ideally ceramic or titanium.

### 5. Entry procedure

The only entrance is a narrow crack in black glass. You may have to remove some external gear and push it through ahead of you.

- Set up a small sealed staging tent outside the crack if possible, to prevent wind from blowing dust or contaminants in.
- Use a flexible boroscope to check the crack for sharp edges before squeezing through.
- Enter slowly. The suit will scrape against glass. That is normal; do not fight it.
- Have an outside assistant guide you by tether and voice.
- Do not widen the crack. It could change gas flow, destabilize the blister, or shock the ecosystem.
- As soon as you are inside, place a small insulated pad or collapsible platform just beyond the entrance to use as a staging area.

---

### 6. Moving inside

The roof is only about an arm’s length above the floor. You will be hunched over. The floor is a mosaic of basalt plates, all warm, many coated with living rind.

- **Move extremely slowly.** Every step crushes something. Accept that, but minimize it.
- Test each foot placement with a thermal probe or walking stick.
- Avoid the glowing red cracks in the floor. They are hot, possibly unstable, and may vent gas.
- Avoid yellow molten sulfur rivulets. They are near the main vent and will melt or ignite ordinary suit materials.
- Do not touch the walls or roof. They are fragile, sharp, and covered with life.
- If you must cross a dense tube colony, use a lightweight folding bridge or overlapping pads. Do not walk directly on the tube-dwellers.
- When the gas pulse changes and the floor plates clack, stop moving. Freeze in a stable stance and wait for the shift to pass.
- Keep your center of gravity low. A fall into glass tubes could cut your suit and crush hundreds of slow-growing animals.

---

### 7. Lighting and observation

Use low-intensity red or amber light at first. The animals are blind, but bright white light may heat surfaces and disturb the local chemistry.

- The blue-white static sparks already provide brief full-room illumination. Let your eyes adjust.
- For detail, use an infrared or thermal camera rather than a bright lamp.
- If you need white light, use brief flashes. Do not leave a constant beam on one spot for long.
- Stay still for 10–15 minutes after entering. The tube-dwellers may retract when they sense vibration or heat; they will extend again if you are patient.
- The arthropod crust moves slowly away from your shadow and heat. Watch from a distance.
- The centipede predators move only when the gas pulse changes. If you see one ripple through the tubes, stay still and do not block its path.
- Do not reach toward the static discharge points where wingless moths cluster. The electrical gradients there may be strong, and the moths are fragile.

---

### 8. Contamination and ecological protocol

This place has no water, no soil, and no human microbes. You are a bag of water, oil, and bacteria. Seal yourself completely.

- No exposed skin, hair, breath, or sweat.
- No food, no water spills, no saliva.
- Decontaminate the suit exterior before entry using dry heat, UV, or a dry chemical wipe that leaves no residue.
- Do not use water-based cleaning products near the entrance.
- Do not leave any equipment, markers, or waste inside.
- If you must sample, use sterile, dry, heat-tolerant containers. Take only already-fallen sulfur crystals or dead mineral casings. Do not collect living animals or disrupt colonies.
- Remember: you are walking on a living rind. Even careful steps will kill microbes. Move as little as possible.

---

### 9. Time budget and thermal limits

The heat is the main constraint. Even with a perfect suit, heat soaks through insulation and your own body adds heat.

- Plan a first visit of 20–40 minutes maximum.
- Use thermal imaging to avoid hot spots.
- Do not sit or kneel directly on the floor. Use an insulated pad.
- If your cooling system struggles or core temperature rises, exit immediately.
- Do not wait until you feel hot. The suit’s thermal alarms should trigger exit long before subjective discomfort.
- Multiple short visits are safer than one long one.

---

### 10. Emergency procedures

- **Suit breach:** Exit immediately. The atmosphere is lethal. Do not try to patch a large tear inside. Have an outside buddy with rescue gear and a sealed stretcher.
- **Cooling failure:** Turn around and exit. Use emergency phase-change packs if available.
- **Heat illness:** Dizziness, nausea, confusion, rapid heart rate. Exit at once.
- **Static discharge:** If you feel tingling or see arcs near you, move slowly away from discharge points. Do not touch metal objects or each other.
- **Floor shift:** Freeze, widen your stance, and wait. Do not stand on cracks.
- **Falling mineral spires:** Helmet and visor are mandatory. If you bump the roof, sharp crystals may fall.
- **Fire or ignition:** Hydrogen sulfide is flammable, but oxygen is low. Still, do not create sparks. Use non-sparking tools.

---

### 11. Exit and decontamination

Exit slowly, feet first if possible, guided by your tether.

- Outside, do not open the suit in the open air. Enter a sealed decontamination tent.
- Use dry brushing and vacuum to remove sulfur dust, spores, and mineral crystals from the suit exterior.
- Then use a dry chemical decon or heat to neutralize any acids or reactive residues.
- Inspect the suit for cuts, cracks, or acid damage. Pay special attention to gloves, boots, and visor seals.
- Remove the suit in a clean area. Get a medical check for heat stress, dehydration, and heavy metal exposure if any breach occurred.
- Do not use water for cleaning near the entry crack. The environment is dry by nature; introducing water could damage both the equipmentsuit and the site.

### 12. What it will feel like

You squeeze through the crack, and the first thing that hits you is heat—dry, pressing, everywhere. The roar is physical, not just sound. Your light reveals a wall of black glass and a dense crowd of tubes, their crimson and white crowns waving in gas currents. The floor is warm through your boots. A blue-white spark snaps, and for an instant the whole chamber appears: mineral spires, sulfur rivulets, millions of tiny bodies packed edge to edge. Then darkness again.

You take a step. The floor clacks. You freeze. The gas pitch drops, a centipede predator ripples through the tubes, and then the roar returns. You wait. The tube-dwellers extend their crowns over minutes, not seconds. The arthropods move with the patience of a glacier. You feel the heat seeping through your suit, your own breathing loud inside the helmet, the cold thin air of the outside world impossibly far away.

After twenty minutes, your thermal alarm chimes. You turn carefully, leaving no trace except maybe a few crushed microbial films. You exit into thin, cold air. The outside world is bright and silent by comparison. You have been inside a place that is still being born—a small, dark, roaring, hot, dry, crowded, unborn world hidden in a crack in black glass.
