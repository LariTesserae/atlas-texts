# Field Manual: Preparing for the Singing Pan (The Cistern)

Before you go, understand this: the Singing Pan is not a landscape you walk into. It is a small, hot, deafening, half-finished organ pipe of a world. It is a place that is still being made, and it does not care about your eardrums, your lungs, or your sense of distance. As an ordinary human multiverse traveler, your job is not to conquer it. Your job is to borrow a narrow edge of it for a short time and come back with your body and mind intact.

The following is not a guarantee of safety. It is a preparation.

---

## 1. Know What You Are Walking Into

Imagine standing on a broken black knife-edge of basalt. On one side, a cloud sea drops away into nothing. On the other, warm green-brown water steams and heaves. The light is dim, like permanent late dusk. The air is thin, so every breath is shallow, but it is also hot and heavy with sulfur and silica. The sound hits you before you can name it: a low organ note that sits in your chest, a high shriek of steam, and a rhythmic concussive thud like a door being slammed deep underwater. Your teeth buzz. Your skull vibrates. You cannot hear your own voice.

Now imagine the far rim seems close enough to touch. You take a step, and suddenly it is two hundred paces away. The water surface is mounded, domed upward in the center, as if something below is pushing it. Droplets run uphill along the rock. The reflection in the water is wrong—darker sky, unknown clouds, or no sky at all, only a slow spiral of green light. Somewhere below the surface, white chimneys grow and collapse. The whole basin is arguing with itself.

That is the Singing Pan.

## 2. Physical and Medical Preparation

### Altitude and Heat

The altitude is high enough to hurt you. The air is thin, even though it is warm. An ordinary human will fatigue quickly, become short of breath, and may develop altitude sickness. Prepare as if for a high-altitude geothermal field:

- **Acclimatize** at similar altitude for several days before arrival, if possible.
- Train for exertion in hot, humid, low-oxygen environments.
- Carry supplemental oxygen and know how to use it. A small portable oxygen bottle with a nasal cannula or mask that can be worn under your respirator is ideal.
- Monitor your blood oxygen saturation. If it drops below your personal safe threshold, retreat and recover.

### Heat and Burns

The heat comes up through the stone and the water. The rock is warm, even hot in places. The water is scalding and chemically aggressive. You will need:

- Heat-resistant clothing, including boots with high-temperature soles.
- Gloves that can handle hot, sharp basalt.
- Severe burn first aid supplies, including sterile dressings, burn gel, and a plan for evacuation.

### Sound Exposure

This is not ordinary noise. The sound is the main occupant of the place. It is a continuous physical force. Prolonged exposure can cause permanent hearing damage, chest discomfort, sinus pain, and disorientation.

- Use double hearing protection: custom-molded earplugs plus high-attenuation earmuffs.
- Even with that, low-frequency thud may be conducted through your body. You cannot fully block it. You must limit your time in the basin.
- Carry an acoustic dosimeter with a visible alarm. Set conservative exposure limits. If you start to feel the thud in your chest more strongly than before, or if your skull begins to ache, leave.
- Consider a mouthguard to reduce teeth-rattling, but do not let it impede breathing or communication.

### Respiratory Hazards

The air is full of mineral haze, steam, sulfur compounds, and possibly hydrogen sulfide, sulfur dioxide, and carbon dioxide. The water is clouded with silica and sulfur. You must not breathe this air unfiltered.

- Wear a full-face respirator with acid-gas and particulate cartridges, or better, a powered air-purifying respirator.
- If oxygen is low, wear a positive-pressure supplied-air system or use supplemental oxygen under the mask.
- Carry a multi-gas monitor for O2, H2S, SO2, CO2, and combustibles. Do not rely on smell; hydrogen sulfide can deaden your sense of smell and then kill you.
- If gas alarms sound or you feel burning in your lungs, switch to your emergency air supply and leave immediately.

### Psychological Readiness

The Singing Pan is sensory overload and sensory deception at the same time. It is loud, dim, hot, and visually unreliable. You may feel as if the place is alive, or as if you are losing your sense of self. You may feel a strange pull toward the water or the ruins. Know this before you go.

- Train in sensory deprivation and overload environments, if possible.
- Practice grounding techniques that work for you: focusing on the feel of a rope in your hand, counting breaths, repeating a phrase.
- Accept that your eyes will lie to you. Accept that the water will lie to you. Trust your physical anchors and your team.
- If you begin to feel detached from your body, dizzy, or unusually compelled to stare into the water or the reflection, stop. Look away. Anchor yourself physically.

## 3. Equipment List

This is not a hiking trip. You are entering a small, active geothermal vent field with unstable rock, scalding water, toxic gases, and noise that can damage you. Pack accordingly.

### Life Support and Air

- Full-face respirator with acid-gas/P100 cartridges, or equivalent
- Spare cartridges in sealed bags
- Portable oxygen cylinder with regulator and mask/nasal cannula
- Multi-gas monitor (O2, H2S, SO2, CO2, LEL)
- Emergency escape air bottle
- N95 or P100 backup mask for lower-risk moments, if needed

### Clothing and Body Protection

- Full-body harness, rated for fall arrest and rescue
- Heat-resistant outer suit, preferably aluminized or made of Nomex/Kevlar, with sealed seams
- Moisture-wicking base layer and insulating midlayer, because even though it is warm, you may sweat heavily and then chill if you stop moving
- High-temperature boots with steel toe, metatarsal guard, and excellent grip on wet, sharp rock
- Cut-resistant gaiters and shin guards
- Heavy-duty heat-resistant gloves, plus thin inner gloves for dexterity
- Knee pads and elbow pads for crawling on sharp basalt
- Helmet with chin strap, integrated face shield, and earmuffs
- Fire-resistant hood or balaclava
- Eye protection: sealed goggles or full-face shield to keep sulfur and silica out of your eyes

### Hearing and Vibration Protection

- Electronic earmuffs with high noise reduction rating (NRR 30+ dB) and low-frequency attenuation
- Custom-molded silicone earplugs as backup
- Acoustic dosimeter with alarm
- Vibration-damping insoles and gloves
- Mouthguard, optional but recommended

### Navigation and Fall Protection

- Full-body harness
- Multiple locking carabiners, slings, and prusik cords
- Dynamic rope for fall arrest; static rope for fixed lines
- Rope protectors for sharp edges
- Anchor slings of various sizes, heat-resistant
- Ascender, descender, and pulley
- High-temp flagging tape or removable markers
- A marked physical line of known length, e.g., a 50-meter Kevlar cord with tape marks every meter
- Headlamp and handheld light with amber/fog lenses
- Spare batteries
- Compass, inclinometer, and GPS as advisory tools only; do not trust them fully
- Portable laser rangefinder, but treat its readings as approximate

### Communication

Because the sound is too loud for voice, you need non-audio methods:

- Throat microphone and bone-conduction earpiece for radio, but expect interference
- Text-based communication device, e.g., ruggedized tablet or pre-arranged written cards
- High-intensity strobe lights for visual signaling
- Pre-arranged hand and rope-tug signals
- Whistles are useless; do not rely on them
- Satellite messenger or portal recall beacon

### Water and Food

- Bring all your own drinking water, at least 4–6 liters for a short visit, in insulated containers
- Electrolyte tablets or drink mix
- High-energy, easy-to-eat food that does not require cooking
- Do not drink, touch, or wash with the local water
- If water contact occurs, flush with your own clean water, not the local water

### Shelter and Emergency

- Emergency bivvy sack or heat-reflective blanket
- Sitting pad, because the rock is hot, sharp, and hard
- Small heat-reflective tarp if shelter is needed, but do not plan to sleep inside the basin
- First aid kit with burn dressings, eye wash, altitude medication, painkillers, anti-nausea, respiratory support, splints, and tourniquets
- Personal locator beacon or multiversal recall beacon
- Chemical light sticks
- Spare batteries and power bank
- Emergency repair tape, wire, and zip ties
- Spare gloves and socks
- A small notebook and waterproof pencil for notes

### Observation and Documentation

- Camera with high-temperature, waterproof housing
- Thermal imaging camera if available, useful in steam and poor visibility
- Sound recorder with high SPL microphones
- Waterproof field notebook
- Sample containers only if you have permission; use long-handled tools and assume the water and rock are contaminated

### What Not to Bring

- Open flames, lighters, flares, or anything that could ignite sulfur gases
- Glass equipment that can shatter under vibration
- Loose items that can fall into the water or from the rim
- Pets, drones without shielding, or anything you cannot afford to lose
- Heavy gear that will slow your escape

## 4. Arrival Protocol

When you first step through your portal or arrive at the rim, do not assume you are safe. The place may have shifted since your scouting. The rim may be narrower than expected. The water may be higher. The sound may be louder.

Follow this sequence:

1. **Anchor immediately.** Before you look around, clip into a pre-placed anchor or set a sling around a large, stable rock. Use multiple anchors if possible. The rock is fresh, sharp, and may be brittle; test anchors slowly.
2. **Assess the air.** Use your gas monitor before removing any part of your respirator. If the air is unsafe, keep your mask on and consider retreat.
3. **Check your bearings.** Do not trust the far rim or the water surface. Use your physical line and markers to establish a local baseline.
4. **Note the water level.** Use a fixed mark on the rock or a removable staff. Remember that the water level may rise or fall without cause. Set a high-water limit and do not camp or linger below it.
5. **Establish a fixed line.** Run a static rope or marked line along the section of rim you will traverse. This is your lifeline. Clip into it with two energy-absorbing lanyards.
6. **Brief your team.** Confirm hand signals, rope-tug codes, and emergency procedures. Do not assume everyone can hear.
7. **Set a time limit.** The basin is not a place to linger. Short, planned sorties are safer than prolonged exposure.

## 5. On-Site Safety Rules

The place has three rules, and they are not suggestions:

### Rule 1: Do Not Trust the Water

The water is not water. It is a hot, silica-and-sulfur suspension, likely acidic or alkaline, and it is sometimes superheated. It moves against the slope. It mounds in the center. It reflects a sky that is not yours.

- Do not touch the water.
- Do not drink the water.
- Do not use the water to wash anything.
- Do not enter the water, even if it looks shallow.
- Do not follow the stone steps into the water, no matter how deliberate they seem.
- If you see droplets running uphill or climbing a vertical stone, do not touch them. They may be boiling, chemically corrosive, or moving through a local anomaly. Avoid them.
- If the water level rises, move uphill immediately. Do not try to wade or swim.
- If you must sample the water, use a long-handled tool and a sealed container, and only if you have permission. Assume the sample is hazardous.

### Rule 2: Do Not Trust Distance

The basin is still arguing with itself. The far rim may seem thirty paces away, then two hundred. Your eyes will lie. Your sense of scale will break.

- Never judge distance by sight. Use a marked physical line, a measuring tape, or a known rope length.
- Do not throw anything to the far rim. It may be farther or closer than you think.
- Do not jump over any gap, even if it looks small.
- If you are using a laser rangefinder, treat the reading as advisory. The only reliable measurement is a physical line.
- If you feel that the distance is wrong, stop. Anchor yourself. Wait. The perception may shift again.
- Use fixed physical markers along the rim. Do not rely on the shape of the water or the position of the sun.

### Rule 3: Treat the Sound as Terrain

The sound is not a background. It is a force. It shapes the water, vibrates the rock, and can damage your body.

- The sound can change pitch suddenly when a vent opens, a rock falls, or a chimney collapses. If you feel a sudden pressure shift, stop and look for steam or water movement.
- Do not stand directly in front of a known steam jet. The high shriek may precede a blast.
- If your teeth buzz more than usual, or if your chest begins to hurt, reduce your exposure.
- Use your acoustic dosimeter. Do not exceed your planned time.
- If the sound makes you feel nauseated, dizzy, or disoriented, leave. You are not adapting; you are being harmed.
- Remember that the water surface is never flat. The low dome in the center, the rings of boiling rosettes, and the waves against the wind are all symptoms of the sound and the heat. Do not attempt to boat or swim in it.

## 6. How to Move on the Rim

The rim is the only standing ground. It is a collar of freshly fractured basalt, sharp as new flint, no wider than a road in places. One side falls into cloud; the other falls into hot water.

- Move slowly and deliberately.
- Stay clipped into the fixed line at all times.
- Test every foothold before putting weight on it. The rock is new and may be loose.
- Avoid stepping on cracks or edges that look like they may cleave off.
- If a slab cracks off and slides into the water, the whole basin changes its note. Move away from the edge and re-anchor.
- Do not scramble or jump. The rock is knife-edged and will cut through clothing and skin.
- Crawl if necessary. It is safer to be low and spread your weight.
- Use a second person as a spotter and belay when near the waterline or the cloud edge.

## 7. Approaching the Ruins

There are traces of someone before you: nine low cairns on the eastern rim, a collapsed shelter of flat basalt slabs, a flat stone with shallow grooves of chevrons and circles, and six or seven deliberate steps descending into the water.

Treat these as sacred, or at least as a scientific site. They are old and fragile, and they may be the only record of another traveler’s time here.

- Do not move the cairns, even if they seem tumbled. Do not add stones to them.
- Do not enter the collapsed shelter unless absolutely necessary. Its roof may be unstable.
- Do not touch the grooved stone. Do not make rubbings or casts. Photograph it obliquely if you can, but do not use flash or pressure.
- Do not follow the steps into the water. They may mark a route that was once dry, but the water is unpredictable now.
- Do not leave any marks of your own. No carving, no stacked stones, no flagging left behind.
- If you must leave a temporary marker, use removable high-temperature tape or a small LED beacon, and remove it when you leave.
- The absence of metal, glass, cord, and wood at the ruin suggests that the place does not preserve organic materials or that the previous traveler took everything with them. Do whatever you can to pack out everything you brought.

## 8. Emergency Procedures

### Rockfall or Rim Collapse

- Stay clipped into your line.
- Move away from the edge if possible, but do not run blindly. The rock is sharp and unstable.
- If you fall, use your self-rescue gear: prusik, ascender, or pulley to climb back up. Do not thrash; the rock will cut you.
- If a teammate falls, lock off the line and call for help using non-verbal signals.

### Water Level Rise

- The water may rise without cause and fill to the rim.
- If you notice the water level rising, move to higher ground immediately.
- Do not try to cross rising water. Do not enter the water.
- If you are trapped by water, climb to the highest stable point, anchor yourself, and call for evacuation.

### Steam Jet Eruption

- If you hear a sudden high shriek or see a jet of steam, move away from the source immediately.
- Cover any exposed skin. Turn your back to the steam.
- If burned, cool the burn with your own clean water, not the local water. Cover with sterile dressing and evacuate.

### Gas Alarm

- If your gas monitor alarms, do not remove your mask.
- Switch to supplied air or leave the area immediately.
- Move to higher ground if possible; gases can pool in low spots.
- If a teammate collapses, do not approach without your own mask. Perform a rescue only if you can do so safely.

### Acoustic Injury

- If you feel sudden ear pain, dizziness, nausea, or chest discomfort, leave the basin immediately.
- Do not try to “push through” the sound. It can cause permanent damage.
- After leaving, seek medical evaluation for acoustic trauma and barotrauma.

### Disorientation or Anomalous Perception

- If the distance seems wrong, or if you see water moving uphill, or if the reflection shows a spiral of green light, stop.
- Anchor yourself to a fixed line.
- Look away from the water. Focus on your hands, your rope, or a nearby rock.
- If you feel a compulsion to approach the water or the edge, call for your teammate. Do not trust your own judgment until the feeling passes.
- If the feeling does not pass, retreat along your line to safety.

### Lost or Separated from Team

- Because voice is useless, rely on your fixed lines and pre-arranged signals.
- If you become lost, stop moving. Anchor yourself. Use your strobe light and radio. Do not wander.
- The distance anomalies can make it extremely difficult to find someone visually. Use physical tethers and marked lines at all times to prevent separation.

## 9. Psychological Preparation and Aftercare

Visiting the Singing Pan may leave marks on you. The sound, the heat, the wrong reflection, the sense of being inside something unfinished—these can linger.

After you leave, you may notice:

- Ringing in your ears or a persistent low thud
- A feeling of being off-balance
- Dreams of green water or spirals
- An urge to count or make marks
- A strange grief or awe

This is normal after exposure to such a place. Talk to someone. Get medical attention for your ears, lungs, and any burns or cuts. Wash your gear thoroughly; the silica and sulfur will corrode it. Do not go back until you have fully recovered and debriefed.

If the place feels like it is calling you back, be very careful. It is not a call. It is a sound.

---

## 10. Final Words

The Singing Pan is a small room, but it contains an ocean’s violence in a cup. It is a high drowned throat, a warm, dim, deafening engine of creation and erasure, with just enough life to notice and just enough ruin to show that someone else once stood at the rim and listened.

You are not that someone. You are an ordinary human. Prepare as if your lungs, your eardrums, your skin, and your mind are all at risk—because they are.

Bring ropes. Bring oxygen. Bring silence and humility. Do not trust the water. Do not trust distance. Do not trust the sound. Trust your anchors, your team, and your exit.

And when you stand on the rim, listening to the hammer that is not a song, remember: the place is not finished. Do not try to finish it. Do not leave anything behind. Just look, record, and leave.

That is how an ordinary human survives the Singing Pan.
