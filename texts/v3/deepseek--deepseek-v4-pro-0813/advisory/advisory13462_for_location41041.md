# Preparation for Visiting the Underworks

The Underworks is not a dive site in any normal sense. It is an industrial floor laid beneath ten or fifteen metres of cold, grey sea. The water is shallow enough for daylight to reach, loud enough to feel in your chest, and busy enough that forgetting the rules can get you killed. You are not going to a reef or a wreck. You are going into a working machine that happens to be underwater.

An ordinary human multiverse traveler should prepare as if for cold-water industrial diving, with extra attention to entanglement, pressure differentials, moving machinery, disorientation, and the psychological weight of standing—or floating—over an endless man-made floor. The following is a practical guide built around the specific conditions described.

---

## 1. Before You Go: Know What You Are Entering

### The basic environment

- Depth: 10–15 metres. That is shallow by diving standards, but still two to two and a half atmospheres of pressure. Nitrogen absorption is real over long exposures.
- Light: there is daylight. The surface overhead is a bright, shifting ceiling. The water is green-grey, not abyssal. But visibility can change in minutes when a sediment cloud rolls through.
- Temperature: likely cold. The text does not give exact temperature, but the presence of herring, cod, dogfish, seals, and the feeling of industrial cold water strongly suggests temperate or cold water. Prepare for cold, with some warm plumes that you should not trust.
- Sound: constant and invasive. Pumps throb, valves hiss, tools bang. The noise arrives through your bones. You will feel it before you hear it clearly.
- Ground: there is no natural ground. The deck is poured concrete, steel grating, rubber matting, bolted plate. Stencilled numbers mark its seams. The floor may be lifted, opened, or moved. It is not terrain; it is infrastructure.

### The rules as written

The Underworks has three explicit rules:

1. **The ground is not ground. It is a floor and it may be lifted.**
2. **The water is not wild sea. It is working fluid, pumped and filtered.**
3. **The lights are instructions—red closed, white open, blue safe.**
4. **The animals are not intruders. They are part of the system.**

Memorise these. Treat them as law. They are not decorative. They describe exactly how the place will behave when you are inside it.

### The operational reality

The Underworks is active. Locks fill and drain. A rail-mounted crane swings pipe bundles. A maintenance tram crawls along the deck. Sediment traps open and suck up grey drifts. Currents change with pump schedules. Warm outflow rises, cold water slides beneath, and the meeting point makes the water go streaky. The place has its own internal weather.

That means you cannot treat it as a static dive site. You must treat it as a live industrial environment.

## 2. Physical and Mental Preparation

### Diving certification and experience

Do not attempt this if you are not already a certified cold-water drysuit diver. Ideally you should also have:

- Low-visibility / limited-visibility diving experience.
- Current diving experience.
- Some familiarity with industrial or overhead environments, even if the Underworks is not a cave or wreck overhead environment in the traditional sense.
- Comfort using a full-face mask and voice or line communications.

If you are an “ordinary human” without these qualifications, your preparation begins with getting them in safer water. There is no shame in that. The Underworks will still be there.

### Cold tolerance and stamina

The water is cold, the distances between structures are large, and the constant noise is draining. Build cold tolerance through repeated exposure. Improve cardiovascular fitness. You may need to swim hundreds of metres between features. Fin efficiently. Conserve gas.

### Psychological preparation

The Underworks is empty the way a warehouse is empty when the lights are on and the machines are far apart. Most of any given view is water. There are no natural landmarks, no hills, no rocks. Only deck, steel, grating, pipes, and yellow lamps.

This place can trigger:

- Agoraphobia from the vast openness.
- Vertigo from the unbroken floor stretching beyond sight.
- Claustrophobia inside pipe shadows or near large structures.
- Sensory overwhelm from the constant mechanical noise.
- A strange loneliness, because it is not wild and not human-scaled.

Prepare for that. Practice diving in similar industrial or artificial settings. Practice maintaining calm when disoriented. Have a buddy. Use a line. Know your exit at all times.

### Medical

- Get a dive medical. Ensure ears, sinuses, lungs, and heart are fit for cold water and pressure.
- Carry seasickness medication for the surface boat ride. The surface is described as an endless grey, wind-chopped sea, so the boat ride may be rough.
- Carry ear drops for after-dive irritation. Water in an industrial setting may be contaminated with fuel, rust, organic waste, and fine sediment.
- Carry antihistamines or sting relief for accidental jelly contact.

## 3. Equipment List

### Exposure protection

- **Dry suit** with thick undergarments. Assume cold water throughout, even if you pass through warm plumes.
- **Dry gloves** or thick wetsuit gloves. Barnacles, mussels, rust, and sharp metal edges will be everywhere.
- **Hood** covering ears, but still allowing equalisation. The noise is loud; a hood will muffle it slightly.
- **Rock boots or sturdy dive boots** if you will stand on deck. Much of the deck is grating, which is hard and may shift.
- **Fins** suitable for frog-kick and precision manoeuvring around machinery. Avoid long freediving fins; they catch on pipes.

### Buoyancy and gas

- **Wing-style BCD or twin-set with redundant buoyancy.** The deck may be sharp and the environment industrial. Redundancy is important.
- **Twin cylinders or a rebreather** if you plan a longer dive. Open-circuit single tank may be acceptable for short visits, but distances between structures are large and currents can increase gas consumption.
- **Stage cylinder** for longer exploration or as emergency reserve.
- **Dive computer** set to conservative settings. At 10–15 m, no-decompression limits are generous, but repeated dives and cold water shorten them.
- **Depth gauge and timing device** as backup.

### Breathing and communication

- **Full-face mask** with through-water communication is strongly recommended. It protects your face from sediment and allows you to talk to your buddy.
- If using a standard mask and regulator, have a **backup mask** and **backup second stage**.
- **Wet notes and slate** for written communication.
- Hand signals familiar to both divers.

### Navigation

- **Primary dive light** and **backup light**. Even in daylight, shadows under structures and inside pipe gantries are dark.
- **Line reel and spool** for running a guideline if you leave the immediate descent line. Be careful: line can foul moving machinery.
- **Compass**—but be aware that steel structures may affect magnetic compasses. Do not rely on it alone.
- **Waterproof map or slate** showing deck numbers, structures, and known hazards. The stencilled numbers on deck seams are your best navigation aids.
- **Surface marker buoy (SMB)** and **reel** for ascent and marking your position.
- **Strobe light** for location in low visibility.
- **Underwater acoustic locator or sonar beacon** if available in your multiverse tech. Not essential but helpful in a vast artificial landscape.

### Safety and cutting tools

- **Line cutter** and / or **shears** on your harness, accessible by both hands. Entanglement is a real risk around cables, nets, and industrial debris.
- **Dive knife** with blunt tip for prying, not just cutting.
- **Bolt snaps and double-enders** for clipping off gear.
- **Jon line** or tie-off line for securing yourself while resting.
- **Whistle or noisemaker** for surface use.

### Protection and comfort

- **Gloves** with reinforced palms.
- **Knee pads** if crawling on grating.
- **Small first aid kit** for cuts, stings, and abrasions, carried on the boat.
- **Spare mask strap, fin strap, and drysuit repair kit** on the surface.

### For the surface support

- **Support boat with GPS, radio, and dive flag.**
- **Shot line / descent line** with weighted bottom. Use this to descend and ascend, especially in current.
- **Oxygen kit** and first aid.
- **Warm drinks, blankets, and dry clothes** for after the dive.
- **Tool kit** for gear repair.
- **Weather monitoring.** Surface storms pass as pressure changes; the sea is wind-chopped and endless.

## 4. Entry and Descent

The surface is only a few black stacks and yellow buoys in a grey, wind-chopped sea. There is no shore, no natural shelter. Your support boat is your only base.

- Do not moor to the black stacks or yellow buoys unless explicitly authorised. They are active structures, not moorings.
- Use your own shot line or descent line. Lower a weighted line to the deck.
- Descend along the line. Do not free-descend, because currents can push you away from your intended spot.
- Equalise early and often. Stop at 5 m to check gear, buoyancy, and communication.
- Watch for the thermocline where warm outflow meets cold water. The temperature change can be sudden and the water turns streaky.

At ten to fifteen metres, the light will still be green-grey. The surface overhead will look like a bright, shifting ceiling. The deck below will come into view not as a seafloor but as a floor—poured concrete panels, steel grating, stencilled numbers. That first sight is the moment you understand: there is no ground here. Only deck.

---

## 5. Moving Around the Deck

- Stay neutrally buoyant and slightly above the deck. Do not walk unless you must. Much of the deck is grating, and some panels may be lifted by machinery.
- Swim with a frog kick to avoid stirring up sediment. The sediment is not sand; it is concrete dust, rust, crushed shell, and industrial fines. It will turn the water milky and irritate your eyes and skin.
- Use the stencilled numbers on deck seams as waypoints. They are your street signs.
- Follow blue lights. The rules say blue means safe. Red means closed—do not enter. White means open—treat as active or hazardous. Do not second-guess the lights.
- Keep clear of rail lines. The maintenance tram crawls along the deck without warning.
- Watch for the rail-mounted crane. A swinging pipe bundle is heavy and will not stop for you.
- Avoid swimming over hatches, vents, gratings, sluice mouths, or sediment traps. They may open or create suction.
- If a sediment cloud rolls across the deck like fog, stop. Hold position. Wait for it to pass or move slowly out of it. Do not push on blindly.
- Use a guideline if visibility drops below a few metres. But keep the line off the deck and away from moving parts.

### Internal weather

The Underworks has its own currents. Warm outflow rises in slow columns. Colder water slides beneath. A front of clearer water may move through. A cloud of fine industrial sediment may roll across the deck. Bubbles rise in curtains from vents. The current will tell you which valves are open.

- Before moving to a new area, pause and read the water. Look for streaks, bubbles, sediment drift, and moving fish.
- Fish are excellent current indicators. If they are all facing one direction or clustered around a plume, note it.
- Do not fight a current. Swim across it or wait for the cycle to change. Pump schedules may shift the flow every few minutes.
- Be prepared for the silence when a pump cycles off. The text says the silence feels like pressure for a moment, and then another pump starts. It can be disorienting. Stop, breathe, reorient.

## 6. Around the Machinery

The structures rise from the deck: pump houses, transformer platforms, sluice gates, heat exchangers, lock chambers, pipe gantries, elevator shafts. They are industrial—riveted steel, I-beams, heavy valve wheels, cable trays, zinc anodes, thick glass portholes, yellow lamps.

- Do not touch valve wheels, sluice handles, cable trays, or electrical panels unless you are an authorised maintenance worker. The Underworks is active. A valve may be about to cycle.
- Do not enter pipe mouths, lock chambers, or elevator shafts. Water movement inside them can be much stronger than outside. A lock filling or draining can create a current that traps you against a wall or grille.
- Beware of pressure differentials—delta P. Any opening with water moving through it can suck you in. The rule of thumb is to stay far away from any intake, outfall, sluice, or drain. If you see water moving into an opening, treat it as a hazard, not a curiosity.
- Keep your hands to yourself around barnacle-encrusted columns. The barnacles form rough collars. Mussels line the upper levels in dark bands. Sea stars lie on the concrete. Anemones open on valve wheels. These are living organisms, but they also hide sharp metal edges and pinch points.
- If you must hold onto something, choose a fixed, non-moving structural member, not a valve wheel, cable, or pipe that may move.
- Pay attention to sound. A pump clank may repeat. A valve hisses. A repair tool bangs far away. If the sound pattern changes suddenly, reassess your location.

### The lights

The rules say: **red closed, white open, blue safe.**

Interpret this strictly:

- **Red light**: the system is closed, or you are not permitted. Do not go through that doorway, hatch, sluice, or passage. Treat it as a wall.
- **White light**: the system is open. That may mean an active flow, an open hatch, or a moving part. Do not assume it is safe. Approach only with caution and if your purpose requires it.
- **Blue light**: safe. Blue lights mark safe routes, safe areas, or safe holds. Use them as navigation and rest points.

If you are colour-blind or using a monochrome vision system, learn the positions and flash patterns. The lights are instructions, not suggestions.

## 7. Animals and Ecology

The animals are abundant, but they are not intruders. They are part of the system.

- **Fish**: schools of herring, smelt, young cod move tightly around structures. They scatter when a striped bass or dogfish passes. Do not chase them. A predator may be behind the school.
- **Crabs and lobsters**: live in pipe mouths. Keep your fingers out of pipe mouths. Their pinch is strong.
- **Barnacles and mussels**: sharp. Do not brush against them.
- **Sea stars and anemones**: avoid touching. Anemones sting mildly, but repeated contact is unpleasant.
- **Moon jellies**: drift through the water like pale paper. Their sting is usually mild, but avoid contact with face and eyes.
- **Seals**: pass as long shadows. They are curious but large. Keep your distance. Do not block their route.
- **Cormorants**: dive from the surface with trails of bubbles. They may startle you. Give them space.

The ecology is ordinary, but busy. The animals have grown into the noise and the machinery. They will not treat you as an intruder either, unless you touch, chase, or block them.

## 8. Water Quality and Exposure

The water is not wild sea. It is working fluid. That means:

- **Do not drink it.** It may contain fine organic waste, rust, industrial fines, or other by-products of the machinery.
- **Avoid swimming directly into warm outfall plumes.** They may contain bacteria, low oxygen, or suspended material. The short brown kelp sways there, but that does not mean the water is clean.
- **Do not disturb sediment.** The grey dust will cling to your gear and can irritate your eyes, skin, and airways.
- **Wear a dry suit.** Minimise skin exposure.
- **Rinse all gear and yourself after the dive.** The Underworks is perpetually renewed, but that renewal is industrial, not sanitary.

Surface storms pass as pressure changes overhead. The internal weather may shift. If a storm builds, the surface boat may be rough. Plan your exit well before weather deteriorates.

## 9. Emergency Procedures

Have written and practised emergency protocols before entering.

### Loss of gas

- Signal your buddy. Share air using your redundant second stage.
- Ascend along the descent line if possible. At 10–15 m, a controlled emergency swimming ascent is possible but not ideal. Use your own judgement and training.
- Deploy SMB if you must ascend away from the line.

### Entanglement

- Stop. Do not roll or thrash. Use your line cutter to cut yourself free.
- If you cannot reach the entanglement, your buddy can cut it.
- Keep your cutting tool sharp and accessible by either hand.

### Entrapment or strong current

- Swim perpendicular to the current, not against it.
- Grab a fixed structural member if you need to stop. Do not grab moving parts.
- If caught near an intake or sluice, get away immediately by any means. Do not wait to see if the current will ease.

### Lost buddy

- Search for one minute, then surface as per your training.
- Use your light and SMB to signal.

### Lost line / disorientation

- Stop. Look around for blue lights. The blue safe lights are your best reorientation cue.
- If you have a map, identify the nearest stencilled deck number.
- If still lost, ascend slowly to the surface. At 10–15 m, you can surface safely if you have enough gas and no ceiling. The surface is a bright, shifting ceiling, but it is the exit.

### Injury

- On the surface, treat cuts, stings, hypothermia, and ear problems.
- Administer oxygen if available.
- Seek medical care for any sign of infection after exposure to industrial water.

---

## 10. Exiting

- Begin your ascent with plenty of gas. A 3–5 minute safety stop at 5 m is prudent, even if not required.
- Ascend along your descent line. Watch for surface boat traffic, gulls, and the black stacks or yellow buoys.
- The surface may be rough. Be ready for wind chop. If a storm has built, exit calmly but without delay.
- Once aboard, remove gear carefully. Check yourself for cuts, stings, and attached sea life.
- Rinse everything. Log the dive. Note any changes in machinery, currents, or animals.

## 11. The Deeper Preparation: Mentality

The hardest part of the Underworks is not the cold, the noise, or the machinery. It is the mental shift.

Visitors often expect a natural seafloor. Instead, they find a made landscape: poured concrete, steel grating, stencilled numbers, rail tracks, valve wheels, yellow lamps. The place is vast and empty. The ground is a floor. The water is a working fluid. The animals are part of the system.

You are not entering wilderness. You are entering a warehouse the size of a continent, running under ten metres of green-grey sea. It is not hostile, but it is indifferent. It will not notice you unless you get in the way of a crane, a sluice, or a current.

So prepare for that. Bring humility. Bring patience. Bring a good line cutter and a reliable buddy. Obey the lights. Watch the water. Trust the stencilled numbers. The Underworks will let you visit, if you move like a careful worker and not like a tourist.
