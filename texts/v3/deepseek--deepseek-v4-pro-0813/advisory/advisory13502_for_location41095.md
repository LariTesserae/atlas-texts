# Preparation for visiting the Sort / Upcast / Ledger

You are not preparing for a wilderness. You are preparing for a cool, damp, vertical machine-city where the ground is not ground, the sun is not sun, and distance is not always trustworthy. The place is more benign than hostile, but it is indifferent. It will not catch you if you fall. It will not wake you when the light fails to become night. It will not correct your map.

An ordinary human multiverse traveler can visit this place and even move through it safely, but only if you treat it less like a hike and more like entering a clean industrial habitat with mild spatial asthma. The main risks are not monsters or storms. The main risks are:

- slippery metal and long falls,
- thin or poor air,
- disorientation from constant light and swallowed sound,
- spatial loops and unreliable navigation,
- cold damp, foot rot, and fatigue,
- the slow psychological erasure of having no natural ground beneath you at any time.

Below is a practical preparation guide.

---

## 1. Before you go: physical and mental preparation

### Altitude and air
The air is thin, but not because of open sky. It feels like high altitude because of ventilation shafts, exhaust pull, and the sheer built height of the plateau. You may not be able to rely on ordinary mountain acclimatization, but the same symptoms can occur:

- headache,
- nausea,
- dizziness,
- shortness of breath on stairs,
- poor sleep,
- vague confusion.

Prepare as if you were going to 3,000–4,000 meters, even if the local pressure does not match that model. Spend a few days before departure doing brisk stair training with a loaded pack. If you have any history of altitude sickness, bring acetazolamide and a small pulse oximeter.

Once there, climb slowly. The city is vertical, and every route is full of switchbacks, gantries, and staircases. Do not judge yourself by ordinary walking pace. Do not run.

### Stairs, balance, and load
You will walk on wet metal, grid decking, smooth concrete, and narrow bridges. Train with a loaded pack on uneven stairs. Practice stepping onto wet surfaces without jerking your weight forward. Practice using railings without grabbing them too hard, because the railings may have burrs or condensation.

If you are afraid of heights, do not assume you will “get used to it.” The city opens downward and sideways into more city. Some balconies nearly touch across alleys, but others drop through stacked walkways into a glowing haze. A person prone to vertigo should stay on lower, enclosed routes, use guides, and avoid leaning too far over any edge.

### Mental preparation for the light and quiet
The light is huge, even, and almost shadowless. The dimming is so slow that “night” may arrive without you noticing. The quiet is not peaceful in the ordinary way; it is absorbed, as if the city has been tuned to swallow noise. Voices do not carry. Machines hum below hearing. A dropped bolt lands with a small private tick.

This combination can be deeply disorienting. You may feel like you are in an anechoic chamber, or inside a clean machine that is holding its breath. You may hear your own pulse more than the city. You may begin to whisper even when alone.

Before you go, practice being alone in silence without constantly filling it. If you are traveling with others, agree on hand signals and soft check-ins. Do not plan to shout across a gantry. Do not plan to hear someone coming around a corner.

### Accepting the spatial weirdness
The city has ambient anomalies. They are usually not violent. A shadow may lag. A reflection may show the same street with lanterns in a different order. A corridor may repeat itself exactly but for a missing rivet. A staircase may descend for half a kilometer and return you to the same landing.

Prepare yourself mentally for this. The best mental stance is not to fight it, not to measure it obsessively, and not to call it impossible too loudly. Locals tend to accept it as a fact of the place. You should too.

## 2. Clothing and personal equipment

### Clothing: cool, damp, no real weather
The place is cool and wet. Water does not often fall as rain, but it is always leaving the air. Condensation beads on every surface. A fine mist occasionally releases from overhead grids. There is no strong wind, but there is a constant damp chill.

Dress in synthetic or wool layers:

- **Base layer:** quick-drying, wicking, not cotton.
- **Mid layer:** fleece or synthetic insulation. Down is risky unless you are sure it will stay dry.
- **Outer layer:** waterproof and breathable. The wetness is mostly condensation and film water, not heavy rain, but it gets into everything.
- **Hat:** a brimmed cap or hood to keep overhead drips off your face and neck.
- **Gloves:** grippy, cut-resistant work gloves. The metal edges still have their cutting burrs.
- **Neck gaiter or buff:** useful for cold air and to cover your mouth when mist carries oil and ozone.
- **Spare socks:** far more than you think. Plan to change into dry socks several times a day.

### Footwear
This is critical. The ground is wet, smooth in places, gridded in others, and often sloped by a fraction. You need:

- waterproof or at least water-resistant boots,
- high ankle support,
- non-marking, non-slip soles,
- enough stiffness for long stair descents,
- broken-in but not worn out.

Do not bring smooth-soled shoes, sandals, open shoes, or anything you cannot lace tightly. The wet metal is unforgiving.

Carry foot powder, antifungal cream, and blister supplies. Treat your feet every evening. The damp will try to rot them.

### Eye protection and light management
There is no sun, but the light is bright as noon overcast from every direction. The glare off wet metal and condensation can cause headaches. Bring:

- polarized or lightly tinted glasses,
- clear safety glasses for working or walking near pipes,
- a good sleep mask, because darkness will not arrive on schedule.

If your eyes feel raw from the constant light, use artificial tears.

### Respiratory protection
The air smells faintly of oil and ozone. Mist is released to settle those smells, but that does not make the air clean. If you are sensitive, bring:

- an N95 or P100 mask for routine walking,
- a half-face respirator with vapor cartridges if you are working near exhausts or sumps,
- a puffer/inhaler if you have asthma or reactive airways.

The thin air plus a mask can feel suffocating at first. Practice walking with your mask on before you arrive.

## 3. Water, food, and cooking

### Water
There is water everywhere, but not all of it is potable. The condensation on railings and pipes may be relatively clean, but it may also have picked up oil, ozone residue, or metal dust from the city. Sumps, gutters, and wet seams are not safe drinking sources.

Carry:

- a robust water filter,
- purification tablets or drops,
- a small collapsible container for collecting clean condensation,
- electrolyte packets.

If you must collect water, use a clean tarp or a smooth, freshly wiped surface to catch condensation or mist. Avoid water that has run over heavily traveled decking. Do not drink from sump-catchers.

Ask locals where safe water is drawn. They will know. In such a damp city, there is usually a potable line somewhere in each residential block.

### Food
The local vegetation is sparse: gray cable-moss, pale fungi, drip-ferns. Do not graze on it. It is not ordinary wilderness forage, and it may be part of the city’s water-handling biology.

Bring high-calorie, compact, low-residue rations:

- energy bars,
- nuts and dried fruit,
- dehydrated meals,
- electrolyte drink powders,
- caffeine if you use it.

Bring more than the route length suggests. The spatial anomalies can make a “short” corridor into a long day. You do not want to be rationing on a staircase that arrives at the same landing.

### Cooking and fire
Avoid open flames. The air carries oily mist and ozone; sparks and flames are an unnecessary risk. Use chemical heat packs, an electric coil if you have access to power, or eat cold rations. The city has electricity, but you should not assume outlet compatibility. Carry extra batteries and a small power bank for personal electronics.

## 4. Navigation and the rules of space

This is where most travelers fail. The city is not intentionally malicious, but its geometry is not fully stable on the human scale.

### The 200-meter rule
Do not trust a straight corridor longer than about two hundred meters. After that, the corridor may not end where its perspective says it should. It may repeat itself, open onto a far side of the plateau, or fold into a street the map says is elsewhere.

If you must take a long straight corridor, mark the entrance. Place a small object, chalk mark, or wire tag at the threshold. Look back often. If you see the same mark before you should have, stop and reverse.

### Trust painted handprints and wire shrines
Locals mark stable paths with painted handprints and small shrines of wire. These are not decorations. They are navigational aids.

Learn to read them as soon as possible. A handprint may point toward a stable doorway. A wire shrine may indicate a loop-safe junction. Do not remove or “improve” them.

If you add your own marks, make them small, consistent, and non-destructive. Use:

- wax pencils or paint markers,
- bright flagging tape,
- small wire tags,
- removable chalk.

Condensation will wash away ordinary chalk over time. Wax or paint lasts longer.

### Doors and rooms
Some doors lead to rooms larger than the buildings that contain them. Some staircases descend for half a kilometer and arrive at the same landing.

Do not enter a room that seems larger than its outer footprint unless you have a guide or a very good reason. If you do enter, keep the door visible, stay close to the threshold, and trail a line if possible.

If a staircase returns you to the same landing, do not panic. That is a known event. Sit down, drink water, check your markers, and try a different route. Many stable staircases exist, especially the north staircases with slow pistons. Use those if you can, even though they are slow.

### Do not trust perspective
The city is dense and stacked. Balconies nearly touch. Walkways run above walkways. The light is even and shadowless, so depth and distance are harder to judge. A bridge may be farther than it looks. A corridor may be shorter than it appears.

Use touch and structure more than sight. Keep a hand near a rail. Step where the surface looks most worn, even if that is not the straightest line.

### Navigation tools
Bring simple, robust tools:

- a waterproof notebook,
- pencil or space pen,
- a small mechanical compass, though it may not be fully reliable,
- a pedometer or simple tally counter,
- a watch with an alarm,
- a small rangefinder if you have one,
- a piece of string or 15 meters of light cord for checking thresholds.

Do not assume GPS, phone maps, or electronic navigation will work. The city may interfere with signal, and the spatial anomalies may break their assumptions.

## 5. Shelter and sleep

### Sleeping without natural ground
There is no soil. You cannot drive tent stakes into earth. If you camp, you must anchor to structure.

Bring:

- a bivy bag or small one-person tent,
- a sleeping pad with insulation against cold metal,
- a sleeping bag or quilt rated for cool damp conditions,
- small C-clamps or spring clamps,
- lightweight line for tying to rails, grates, or pipe supports,
- a groundsheet that can double as a condensation collector.

If you use a hammock, ensure the anchor points are load-bearing. Not every pipe is safe. Do not hang from electrical conduit or cable trays.

### Sleeping in the light
The city dims too slowly for the eye. You will need to impose your own darkness.

Use a blackout sleep mask. If you are in a room, seal or cover the window gaps. If you are in a tent, use a dark fly. The light will still leak around edges.

Set an alarm for morning and night. You cannot rely on the sky to tell you when to sleep. Stick to a personal schedule, even if the city does not.

### Noise and silence
The quiet may make it hard to sleep because you become aware of your own breathing and heartbeat. Earplugs can help, but some travelers find they make the silence worse. Test what works for you.

You may hear drips, the click of thermal joints, or the slow exhalation of steam from the north staircases. These are normal. If a machine suddenly becomes louder, change position. That is more unusual than the silence.

## 6. Health and safety

### Altitude sickness
Monitor yourself and your companions. Mild headache and fatigue are common. If you become confused, nauseated, or breathless at rest, descend to lower tiers if possible. Lower areas may have denser, better air, though they may also have more haze.

Do not push through serious altitude symptoms. The city is large, and evacuation may be slow.

### Damp, cold, and hypothermia
The cool damp can chill you without making you aware of it. You may feel fine while walking and then begin shivering once you stop.

Change out of wet base layers promptly. Keep your sleeping bag dry in a waterproof stuff sack. Carry at least one full dry set of clothing reserved for sleeping.

Symptoms of early hypothermia:

- shivering,
- clumsiness,
- confusion,
- slurred speech,
- loss of fine coordination.

If you notice these, stop, shelter, change into dry clothes, eat, and warm up.

### Cuts, scrapes, and infections
The metal is new and sharp. The concrete has deliberate expansion joints. The vegetation is sparse, but fungi and moss may hold irritants.

Wear gloves. Clean cuts immediately with purified water and antiseptic. Carry antibiotics if you are prone to infection. Do not let small cuts go untreated in the damp.

### Foot care
Check your feet every evening. Wet feet for hours will macerate. Blisters form quickly on stairs.

Keep dry socks available. Use zinc oxide or tape on hot spots. Antifungal powder is useful.

### Waterborne risks
Do not drink from sumps, gutters, or wet seams. Treat all collected water. Boiling is effective but may be difficult without open flame. A good filter plus chemical purification is better.

### Fauna
The fauna is abundant but mostly quiet:

- pale moths above lamp panels,
- sparrow-things in cable runs,
- eel-like creatures in sumps,
- beetle-things in gutters,
- wall-lice that eat condensation film.

Most are harmless if left alone. Do not grab eels. Do not step unnecessarily on the wall-lice; they clean the metal. Do not disturb bird roosts in the cable runs. The animals are part of the city’s living surface.

If a bird opens its beak and no sound comes out, do not assume your hearing is broken. The sound may simply be taken by the place.

## 7. Local customs and interaction

### Quietness
The city is quiet. Voices do not carry, and locals seem used to this. Keep your voice low. Do not shout unless it is an emergency. If you must call someone over a distance, use a whistle, a light, or a hand signal.

A dropped bolt makes only a small tick. You should try to be as polite as the architecture.

### Do not look too closely at the weirdness
There is a local habit of not looking too closely at certain things:

- birds that double in number above the fourth gantry,
- shadows that lag a quarter-second,
- reflections that show lanterns in a different order,
- drips that fall upward.

This is not necessarily cowardice. It is a survival posture. The anomalies are ambient. Staring at them may not harm you, but it can pull your attention away from stable footing and stable paths.

Follow the local example. If you notice something wrong, note it quietly in your log and keep moving.

### Respect the handprints and shrines
Do not remove or deface the painted handprints and wire shrines. They are often the only reliable navigation. If you wish to add a marker, make it clearly temporary and remove it when you leave if possible.

### Trade goods and payment
The city is not a tourist economy. You may need to trade labor, supplies, or small useful items for shelter and guidance.

Good trade goods for this environment:

- fine wire,
- clean cloth,
- small desiccant sachets,
- polished metal blanks,
- spare gaskets or O-rings,
- small sealed packets of salt or sugar,
- waterproof notebooks or pencils.

Do not assume local money works. Offer to record your presence in a ledger if the place goes by that name. Carrying a small personal logbook is both practical and respectful.

### Asking for help
If you are lost, find a local or a stable shrine and wait. The locals know the safe paths. They may guide you if you are quiet, polite, and do not demand immediate answers.

When you ask for directions, do not argue about whether a corridor “should” go where it goes. Accept the local route, even if it seems longer.

## 8. First hours and daily rhythm

### First hour after arrival
If you arrive high up, sit down. Do not immediately explore.

- Place your hand on the deck.
- Breathe slowly.
- Drink water.
- Check your pulse oximeter.
- Do not stand near an unguarded edge until you have assessed your balance.

The light and quiet may give you false confidence. The city looks clean and stable. It is clean and stable, but it is not forgiving of overconfidence.

### First day
Stay near your arrival point or first settlement. Learn:

- where the stable path markers are,
- where potable water is,
- where you can sleep,
- how the locals move,
- how the staircases feel underfoot.

Do not go far. Your first job is not exploration. It is acclimatization.

### Daily rhythm
Because the day/night cycle is weak, impose your own:

- wake by alarm,
- eat,
- change socks,
- check feet,
- record time, location, and condition,
- travel in short legs,
- rest at stable junctions,
- return to base before your subjective evening,
- sleep by alarm.

Travel in 2–4 hour legs, less if carrying a full pack. The thin air and disorientation tire you faster than normal walking.

At every rest stop, write down:

- time,
- route taken,
- landmarks,
- the color and position of any handprint,
- anything odd you noticed,
- how you feel.

This log will help if you loop. It may also help locals if you become lost.

## 9. Emergency and exit plan

### Abort criteria
Set clear conditions for turning back or aborting the trip:

- repeated loops without a guide,
- inability to find stable path markers,
- altitude sickness not improved by rest,
- food or water shortage,
- injury affecting mobility,
- loss of a companion,
- severe disorientation or panic.

Do not be ashamed to abort. The city will still be there later.

### Getting lost
If you are lost:

1. Stop.
2. Find a wall, rail, or stable surface.
3. Sit down.
4. Drink water.
5. Check your log.
6. Look for painted handprints or wire shrines.
7. If none, stay put for a while. Do not wander in panic.

The city is inhabited. Someone will likely come if you are near a residential area. If you have a signal device, use it sparingly.

### Exiting
If you have a multiverse recall device, test it in a stable area before relying on it. The spatial anomalies may interfere with exit signals. If it fails, move to a known stable junction, such as a shrine-marked doorway, and try again.

Do not wait until your supplies are exhausted to begin your exit. The city can stretch time and distance. Begin your return earlier than you think necessary.

---

## 10. What to leave behind

Some things are not worth bringing:

- tent stakes for soil,
- heavy winter down gear that cannot stay dry,
- open flames, flares, or fireworks,
- noisy drones or airhorns,
- bright blinking electronics that may disturb local quiet,
- cotton clothing,
- leather that may mold,
- a rigid expectation that the city will behave like a normal place.

Also leave behind the need to “solve” the city. It is not a puzzle to be defeated. It is a place to be walked through carefully.

## 11. In short: how to imagine it

Visiting the Sort/Upcast/Ledger will feel like being inside a brand-new machine that has already been running for a long time. The air will smell like wet metal, faint ozone, and clean stone. The light will make your shadow look weak and doubled. The ground will hum through your soles. The silence will make your own breathing seem loud. Water will drip somewhere nearby, always. The staircases will be wet. The railings will be cool. The birds will open their beaks and no song will come.

You will learn to step where the painted handprints are, not where your eyes say the straight path should be. You will learn not to trust a corridor longer than two hundred meters. You will learn to sleep by alarm, not by darkness.

Pack for a cool, wet, fluorescent mountain hike inside a factory. Train for stairs without end. Arrive as a guest in a place that is not old enough to be a ruin and not new enough to be unfinished. Walk quietly. Watch for the handprints. And do not look too closely at the shadows.

### If you remember only five rules

1. **Wear grippy, waterproof boots and carry dry socks.**  
2. **Bring more food and water than the route suggests.**  
3. **Do not trust straight corridors over 200 meters.**  
4. **Follow painted handprints and wire shrines, not your sense of perspective.**  
5. **Keep a log, keep a sleep schedule, and keep your voice low.**
