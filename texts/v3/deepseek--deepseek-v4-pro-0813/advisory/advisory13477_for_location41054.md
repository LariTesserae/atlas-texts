To prepare for the Drown, do not think of it as a normal expedition. It is not a forest, not a mountain, not a cave, not a hot spring, though it is half all those things. It is a high, warm, fog-sealed plateau of living stone and wet moss, where the air itself is a slow mineral solution and anything left still for too long begins to turn into rock. An ordinary human traveler going there must prepare for four constant enemies: thin air, endless moisture, low light, and accretion. You will never be fully dry. You will never see far. You will never be able to put something down without thinking about it.

Here is how to prepare.

---

## 1. Before you go: physical and mental conditioning

### Altitude
The Drown is a high plateau. You will not know its exact elevation until you arrive, but the air is thin enough that you will breathe harder for the first several days. If you live near sea level, spend at least two to three weeks before departure doing regular aerobic exercise: stair climbing with a pack, steep walking, anything that makes your lungs and legs work while carrying weight. More importantly, if you can, stage your ascent. Sleep one or two nights at an intermediate altitude before entering the Drown. Do not fly or portal directly from sea level into high mist and then try to carry heavy gear.

Bring acetazolamide (Diamox) if you have access to it, and start taking a low dose one to two days before arrival. It helps with altitude acclimatization. Also bring ibuprofen for altitude headache and anti-nausea medication. Learn the early signs of high-altitude pulmonary and cerebral edema: severe breathlessness at rest, crackling lungs, confusion, loss of coordination. If you develop them, descend, no matter how beautiful the terraces are.

### Humidity and heat
The stone itself gives off warmth, and the mineral pools are warm as a bath. The air is saturated. If you have never spent time in a steam room or tropical wet cave, start now. Train in a hot, humid environment: sit in a sauna, then do light exercise in a shower room, then sit again. Learn what it feels like for your body to never quite cool down or dry off. You will sweat, but it will not evaporate. Your clothes will cling. Your skin will soften and wrinkle. That is the normal state of the Drown.

### Mental preparation
The light is a perpetual gray-green twilight. The sun is not visible. There are no strong shadows. The horizon is usually ten to fifty strides away. Sound is muffled: voices are swallowed, distance collapses, and the dominant noise is a fine continuous hiss, as if the whole landscape is breathing through its teeth.

This can be deeply disorienting. Some travelers find it soothing. Others find it claustrophobic, lonely, or maddening. Before you go, spend time in dim, quiet, humid places. Turn off your phone. Sit in fog or steam. Practice being without birdsong, without insect noise, without wind. Bring a reliable watch with a date and alarm, because you will lose track of time. Set a simple daily routine and keep it, even if your body says it is always late afternoon.

Bring a small object from home: a smooth stone, a photograph in a waterproof case, a piece of jewelry. You may need it to anchor your sense of self in a land that is always half-finished.

## 2. Clothing: dress for a warm, wet cave with no dry corners

The single most important rule: **no cotton**. Cotton absorbs water, dries slowly, and will chill you or chafe you to rawness. Everything you wear should be synthetic or wool, quick-drying, and intended for constant dampness.

### Base layers
- Long-sleeved merino wool or synthetic base layers. Merino wool is warm when wet and resists odor for a while.
- Tights or long underwear for your legs, because you will often wear wet trousers against your skin.
- A high-necked top to keep mineral mist from running down your chest.

You will want long sleeves and long pants, not because of cold, but because the moss, rough stone, and mineral edges will scrape you. The water is not dirty, but constant wetness makes skin fragile. Cover it.

### Mid layers
- A thin fleece or wool pullover. In the warm pools you may not need it, but the air at the edges of the mist can be cooler than you expect, especially when you stop moving.
- A synthetic insulated jacket for resting periods. Do not bring down. Down loses all warmth when wet, and it will get wet.

### Outer shell
- A waterproof-breathable jacket and pants are useful, but understand that in 100% humidity they will eventually wet out from the inside. Look for pit zips and thigh zips for ventilation.
- A wide-brimmed or billed waterproof hat, or a hood that covers your face. The mist is not rain, but it condenses on your hair and runs down your neck.
- A lightweight waterproof poncho or tarp can also serve as a portable dry space.

### Feet
This is the hardest part. Your feet will be wet constantly. Walking through ankle-deep channels, warm pools, and moss is unavoidable.

- Do **not** wear waterproof boots unless they are high enough to keep out water and you are certain you will not wade deeper than their tops. Once water gets inside a waterproof boot, it stays there, and the boot becomes a bucket.
- Drainable, quick-drying trail runners or approach shoes with aggressive grip are often better. They let water out.
- Bring a pair of wading sandals or water shoes with sturdy soles for inside the town and for crossing pools.
- Consider felt-soled wading boots or neoprene reef shoes if you can get them. The stone is covered in algal film and fresh mineral rind, and it is slippery.
- Bring many pairs of synthetic or wool socks. More than you think. Keep each pair in its own small waterproof bag. Change socks at least twice a day. Hang wet socks on your pack, but they will not dry much. The goal is to give your skin a break from the same wet sock, not to achieve dryness.
- Bring a pair of waterproof socks or vapor-barrier socks to wear inside your sleeping bag over dry feet. This creates a dry microclimate for sleep.
- Bring foot powder, antifungal cream, and a barrier cream like zinc oxide. Use them every night.

### Hands
- Thin synthetic or wool gloves for cool air.
- Waterproof or neoprene gloves if you will be scraping stone or handling wet ropes.
- Bring more than one pair; they will become soaked and crusted with mineral.

## 3. Shelter and sleep: staying off the growing stone

The ground is never dry. It is warm, wet, and often actively depositing new mineral. If you put a tent on the ground, the floor will become wet, then crusted, then perhaps stuck. The best shelters are elevated.

### Hammock and tarp
A hammock is often ideal, if you can find strong anchor points. The tree-like plants with dark aerial roots may hold, but inspect them carefully. The stone itself may be soft or brittle in places. Use wide tree straps to avoid cutting into moss and bark. Hang a large waterproof tarp overhead, because water drips from leaves, stone edges, and the air itself. Set the tarp low and steeply pitched so condensation runs off rather than pooling.

### Tent
If you use a tent, choose a freestanding one with a bathtub floor. Do not expect to stake it into solid ground; use rocks or your pack to anchor it. Wipe the floor every morning, because mineral film will form. A footprint or groundsheet is useful, but it will become wet and crusted. Store it in a separate wet bag.

### Sleeping bag and pad
- Use a synthetic sleeping bag or quilt, not down.
- Use a closed-cell foam sleeping pad rather than an inflatable if you can tolerate it, because it cannot puncture and it insulates even when wet. The ground is warm, so you may not need heavy insulation, but you do need a barrier between your body and the mineral water.
- If you carry an inflatable pad, bring a patch kit and use a high-R-value model. Keep it inside a plastic bag inside your pack.

### Vapor barrier
Because the air is so humid, your body moisture will condense inside your sleeping bag. A vapor-barrier liner—a silnylon or plastic sack that goes inside the bag—will keep your sweat from soaking the insulation. It feels clammy but it works. Use it only when sleeping.

### Critical rule: hang or elevate everything
Never place boots, pack, tools, or clothing directly on wet stone or moss overnight. They will be wet and may be lightly mineral-crusted by morning. Use a line tied between supports. Use carabiners, loops, and mesh bags. Keep your sleeping kit in a dry bag until the moment you enter it.

## 4. Water and food

### Drinking water
The water everywhere is warm, milky, mineral-rich, and probably not safe to drink untreated. It carries dissolved stone. Drinking large amounts of it could upset your stomach, strain your kidneys, or give you mineral overload. Do not assume the clear pools are clean. They are warm and full of dissolved calcium carbonate, and they may contain microorganisms.

Ask the local people where they get drinking water. They may have cisterns of condensed mist or cooler springs with less mineral content. Follow their lead.

Bring:
- At least two liters of potable water in collapsible containers for the first day or two.
- A water filter rated for heavy mineral and microorganism removal, with a pre-filter cloth to remove sediment and mineral flakes.
- Chemical purification tablets as backup.
- Electrolyte powders or tablets, because you will sweat and may not feel thirsty in the saturated air.

If you collect mist or condensation from leaves, filter and purify it. Do not collect from surfaces covered in fresh mineral rind unless you have no choice.

### Food
The local vegetation is dense and strange. The people cook roots and moss in the hot pools. You do not know which local plants are edible, and many may concentrate minerals or harbor irritants. Bring enough food for your entire stay, plus two extra days.

- High-calorie, waterproof packaged meals.
- Dried fruit, nuts, jerky, hard cheese if you can keep it cool and sealed.
- Energy bars that do not crumble too much.
- Instant coffee or tea for morale.
- Vitamin supplements, especially vitamin D, because there is almost no sunlight.

Do not rely on hunting or fishing. The fauna is scarce, small, and mostly hidden. The pale shrimp in the warm pools may or may not be edible. The locals do not hunt. There is no firewood. Do not plan to cook over a fire. Bring a small canister stove with enough fuel for your meals. Use it only in a well-ventilated place, not inside a sealed stone room.

Clean your pot and utensils daily with a weak acid solution, because mineral scale will form on them.

## 5. Navigation and movement

Visibility is often less than thirty feet. There is no sun. There are no distant landmarks. Sound does not carry. The terrain is a staircase of terraces, pools, and moss-covered stone, and every surface is slippery.

### Navigation tools
- A reliable compass and a map, if maps exist. The local topography shifts slowly, so maps may be only a rough guide.
- A GPS unit in a waterproof housing, with spare batteries in sealed containers. Do not rely on it exclusively. Satellite signals or multiverse positioning may be weak or nonexistent.
- Bright-colored flagging tape for marking your route, but remove it when you leave. The stone will quickly coat it, and you do not want to litter the landscape with plastic ghosts.
- A small altimeter/barometer can help you understand elevation changes even when you cannot see them.

### Movement rules
- Use a trekking pole or staff to probe ahead. The water may be deeper than it looks. The mineral floor may be thin over a warm pool. The moss may hide a gap or a submerged channel.
- Do not trust the ground to stay where it is. Not because it will collapse, but because it is always growing. A ledge that was clear yesterday may have a new rind today. A dry-looking stone may be a thin crust over soft mud.
- Stay on established paths if possible. The townspeople scrape the main channels every morning. Follow those channels and edges. Do not wander across pristine terraces: the mineral formations are delicate, and your footprints will become hard permanent bumps.
- In thick standing fog, tie yourself to your companions with a short rope, or keep one hand on a guide line. The fog can be so dense you cannot see your own feet.
- Carry a whistle, but know that sound is swallowed by the wet air. It may be useful at close range. A bright flashing LED may be more visible than sound.
- Do not enter warm pools alone. The water is warm, but immersion can still be disorienting. Some pools are hip-deep or deeper, with soft mineral floors.
- Move slowly. The light is dim and shadowless, which flattens depth perception. A step may be six inches or three feet; your eye cannot always tell. Test with your pole before committing your weight.

## 6. Tools and gear maintenance: fighting the mineral crust

The Drown is a place of accretion. Anything left on a ledge may be crusted by afternoon. A footprint in soft lime becomes a hard bump within a week. Your gear will constantly collect a fine white rind.

### Essential tools
- A small stainless steel or titanium scraper or putty knife.
- A stiff nylon brush for cleaning boot soles and gear.
- A soft cloth for wiping optics.
- A toothbrush for zippers and buckles.
- A bottle of dilute citric acid or white vinegar for dissolving mineral scale.
- Silicone grease for zippers and O-rings.
- A multi-tool with pliers, knife, and scissors.

### Daily routine
Every evening, before you sleep:
1. Wipe down all metal, plastic, and rubber with a cloth dampened with dilute acid.
2. Rinse with clean water.
3. Dry as much as possible with a small synthetic towel.
4. Apply silicone grease to zippers and seals.
5. Store electronics in waterproof cases or dry bags.

If you do not do this, zippers will seize, buckles will crust, lenses will fog with mineral film, and knife blades will dull with scale.

Use waterproof cases for cameras, phones, batteries, and maps. Put desiccant packets inside the cases, but accept that they will soon be saturated. Recharge or replace them if you can. A small airtight Pelican-style case with a silicone gasket is better than a soft dry bag for electronics.

Bring several large plastic bags or dry bags of different colors for sorting wet and dry gear. Pack wet things separately from dry things. There is no such thing as dry, but you can preserve small dry zones.

## 7. Medical kit and health

The Drown is warm, wet, and dim. It is not a place of obvious danger—no venomous swarms, no large predators—but the constant moisture and mineral water create specific risks.

### Foot and skin care
- Antifungal cream for athlete’s foot and groin fungus.
- Antibacterial ointment for scrapes.
- Zinc oxide barrier cream for chafing and macerated skin.
- Moleskin or blister pads.
- Liquid bandage for small cuts, because ordinary bandages will not stay on wet skin.
- Sterile alcohol wipes for cleaning wounds.
- A small foot basin or plastic bag for a nightly clean-water foot soak, if you can spare water.

Your feet will wrinkle and soften. Check them every night. Do not ignore small cuts; they can become macerated and infected. Dry them as best you can, apply antifungal or antibacterial cream, and put on dry socks before sleep.

### Ears and eyes
The warm water may contain microorganisms. Avoid getting it up your nose or deep in your ears. Do not submerge your head in warm pools. Bring:
- Acetic acid ear drops to use after water exposure.
- Sterile saline eye wash.
- Clear or lightly tinted wraparound glasses to keep mineral mist out of your eyes.

### Respiratory and allergies
The air is full of moisture, mold spores, and fine mineral droplets. If you are prone to asthma or allergies, bring antihistamines and any inhalers. A lightweight N95 or surgical mask can help if the air irritates your lungs, but it will become wet and less effective. Change it often.

### Gastrointestinal
Bring oral rehydration salts, anti-diarrheal medication, and a mild laxative. The change in water and diet may upset your stomach. Do not drink from unknown pools.

### Altitude medications
As above, bring acetazolamide, ibuprofen, and anti-nausea medication. If you have a history of severe altitude sickness, bring dexamethasone and know when to use it. Have an evacuation plan.

### Other
- Tweezers for splinters and small mineral shards.
- Small scissors.
- Bandages of various sizes.
- Safety pins.
- A small emergency blanket, but understand it will be wet.
- A thermometer.

## 8. Cultural preparation: how to live among the stone-gardeners

The people of the Drown are not villagers in the usual sense. They are stone gardeners. Their town is crowded, layered, and built by directing mineral water over wooden lattices until it becomes shell. They understand that the world is unfinished and that their homes will eventually forget their doors. They are patient, quiet, and at ease in the wet dimness.

### Etiquette
- Speak softly. Voices do not carry far, but loudness feels intrusive in the muffled air.
- Do not touch or scrape the walls of houses without permission. Some walls are still growing.
- Do not walk on a roof that is also someone’s terrace without invitation.
- Do not bathe in public cisterns or drinking pools. Ask which pools are for what.
- Do not light fires. Fire is rare and precious because almost nothing is dry enough to burn. A small oil lamp is the local light.
- Do not leave trash. Anything you drop will be crusted into the stone and may remain as a foreign inclusion. Pack everything out.
- If you see someone scraping a channel or drain, do not interrupt or walk through their work. Offer to help if you wish, but follow their instruction.

### Trade and gifts
Metal from the lowlands is valuable. If you want to bring gifts, bring small stainless steel tools, needles, fishhooks, or knife blades. Salt may also be useful. But do not assume you can buy your way with trinkets. The people are not simple; they are experts in their own environment. Treat them with respect.

Ask before taking photographs or making recordings. Some may refuse. The mist and dim light may ruin most images anyway.

### Learning from locals
The most important preparation is to watch and ask. The locals know which stone is safe, which water is drinkable, which moss is soft to walk on, and which fog means a storm or a shift in the springs. If a local guide offers to walk with you, accept. They will move with a quiet surefootedness that you cannot imitate, and they will keep you from stepping into a hidden pool or a fresh mineral rim.

Learn a few phrases in the local language if possible:
- “Where is safe water?”
- “May I pass?”
- “Is this stone stable?”
- “Thank you.”
- “I am a traveler from far away.”

Do not say that their land is gloomy or unfinished as if it were a flaw. They know. They live in the condition of being inside a world being made.

## 9. Psychological survival: the long gray silence

The Drown can erode your sense of time and self. The light never changes much. There is no sunrise, no sunset, no weather except the slow movement of fog. The sound is a continuous hiss, like the world breathing. There are no birds. No insects. The only music is water dripping.

Some travelers find this deeply peaceful. Others find it slowly overwhelming. Prepare for both.

### Keep a rhythm
Use your watch. Set an alarm for morning and evening. Eat at regular times. Sleep and wake on a schedule, even though the light does not tell you when. Tell yourself the day and date every morning, aloud if necessary.

### Carry light
Bring a small warm-colored LED lantern or headlamp. Use it for an hour or two each “evening” in your shelter. That small pool of warm light can be a psychological anchor. Do not keep it on all the time, or you will lose the local sense of the mist.

### Accept the wetness
You will never be dry. If you spend your days fighting it, you will exhaust yourself. Instead, create one or two small dry rituals: a dry pair of socks before sleep, a dry cloth for your face in the evening, a dry bag containing a single dry shirt. Protect those small dry things fiercely. They are your morale.

### Manage isolation and silence
If you travel with a companion, stay close. The mist swallows voices, so you may feel alone even when someone is ten strides away. Use a rope, a blinking light, or regular check-ins. If you travel alone, consider carrying a small notebook and writing often. The act of writing can help you remember that you are a person moving through a place, not a piece of the fog.

### Know the signs of mental strain
Long periods in low light and constant moisture can cause low mood, irritability, sleep disturbances, and a feeling of unreality. Vitamin D supplements may help. Bright light therapy for short periods may also help, if you have a light. If you feel deeply disoriented, stop, sit down, drink water, eat something, and breathe slowly. If the feeling does not pass, leave the mist for a day or descend if possible.

## 11. The first few days after arrival

When you first step into the Drown, you will likely feel two contradictory things at once: the air is warm and soft, and your breath is short. The light is gray and green, like the inside of a shell. The hiss of water is everywhere. Your skin will begin to feel damp within minutes.

Move slowly. Do not try to explore far on the first day. Find a place to sleep or ask for lodging. Learn where the safe drinking water is, where the latrine is, and which paths are public. Watch how the locals walk: they place their feet flat, test the stone, and never trust a surface that looks freshly filmed with white.

Spend the first evening doing gear maintenance. Check your feet. Drink water. Eat. Set your watch. Tell yourself the date and place.

Do not be surprised if you dream strangely. The constant sound, the moist warmth, and the low light combine to blur the line between waking and sleep. Many travelers report dreams of rooms growing slowly smaller, of doorways closing like wounds. The people say this is not a bad sign. It is only the mind learning the local truth: that the world is not finished, and that all things tend toward stone.

If you accept that, you may come to love the Drown. You may find peace in the soft moss, the warm pools, the patient scraping of channels in the gray morning. You may understand why the people say a house is not complete until it has forgotten its door. You may leave with a thin white rind on your boots and in the seams of your pack, and you may, for a long time afterward, miss the hiss of the world being made.
