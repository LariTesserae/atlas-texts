# Preparing to Visit Sumpfold: A Briefing for an Ordinary Multiverse Traveler

You are not packing for a swamp, a ruin, or a normal town. You are packing for a small, working settlement that is half greenhouse, half engine room, half canal-side tenement—and all of it set below the water table. The air will be thick. The ground will be wet. The pump will never stop. The plants are not decoration; they are load-bearing architecture. The water is not scenery; it is the district’s circulation, clock, and main street.

Go as a careful guest, not as a tourist. Sumpfold is not hostile, but it is a functioning place. It will not adapt to you. You must adapt to it.

---

## 1. Before You Travel

### Understand where you are going

Sumpfold is small—cathedral-nave scale—but extremely dense. It is layered vertically: sump water and submerged pipes at the bottom, boardwalks just above the water, lower galleries and workshops, tenement rooms and living bridges above that, and a canopy of leaves, wires, steam vents, and upper walkways higher still.

The place is warm and humid, never cold. The air smells green and wet, then hot oil and wet brick. Water drips from leaves for hours after rain. The pump house at the center produces a slow, deep, constant rhythm: one full stroke every few seconds. That sound travels through the ground, the boardwalks, and the water.

The ecology is mostly botanical and microbial. There are very few animals. You may hear no birdsong, no frogs, no insects. That absence is not an accident; it is part of the balance of the place. Do not bring an animal. Do not bring seeds, eggs, soil, or anything that might introduce a new living thing.

### Physical preparation

You do not need to be an athlete, but you should prepare for:

- Constant heat and humidity
- Slippery, uneven, sometimes living walkways
- Stairs, ladders, narrow passages, and low overhead pipes
- The need to move slowly and deliberately

If you can, spend time in a sauna, steam room, or hot greenhouse before departure. Practice walking on wet wood and slimy stone. Train your ankles. Learn to step over a narrow channel of moving water without hesitating. If you are not comfortable on a wet log or a worn brick arch, Sumpfold will feel dangerous until you slow down.

### Permissions and introductions

Sumpfold is not a public park. It is a working settlement. If possible, send word ahead through the canal side or the keeper’s house. If you cannot, arrive at the top of the old dike stairs on the north side and make yourself known.

Do not wander into the lower lanes uninvited. Look for the keeper, a tender, or anyone working. Say plainly that you are a traveler, that you mean no harm, and that you would like to ask permission to visit. Ask where you may set your pack, where you may walk, and where you may sleep.

A small useful gift is appropriate if you have one: spare batteries, good cord, copper fittings, dry tea, a strong canvas bag, or a decent multi-tool. Do not offer junk. They live in a place where everything must survive damp.

## 2. Clothing: Dress for a Wet Engine-Garden

The most important rule is **no cotton against your skin**. Cotton will soak up sweat and humidity, chafe you, and never dry.

### Base layer

- Lightweight merino wool or synthetic wicking shirt.
- Quick-dry underwear. Bring more changes than you think.
- Synthetic or wool socks. Several pairs. You will want dry feet.

### Outer layer

- Lightweight long-sleeved shirt and long pants. Not shorts. The plants, hot pipes, rough brick, and wet iron can scrape you.
- Breathable rain shell with vents. It will rain, and the canopy will drip long after rain stops, but you will also sweat. A non-breathable poncho can become a sweatbox.
- Avoid heavy canvas or denim. They become waterlogged and heavy.

### Footwear

This is the single most important choice.

- High-ankle waterproof boots.
- Non-slip lug sole. Algae-slicked brick is extremely slippery.
- Steel or composite shank to protect against old nails, iron plate edges, and uneven brick.
- Toe protection if possible.
- Break them in before arrival.

Bring a second pair of lightweight shoes or sandals for indoors. Never go barefoot. The ground is wet, and much of it is not soft moss—it is worn brick, iron plate, and root.

### Gloves

Full-fingered, grippy, quick-dry gloves. You will grab wet roots, rough handrails, slimy iron, and rope. Rubber-dipped palms are good.

### Head and eyes

A brimmed hat keeps canopy drip out of your eyes. A hood alone is not enough. Clear or yellow-lensed glasses help with steam, splashes, and low twigs. Not essential, but useful.

## 3. Packing: What to Bring

### Water system

Bring more water than you think. The humidity will make you sweat constantly. Carry at least 2–3 liters per day, plus purification tablets or a filter.

Do not drink the sump water, the runnels, or canal water without asking and treating. Some water may be safe from a particular cistern; ask the keeper. Electrolyte powder is genuinely valuable.

### Food

Bring dense, non-perishable, low-odor, low-crumb food. Energy bars, dried fruit, hard cheese if you tolerate it, sealed rations, dried meat if allowed. You will probably not have access to open flame, so bring food you can eat cold or ask about the local kitchen.

Pack out every crumb and wrapper. Do not drop food. Sumpfold has a very controlled ecology, and you do not want to feed anything.

### Light

- Headlamp with red mode.
- Small flashlight.
- Spare batteries in a waterproof case.

**No open flame.** No candles, no oil lanterns, no lighters, no camp stoves, no smoking materials. Marsh gas can collect near sump vents. Use electric light only.

### Sleeping

If you are offered a room or berth, excellent. If not, bring a compact sleep system:

- Sleeping bag liner or light bag.
- Inflatable mat or bedroll.
- A small hammock only if you already know how to hang one safely and only with permission.

Do not hang anything from structural roots unless a local tells you the specific root is safe. Even then use wide tree straps, never nails or screws. Earplugs are essential for sleep. The pump does not stop, and the rhythm will be in the floorboards. An eye mask helps with early light through leaves.

### First aid

- Blister care.
- Waterproof bandages.
- Antiseptic wipes or spray.
- Antifungal powder or cream.
- Electrolyte tablets.
- Antihistamines.
- Personal medications.
- Barrier cream for constantly wet skin.

Small cuts in Sumpfold should be cleaned and covered immediately. The environment is wet and slow-healing.

### Electronics and tools

Keep phone, camera, and power bank in dry bags or waterproof cases. Humidity is hard on electronics. Carry silica gel packets.

A small repair kit is useful:

- Duct tape.
- Cord.
- Zip ties.
- Carabiners.
- Small multi-tool.

You are not there to cut vegetation or modify structures. The multi-tool is for your own gear.

### A walking stick

Optional but useful. A stick with a rubber tip can help you test footing and depth. Do not use it to poke holes in brick or living roots.

---

## 4. What NOT to Bring

- No open flame of any kind.
- No pets or live animals.
- No seeds, soil, potted plants, raw wood, eggs, or insects.
- No drones or flying cameras. The space is threaded with wires and branches.
- No large blades, saws, pruning tools, or machetes.
- No wheeled luggage. Use a backpack.
- No heavy perfumes, soaps, or detergents that might wash into the water system.
- No loudspeakers or music devices used without headphones.

Clean your boots and gear of mud and seeds before entering. Treat Sumpfold as a closed botanical system, because it mostly is.

## 5. Arrival and Movement

### Entering

The best approach is from the north, by the canal and down the old dike stairs. Stop at the top. Listen. The pump will be the first thing you notice—a slow deep thump coming up through the wet ground.

Descend carefully. The dike stairs may be dark, wet, and worn. Use the handrail if there is one. If no one sees you, wait at a visible dry spot and call out. Do not wander into the lower lanes until someone speaks with you.

### Moving through Sumpfold

Move like a guest in a machine:

- Look before you step.
- Test wet boards before trusting them.
- Use three points of contact on ladders.
- Watch for low pipes, wires, steam vents, and hanging roots.
- Never block a runnel or channel. Put your pack on raised dry ground, not in a waterway.
- If you need to cross water, use a bridge or arch. Do not wade unless a local is leading you.

The roots are load-bearing. Some of them are handrails. Some green boards are older than the town charter. Do not peel bark, swing on roots, or cut anything. If you are unsure whether a surface is path or structure, ask.

### Around the pump house and machinery

The pump house, gantry crane, sluice gate, workshops, and boiler repair bay are working areas. Do not enter unless invited. Keep loose clothing, straps, and hair away from the flywheel and piston rods. The pump is slow but immensely strong.

Do not touch gauges, valves, pump controls, sluice gear, or crane controls. If the rhythm changes and you are nearby, tell the keeper. Do not try to fix anything.

## 6. Health and Hygiene

### Skin and heat

The humidity is relentless. Drink more water than you think you need. Use electrolytes.

Dry your feet whenever you stop for a while. Change socks frequently. Use antifungal powder. If you get a cut, clean it and cover it immediately. Small wounds in wet environments can get stubborn.

### Air and breathing

The air is thick, warm, and full of green scents, oil, steam, and brick dust. If you are asthmatic or mold-sensitive, bring your medication and consider a mask for lower galleries and enclosed spaces.

If you feel dizzy, nauseated, or headachy near the sump vents, move upward and upwind. Marsh gas can collect in low pockets. No open flame anywhere near those places.

### Water contact

Do not swim in the sump. Do not put open cuts into the sump or runnel water. If you do touch it, wash with clean water as soon as possible.

### Waste

Use whatever latrine or privy the locals direct you to. Do not wash soap or waste into runnels or the sump. Pack out your trash.

## 7. Etiquette and Daily Life

Sumpfold is a small, stacked, working community. Privacy is limited. Be unobtrusive.

- Ask before entering homes, workshops, or upper galleries.
- Ask before taking photographs.
- Do not touch tools, gauges, valves, crane controls, or pump controls.
- Do not pick leaves, fruit, moss, or flowers.
- Do not clear debris from a runnel without asking. It may be intentional water management.
- Do not feed any animals. There are few, and the locals may know them individually.
- If you see the heron or the eels, observe quietly from a distance.

The local rule—*people, plants, and water have right-of-way*—means in practice:

- Yield to people carrying tools or baskets.
- Do not damage living wood.
- Never obstruct the movement of water.

If you are unsure, step aside and let the place do what it is doing.

Children in Sumpfold may know more about safe paths than you do. If a child tells you a board is unsafe or a root is a handrail, listen.

## 8. What It Will Feel Like

You will hear the pump before you see the place. The thump comes up through the ground like a heartbeat. As you descend, the air thickens. The light turns greenish and humid, like a greenhouse on a warm overcast day.

The first hour may feel claustrophobic. The lanes are narrow, the plants press close, water runs under the boardwalk, and the sound is constant. You will be damp almost immediately. Water beads on handrails, drips from the canopy, and runs down walls in humid streaks.

Then something shifts. The rhythm settles. The place begins to feel less like a flooded ruin and more like a single large body: plants as bones, water as blood, the pump as heart.

You may notice the absence of animal sound. It is not silent—water and machinery make plenty of noise—but it is not alive with birdsong or frogs. That absence can be eerie until you understand it. The ecology is mostly green and patient.

At night, the pump house lights spill across the sump in long yellow bands. The leaves above go black and glossy. The pump keeps its slow stroke. If you let go of hurry, the place’s pace becomes almost soothing: measured in piston strokes, drips, and the settling of silt.

---

## 9. Departure

Before you leave:

- Clean your boots and gear. Do not carry mud, seeds, or water out into another world.
- Dry what you can.
- Check your skin for cuts, rashes, or fungal patches.
- Wash yourself with clean water and soap after leaving if possible.

The smell of wet brick and machine oil may stay in your clothes for days. Air them out. You may hear the pump rhythm in your head for a while after you go.

If you arrive with nothing but ordinary clothes and caution, you can still visit Sumpfold—but stay near the top of the dike stairs, wait to be noticed, and ask for guidance. Sumpfold does not need a hero or a rescuer. It needs a careful guest who understands that the water, roots, and pump are not obstacles. They are the town.
