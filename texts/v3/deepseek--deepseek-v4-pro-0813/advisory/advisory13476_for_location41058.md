**Preparation for the Hollow: A Practical Brief**

Before you do anything else, you must accept a contradiction: you are a mobile water source entering a place where water is not merely scarce but prohibited. Your primary preparation is not just surviving the dryness, but preventing yourself from leaking into it. The hollow is a sealed, dry, hot, dim, and endlessly noisy world. Treat it as a combination of an industrial environment, a low-oxygen mine, a fragile archaeological site, and a living organism. The following is what an ordinary human multiverse traveler should do before attempting a descent.

---

## 1. Understand the environment before you go

Do not think of this as a desert. Deserts have water somewhere. This place has none — not in the air, not in the soil, not in the stone. That absence is a rule of local physics. Your body is about 60% water. Inside the hollow, you will be an anomaly, a wet creature in a kiln.

The air is hot, dim, thick with mineral dust and sulfurous gas. It smells of sulfur, hot copper, and resin. The plants are not water-based; they are slow chemical fires feeding on heat and mineral vapor. The city is empty, but not ruined. It breathes, settles, and sings. It is built for a smaller number of presences than it once held.

Your preparation must account for: extreme heat, zero ambient humidity, toxic gases, abrasive dust, explosive spore pods, continuous loud noise, unstable ground, and the psychological pressure of being inside a sound-amplifying stone body.

---

## 2. Timing and approach

Choose your time carefully.

- **Avoid the hottest part of the day.** That is when updrafts carry plumes of dust and spores above the rim, gas vents erupt most violently, and the pods burst in dry storms. The heat is at its worst, and the air becomes thick with pale dust.
- **Evening brings shifting winds** and the slow rattling of the dry forest as plants turn toward heat. It may be cooler, but the changing air currents and moving vegetation add sensory confusion.
- **The best window is likely early morning**, after the coolest part of the cycle but before the basin fully heats. “Cool” here is relative; do not expect comfort.

Locate the spiral descent from above using thermal imaging. The seam in the salt plain may show only as a heat shimmer like dirty amber. Mark the rim with a radio beacon or high-visibility flag — one you can find again from below.

---

## 3. Life support: water, air, heat

Your suit is your world. Make it a good one.

### Sealed suit

You need a fully sealed, vapor-proof suit with integrated boots, gloves, hood, and a full-face mask. It must work both ways:

- **Keeping the hollow out** — sulfur gases, spores, mineral dust, heat.
- **Keeping you in** — your moisture, sweat, breath, CO₂, and any leaked water.

Any exposed skin will dry, crack, or burn. Any damp breath or spilled water violates the local rule. The suit must be worn at all times inside the hollow, from the first step on the spiral stairs to the last step out.

### Hydration

Carry a hydration bladder inside the suit, filled with electrolyte solution, and drink on a schedule, not by thirst. Pre-hydrate for 24–48 hours before descent. The dry air will pull moisture from any breach with frightening speed. If your suit is sealed correctly, you will still be losing water through exertion and heat; plan for at least 6–8 liters for a few hours below, plus reserve.

Do not open water containers inside the hollow. Drink only through a closed tube inside your suit. If you spill water, treat it as a contamination event: seal it, remove it, and end the expedition.

### Air quality

Do not breathe the air. It is not just dry; it is chemically active.

- Use a **powered air-purifying respirator (PAPR)** with acid-gas and particulate filters as a minimum. The air contains sulfur compounds, hot copper vapor, resin volatiles, and mineral dust.
- Better: use a **self-contained breathing apparatus (SCBA)** or a closed-circuit rebreather. Oxygen levels may drop near vents, and filters can be overwhelmed by spore clouds.
- Carry a small escape bottle of breathing gas.
- Wear a **multi-gas detector** for SO₂, H₂S, CO, O₂, and flammable vapors. Set alarms low. Some fissures breathe gas; do not trust smell or visible haze.

### Thermal protection

The temperature rises as you descend, until the stone itself feels feverish. You need active cooling:

- A **liquid cooling garment** worn under the suit is best.
- Use a reflective outer layer to reduce radiant heat.
- Boots must have thick, insulating, puncture-resistant soles. The salt polygons are sharp, and the ground is hot.
- Gloves should be heat-resistant but dexterous enough for ropes and gear.
- Carry spare phase-change cooling packs if possible, but remember they are sealed; do not let them leak.

---

## 4. Descent and mobility

The only way down is a spiral of hand-cut steps, 200 meters to the floor. Their edges have softened; they may crumble.

- Fix a rope at the rim and descend with a harness, descender, and fall protection. Do not trust the steps alone.
- Test each footing. The salt and gypsum can crack or slip.
- The descent will take longer than you think because of heat, poor visibility, and the need to manage gear.

On the floor, the ground is cracked salt polygons and fissures that breathe.

- **Never step on a crack.** Cross at solid intersections only.
- Use a **probe pole** to test the surface ahead. Sinkholes open without warning; old walls settle with grinding cracks.
- Travel roped together in pairs if possible. A fall into a fissure is likely fatal.
- The city’s streets are wide and empty; landmarks repeat. Lay a bright marker tape or high-visibility line from the descent point. Use orange or red tags at intersections. The white salt will swallow anything pale.
- Do not rely on GPS. It may not work here, and the local rules seem tilted. Bring an inertial navigation system or a simple spool of line you can follow back.

---

## 5. Light and visibility

The light below is dim, not from night but from a thick haze of mineral dust and hot gas.

- Use a **headlamp with a wide flood beam** for immediate ground.
- Carry a **handheld flashlight with a tight long-range beam** for navigating wide plazas and streets.
- Warm white or amber light may cut through dust better than blue-white.
- Bring spare batteries in sealed, dust-proof cases. Dust will get into everything.
- A **thermal camera** is extremely useful. Some plants give off a faint heat-glow, and gas vents are hot. It can help you see through the haze and avoid hidden openings.
- Do not use open flames or flares. Sulfur dust and dry vegetation may be flammable or explosive. Use intrinsically safe, spark-free equipment.

---

## 6. Sound and communication

The sound is the loudest thing. It comes from everywhere: wind moaning in colonnades, hissing through dry leaves, gas vents erupting with tearing roars, pods bursting, and beneath it all a deep organ note from the earth breathing. The city is built to catch and amplify this sound.

- Wear **high-attenuation earplugs** or electronic noise-canceling headsets. The hissing and roaring can cause hearing damage.
- The low-frequency organ note will not be fully blocked by earplugs. You will feel it in your chest and the soles of your feet. This can cause nausea, disorientation, and anxiety.
- Use **bone-conduction microphones** and radios with noise suppression. Shouting is useless.
- Agree on hand signals before descent. Limit time in the loudest areas. If you feel chest pressure, vertigo, or sudden fear, start the ascent immediately.

---

## 7. Medical and emergency planning

Carry a first aid kit designed for:

- **Heat illness** — cold packs, electrolyte gels, emergency cooling blankets.
- **Burns** — the stone, resin, and hot surfaces can cause thermal and chemical burns.
- **Lacerations** — salt and gypsum edges are sharp; wounds will dry and crack.
- **Respiratory irritation** — if your mask fails, your airways will desiccate. Have saline gel for nasal and eye use, but only inside the suit.
- **Eye irrigation** — a sealed emergency eye wash system that works while wearing the mask.

Carry a portable oxygen cylinder. If your suit is breached and you are exposed to the air, leave immediately. Do not try to “tough it out.” The dryness will act fast: your mouth, eyes, and airways will dry within moments.

---

## 8. Conduct and contamination

This is not a place where you can leave anything behind.

- **You are a wet animal in a dry place.** Do not spit, urinate, bleed, or exhale into the environment. Carry all waste out in sealed bags.
- Do not open any container of water below the rim. Drink only through a closed tube inside your suit.
- Do not pick the plants, lean on them, or disturb the swollen pods. They burst with sharp reports and scatter dry spores. The spores are not inert; treat them as hazardous particulates.
- The city is a functioning dry organism, not a ruin. Move through it as a guest. Do not chip stone, take samples, or add marks.
- If you must leave a physical marker, use inert metal or plastic, and remove it on exit. The place will not rot it, so it will remain as an intrusion for a very long time.
- Do not touch the vibrating blank wall at the end of the processional way. It may be a resonance surface or a gas vent. Observe from a distance.
- The spiral plaza is a map of the gas vents. When a new vent opens, a new line of paving is added, though no one is there to add it. Document it, but do not interfere.

---

## 9. Psychological preparation

The emptiness is not peaceful. It is a pressure. The streets are too wide, the plazas too vast, the sound continuous. There are no animals, no insects, no birds. You will hear your own breath echo from a wall a hundred meters away.

- **Do not go alone.** A small team of two or three is best. The place feels built for fewer presences, and a large group may disturb it — or be overwhelmed by it.
- Train in hot, noisy, confined environments before you go. Practice using all gear blindfolded, with noise playing, under heat stress.
- If you are not comfortable in a sealed suit for six hours, do not descend.
- Accept that you will not fully understand the place. Its rules are slightly tilted. Some buildings seem grown rather than built. The city settles, breathes, and sings. Treat it as alive in a way different from animals.

---

## 10. Exit and decontamination

The ascent is more demanding than the descent. Plan for twice the time and water you think you need.

- Rest in the coolest safe spot, but there is no true cool spot.
- Before leaving, **dry-brush or vacuum** your suit and gear to remove dust and spores. Seal contaminated outer layers in airtight bags.
- Do not use water for decontamination inside or near the hollow. Water is the wrong tool here.
- Move to a clean area, such as your transport or a sealed shelter at the rim, before removing your suit.
- After exit, rehydrate slowly, monitor for heat strain, and inspect skin for salt burns, cracks, or irritation.
- Shower only after you are sealed away from the hollow. Your own wetness is normal again.

---

## Final checklist

- Fully sealed vapor-proof suit with integrated gloves, boots, and hood
- PAPR or SCBA with acid-gas and particulate filters; spare breathing gas bottle
- Multi-gas detector for SO₂, H₂S, CO, O₂, flammables
- Active cooling garment or phase-change cooling packs
- Hydration system (6+ liters minimum for a few hours)
- Thick-soled, puncture-resistant, heat-insulating boots
- Climbing harness, rope, descender, anchors, carabiners
- Helmet, full-face mask, headlamp, handheld flashlight, spare batteries
- Earplugs or electronic noise-canceling headset, bone-conduction radio
- Probe pole, bright marker tape, reel of line or inertial navigator
- First aid kit with burn treatment, eye irrigation, and emergency oxygen
- Waste bags, dry decontamination kit, sealed sample containers if needed
- High-energy food in sealed pouches
- Emergency beacon and scheduled check-in protocol

---

If you prepare meticulously, you may be allowed to stand in the central plaza and hear the hollow breathe — a low, hot, dim, dry pocket of earth where the city is empty and the sound never stops. But remember: the most dangerous thing you carry is your own water. Keep it sealed, keep yourself cool, and treat the place not as a destination to be conquered, but as a living machine that will continue sighing long after you climb back to the salt plain.
