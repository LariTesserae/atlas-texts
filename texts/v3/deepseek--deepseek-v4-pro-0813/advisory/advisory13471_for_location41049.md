Prepare for the plateau as if you are going to a high-altitude bog that is also a live electrical plant and a slow temporal accident. You are not going to a landscape; you are going inside a continuous, wakeful, living industrial body. Your own body is still an ordinary human body—soft, waterproof only in patches, easily chilled, easily lost, easily aged. The plateau will not care.

---

## 1. Understand what you are walking into

Before packing a single piece of gear, adjust your mental model. This is not rock, soil, and weather. The ground is a living mat several metres thick, saturated like a sponge. The structures are not buildings; they are organs. The engines are alive. The fog is food. The stone beneath has already finished all the erosion it will ever know, and the life above is manufacturing itself out of water, electricity, and perhaps time.

You should expect:

- Constant wetness.
- Constant noise: hissing steam, low mechanical pulses, ticking growth.
- The smell of ozone, wet iron, and anaerobic peat.
- A sky so dim and close that it seems like a ceiling.
- A ground that may change shape overnight.
- The feeling that you are a temporary inconsistency in a process that has been running forever.

If you go there treating it only as a weird wilderness, you will make mistakes. Go as a careful visitor inside a living body.

---

## 2. Physical preparation

Your ordinary human body needs conditioning. You will be walking on a saturated, quivering surface that presses water up around your ankles with every step. Each step costs more energy than normal walking. You may be carrying a heavy pack. The air is cold, wet, and possibly thin at high altitude.

Train for:

- Long hikes in cold rain.
- Walking through bogs, mudflats, snow, or deep wet ground.
- Carrying 20–30 kg while maintaining balance on unstable surfaces.
- Navigating in mist or whiteout.
- Breathing comfortably during sustained exertion in cold air.

If you can acclimatize to high altitude before departure, do so. Even if the plateau’s altitude is not extreme enough to cause severe altitude sickness, the cold and humidity will reduce your comfort and endurance. Cardiovascular fitness is less important than the ability to stay warm while moving slowly, because the terrain will force you to move deliberately.

Also prepare your skin. Wet conditions cause maceration, blisters, fungal infections, and slow-healing wounds. Before departure, trim nails, treat any athlete’s foot or skin irritation, and bring barrier cream. If you have any open wound, delay travel. The plateau is wet and biologically active; infections may behave strangely there.

## 3. Clothing and personal wear

Your clothing must keep you warm, dry, visible, and alive without becoming a hazard around electrical fields.

### Layering system

- **Base layer:** merino wool or synthetic, long-sleeved, fitting close. No cotton.
- **Mid layer:** high-loft synthetic insulation. Down is wonderful until it gets wet, and this place is wet. Choose synthetic.
- **Outer shell:** waterproof and breathable, with sealed seams, a storm hood, and cuffs that seal over gloves. It should be loose enough to trap warm air but not so loose it catches on the mat or the growing structures.
- **Legs:** waterproof overtrousers or wading trousers. You may be walking through knee-deep mist and saturated ground. Your lower body will be wet often.
- **Boots:** insulated, waterproof boots with good ankle support. They must be broken in but not softened by water. Bring waterproof gaiters to keep water from entering the tops.
- **Footgear for bog:** consider snowshoe-like or bogshoe attachments to spread your weight. The mat is alive and fragile; you do not want to punch through it. Wide footgear also keeps you from sinking too deeply.

### Hands and head

- **Gloves:** bring thin liner gloves plus insulated waterproof mitts or gloves. You need dexterity for gear, but you also need warmth.
- **Neck gaiter and balaclava:** your breath will become a small personal weather system. Protect your face and neck.
- **Hat:** insulated, close-fitting, under the hood.
- **Goggles:** anti-fog, wrap-around, with polarized or photochromic lenses. The air is dim, but there may be sudden blue static discharges. Eye protection also helps against spores, steam, and wind.
- **Respirator:** a fit-tested P100 or N100 mask with organic vapor cartridges. The air carries ozone, wet iron, anaerobic gases, spores, and possibly more. Do not breathe raw plateau air for long.
- **Ear protection:** earplugs that reduce the constant industrial hum while still letting you hear changes. The noise is not deafening like a city, but it is relentless. Bring both foam plugs and a way to keep them clean.

### Electrical considerations

The engines produce blue static discharge, electrical fields, and ground currents. Your normal outdoor clothing may build static. Avoid wearing large metal items. Use natural or anti-static fabrics. Insulated boots protect you from ground current, but they can also cause static build-up. Carry a small static detector if possible. If your hair stands on end or you hear crackling, move away from the area.

Do not wear long trailing straps, metal carabiners, or conductive jewellery near engines. If you must pass near one, keep a wide distance, move slowly, and avoid touching anything.

## 4. Shelter and camping

Your tent is your only safe dry space. Choose it carefully.

- Use a **freestanding tent**. The mat is soft and alive; stakes will not hold well, and driving them into the living ground is both destructive and unreliable.
- Bring **snow or sand anchors** instead of regular stakes. Bury them in the mat or use deadman anchors.
- Use a waterproof **groundsheet** under the tent, but expect the mat to grow over it overnight. In the morning, peel it off gently.
- Use a sleeping pad with high insulation value. The mat is cold and wet; it will suck heat from you.
- Use a synthetic sleeping bag rated for cold, damp conditions.
- Bring a bivy sack as backup.

### Choosing a campsite

- Stay **far from engines**—at least a few hundred metres if possible. Their steam vents, electric fields, and falling debris can kill you.
- Stay **far from hollow bowls** where the mat grows inward. Animals avoid them. You must too.
- Avoid **exposed mirror-like black rock**. Its reflections are wrong, and standing on it for long may not be safe.
- Avoid **channels** that carry water. They may be dry by noon and flooded by evening.
- Avoid **vegetation domes** that push up overnight. They burst and release gas and seed.
- Look for a relatively open, flat area on the mat, away from obvious animal trails but not inside a negative space.
- If you see animal tracks or grazers avoiding a place, do not camp there.

### Sleeping

The plateau is loud and active. Use earplugs. Keep a headlamp with red light inside the tent. Keep boots and warm layers inside the tent with you, not in the vestibule, because the mat may grow around them or water may reroute overnight.

In the morning, check the area before stepping out. The landscape may have changed. New domes may have formed. A water channel may now run beside your tent. Move camp if necessary.

## 5. Navigation and communication

Do not rely on any single navigation system. The plateau disrupts cause and effect, and its electrical and temporal anomalies may interfere with electronics.

### Navigation tools

- **Inertial navigation unit** if you have access to one. This is your best primary tool because it does not rely on external signals.
- **GPS** as backup, but expect it to fail or give false readings near engines, bowls, and exposed rock.
- **Compass** as a tertiary tool, but be aware that magnetic fields near engines may be disturbed.
- **Altimeter** and **pedometer** for dead reckoning.
- **Notebook and pencil** for recording bearings, distances, and sound signatures.

Visibility is low. Engines are widely spaced. Landmarks may change. Use multiple methods to keep track of your position.

### Marking your route

- Carry **telescoping marker poles** with bright flags or lights. Place them as you move. The mat may overgrow them, so check behind you frequently.
- Use **flagging tape** on living structures only if you must, but do not damage them. Better to use freestanding poles.
- Use **audio landmarks**: each engine has its own hum, thrum, or clank. Record them mentally or on a small audio device. Sound may reach you before its cause, but the signature can still help you orient.
- Do not trust pools or rock reflections for navigation. They may show another sky or another time.

### Communication

- Carry a **high-powered radio** with spare batteries, but expect interference.
- Carry a **whistle** for short-range signalling.
- Carry a **signal panel** or bright cloth for visual signalling.
- Carry an **emergency beacon** with a dead-man switch if possible. Pre-arrange check-in times. If you miss a check-in, extraction should be triggered.
- If you have a team, stay within line of sight or sound. Use tethers in thick mist if necessary.

## 6. Food, water, and hygiene

### Water

Water is everywhere, but almost none of it is safe.

- Do not drink from pools, especially pools that reflect a sky from another hour or a sun that is not there.
- Do not drink from the mat directly.
- Collect rain or steam if you can, but purify it anyway.
- Boil all water. Then filter it. Then treat it chemically if possible. The water may contain anaerobic microbes, iron, tannins, and other compounds.
- A small distiller is ideal if you can carry one.
- Insulated bottles prevent water from freezing or becoming too cold.

### Food

- Bring high-calorie, low-smell rations. You need energy for cold, wet, and difficult walking.
- Do not eat local flora or fauna. The plant chemistry here is not standard biology, and grazers may have metabolisms that are unsafe for you.
- Use a stove only on a fireproof base. The mat may release methane or other flammable gases. Open flame could ignite a pocket.
- Eat inside your tent or in a sealed shelter to avoid attracting swarms or larger animals.

### Hygiene and waste

- Pack out all solid waste. Use wag bags.
- Urine should be collected and carried out if possible. If you must urinate, do so in a designated container, not directly into the mat.
- Change socks frequently. Dry them inside your jacket. Wet feet break down quickly.
- Use antifungal powder or cream on feet, groin, and anywhere skin stays damp.
- Clean your mask and mouthpiece daily.

## 7. Medical and hazard preparedness

### Medical kit

Bring a full wilderness medical kit plus additions:

- Blister care, moleskin, second skin.
- Wound care, steri-strips, sutures, antiseptic.
- Burn dressings for electrical or steam burns.
- Splint material.
- Thermal blanket.
- Epinephrine and antihistamines for allergic reactions to spores or fauna.
- Broad-spectrum antibiotics and antifungals if your medical protocols allow.
- Eye wash. The air is full of spores, steam, and ozone.
- Gas detector for hydrogen sulfide, methane, and oxygen levels.
- Static detector for electrical fields.
- Personal medications, plus extras.
- A small mirror for self-examination, but do not use the plateau’s mirrors.

### Hazard-specific protocols

#### Engines

- Do not approach within at least 100 metres.
- If you must pass near one, watch for steam vents, falling debris, and blue discharge.
- The sound may change before the event. If the hum deepens or a clank rhythm changes, move away.
- Do not touch any surface of an engine.

#### Hollow bowls

- Identify them from a distance. They are negative spaces where the mat grows inward.
- Mark them on your map and with marker poles.
- Do not enter them for any reason.
- If an animal avoids a bowl, you avoid it too.
- If a companion enters a bowl and begins to look smaller, older, or absent, do not follow. You cannot save them safely. This is one of the worst truths of the plateau.

#### Mirror rock

- Avoid stepping on exposed black rock.
- If you see your reflection in it, look away immediately.
- Do not gaze into the rock. The reflection may show a different sky, wrong horizon, or figures that are not there.
- Use polarized lenses if you must look near it.

#### Vegetation domes

- If you see a dome pushing up from the mat, move away.
- They burst, releasing gas and seed. The gas may be harmful.
- Do not camp near fresh domes.

#### Water channels

- Do not camp in a channel.
- If a channel forms near you, be ready to move quickly.
- Cross channels only with caution; the mat may be weaker there.

#### Storm-state

- When the cloud ceiling lowers, the wind shifts, and the engines roar, the ground may undulate visibly.
- Seek open ground away from engines, bowls, and exposed rock.
- Sit or lie down to lower your centre of gravity.
- Anchor yourself with trekking poles or by bracing against your pack.
- Do not try to navigate during a storm-state. Wait it out.

## 8. Temporal and perceptual hazards

The plateau does not always keep time in order. Sound may arrive before its cause. Pools may show other skies. The mat may metabolise time as well as light.

- Do not trust cause and effect. If you hear a bell before you see the bell, or steam before sound, do not panic. This is local behaviour.
- Do not use pools or mirror rock for navigation, reflection, or timekeeping.
- If you feel sudden nausea, dizziness, or a sense that time is moving strangely, stop and assess. Move away from hollow bowls and exposed rock.
- If you notice your watch, hair, skin, or body changing in a way that seems wrong, leave the area immediately along the same bearing you entered.
- Write your name, age, date, and baseline physical details on a card and keep it in your pocket. If you begin to suspect temporal displacement, compare yourself to the card.
- Do not enter places where the animals do not go. Their avoidance is the best map of temporal danger.

## 9. Mental and emotional preparation

The plateau will make you feel small, temporary, and perhaps unreal. That is an honest response. You are a temporary inconsistency in a process that has been running forever. The trick is not to fight that feeling, but to stay anchored.

Prepare for:

- Long hours of low visibility and relentless noise.
- The feeling of being watched by the ground.
- The urge to touch or enter structures that are clearly alive but look like machines.
- The sense that the landscape is not stable, that the world is being rebuilt around you.
- The fear that you may be corrected at any moment.

Bring small anchoring routines:

- Keep a logbook. Write the date, time, position, and what you observe. If time behaves strangely, the log may help you notice.
- Keep a personal object from home—a coin, a ring, a photo. Touch it when you feel unreal.
- Breathe slowly. Your exhalations will become small clouds, which can be grounding.
- If possible, travel with a companion. Check in verbally at regular intervals. One person’s voice can anchor another.
- Do not try to understand everything. Some things on the plateau will not make sense. Observe, record, and move on.

## 11. Final rules

- Treat the mat as a living body. Do not cut, burn, or break it casually.
- Do not step on mirror rock.
- Do not enter hollow bowls.
- Do not approach engines.
- Do not drink from pools with wrong reflections.
- If the animals avoid a place, you avoid it.
- If the sound precedes its cause, do not trust cause and effect.
- If the ground undulates, stop and anchor yourself.
- If you feel like a temporary inconsistency, remember that is exactly what you are. Move slowly, breathe, and stay grounded.
- Have an extraction plan and stick to it.
- Leave no trace of your own body or camp.

No preparation will make the plateau safe. It is not a place that wants you. It is a place that may tolerate you for a while. Prepare to be tolerated. Prepare to walk lightly, listen carefully, and leave before you are corrected.
