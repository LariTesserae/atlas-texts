# Field Preparation Brief: High Hot Wet Plateau, Escarpment Country

This is not a place you conquer. It is a long-running system of heat, water, growth, and abandoned-but-still-working technology that has reached a kind of equilibrium. The main threats are not predators or altitude. They are heat, humidity, constant wetness, disorientation, and your own curiosity about things that have been running longer than you have been alive.

Prepare as if you are visiting a hot, high-altitude cloud forest with feral infrastructure and mild spatial anomalies. You are an ordinary human. The multiverse may move you there, but it will not change your physiology. The place will not adjust to you.

---

## 1. Understand What You Are Walking Into

The site announces itself through heat first: a greenhouse, saturated heat that makes shade useless. The air is loud — insects, frogs, falling water, and a low mechanical hum from condenser towers. The ground is wet and gives slightly underfoot. Rain comes daily, usually in the middle afternoon. Mist forms at dawn. The night does not cool down meaningfully. The temperature barely drops, and the wet ground leaks heat back into the air.

The landscape is a tilted, terraced plateau at the top of a two-thousand-meter escarpment. It is high, but the heat does not thin with altitude; if anything, it thickens at the rim. The forest is dense but not tall: stunted wide-crowned trees, tree ferns, bamboo-like grass taller than a person, moss on everything, and a constant rain of small debris from the canopy. Streams are slow, tea-dark, and braided. Some are fed by hot springs. The soil is red-brown laterite over clay, so water sits near the surface.

There are built things — stone paths, small shelters, condenser towers — but no visible inhabitants. The technology is not derelict. It is feral and still working. Some paths are longer than they should be. Water pauses on stones. Small animals are slightly wrong in harmless but noticeable ways. The whole place is loud, wet, hot, and almost too alive. It does not hurry. It does not need you.

Your preparation should respect that.

## 2. Physical Conditioning Before Departure

If you have time before travel, spend at least seven to ten days acclimating to high heat and high humidity. Exercise lightly in hot, humid conditions. Get used to sweating without cooling down. If you cannot do this, arrive with the understanding that your first day or two will be physically miserable.

- **Heat tolerance**: Work up to walking for an hour in humid heat with a pack. Learn your sweat rate.
- **Humidity tolerance**: Practice sleeping in warm, damp air. If you cannot, do not expect to sleep well the first night.
- **Foot care**: Walk in wet shoes or damp socks for several hours. Your feet will be wet almost constantly here. Toughen them, but also learn to change into dry socks at rest stops.
- **Cardiovascular fitness**: The terrain is not extremely steep, but the heat and wet ground make every kilometer feel longer. Being generally fit helps.

The altitude itself — roughly two thousand meters above the lowlands — is moderate. If you are coming from sea level, you may feel slight breathlessness on exertion for the first day or two. Do not push hard on arrival. The heat will do more to limit you than the altitude.

## 3. Medical Preparation

Your ordinary human body will be stressed by heat, moisture, and the constant assault of small organisms. Cuts and scrapes will not heal quickly in this environment; they will stay wet and may become infected. Treat every small wound seriously.

### Vaccinations and Prophylaxis
- Bring broad-spectrum protection against insect-borne and waterborne diseases. The exact pathogens may not be known across multiverse boundaries, so carry a general antimalarial or equivalent if your travel clinic provides one.
- Consider prophylactic antifungals if you are prone to fungal skin infections. The constant wetness will encourage them.
- Bring a broad-spectrum antibiotic for wound infections and gastrointestinal illness. Consult a multiverse travel physician for appropriate options.

### First-Aid Kit
At minimum, carry:
- Rehydration salts and electrolyte tablets
- Antidiarrheal medication
- Antihistamines
- Painkillers (anti-inflammatory and non-aspirin)
- Broad-spectrum antibiotic ointment and oral antibiotics
- Antifungal powder and cream
- Blister care: moleskin, hydrocolloid bandages, medical tape
- Liquid bandage or skin glue for small cuts
- Burn dressing and hydrogel for steam or hot-ground burns
- Tweezers and a tick/leech removal tool
- Thermometer
- Multi-gas personal monitor if you plan to approach steam glades
- Small mirror for self-checking and signaling
- Sterile saline or antiseptic wipes

### Heat Illness Prevention
Bring a small wet-bulb thermometer or heat stress monitor. If the wet-bulb temperature approaches 30°C (86°F), stop all exertion. This landscape may exceed that threshold, especially in sheltered hollows.

Learn the signs of heat exhaustion and heatstroke: headache, nausea, confusion, cessation of sweating, hot dry skin. In this humidity, sweat may be everywhere, so do not use sweat as a reliable indicator. If you feel confused or unsteady, stop, rest, drink electrolytes, and cool yourself with water and a fan. Do not push through.

## 4. Clothing and Personal Wear

Dress for permanent moisture. Nothing will stay dry. Choose fabrics that dry quickly and protect you from insects, sharp grass, and sun.

### Base and Mid Layers
- **Shirt**: Lightweight, long-sleeved, quick-drying synthetic or merino blend. Long sleeves protect against insects, sun, and sharp vegetation.
- **Pants**: Lightweight, quick-drying, durable. Avoid cotton. Convertible pants are fine but keep lower legs covered.
- **Underwear**: Quick-drying, anti-chafe. Bring several pairs.

### Outer Layer
- **Rain shell**: A breathable waterproof jacket with large pit zips. It will rain daily, but the heat means you will sweat inside waterproof clothing. Pit zips are essential.
- **Rain poncho or pack cover**: For heavy rain, a poncho can cover both you and your pack. However, it may snag on dense understory. Use a pack cover and dry bags regardless.

### Footwear
- **Primary footwear**: Trail shoes or low boots with aggressive grip and good drainage. Waterproof membranes are not recommended for general use because water will get in over the top and stay there. Non-waterproof, quick-drying shoes are better for wet ground and stream crossings.
- **Insulated/heat-resistant soles**: If you must walk near steam glades or hot ground, bring an additional pair of shoes or boots with heat-resistant soles. Test hot ground with a long stick before stepping.
- **Gaiters**: Tall, breathable gaiters keep mud, debris, sharp grass, and possibly leeches out of your boots.
- **Socks**: Several pairs of quick-drying wool or synthetic socks. Change frequently. Use toe-sock liners if prone to blisters.

### Head and Hands
- **Wide-brimmed hat**: For sun and rain. A hat with a neck cape helps keep rain off your collar.
- **Head net**: Essential for insects. Wear over hat when needed.
- **Gloves**: Lightweight work gloves for gripping wet vegetation, hot stone, and for protection from thorns or sharp grass.
- **Eye protection**: Clear or lightly tinted safety glasses for bushwhacking. Sunglasses for glare off low clouds.

### Insect Protection
- Treat outer clothing, gaiters, and socks with permethrin before departure.
- Bring strong insect repellent for exposed skin. Reapply after rain. High humidity will wash it off, so physical barriers (net, long sleeves) are more reliable.

## 5. Shelter and Camp Systems

Because the ground is wet, possibly hot, and often waterlogged, a hammock is often the best shelter. However, trees may be dense, and not every tree is safe to hang from. A lightweight tent with a bathtub floor and a strong rain fly is a good backup.

### Recommended Shelter
- **Hammock with integrated bug net and rain fly**: Keeps you off the wet ground, allows airflow, and protects from insects. Bring tree straps that do not damage bark.
- **Alternative**: A one-person freestanding tent with a waterproof bathtub floor, strong rain fly, and good ventilation. Do not rely on a tarp alone unless you are very experienced, because ground moisture and insects will be constant.
- **Ground sheet**: A small piece of waterproof fabric to stand on or place gear on when not in the hammock.

### Sleeping Gear
- **Sleeping bag or quilt**: A very light, synthetic summer bag or quilt. The night does not get cold, but you may want a thin layer for comfort.
- **Sleeping mat**: If in a tent, use a closed-cell foam pad with a high R-value not for warmth but for insulation from the hot ground and for moisture isolation.
- **Battery-powered fan**: A small fan can make sleep possible in the hot, still air. Bring extra batteries or a power bank.

### Camp Location
- Avoid camping in narrow valleys where steam or sulfur gases may accumulate.
- Avoid camping directly on hot ground or near steam basins.
- Avoid camping under dead branches or heavy canopy debris.
- If using a built shelter, treat it as if it is someone else’s home. Do not alter it, do not leave trash, and do not stay long. Some shelters contain anomalies; if a shelter feels wrong — a doorway too narrow, a wall reflecting the forest upside down, a corner where the air tastes like cold metal — do not enter. Use your own shelter instead.

## 6. Water and Food

Water is everywhere, but it is not necessarily safe. Streams are slow, tea-dark, and may contain fine filaments from the old paths. Hot springs are mineral-rich and may be scalding or contain volcanic gases. Rain is generally the cleanest source, but it must still be treated.

### Water Collection and Treatment
- Collect rainwater directly in clean tarps, bottles, or cook pots. Do not collect water dripping from leaves unless you filter and treat it, because it may pick up organic material.
- Pre-filter all water through a cloth or coffee filter to remove sediment and filaments.
- Use a high-quality water filter or purifier. Carry backup chemical treatment (chlorine dioxide or iodine) and know how to use it in warm, turbid water.
- Boiling is the most reliable method. Bring a stove that can run in wet conditions. At this altitude, bring water to a rolling boil for at least one minute.
- Do not drink from slow, braided streams without treatment. Do not drink from hot springs.
- Carry enough water capacity for a full day: at least three liters, preferably more. In this heat, you may need four to six liters per day.

### Food
- Bring calorie-dense, no-cook or easy-to-cook foods. Energy bars, dried meals, nuts, and electrolyte powders.
- A small liquid-fuel or canister stove is useful for boiling water and simple meals. Make sure it is safe to use in damp conditions.
- Do not eat local plants, insects, or animals unless you are absolutely certain of their safety. You are not a local.
- Store food in odor-proof bags and hang them away from camp to avoid attracting understory mammals.
- Carry all food waste out. Do not bury or leave anything.

## 7. Navigation and Communication

Navigation in this landscape is complicated by several factors: dense vegetation, low cloud, rain, and spatial anomalies on the old paths. A path that should take one hundred paces may take one hundred and twenty, and the extra steps do not feel like motion. Water moves too slowly for the slope. The place can quietly erode your sense of distance.

### Navigation Tools
- **GPS**: Bring a robust, waterproof handheld GPS with spare batteries. It may work, but do not rely on it exclusively. The hum from the towers and the dense canopy may degrade signals.
- **Magnetic compass**: Bring a reliable compass and know how to use it. The basalt bedrock may cause local magnetic variations, so cross-check against visible landmarks.
- **Maps and aerial imagery**: Carry physical maps or printed satellite images. Mark your route as you go.
- **Altimeter**: Useful for tracking elevation changes, though the terrain is more plateau than steep climb.
- **Pacing and time**: Expect pacing to be unreliable. Use time as a rough guide, but do not panic if a walk takes longer than it should. The path may simply be “more thorough” in some sections.
- **Flagging tape**: Bright, biodegradable flagging tape can help you mark a route. Remove it on your way out. The forest is dense and grows over things quickly, so do not rely on old marks.
- **Signal mirror and whistle**: Carry both for emergencies.

### Communication
- Bring a satellite messenger or equivalent multiverse communication device. Test it before departure.
- The mechanical hum of the towers may interfere with electronics. If your device fails, move to an open clearing or the rim (away from towers) and try again.
- Pre-arrange check-in times with a support team. If you miss a check-in, have a plan for extraction.

### Spatial Anomaly Protocol
If you notice that a path is taking longer than it should, do not stop to measure it. Do not retrace your steps in confusion. Keep moving at a steady pace. Use your compass and visible landmarks. The path is not malicious; it is simply running on a different geometry. If you feel disoriented, stop, eat something, drink water, and ground yourself in the present moment. Then continue.

## 8. The Feral Technology: Towers, Paths, and Shelters

The condenser towers release rings of vapor at dawn. They hum at a frequency that feels like pre-storm pressure. The paths are warm. The shelters are still warm and sometimes contain strange features.

### Rules for the Built Things
- **Do not interfere with the towers.** Do not climb them, touch them, or attempt to repair them. They are working. Observe from a distance. If you are near one when it releases its vapor ring, the pressure may feel unpleasant. Move away if you feel chest tightness, headache, or tinnitus.
- **Do not camp within sight or hum of a tower** if you can avoid it. The constant low-frequency sound may disrupt sleep and cause unease.
- **Stay on the stone paths if possible.** They are stable and warm, and the forest has grown around them. Do not pry up stones or dig into the path.
- **Treat the shelters as attended.** They may be empty, but they are not abandoned. If you must use one, leave it exactly as you found it. Do not place your bedroll in a room with a too-narrow doorway or a cold-metal air taste.
- **Do not attempt to collect or activate any feral technology.** The place has outlived its makers. You are not here to fix it or take it with you.

## 9. Daily Rhythm and Movement

The place has a daily rhythm: mist at dawn, rain at midafternoon, frog song at dusk. The night is loud and warm. The light at noon is clear but gray-white.

### Suggested Daily Schedule
- **Early morning**: Move soon after first light, once the worst of the dawn mist begins to clear. The heat is still building, and the air may be slightly less oppressive. The towers will release their vapor rings; avoid being directly downwind.
- **Late morning**: Continue movement, but slow down. Take frequent breaks in your own shade. A silvered umbrella or reflective tarp can create shade, even if the air itself is hot.
- **Midafternoon**: Expect heavy rain. Either move in rain gear or stop and set up shelter. Use the rain to collect water. The rain may briefly cool the air, but the ground will release heat afterward.
- **Late afternoon**: If the rain clears, the plateau opens up with long sightlines. This can be a good time for navigation and moving through open areas.
- **Night**: Do not travel after dark unless absolutely necessary. The ground is uneven, the streams are hard to see, and the constant frog noise can disorient you. Set up camp before dusk.

### Movement in the Forest
- The understory is dense and layered. There are roots overhead, vines between trunks, moss underfoot, and a constant rain of small debris. Wear your head net and gloves.
- The bamboo-like grass can be taller than a person and sharp. Push through carefully. Do not let it whip back into your face.
- The ground squelches even on slopes. Use trekking poles to probe for deep mud or hot spots. Step on roots and stones when possible, but be aware they may be slick.
- Water wells up around your footsteps. Expect wet feet. Stop periodically to dry and treat your feet.

### Movement on the Escarpment Rim
- The rim drops two thousand meters. The edges are weathered and softened, but they can be crumbly. Stay back from the edge, especially in low visibility.
- The clouds below can create a false horizon. Do not approach the edge when mist obscures the drop.
- If you need a view, use a safe, solid rock platform and secure yourself if you are close to any drop. Do not lean over.

## 10. Specific Hazards

### Heat and Humidity
The greatest hazard. Shade does not help. Sweat does not evaporate well. Your body can overheat quickly, especially while carrying a pack.

**Mitigation**:
- Move slowly. Take frequent rests.
- Carry a reflective umbrella to create your own shade.
- Drink electrolytes, not just water.
- Use a battery-powered fan during rests.
- Monitor wet-bulb temperature. Stop if it is too high.
- Recognize heat illness early.

### Hot Ground and Steam Glades
The ground is too hot for roots in some areas. Steam rises in columns. The air smells of hot clay, sulfur, and wet leaves.

**Mitigation**:
- Avoid steam glades entirely unless you have specific training and equipment.
- If you must cross, wear heat-resistant soles and test each step with a long stick.
- Do not breathe concentrated steam or sulfur gases. A half-face respirator with acid-gas cartridges can help, but it is heavy and hot. Better to avoid the area.
- Do not step on the pale mineral terraces. They are fragile and may be scalding.

### Water and Slippery Ground
The ground is permanently wet. Streams are slow and braided. The rock is slick with moss.

**Mitigation**:
- Use trekking poles.
- Cross streams at wide, shallow braids rather than deep pools.
- Be especially careful on wet stone paths. The warm stones may be slippery.
- If a stream pauses at a stone, do not assume the water is normal. It may be full of fine filaments from the paths. Do not drink it untreated.

### Insects and Small Organisms
The air is thick with dragonflies, moths, beetles, and small flying things. Some are slightly wrong in harmless ways. Frogs call from every pool. Mammals are heard but rarely seen.

**Mitigation**:
- Wear long sleeves, pants, and a head net.
- Use permethrin-treated clothing and repellent.
- Sleep under a bug net.
- Check your body for ticks, leeches, or other attached organisms at the end of each day.
- Do not handle the strange animals. Observe and move on.

### Spatial and Temporal Anomalies
Paths can be longer than they should be. Water moves too slowly. Droplets cling to leaves a half-second longer than they should. The whole place feels like it has been running so long that it has developed quirks.

**Mitigation**:
- Do not fixate on the anomalies. They are not alarming, but they can erode your sense of time and distance.
- Stick to a routine. Use a watch. Note the time when you start and finish a leg.
- If you feel disoriented, stop, eat, drink, and rest. Do not make navigation decisions while confused.
- Trust that the path will eventually get you where it goes. If you are truly lost, stay put and signal for help.

### Loud Noise
The continuous many-layered roar — insects, frogs, falling water, mechanical hum — can be mentally exhausting. It may interfere with hearing hazards.

**Mitigation**:
- Bring earplugs for sleep. Use silicon or wax earplugs that stay in place despite humidity.
- During the day, accept the noise. Do not try to tune it out completely; you need to hear changes in the environment.
- If the noise becomes overwhelming, take a break in a quieter spot, such as an open clearing or the rim (away from towers).

## 11. Mental Preparation

This place is not hostile, but it is indifferent. It is crowded, layered, near-touching, and yet huge. The sky is large and low. The next escarpment is far away. The heat does not ease at night. The frogs do not stop. The towers glow faintly.

You may feel:
- **Claustrophobic despite open sky**: The vegetation is dense, the air is thick, and the noise is constant.
- **Unsettled by the anomalies**: A path that takes extra steps, water that pauses, animals that are slightly wrong.
- **Overwhelmed by the heat and wetness**: Nothing dries. Your clothes, your skin, your gear.
- **A sense of being watched**: The shelters are still warm. The paths are warm. The technology is working. It feels attended.

These feelings are normal. The place has been running for a very long time and plans to remain exactly so. You are a temporary visitor. Do not try to change it. Do not try to fix it. Do not take souvenirs.

### Grounding Techniques
- Keep a small journal and write in it daily. Record the time, weather, location, and how you feel. This helps maintain a linear sense of time.
- If you feel unreal or disconnected, focus on physical sensations: the texture of stone, the heat of the path, the weight of your pack.
- Eat and drink on a regular schedule. Low blood sugar or dehydration can amplify disorientation.
- If traveling with others, talk about what you are experiencing. Do not isolate yourself in your own head.

## 12. Leave No Trace and Ethics

This is not a pristine wilderness. It is a long-running system of stone, water, plant, animal, and machine. It is in equilibrium. Your presence is a disturbance. Minimize it.

- **Do not cut or damage vegetation** unless absolutely necessary for safety.
- **Do not remove stones, artifacts, or biological samples** from paths, shelters, or terraces.
- **Do not disturb the condenser towers** or any other working technology.
- **Do not feed or handle animals**.
- **Do not enter hot spring terraces** or damage thermophilic bacteria mats.
- **Carry out all solid waste**. The ground is too wet for catholes, and the system is too slow to process human waste.
- **Carry out all trash**, including food waste.
- **Do not build new structures** or rearrange existing ones.
- **If you use a shelter, leave it exactly as you found it**.

## 13. Extraction and Emergency Plan

This is not a place where you can call for help easily. The terrain is difficult, the heat is dangerous, and the anomalies can complicate rescue.

- **Pre-arrange an extraction window.** Do not enter without a way out.
- **Carry a personal locator beacon or multiverse transit anchor.** Keep it waterproof and easily accessible. The hum from the towers may interfere; test it at intervals.
- **If electronics fail**, move to an open area or the rim (away from towers) and try again.
- **Have a fallback signaling plan**: signal mirror, whistle, bright cloth, and possibly a small strobe light.
- **If someone is injured or overheated**, stop, treat them, and call for extraction. Do not try to push through.
- **If you are lost or disoriented**, stay put. Find a safe spot away from hot ground and water. Use your whistle and mirror. Do not wander.

## Closing Note

You are not preparing for a fight. You are preparing for a long, hot, wet walk through a place that has already made up its mind about how it wants to be. It will be loud. It will be exhausting. It will be strange in ways that do not announce themselves.

Move slowly. Drink water. Rest often. Do not touch the machines. Do not sleep in the wrong shelters. Let the path be longer if it needs to be. Let the water pause.

The place will not harm you if you do not demand that it make sense. But it will test your patience, your body, and your bearings. Prepare for that, and you may come back with a true sense of what it means to visit a long-running system that has outlived its makers and intends to remain exactly so.
