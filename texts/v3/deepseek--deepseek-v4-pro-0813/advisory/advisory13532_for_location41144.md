This is not a tourist cave. It is a working underground industrial room with a resident ecosystem, and your preparation should treat it as a confined-space industrial site with serious biological, thermal, and mechanical hazards. You will be hot, wet, deafened, and surrounded by animals. The most important thing is to keep yourself from becoming part of the machinery or the food chain.

---

## 1. Before You Go

### Understand the environment
- **Heat:** Air temperature near body temperature, extremely high humidity. Your sweat will not evaporate, so your body cannot cool itself effectively. This is a heat-stress environment even if it does not feel scorching.
- **Wetness:** Warm air condenses on cool rock, walls, and railings. Every surface is slick. Water drips from overhead. The floor may be covered with a thin film of water or slime.
- **Noise:** Continuous layered industrial noise — transformers, pumps, steam, fans, bats. You will need hearing protection and a way to communicate without removing it.
- **Dim amber light:** Low-pressure sodium lamps, red gauge lights, occasional welder flashes. Large areas fall into near-blackness. Your eyes will struggle to adapt.
- **Biology:** Bats, rats, midges, cave crickets, cockroaches, eyeless fish, mold, algae, guano, ammonia. This is not a sterile place.
- **Industrial activity:** Steam lines, electrical cabinets, sluice gates, pumps, scaffolding, unset concrete, rebar, open grates. Machinery may be live, hot, or both.

### Obtain local permission and a guide
Do not enter alone. You need someone who knows:
- Which pipes are hot or pressurized.
- Where the live electrical hazards are.
- How the water level behaves after surface rain.
- How to read the local alarms and signals.
- Where the bats roost and how to avoid disturbing them.
- The quickest safe route back to the surface.

If you are an ordinary human from another universe, assume your immune system has no memory of the local microorganisms. Treat every animal bite, scratch, or exposure to guano as potentially serious.

### Check the surface weather
Rain on the surface raises the water level below, slowly but steadily. If rain is forecast, reconsider the visit or keep it very short. The first law of the place is: **do not block the sump grates.** The second is: **if the water starts rising, you leave early.**

## 2. Physical Conditioning and Acclimatization

You do not need to be an athlete, but you should be comfortable with:

- **Heat and humidity:** Spend time in a sauna, steam room, or hot shower before the visit. Learn how your body reacts to high humidity heat. Know the early signs of heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea, clammy skin, muscle cramps.
- **Uneven and slippery surfaces:** Practice walking carefully on wet metal grating, polished concrete, and loose boards. Good ankle strength and balance reduce your risk of falls.
- **Wearing PPE for extended periods:** A respirator, safety glasses, hearing protection, hard hat, and coveralls will make you hotter and less mobile. Practice moving while wearing them.
- **Mild claustrophobia:** The space is not tiny, but it is enclosed, dark, loud, and full of movement. If you are prone to panic in caves or industrial basements, prepare mentally or do not go.

### Hydration before arrival
Begin hydrating 12–24 hours before. Drink water with electrolytes, not just plain water. Avoid alcohol and heavy meals before entering. Your body will lose fluid through sweating even if it does not feel like sweating because the air is already saturated.

## 3. Clothing

### Base layer
- **Moisture-wicking synthetic or thin merino wool.** Avoid cotton — it will soak through, stay wet, chafe, and pull heat away or hold it against you in unpredictable ways.
- Long sleeves and long pants. No exposed skin if possible.

### Outer layer
- **Lightweight, durable long-sleeved coveralls or a work suit.** Choose a fabric with some abrasion resistance and, ideally, flame-resistant properties if you will be anywhere near the boiler hall or welding.
- High collar and tight cuffs to keep insects out.
- Avoid loose flaps, drawstrings, or dangling straps that can catch on machinery.
- Tuck pants into boots or use gaiters to prevent cockroaches, crickets, and rats from running against your skin.

### Boots
- Waterproof or highly water-resistant.
- Slip-resistant soles rated for wet and oily surfaces.
- Steel or composite toe — there is rebar, machinery, and falling debris.
- Electrical hazard rating if possible, because water and electricity mix.
- Ankle support is helpful on uneven grating and concrete.
- Break them in before the visit. Wet boots almost always cause blisters faster.

### Gloves
- **Nitrile-coated work gloves** as a base for grip and water resistance.
- **Leather work gloves** over them for rough surfaces and general protection.
- **Heat-resistant gloves or gauntlets** if you will be anywhere near steam lines or boiler equipment. Assume every exposed pipe is hot.
- Bring one spare pair in a sealed dry bag.

### Head protection
- Hard hat with a chin strap. Low ceilings, scaffolds, rebar, and falling droplets of condensation are all possible.
- Mount a headlamp to the hard hat if possible.

## 4. Respiratory Protection

The air is not safe to breathe casually for long periods. It contains:

- **Ammonia** from bat guano.
- **Fungal spores** from guano, mold, and decaying organic matter.
- **Particulate matter** from concrete dust, rust, insect cases, and welding smoke.
- **Steam and mist** that can carry dissolved irritants.

### Minimum
- A fit-tested **N95 or better particulate respirator**. This will handle dust and many spores but not ammonia gas.

### Recommended
- A **half-face elastomeric respirator with P100 particulate filters and ammonia/amine gas cartridges**.
- Ensure the mask is fit-tested and you have no facial hair breaking the seal.
- Bring spare cartridges and pre-filters in a sealed bag. High humidity can shorten filter life and cause them to load with moisture.
- If you wear a full-face respirator, it also protects your eyes from ammonia and midges, but it will fog quickly in this humidity unless you have excellent anti-fog or a powered air-purifying system.

### Practical note
Breathing through a respirator in hot, humid air is exhausting. You will feel like you are not getting enough air, even if you are. This is normal but can trigger panic. Practice wearing the respirator while doing light exercise in warm conditions before the visit.

---

## 5. Eye and Face Protection

- **Safety glasses or goggles** with anti-fog coating are essential.
- The air is full of small wings, midges, mist, and occasional droplets from above. You will want sealed or wrap-around eye protection.
- If you are near welding, use a welding-specific face shield or appropriate shade. Do not look at the arc without protection.
- A **fine mesh head net** may be useful if you must stand near a lamp where midges rise in dense columns. It keeps them out of your mouth, nose, and eyes without blocking too much light.
- If you wear a half-face respirator, safety goggles that seal against the face are better than open glasses because ammonia can irritate the eyes.

---

## 6. Hearing Protection

The sound is continuous and layered. Even a few hours may cause temporary or permanent hearing damage.

- Use **earplugs with a high Noise Reduction Rating**, at least NRR 28–33.
- Or **earmuffs** over earplugs if you are near the pumps or transformers.
- If you need to communicate by radio, consider **electronic hearing protection** that amplifies speech but blocks harmful noise.
- Bring spare earplugs. They get lost or wet.

### Communication
In this noise, shouting is useless. Agree on hand signals with your guide before entering. If using radios, use earpieces and microphones that work with hearing protection. Keep radio volume low enough to avoid adding to the noise load but high enough to hear.

---

## 7. Hydration and Nutrition Inside

### Hydration
- Bring **electrolyte fluids**, not just water. Use insulated bottles or a hydration bladder with a bite valve.
- Drink small amounts frequently, even if you do not feel thirsty. In high humidity, your thirst mechanism may not keep up.
- A hydration bladder hose must be compatible with your respirator. You may need to lift the mask briefly to drink, which is risky near guano or ammonia. If you must lift the mask, move to a relatively clean area, hold your breath, drink, and reseal.

### Food
- **No food left out.** The rats will find it, and the rules of the place are clear.
- Eat only in the canteen or a designated safe area, and only if allowed.
- Bring sealed, high-energy snacks like energy bars or gels. Avoid crumbly, aromatic foods that will attract animals.
- Clean up every crumb and wrapper. Pack out all trash.

### Cooling aids
- Because evaporative cooling does not work, you may want **phase-change cooling vests, cold packs** for neck and wrists, or a simple damp cloth. Do not rely on a wet bandana around the neck as a cooling method; it will only make you wetter and more uncomfortable.
- Cold packs are especially useful for the back of the neck, armpits, and groin in a heat emergency.

---

## 8. Lighting and Navigation

### Lighting
- **Headlamp with a red-light mode** for use near bats. White light will disturb them.
- **Handheld flashlight** with a strong beam for far corners, the sump, and the dark end.
- **Backup light** — a small keychain light or second flashlight — in a sealed bag.
- Spare batteries in waterproof containers. Humidity and heat can drain batteries quickly.
- Avoid strobe or flashing lights, especially near bats or workers.

### Navigation
- The place is small but confusing. Steam, mist, and dim amber light erase depth perception.
- Mark your route mentally: the corkscrew ramp is your primary exit. Note its direction from every point.
- If allowed, use reflective tape or chemical light sticks as temporary markers, but do not place anything on or near sump grates.
- A physical map or written instructions are better than a phone, which may overheat, fog, or die in the humidity.
- A compass is not useful underground unless you understand the local magnetic environment, but a simple direction log can help.

## 9. Safety Around Machinery and Structure

### Rules to follow absolutely
- **Do not touch any pipe, valve, gauge, switch, cable, or electrical cabinet unless your guide explicitly says it is safe and you are authorized.**
- Assume all metal surfaces are hot, live, or slippery.
- Do not lean on railing near steam lines. A steam leak may be invisible and can cause severe burns.
- Watch for trip hazards: cables, hoses, scaffolding boards, rebar, open hatches.
- The far concrete floor may not be fully set. It gives slightly and takes a print. Step carefully.
- Never place tools, bags, or equipment on sump grates. Blocking a grate is a violation of the first law and could cause the water to rise and kill machinery and people.

### If water begins rising
- Note the rate. The water rises slowly, over days, not hours, but local conditions can change.
- If you hear alarms, see water over the floor, or notice the pumps stopping, leave immediately.
- Do not wait to see if it recedes. The exit ramp is only thirty meters up. You can be out in minutes.

### Heat sources
- Some corners are ovens because buried steam lines run close to the surface. Test a surface with the back of your hand before stepping or kneeling. Better yet, avoid unknown warm areas.
- The furnace door can open and throw heat and light. Do not stand in front of it unless you are a trained worker.

## 10. Biological Hazards

### Bats
- **Leave them alone.** They are the reason the midges are tolerable.
- Do not shine white light on the roost. Use red light if you must look.
- Do not make sudden loud noises. The bats may take flight in a body, creating a leathery rush that drowns everything and fills the air with guano dust.
- Stay away from the area below the roost. The concrete is slick with guano, ammonia, and shed insect cases.
- If a bat is on the ground, do not touch it. Report it to your guide. A grounded bat may be sick.
- If bitten or scratched, wash the wound immediately with soap and water and seek medical care. Rabies is a real risk in many bat populations.

### Rats
- Do not feed them. They are tolerated, not encouraged.
- Avoid rat droppings and urine. Do not touch surfaces where rats nest.
- Wash hands thoroughly before eating or drinking, even if you wore gloves.

### Insects
- Midges will fly into your mouth, nose, and eyes if you let them. Use a head net if stationary near lamps.
- Cave crickets and cockroaches are unsettling but generally harmless. Do not crush them with bare hands; they may carry pathogens.
- Check your clothing before leaving. Cockroaches and crickets may hitchhike.

### Fish and water
- The water under the grating is not clean. It may contain sewage, industrial runoff, or biological contamination.
- Do not touch it, wade in it, or drink it.
- The eyeless fish are part of the ecosystem. Do not disturb them.

### Guano and ammonia
- Ammonia levels near the roost may be high enough to irritate eyes and lungs. Wear respiratory and eye protection.
- Do not kick or disturb guano. Disturbing it releases spores and ammonia into the air.
- If you smell ammonia strongly through your respirator, the cartridges are saturated or the seal is bad. Leave the area and replace them.

## 11. First Aid and Emergency Kit

Bring a small waterproof first aid kit containing at least:

- Blister dressings and moleskin
- Antiseptic wipes and wound cleaner
- Sterile gauze and bandages
- Medical tape
- Burn gel or hydrogel dressings
- Sterile saline eye wash
- Antihistamines for bites and allergic reactions
- Oral rehydration salts or electrolyte tablets
- Glucose tablets or fast sugar
- Instant cold packs
- Tweezers for splinters and insect parts
- A space blanket — not for cold, but to sit on or use as a ground cover in a clean area
- Personal medications
- A small emergency whistle
- A small notebook and pencil for noting symptoms or times

### Heat emergency recognition
- **Heat exhaustion:** heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea, clammy skin, muscle cramps.
- **Heat stroke:** hot dry skin, confusion, fainting, rapid pulse, nausea. This is a medical emergency. Call for help and begin cooling immediately while moving toward the surface.

In this environment, moving to a cooler area means **going up the ramp toward the surface**. Start early. Do not wait until you are unable to walk.

## 12. Psychological and Sensory Preparation

This place will overwhelm you. That is normal.

- **Heat** feels like a wet blanket that rests on you. You may feel like you are swimming through the air.
- **Smell** is a mixture of wet rock, machine oil, steam, guano, ammonia, and decay.
- **Sound** is never quiet. Even the silences are full of clicks, drips, and splashes.
- **Movement** is everywhere: bats stir, rats run, insects crawl, water flows.
- **Light** is dim and amber, with sudden blue flashes from welding and red points from gauges.
- **The far end** disappears into black where the roof dips down. You may feel like the darkness is watching you.

### Coping strategies
- Focus on small tasks: walk carefully, breathe slowly, follow your guide.
- Do not remove your respirator to “catch your breath” in an unsafe area. Move to a cleaner area first.
- If you feel panic, stop moving, plant your feet, and take slow breaths through your nose if possible. Signal your guide that you need a moment.
- Remind yourself that every strange thing has an explanation: the heat is from the thermal main, the fish lost their eyes in the dark, the sound is a pump impeller.
- But also accept that the sum of it is something else, a place still deciding what it will become. You are a visitor in that process.

## 13. Exit and Decontamination

### Leaving
- Do not rush up the ramp. The heat will lessen as you climb, but your body may still be under stress. Stop if you feel faint.
- Check your clothing and equipment for stowaway insects before exiting.
- If you used disposable coveralls, remove them inside-out and seal them in a heavy-duty trash bag.
- Wipe down boots, tools, and gear with a disinfectant or soapy water before leaving the immediate area.

### After exit
- Shower thoroughly with soap as soon as possible. Wash your hair.
- Put all clothing in a sealed bag and wash it separately in hot water if possible.
- Clean and dry your boots inside and out. Humidity encourages mold.
- Clean your respirator, replace filters if needed, and store it dry.
- Rehydrate and eat something salty.
- Monitor your health for several weeks. Watch for:
  - Fever, cough, shortness of breath (possible histoplasmosis or other fungal infection)
  - Skin infections at any cut or scrape
  - Redness, swelling, or pus around a bite
  - Persistent headache or muscle aches
  - Eye irritation or respiratory symptoms that do not improve
- If you were bitten or scratched by any animal, seek medical care immediately and mention the cave, bats, and rats.

---

## 14. The Simple Version

If you remember nothing else:

- **Wear a respirator, eye protection, hearing protection, hard hat, gloves, and waterproof boots.**
- **Bring a guide.**
- **Do not touch anything.**
- **Do not block the sump grates.**
- **Leave the bats alone.**
- **No food left out.**
- **Hydrate constantly.**
- **If heat or water rises, leave early.**

The place is beautiful in its own way, but it is not kind to the unprepared. Go with respect, prepare for the heat and the dark and the noise and the animals, and you may come back with a changed understanding of what lies beneath the city.
