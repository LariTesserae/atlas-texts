If you are an ordinary human multiverse traveler bound for the Throat-House—called also the Driproom—you should prepare not as if you are going to a building, but as if you are going to be swallowed. Not harmed, not necessarily welcomed, but taken into a warm, wet, noisy, living body made of brick, clay, smoke, and sound. The place is one room, yes, but it is a small world, and it has rules. The best preparation is to become quieter, cleaner, more observant, and more useful than you are used to being.

Below is a practical and intuitive guide for the traveler.

---

## 1. Understand what you are entering

The Throat-House is not a ruin. It is not a tourist site. It is not empty. It is an inhabited, maintained, actively breathing organism of clay and animal life. The first thing to accept is that the room is a **container**—a clay body, an instrument, a stomach. It has survived by being watched, repaired, and listened to.

Its unspoken rules are simple:  
- Nothing enters that is not used.  
- Nothing is wasted.  
- Every sound means something.  

You are an entering thing. Prepare to be either useful or at least harmless. If you cannot be useful, be quiet. If you cannot be quiet, do not go.

---

## 2. Physical preparation

The descent is only a few meters, but the change is immediate. The dry plain above gives way to warm, humid, loud, dense air. You will feel it on your skin, in your chest, in the back of your throat.

### Heat and humidity
The room is almost body-warm, and the air is wet. It is not a sauna, but it is close and clammy. Before you go, spend time in warm greenhouses, animal barns, or a small boiler room. Learn to breathe slowly in air that feels thick. If you are prone to overheating, you will need to move slowly, rest often, and drink more water than you think.

The humidity means sweat does not dry. You will be damp. Accept it. Synthetic “moisture-wicking” clothing is not the answer; it will shed fibers and disturb the room. Natural fibers—wool, linen, cotton—will become damp too, but they belong there.

### Noise
The room is never silent. The water drum thumps irregularly. Birds chirr. Pipes moan. Crickets sing. Geckos click. Baskets creak. The noise is layered and constant, like dusk in a forest but enclosed. If you need total silence to sleep or think, this place will wear you down.

Prepare by spending a night in a room with a dripping tap, a ticking clock, a fan, and a radio at low volume. Learn to let sound become texture. Do not block your ears with plugs unless absolutely necessary, because the sounds of the Throat-House are information. The water drum tells the speed of the water. The birds alarm when something climbs the wall. The pipes speak about the weather above. If you block your ears, you are deaf to the room’s vital signs, and you become a burden.

### Smell
The air smells of wet clay, lamp oil, animal musk, old straw, and mineral damp. It is not a bad smell, but it is strong and alive. If you are sensitive to perfumes or strong odors, you will adapt or you will not. Do not bring perfume, cologne, scented lotion, or strong soap. You would only add an alien layer to the room’s own breath, and some of those chemicals might harm the fish, geckos, or moss.

### Small enclosed space
The chamber is perhaps twenty-five strides across, domed like a beehive. It is not huge, but it is densely filled. There is no distant horizon, no open sky, no blank wall. The floor is patchwork, the walls are covered with niches, baskets, shelves, pipes, and ladders. The ceiling is a shell of reed and brick. If you are claustrophobic, this may be difficult. Practice being in a small, warm, cluttered room with no window, and sit with the feeling of being enclosed. The room feels like the inside of a mouth. It is not sinister, but it is strange. If you panic, sit down, close your eyes, and breathe slowly. The exit is always behind you, up the steps, but do not run.

## 3. Clothing and gear

Every object you bring will become part of the room, at least temporarily. There is no trash service. There is no landfill. There is only the room’s own digestion. So bring almost nothing, and what you bring should be natural, durable, and quiet.

### Clothing
- **Fabric:** Wool, linen, cotton, leather. No synthetics, no Gore-Tex, no polyester, no nylon. These shed microfibers and make unnatural sounds.
- **Colors:** Muted earth tones—browns, grays, undyed fabrics. Bright colors may startle the birds or seem jarring.
- **Layers:** A loose linen or wool shirt, long sleeves to protect against insects and rough surfaces. Durable trousers, not shorts; the floor may have splinters, damp spots, and animal droppings.
- **Head covering:** A light linen scarf or hood can keep dust and moisture out of your hair, and can be pulled over your nose if the smoke or animal smell becomes overwhelming.
- **Footwear:** Soft leather shoes or moccasins that can be removed. The floor is made of fitted clay tile, wood slat, and packed ash-and-clay. Heavy boots may damage it. Ask before entering whether shoes are allowed; if not, have clean feet. Clean your shoes thoroughly before going in, as foreign dirt and seeds are dangerous.
- **Jewelry:** Avoid anything that can snag on ladders, baskets, or hide curtains. Do not wear rings or bracelets that could fall into the water channel.

### Pack
A small shoulder bag of canvas or leather, not a large backpack. You are not going for a long expedition; you are entering one room. What you carry should be minimal:

- **Water:** Bring your own, in a gourd, copper flask, or leather bottle. Water is rationed in the Throat-House. Do not assume the locals will share. Do not drink from the water channel unless you have boiled it first, and ask before doing so.
- **Food:** High-energy, low-waste food: dried meat, dried fruit, parched grain, hard bread. Keep it in a cloth bag. No wrappers, no plastic, no crumbly biscuits that will scatter and attract insects.
- **Bedding:** Ask first. If you must bring something, a wool blanket or a canvas ground cloth is enough. You will not be given a private room. You will sleep in a corner or on a ledge, among the sounds and animals.
- **Tools:** A small knife, a length of hemp or sinew cord, a bone or wood needle and thread. These are useful for mending, and the locals will respect them.
- **Medical kit:** Linen bandages, a small pot of honey or salt, a few healing herbs if you know them. Avoid modern packaging. Honey is a natural antiseptic and a useful gift.
- **Gift:** The best gift is something useful and simple: beeswax, linen thread, salt, a small piece of copper, a length of good cord. Do not bring plastic, electronics, or anything that cannot be repaired. A gift of labor is also acceptable.

### What not to bring
- No plastic water bottles, wrappers, or packaging.
- No electronics—no phone, camera, flashlight, audio recorder, or watch with an alarm. The room has its own light and its own rhythms. Artificial light will startle the geckos and birds.
- No perfume, deodorant, insect repellent, sunscreen, or scented soap.
- No weapons beyond a small knife, and keep that sheathed.
- No musical instruments unless invited. The room already has its own music: water drum, pipes, birds, crickets.
- No food with strong odors or loose crumbs.
- No books or paper. They will mold in the damp. Use memory.

## 4. Biosecurity and health

This is not a place for the careless. The Throat-House is a closed ecosystem. It has its own balance. You are a foreign organism, and you carry a thousand invisible passengers.

### Before entering
Wash your body and hair with a mild, unscented soap. Clean under your nails. Brush your clothes and shoes to remove seeds, spores, and dirt from other worlds. If you have been in another biome recently, change into clothes that have not been worn there. The room’s plants, animals, and people are vulnerable to outside microbes, and you are vulnerable to theirs.

### Zoonotic risks
There are birds, geckos, fish, beetles, crickets, weasel-like animals, and something longer and paler in the dark channels. Do not handle any of them. Do not touch droppings, nests, or eggs. Wash your hands with ash and water if you touch anything dirty. The locals probably do this; watch and imitate.

Bird droppings can carry disease. Geckos may carry salmonella. Weasels may bite if cornered. The snake or eel under the floor may be venomous or not; do not find out. Keep your hands out of dark holes.

### Smoke and air
The charcoal hearth burns slowly. The room is ventilated through the central shaft and pipes, but the air is thick. If you feel headache, nausea, dizziness, or confusion, go up the stairs immediately. Those are signs of carbon monoxide or heat exhaustion. Do not be brave. The exit is only a few meters away.

### Water and food
Do not drink the room’s water unless you have boiled it or the caretakers tell you it is safe. The channel may look clean, but it carries waste from the room. Bring your own water. If you eat local food, be cautious. Your gut is not adapted to their bacteria. Eat small amounts at first.

### Insects and bites
Expect bites. Fleas, mites, crickets, maybe flies. Do not bring chemical repellent; it could kill the geckos and fish. Wear long sleeves, shake out your bedding, and accept that some itching is part of the experience. If you have severe allergies to mold, bird dander, or insect stings, this may not be the place for you.

## 5. Social and cultural preparation

The people of the Throat-House are caretakers, not masters. They move through the room like parts of it: a woman feeds the hearth, a child scrapes algae from the channel, an old man packs fresh clay into a crack, a girl climbs a ladder to check the birds’ nests. Their work is slow and constant.

You are not a customer. You are not a guest in a hotel. You are something that has entered a living body. The locals may ignore you until you prove harmless. Do not expect food, water, or conversation. Bring your own. Offer help by gesture or demonstration, but wait to be shown.

### Rules for behavior
- **Speak very little.** The room is already loud. Your voice is noise. If you must speak, whisper. Better to use gestures and open hands.
- **Do not touch anything without permission.** The carved figures on the walls are nearly blank from centuries of touch. The water channel is cracked and mended. The pipes are tuned with beeswax and stoppers. Your hands could undo a thousand years of care.
- **Do not step in the water channel, the compost corner, or the hearth.** These are working organs.
- **Do not chase, feed, or pet the animals.** They are tenants with duties. The birds carry seeds, the geckos eat flies, the fish clean the cistern, the weasels keep the birds from overbreeding. They are not pets.
- **Do not take souvenirs.** No tile shard, no feather, no eggshell, no moss. The room keeps everything. You leave with nothing but what you carried in.
- **Ask where to sit, sleep, and relieve yourself.** Do not assume. There is no public restroom. The water channel is not a toilet. Ask about a chamber pot or go above to the dry plain and bury waste far from the flagged yard.
- **Do not take photographs or make recordings** unless the caretakers explicitly invite you. The room is not a spectacle. Your memory is better, and it does not disturb the birds.

### Offering help
If you want to be useful, watch first. Learn the rhythm of the work. Then offer yourself by pointing to a task and waiting. You might be asked to fetch water, carry clay, sweep ash, or simply sit and listen. Do not try to repair anything on your own. The room’s technology is old and simple, but it is finely tuned. A small mistake could break the water drum or silence a pipe.

### Gifts
A gift is not required, but it can smooth your entry. Beeswax, linen thread, salt, a small piece of soft copper, or a length of good cord are all useful. Give it quietly, with both hands, and do not expect anything in return. Do not give money, plastic, or anything that cannot be repaired or consumed.

### Privacy and modesty
There is no privacy in the Throat-House. You will be seen, heard, and smelled. The locals are used to this. Be modest but practical. If you need to change clothes, do so quickly and without fuss. If you need to manage menstruation or other bodily functions, bring your own supplies and carry out all waste. Do not leave blood or soiled cloth in the room.

## 6. Arrival protocol

When you come to the dry plain above and see the flagged yard with the shaft opening, pause. Look at the sky—bright, dry, dusty—and know that below you is another world only a few meters down.

Descend the steps slowly. The treads are cupped like shallow spoons, worn by centuries of feet. The lintel is smoke-stained. As you go down, the air will thicken and warm. Stop on the steps for a moment. Let your eyes adjust from bright sun to the diffused light of the oiled hide and mica screen. Let your lungs adjust to the wet, smoky air.

Step lightly at the bottom. Do not stumble into baskets, ladders, or the water channel. The floor is uneven, a patchwork of tile, wood, and packed ash-clay. Keep your hands at your sides or open before you.

If people are present, greet them with a slow nod or open palms. Do not raise your voice. Do not smile too broadly; it is not necessarily a universal gesture. Look at the work being done, not at the people, until they choose to look at you.

Find a spot near the wall, out of the way, and sit. For the first hour, do nothing but listen and watch. The room will reveal its rhythm: the water drum, the birds, the pipes, the small movements of the caretakers. Do not touch anything. Do not try to help yet. Just be small and quiet.

## 7. Night and sleep

At night, the Throat-House does not quiet. The birds settle, but the crickets take over. The water drum continues its slow, irregular beat. The pipes answer the wind. The geckos call from the walls. The embers of the hearth glow under their ash, and the smell of warm clay and animals thickens.

Ask where you may sleep. It will likely be on the floor or a low ledge, not in a bed. Keep your belongings close. The weasel-like animals may steal small shiny objects or food. Store food in a sealed clay pot if one is offered.

Do not expect silence. Do not ask for silence. If you cannot sleep, do not demand that the world stop. Instead, lie still and listen. The water drum may eventually become a kind of pulse, and you may fall asleep not in spite of it but because of it.

If you feel panic at night, the room may seem too close, too warm, too alive. Tell yourself: *I am inside a body. The body is breathing. I am safe.* If the panic does not pass, go up the steps slowly and sit in the dry night air. But do not run. Running in the dark among baskets and ladders is dangerous.

## 8. Emergencies and hazards

The Throat-House is not a dangerous place if you are careful, but it is not a padded room. It is a working organism with fire, water, animals, and tools.

- **Fire:** The hearth is a slow-burning charcoal bed. If a fire starts beyond the hearth, leave immediately. Do not try to fight it with water from the channel; that water is rationed and may not be enough. The exit is the stairs. Use it.
- **Water rhythm changes:** If the water drum stops or changes suddenly, find a caretaker and point to it. This may mean the water source is failing or a pipe is blocked. Do not touch it yourself.
- **Animal bites:** If a weasel, gecko, or snake bites, clean the wound with your own clean water or boiled water. Seek local help; they may have remedies. Do not kill the animal. It is part of the room.
- **Heat exhaustion:** If you feel faint, nauseated, or confused, go up to the surface. Sit in the dry air, sip water, and cool down slowly.
- **Panic attack:** Sit down. Close your eyes. Breathe slowly. Focus on one sound—the water drum, a cricket, your own breath. If you need to leave, leave calmly.

---

## 9. Leaving and aftercare

When you leave, you leave a warm, wet, noisy body and climb back into the dry world. The transition may be jarring. The light will be bright, the air dry, the silence heavy. Go slowly. Let your eyes adjust. Your clothes will be damp and will smell of the room: smoke, clay, animals. That is normal.

Before you leave the flagged yard, check yourself and your gear. Shake out your blanket. Look for stowaways—seeds, insects, eggs, a gecko hiding in your pack. The Throat-House must not spread into other worlds, and you must not bring its life out.

Do not take anything from the room. No shard, no feather, no drop of water. The room is a closed loop. You are only passing through.

Afterward, wash your body and clothes with plain water and sun. Do not bring the room’s microbes to your next destination without cleaning. And know that the rhythm of the water drum may stay in your chest for days. That, too, is normal.

## 10. Packing list — summary

- **Clothing:** Linen or wool shirt, trousers, light scarf, soft leather shoes. Muted, natural colors. No synthetics.
- **Water:** Copper or gourd flask, filled before arrival.
- **Food:** Dried meat, dried fruit, hard bread in a cloth bag.
- **Bedding:** A wool blanket or canvas ground cloth, if needed.
- **Tools:** Small knife, cordage, needle and thread.
- **Medical:** Linen bandages, honey or salt, basic herbs.
- **Gift:** Beeswax, linen thread, salt, or copper.
- **Not to bring:** Plastic, electronics, perfume, insect repellent, weapons, souvenirs, paper.

---

**Final word:** The Throat-House is not a place you conquer or complete. You do not visit it the way you visit a village or a ruin. You enter it the way food enters a mouth. Be clean, be calm, be useful. Listen to the water drum. Watch the birds. Touch nothing unless invited. If you do this, the room may tolerate you—and perhaps, for a little while, you will understand what it means to live inside a small, full, breathing world made of clay and sound.
