**Preparation Guide: The Lifted Karst Plateau**

Before you do anything else, understand what this place is not. It is not a mountain summit. It is not a dry lakebed. It is a high-altitude, ancient seabed that has been lifted, dissolved, fluted, and planed into a kind of final, tuned equilibrium. The wind has been playing the same chord across its limestone flutes for perhaps ten thousand years. The land is finished. Your job as an ordinary human traveler is to move through it without disturbing that chord, without leaving marks, and without being broken by the altitude, the glare, the cold, or the noise. This is not a place for casual tourism. It demands humility, careful planning, and a willingness to be very small.

---

## 1. Altitude and Acclimatization

The first thing that will hit you is the air. The sky is dark blue at noon, the air feels thin behind your eyes, and the temperature never rises much above cool even in full sun. Assume you are somewhere between 4,500 and 5,500 meters in effective altitude. Even if you are fit at sea level, this altitude will slow you, dehydrate you, and can kill you if you ignore the warning signs.

- **Pre-acclimatize if possible.** Spend at least a week at 2,500–3,500 meters, then a few days at 4,000–4,500 meters before entering. If your multiverse portal drops you directly onto the plateau, treat the first 48 hours as a period of minimal exertion. Do not plan any long walking on day one.
- **Carry altitude medication.** Acetazolamide (Diamox) started 24–48 hours before arrival can reduce the risk of acute mountain sickness. Bring dexamethasone as an emergency medication for HACE, and know the symptoms of HAPE (shortness of breath at rest, cough, fatigue) and HACE (confusion, loss of coordination). If any severe signs appear, descend immediately if at all possible and evacuate.
- **Monitor your body.** A pulse oximeter is small and useful. SpO2 in the 80s can be normal at this altitude, but a downward trend with symptoms is serious. Check yourself and your companions frequently.
- **Hydrate aggressively.** The air is thin and the wind is constant; you will lose moisture through breathing and skin without noticing. Drink small amounts often. Electrolyte tablets help offset the mineral taste of local water and prevent hyponatremia.

## 2. Weather, Temperature, and Sun

There is no real “weather” here in the ordinary sense. The altitude is the weather. Daytime air temperature may be 5–7°C in the hollows, but the ceaseless wind can make it feel like -10 to -15°C. At night it drops below freezing, and the plateau sweats as condensation forms on every stone. Some basins freeze at the edges while others hold tadpoles. The sun is ferociously bright, but the air stays cold.

- **Dress in layers.** Start with a moisture-wicking base layer, add a fleece or active insulation, then a windproof softshell, and finish with a waterproof/breathable hard shell for the damp cloud-breath and the nightly condensation. Bring a heavily insulated parka (down or synthetic) for stops and evenings.
- **Protect your extremities.** The wind always comes from the west at the same steady pressure. One side of your body will take the brunt. Wear windproof mitts or gloves with liners, a balaclava, a neck gaiter, and a full hood. Goggles may be necessary if the wind makes your eyes water and the glare is blinding.
- **Sun protection is not optional.** The pale limestone reflects light like snow. Bring category 4 glacier glasses or mountaineering sunglasses with side shields, SPF 50+ sunscreen, zinc-based lip balm, and a wide-brim hat or full-coverage sun hoodie. Cover every patch of skin, including the back of your neck and the tops of your ears.
- **Footwear.** The rock is polished limestone, often glazed with nightly moisture and sometimes black varnish. You will want stiff-soled, high-ankle mountaineering boots with good grip. Light microspikes or traction devices are useful for frozen basins and shaded corridors where ice persists all day. Gaiters keep the fine mineral dust and damp peat out of your boots.

## 3. Shelter and Camping

There are no trees, no walls, no natural overhangs large enough for humans, and no soil to pound stakes into. The wind never stops, and the rock is too hard for ordinary tent pegs.

- **Tent.** Use a 4-season freestanding geodesic or strong tunnel tent built for high winds. Practice pitching it on bare rock using deadman anchors in cracks or by wrapping guy lines around natural pillars. Bring extra guylines, tensioners, and repair sleeves. The constant wind is not gusty, which is a small mercy, but it will press against your tent for hours; weak poles will fail.
- **Campsite selection.** Choose a spot on bare rock away from water pockets, damp moss, and animal trails. A small recess behind a ridge or pillar may reduce the wind slightly, but avoid narrow slots where wind funnels and the noise becomes even louder. Do not camp in a basin that holds water or near the mouth of a damp corridor; you will be wet, cold, and you will contaminate a vital water source.
- **Sleeping system.** Bring a sleeping bag rated to at least -15°C or lower, and an insulated pad with an R-value above 5. A closed-cell foam pad underneath adds insulation from the cold stone and protects the inflatable pad from punctures. The rock is not kind to thin fabrics.
- **Earplugs for sleep.** The sound of the plateau is the first fact of the place. It will fill your tent, your skull, and your dreams. High-fidelity musician’s earplugs will reduce the constant roar while still allowing you to hear a partner or a watch alarm. Set a vibrating alarm so you do not sleep through check-in times.

## 4. Water and Food

Water is present but never abundant. It collects in solution pans, cisterns, shaded slots, and the mouths of damp corridors. It tastes of stone and faintly of moss. It is also home to tiny black frogs, fairy shrimp, caddisflies, and other organisms.

- **Treat all water.** Filter it through a fine filter (0.1 micron absolute) and then purify with UV light or chemical tablets. Boiling at altitude is less efficient because water boils at a lower temperature, but it can still be used as a backup if you boil for several minutes. Better to carry a reliable filter and purification tabs.
- **Carry capacity.** Plan for long dry stretches between water sources. Two to three liters of capacity per person is a minimum. Some of the basins may be frozen or occupied; do not assume water will be available exactly where you expect.
- **Electrolytes.** The local water may be mineral-rich or strangely soft. Bring electrolyte tablets or powders to keep your salts balanced, especially because you will sweat and breathe heavily.
- **Food.** High altitude suppresses appetite. Choose calorie-dense, familiar, easy-to-eat foods: nuts, chocolate, cheese, dried fruit, energy bars, instant soups, and freeze-dried meals with added oil. Do not rely on complex cooking. A remote-canister stove with a windscreen works best; use a liquid-feed or white gas stove if you plan to stay long. Cook in a natural rock alcove, never inside the tent. Pack out every scrap—crumbs attract small animals and leave a mark.

## 5. Navigation and Route-Finding

The plateau is vast, featureless, and disorienting. There are no trails, no cairns, no human structures. The stone ridges run west to east, which helps, but the corridors between them can be twenty feet wide and a hundred feet deep, with undercut walls and hidden pits.

- **GPS and maps.** Carry a GPS with preloaded waypoints and a satellite messenger or personal locator beacon if your home world has such systems. Keep the device warm inside your jacket; batteries die quickly in cold. Bring a solar charger if you can secure it against the wind, but also carry spare batteries.
- **Compass and terrain association.** The wind always comes from the west at the same speed. If your electronics fail, you can orient by the wind and the west-east ridges. The sun is high and bright, but at this altitude shadows are short; use a compass as backup even if you trust your GPS.
- **Travel speed.** Expect to move at half your normal pace, or less. The altitude, the uneven pavement, the deep slots, and the constant wind will slow you. Plan short daily stages—five to eight kilometers is a full day here.
- **Hazards.** Watch for swallow-holes, vertical shafts, and deep solution pans that may be hidden by light or shadow. Use trekking poles to probe ahead if the ground looks suspicious. If you must cross a slot or corridor, look for natural bridges or go around; do not attempt to climb down unless you are roped and experienced.
- **Roped travel?** For an ordinary traveler, the best policy is to avoid vertical terrain entirely. If your route requires entering deep corridors or bypassing pits, bring a lightweight helmet, a 30–40 meter rope, and the knowledge to use it. Otherwise, choose a route that keeps you on the open pavement between ridges.

## 6. Communication and Safety

The sound of the plateau is a sustained, tuned chord—like a turbine hall or ten thousand enormous bees. It never gusts, never fades. It fills every space. A shout does not echo; the fluted walls absorb the frequencies and break them apart. This makes ordinary communication difficult.

- **Radios.** Use hand-held radios with earpieces. Bone-conduction headsets may work better than standard earpieces because they bypass the ear canal, which is already overloaded with low vibration. Prearrange check-in times and keep transmissions short.
- **Visual signals.** Bright clothing, a signal flag, or a mirror can work in the intense sunlight. If you are in a group, agree on hand signals before entering. Whistles may be useless against the roar; do not rely on them.
- **Emergency beacon.** If your multiverse has satellite coverage, carry a personal locator beacon and register it. If not, carry a high-power VHF radio and know the local emergency frequencies. Let someone responsible know your route and expected return time.
- **First aid.** Bring a comprehensive kit: altitude medications, painkillers, anti-inflammatory drugs, blister care, wound closure strips, antiseptic, anti-diarrheal, antihistamine, eye drops for dry and windy eyes, nasal saline, and any personal prescriptions. Add a small roll of duct tape and a multi-tool for gear repairs.
- **Hypothermia and frostbite.** Even at 5°C, the wind and dampness can chill you quickly. Watch for numbness in fingers and toes, uncontrollable shivering, and confusion. Use chemical hand and foot warmers. If someone shows early hypothermia, stop, insulate, feed, and warm them before continuing.

## 7. Waste and Environmental Care

The plateau looks empty, but it is not. It is spaced. The plants are small, old, and tightly pressed into cracks. The animals busy the gaps. The whole system is in equilibrium; it is finished. Your presence is a perturbation.

- **Pack out all solid waste.** There is no soil to bury waste, and the water pockets are fragile. Use WAG bags or a portable toilet system. Do not leave human feces, toilet paper, or food scraps anywhere. Pack them out.
- **Urine.** If you must urinate, do so on bare rock far from water sources, vegetation, and animal paths, or collect it in a container and pack it out. Urine can alter the delicate chemistry of the stone and attract animals to areas they would otherwise avoid.
- **No soap, toothpaste, or lotions near water.** Spit toothpaste into a waste bag. Wash yourself with plain water and a coarse cloth if necessary, away from any basin. Even biodegradable soap can harm fairy shrimp and caddisflies.
- **Stay on rock.** The moss campion, saxifrage, and pale grass are tiny and immensely old. A single footstep can kill a plant that has been growing for decades. Walk on bare limestone pavement whenever possible. If you must step on vegetated ground, choose the most durable surface.
- **Do not disturb the tool sites.** The highland jackdaws maintain flat stone anvils polished white from use. The rock hyraxes keep caches of dried plant matter under slabs. A pale frog digs channels from cistern to lower basin. These are not structures, but they are behaviors repeated over generations. Observe from a distance. Do not touch, move, or add to them.
- **No cairns, no fires, no marks.** The plateau has no human structures. Do not be the first. Do not scratch your name into the varnish, stack stones, or light a fire. There is no wood, and a fire scar on limestone would last centuries.

## 8. Psychological and Sensory Preparation

This may be the hardest part. The plateau is not hostile in the way a storm is hostile. It is indifferent and complete. The same chord blows at dawn, noon, dusk. The same wind presses on your chest. The stone dust lies flat. Nothing changes, and yet everything is moving inside a standing wave.

- **The sound.** It will enter your bones. After a while, your body may stop fighting it, and it becomes a kind of silence. But before that, it can cause auditory fatigue, headache, and a sense of pressure behind the eyes. Use earplugs with flat attenuation when you need to rest your ears. Find small pockets of wind shadow behind pillars or ridges for brief breaks, but do not expect true quiet.
- **The lack of echo.** Your shout will not come back. Your footsteps will sound swallowed. You may feel deaf even while surrounded by a roar. This is normal. Do not shout more to compensate. Speak calmly and sparingly; the noise will carry your voice away instantly.
- **The spacing.** You can walk for twenty minutes without stepping over anything. The plateau does not hide; it simply has almost nothing to hide. This vastness can trigger derealization, a feeling that you are walking inside a painting or a dream. Keep a routine: check your watch, note the wind direction (always west), eat and drink on schedule, and speak to your companion even if the words are lost.
- **The timelessness.** The plateau does not progress. Some basins are freezing while others hold tadpoles and others are dust. Every season is present in pockets. You may feel that time itself has become a closed loop. This is not dangerous, but it can be deeply unsettling. Keep a small journal. Write down the time, your location, and what you see. The act of recording anchors you.
- **Companionship.** If you are alone, the monotony can be heavy. If you can, travel with at least one other person. The simple presence of another human, even in silence, helps.

---

## 9. Ethics and Final Thoughts

This is a place that has already worked out its own truce. The wind that erodes is exactly balanced by the stone’s hardness. The moisture that dissolves is exactly balanced by the plateau’s drainage. The animals time their movements to the same wind. The chord has been blowing for ten thousand years, and the plateau has not needed to change a line of it.

Your goal is not to conquer, discover, or document. Your goal is to pass through like a breath—brief, unnoticed, and leaving no stain. Clean your boots and gear before entering to avoid bringing seeds, spores, or pathogens from other worlds. Do not feed or habituate the animals. Do not collect rocks, water samples, or plants unless you have a clear scientific or cultural mandate.

Respect the inhabitants. The jackdaws, choughs, pikas, marmots, frogs, and hyraxes are not background decoration; they are part of the equilibrium. Their tool use, their alarm calls, their small channels and caches are a kind of technology. Observe, but do not interfere.

Finally, remember that the plateau is not empty. It is spaced. There is a great deal of world between one feature and the next, and all of it is alive. The sound, the wind, the condensation, the lichens, the tiny frogs—all of it is held in a standing wave. You are a visitor in a place that has already become what it is.

Go small. Go quiet. Go briefly. And come back with your ears ringing and your mind strangely still.
