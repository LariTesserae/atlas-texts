**The Sump Exchange: Visitor Preparation for an Ordinary Human Multiverse Traveler**

Assume you are not entering a ruin, a swamp, or a garden. You are entering a small, saturated, self-sustaining engine-room that has been half-digested by vegetation. It does not need you. It may tolerate you. Your preparation should aim to make you as close to invisible as possible: physically safe, biologically contained, and sensorially prepared for a place that is always warm, wet, loud, and slow.

## I. Before You Go: Understand What You Are Entering

The Sump Exchange is not dangerous in the way a collapsing ruin or a predator-filled marsh is dangerous. Its hazards are slower and more cumulative: heat, humidity, poor air in low pockets, live wires hidden in wet vegetation, rotten walkways, exposed machinery, and a constant low-frequency rhythm that can fatigue mind and body.

Key facts to hold in your head:

- Water flows inward from every slope. The whole bowl is a drain.
- There is no dry ground. The floor is a springy mat of roots over water, mud, brick, and old machinery.
- The air is warm, heavy, and full of water vapor, oil vapor, swamp gas, exhaust, and spores.
- The engine runs continuously. The flywheel is exposed. The piston rod moves. The exhaust stack is hot.
- The generator hums. Wires run through branches. Assume any wire may be live.
- Methane may pool in low, still pockets, especially near the sump or the hissing gas line.
- Sound is unbroken. The engine chuffs every few seconds, water drips everywhere, metal ticks, roots creak. There is no true silence.
- Nothing here is abandoned. The chair, the gauges, the throttle root, the handrail of roots—all may be part of a working system. Do not treat them as props.

Your goal is to visit without becoming an input to the cycle, and without taking any part of the cycle away with you.

## II. Physical and Medical Preparation

### Heat and Humidity Acclimation
If you come from a dry or cool world, spend several days before the visit in warm, humid conditions. Saunas, steam rooms, or simply walking in warm rain will help. The Sump Exchange will feel like breathing warm tea. If you are not used to that, you will tire quickly.

### Balance and Uneven Ground
Practice walking slowly on soft, springy, uneven surfaces: moss, sand, rotting logs, bouncy boardwalks. The root floor will give way slightly underfoot. You need to keep your weight on your feet, knees slightly bent, and avoid sudden twists.

### Tetanus and Wound Readiness
The site contains rusted metal, broken glass under moss, old brick, and sharp machinery. Ensure your tetanus vaccination is current. Carry wound-cleaning supplies. Assume any cut you get inside could be exposed to water rich in rot, oil, and microbes.

### Mental Preparation
The place can feel hypnotic. A single-cylinder engine chuffs every three seconds. The rain indoors is constant. The heat wraps around you. Some travelers report losing track of time, or feeling that the machine is breathing through them. Set firm time limits before entering. Decide in advance how long you will stay, and use a timer—not only a wristwatch—because time may feel strange there.

Also prepare yourself for the absence of normal animal sounds. There are no frogs, no birdsong, no insect choir. The place is alive, but its voice is mechanical and vegetative. Some people find this deeply calming; others find it quietly unsettling. Both reactions are normal.

## III. Clothing and Personal Protective Equipment

The rule for clothing is: **cover your skin, stay cool enough to function, and avoid anything that can snag, melt, or conduct electricity.**

### Base Layer
- Use merino wool or a good synthetic moisture-wicking fabric. No cotton. Cotton will soak up water, stay wet, chafe, and make you cold despite the warmth.
- Choose long sleeves and long pants even though it will be hot. Wet leaves hide sharp edges, hot pipes hide under moss, and old brick can scrape.

### Outer Layer
- A lightweight, quick-drying, abrasion-resistant shirt and trousers are best.
- Do not wear meltable synthetic outer layers if you plan to spend time near the engine or exhaust stack. Wool or a flame-resistant blend is safer near hot metal.
- A breathable rain shell with a hood is useful against the permanent indoor rain, but it should be quiet fabric. Crinkly rain gear will feel wrong and may keep you from hearing small warning sounds.

### Footwear
- High-ankle waterproof boots with deep, non-slip soles.
- Composite toe and composite shank rather than steel, if possible, because of electrical risk and rust.
- Rubber or polyurethane soles with good grip for wet roots, slick brick, and mossy metal.
- Gaiters over the boots to keep water and plant debris out.
- Wool or synthetic socks. Bring at least one extra pair in a dry bag.

### Gloves
Carry two pairs:

1. **Thin, cut-resistant, nitrile-coated gloves** for general movement, gripping roots, opening gates, handling gear.
2. **Heavier leather or aramid gloves** for any accidental contact with hot metal, rusty edges, or rough brick.

Do not touch live electrical wiring with any glove. Gloves are for scrapes and heat, not insulation from current.

### Head and Face
- A lightweight bump cap or climbing helmet is wise. Overhead there are low steel struts, wired glass, branches, and leaking tanks.
- Eye protection is essential. Use anti-fog safety glasses or goggles. The humidity will fog ordinary glasses instantly. Photochromic or light-colored lenses help because the light under the canopy can be gray-gold and even, but with bright patches from the roof holes.
- A wide-brimmed hat is less useful under the canopy, but a hood or cap keeps the constant drip off your face and neck.

### Hearing Protection
- Bring high-fidelity earplugs, not full blocking earmuffs. You want to reduce the constant noise, not lose awareness of mechanical changes, gas hisses, or a companion’s voice.
- Be aware that the engine’s low-frequency chuff arrives through your feet and bones, not just your ears. Earplugs will not stop that. You may feel the rhythm in your chest, teeth, or sinuses.

### Respiratory Protection
The air may smell pleasant in a strange industrial way—hot oil, wet brick, crushed leaves, a faint sweetness from flowers in the exhaust plume—but it is not clean.

At minimum:

- Wear an N100 or P100 respirator to filter mold spores, rust dust, and organic particles.
- If you intend to approach the engine, the exhaust stack, the sump, or the methane leak, use a half-face respirator with organic vapor and acid gas cartridges.
- More important than a respirator is a **multi-gas detector** clipped to your pack or chest, monitoring oxygen, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, and combustible gases such as methane. Check it before entering and keep it on.

The smell of burnt swamp gas may be noticeable but not overwhelming. Do not trust your nose. Methane is largely odorless, and hydrogen sulfide can deaden the sense of smell after a short exposure.

## IV. Equipment and Tools

### Lighting
- A waterproof headlamp and a handheld flashlight.
- Warm or neutral-white light, not harsh blue-white. The existing caged lamps throw a dull orange light; bright white will feel intrusive and may ruin your dark adaptation.
- Use intrinsically safe lights if possible, because methane may be present. If you cannot get explosion-proof lighting, do not change batteries or switch lights on and off inside the bowl.
- Carry spare batteries outside in a sealed container.

### Gas and Air Monitoring
- A personal multi-gas detector is non-negotiable for any visit longer than a few minutes.
- Clip it to the outside of your pack so the sensor is in open air.
- Check it at the rim, then again at knee height near the sump, the leak, and the engine. Combustible gases can stratify; some rise, some sink.

### Navigation and Communication
- Do not rely on a magnetic compass near the generator and old machinery.
- Pre-load a handheld GPS or mapping device with your entry point and an exit route. Satellite signals may be weak under the canopy, so mark the bearing to the nearest high ground before descending.
- Carry a satellite messenger or emergency beacon. There is no one to call for help locally.
- If visiting with companions, agree on hand signals for *stop*, *gas alarm*, *I’m too hot*, *leave now*, and *I’m hurt*. The noise may make speech unreliable.

### Probes and Walking Aids
- A trekking pole or wading staff with a rubber tip is invaluable. Use it to:
  - Probe the root floor for hidden voids or deep pools.
  - Part wet leaves before you step.
  - Test the stability of the rotted catwalk.
  - Keep your balance on slick brick.
- Do not use a sharp metal tip. It can puncture roots, damage hidden wiring, or slip into a sump.

### Water and Food
- Carry all your own drinking water. At least two to three liters for a short visit. The water in the Exchange is part of the machine’s circulation, not a drinking source. It may contain oil, rust, engine residue, and biological growth.
- Bring electrolyte tablets or powder. You will sweat heavily in the humidity.
- Eat before entering or above the rim. If you must carry food inside, keep it in sealed containers and pack out every crumb. Do not drop food into the sump or the root floor.

### First Aid and Repair
- Waterproof first-aid kit: antiseptic wipes, sterile saline for wound irrigation, hydrocolloid bandages, blister care, burn gel, antihistamines, antifungal cream, eye wash, and an emergency thermal blanket.
- Not local water for cleaning wounds. Even if it looks clear, assume it carries oil and rot.
- A small multi-tool, waterproof tape, zip ties, and spare boot laces.
- If you become entangled in roots or vines, a fixed-blade knife or shears can free you. Use cutting only in a genuine emergency; do not prune the place.

### Electronics
- Keep cameras, recorders, and phones in waterproof housings or roll-top dry bags.
- Bring desiccant packs. Inside the bowl, condensation will form on lenses, screens, and viewfinders within minutes.
- Anti-fog wipes for lenses and glasses are more useful than you expect.
- Do not rely on touchscreens with wet hands; bring physical buttons if possible.

## V. Approach and Entry

Approach from the rim, not from a downstream valley floor. The slopes drain inward, so entering from below would mean wading through the most flooded, most contaminated ground.

Before descending, stop at the rim for at least ten minutes.

- Watch the exhaust plume from the stack. It should be a soft white puff with each power stroke. Note its rhythm.
- Listen to the engine. Learn the gap between chuffs.
- Check the air at the rim with your gas detector.
- Look for the holes in the canopy and the dull glow of the lamps. Mentally map the high ground and the route out.
- Secure loose hair, drawstrings, necklaces, straps, and anything that could catch on moving machinery or roots.
- Turn off any unnecessary electronics or set them to silent.

Then enter slowly. Do not rush. The place rewards slow movement. The first few steps onto the root floor will feel strange—springy, slightly giving, as if the ground is breathing. It is.

## VI. Movement and Safety Inside

### On the Root Floor
- Step on the broad roots, not the gaps between them.
- Probe with your pole before shifting weight.
- Expect hidden voids. The floor is not solid ground. In places it may be a floating mat over shallow water, loose brick, or old machinery.
- Move with bent knees and a low center of gravity. Do not jump or make sudden turns.
- Avoid stepping on wires, whether they look alive or not.

### The Catwalk
The catwalk is described as rotted. Treat it as decorative, not structural.

- If you choose to cross it, go one person at a time.
- Test every section with your pole and with light foot pressure before trusting it.
- Do not grip any handrail until you have inspected it. Roots may have replaced the original metal; those roots may be strong, but they may also hide loose pipe or live wire.
- If there is any doubt, do not cross. The view from the edge is enough.

### The Engine and Machinery
The hot-bulb engine is the heart of the place. It is also the most physically dangerous part for a visitor.

- Assume all metal near the engine is hot.
- Assume the flywheel and piston rod are moving pinch points. Keep hands, feet, hair, straps, and clothing away.
- Do not lean over the flywheel, even though it moves slowly. One black spoke passing every few seconds can still break a hand or pull you in.
- Do not touch the polished piston rod. It may be hot, oily, and moving.
- Do not attempt to read the hand-painted letters by scraping moss or rust. Photograph them if you wish.
- Do not operate any valve, lever, or throttle linkage. The root grown into the throttle linkage may be a governing mechanism. If you change it, you may break the cycle.

### Electrical Safety
- Treat every wire as live.
- Do not touch a wire and a wet surface at the same time.
- Do not step into water if a live wire may be contacting it. Rubber boots help, but they are not a guarantee of safety.
- If you feel tingling in your hands or feet, leave immediately by the driest route.

### Water and the Indoor Rain
- The permanent rain near the center is not weather. It is the machine’s breath, leaking from the header tank in the strangler fig.
- Do not stand under it long. The water is warm and may smell earthy, but it has passed through pipes, roots, and sump sediment.
- Avoid letting it run into your eyes, mouth, or open wounds.
- If you want to observe the rain, do so from a few feet back, where the droplets are light.

### Methane and Gas Pockets
- The hissing methane leak may sound like a snake that never moves. Do not approach it closely.
- Low areas, the sump, and spaces under the root floor may trap gas.
- If your gas detector alarms, do not switch anything on or off. Do not use your phone. Leave by the shortest upward route, slowly but steadily.
- No open flames, no smoking, no sparking tools, no drones with brushed motors.

## VII. Sensory and Mental Management

The Sump Exchange is a saturated sensory environment. Many travelers find it beautiful; many also find it exhausting.

### Sound
The engine chuff arrives every few seconds. Between chuffs there is no silence—only the drip of water, the hum of the generator, the tick of cooling metal, the creak of roots. The quiet is never quiet.

- Use high-fidelity earplugs if the noise begins to wear on you.
- Take breaks above the rim every hour or so. Let your ears reset.
- If you find yourself counting the engine strokes, or if the rhythm begins to feel like your own heartbeat, that is a sign to step out. The place can pull you into its tempo.

### Heat and Humidity
The air is warm and heavy. Breathing is like drinking warm tea. You will sweat, and your sweat may not evaporate well in the saturated air.

- Drink electrolytes frequently, not just water.
- Rest in any spot where the air moves, even slightly.
- Watch for signs of heat exhaustion: headache, nausea, dizziness, clammy skin, confusion. Leave the bowl immediately if these appear.

### Smell
The smell is strong but not unpleasant: hot oil, wet brick, rust, crushed leaves, and a faint sweetness from flowers near the exhaust plume.

- Do not ignore a sudden stronger smell of gas, burning, or something sharply rotten. Stop and check your surroundings.
- Do not inhale deeply near the exhaust stack, the sump, or the methane leak, even if the smell seems mild.

### Light and Vision
The light is gray-gold and even, like a bright overcast afternoon. Caged lamps glow dull orange, making the wet leaves look like late afternoon inside a machine.

- Anti-fog your glasses before entering.
- Polarized lenses can help cut glare from wet leaves.
- Do not use bright white flashlights unless necessary. They will flatten the strange beauty of the place and may irritate your eyes.

## VIII. Ethical Code: How to Be a Visitor

The Sump Exchange is a closed loop. Everything in it is already circulating: water, gas, leaves, heat, rust, oil. You are the only open variable.

Therefore:

- **Do not remove anything.** Not a leaf, not a snail, not a piece of rust, not a cutting of the metallic-sheened leaves.
- **Do not leave anything.** No food, no wrappers, no bodily waste, no hair, no sunscreen, no insect repellent. Even benign substances can alter the sump’s chemistry or the root mat’s surface.
- **Do not sit on the moss-cushioned chair.** It faces the gauges as though someone has just stepped away. It may be someone’s place, or it may be a part of the site’s memory. Leave it untouched.
- **Do not prune, clear, or adjust the plants or machinery.** The root in the throttle linkage, the ferns growing from brass taps, the roots cupping the pipes—all may be part of the system.
- **Do not introduce seeds, spores, or microbes from other worlds.** Clean your boots and gear before arrival if you have recently been in other biomes.
- **Do not talk loudly or bring music.** The place already has a voice.
- **If the white egret appears, stop moving.** Let it pass. You are the rare animal here.

A useful mindset: **Enter as a closed loop yourself.** Carry in everything you need, carry out everything you bring, and leave no evidence of your passage.

## IX. Length of Stay

For a first visit, limit yourself to ninety minutes to two hours inside the bowl.

The heat, humidity, gas exposure, and constant low-frequency noise are cumulative. You may not notice them wearing on you until you are already tired. Set a timer. When it goes off, leave the bowl and rest above the rim for at least half an hour before deciding whether to re-enter.

Do not plan to sleep inside the Exchange. There is no dry ground, no silence, and no safe flat place. The engine will chuff through the night. The rain will not stop. Methane may pool while the air is still. If you must stay overnight, camp above the rim, outside the inward drainage.

## X. Departure and Decontamination

When you leave:

- Go upward, against the flow of water. The drainage leads inward, toward the sump. Do not follow it.
- Stop above the rim and inspect your boots, clothing, and gear.
- Remove any plant fragments, seeds, snails, or mud. Leave them at the site, not in your pack.
- Dry your gear thoroughly. The warmth and wetness encourage mold, which can cross into other worlds with you.
- Do not carry site water, soil, or artifacts across a multiverse boundary.
- Shower and change clothing as soon as practical. Check your skin for cuts, rashes, or fungal patches in the following days.

If you visited from another biome or world, quarantine your gear until it is completely dry and clean. The Sump Exchange is a stable place partly because it is isolated. Do not accidentally export its biology.

## XI. Emergency Actions

### Gas Alarm
If your gas detector alarms, especially for combustible gas or hydrogen sulfide:

- Do not operate any switch, phone, or electronic device.
- Leave by the shortest upward route.
- Do not light anything.
- Re-check the air at the rim.

### Carbon Monoxide or Hydrogen Sulfide Symptoms
Headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, or a feeling of “not caring” are warning signs.

- Leave the bowl immediately.
- Breathe fresh air above the rim.
- Seek medical evaluation if symptoms persist.

### Injury
- Clean wounds with sterile saline, not site water.
- Control bleeding and evacuate upward. The site’s water is not sterile, despite its beauty.
- Rusted metal and old machinery mean tetanus is a real risk.

### Heat Illness
- Stop, move to shade above the rim, sip electrolytes, and cool the back of your neck.
- Do not push through heat exhaustion.

### Electrical Shock or Tingling
- If you feel tingling through water or metal, do not touch others. Break contact and move away by the driest route.
- Assume the source is still live.

### Entrapment or Fall
- If you fall through the root floor, try to spread your weight. Do not thrash.
- Use your pole or pack to bridge across roots.
- If roots or vines entangle you, cut yourself free only if necessary. Do not cut plants for convenience.

## XII. Final Packing Checklist

Use this as a master list:

**Clothing and PPE**
- Merino or synthetic base layer, long sleeves
- Lightweight abrasion-resistant outer layer
- Waterproof high-ankle boots, composite toe/shank
- Gaiters
- Extra socks in dry bag
- Cut-resistant gloves
- Heat-resistant gloves
- Bump cap or helmet
- Anti-fog safety glasses
- High-fidelity earplugs
- N100/P100 respirator
- Organic vapor/acid gas cartridges if approaching engine
- Rain shell with hood

**Tools and Monitoring**
- Multi-gas detector (O2, CO, H2S, LEL)
- Intrinsically safe flashlight/headlamp
- Trekking pole with rubber tip
- Waterproof roll-top pack and dry bags
- Desiccant packs
- Satellite messenger/beacon
- Timer or alarm watch
- Multi-tool and cutting tool for emergencies

**Supplies**
- 2–3 liters water
- Electrolytes
- Sealed food if needed
- First-aid kit with wound care and burn gel
- Waterproof notebook and pencil
- Camera/recorder in waterproof housing
- Anti-fog wipes and lens cloth
- Waste bags for packing out all trash

**Mindset**
- Leave no trace
- Move slowly
- Do not fix anything
- Do not take anything
- Do not trust the catwalk
- Do not touch the engine
- Do not drink the rain

## A Final Word

The Sump Exchange is small, packed, and saturated. It is not waiting for you, and it will not notice when you leave. But if you prepare well, you may be lucky enough to stand inside its slow, humid breath for a little while—watching the flywheel turn, listening to the indoor rain, and feeling the roots answer the engine’s pulse—without leaving a trace.

That is the best a visitor can hope for.
