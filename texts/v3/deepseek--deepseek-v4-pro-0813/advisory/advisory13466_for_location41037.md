Before you go, accept one thing: you will not stay dry. You will not stay certain. The Ledges, or the Cradle, or whatever the locals call it this week, is a cold, wet, vertical factory bolted to a mountain range that hasn’t finished becoming a mountain range. The air is thin but heavy with condensation. The light is gray-green and perpetual. The noise is a layered drone of steam, crows, pumps, and a distant quarry boom that arrives three times. The city is real enough to fall from and hard enough to break on. And some of its edges are not entirely there.

So pack for a month of damp, sharp, industrial mountaineering, then add things to keep your own mind nailed down.

---

### Physical preparation

Train as if for high-altitude trekking with long stair climbs. You will be climbing and descending thousands of steps cut into raw rock, crossing cable bridges, and walking on wet metal gratings. Build endurance, ankle strength, grip strength, and balance. Practice carrying a loaded pack on narrow ledges in poor light. If you can simulate altitude, do so; the air is cool and thin, and exertion will feel heavier than it should. You don’t need to be an athlete, but you need to be able to move slowly for hours without losing focus.

Your body will be cold and damp most of the time. Learn to manage that without overheating. Sweat will not dry; it will sit against your skin and chill you later. Move slowly, layer deliberately, and rest before you soak your clothing.

---

### Clothing: dress for a cold sauna

The damp is the enemy. It condenses on your skin, your pack, your food, your bedding. Wear merino or synthetic base layers, never cotton. Bring a warm mid-layer and a waterproof-breathable shell that can take abrasion from sharp schist and hot pipe brackets. Trousers should be reinforced at the knee and seat; gaiters keep water and cable-mites out of your boots.

Boots must be waterproof, high-ankle, sticky-soled, and already broken in. Bring microspikes or light traction for wet rock and slimy metal. Two pairs of gloves: thin for dexterity, heavy waterproof for cables and rock. A climbing helmet is not optional—rockfall, low beams, cranes, and crow-dropped debris are constant. Eye protection with yellow or clear lenses helps in fog and against arc-lamp glare.

A respirator or good N95/P100 mask is necessary near the works: smoke, mineral dust, spores, and steam condensate will coat your lungs. Bring earplugs or hearing protection; the drone is relentless, and you will need to sleep.

---

### The pack

Everything goes in dry bags inside a tough, water-resistant pack. Assume nothing will dry once wet.

Bring a headlamp with spare batteries and a red mode; the dimness is never complete, but interiors and deep ledges are black. A handheld white-light torch helps you see true color—the sodium and arc lamps lie. Carry spare batteries in a sealed bag. A compass may work or may not; bring one anyway, but treat it as a suggestion. A watch with an alarm is essential. Local time is slow and rhythmic; your watch is a reality anchor.

Bring chalk, surveyor’s tape, reflective stickers, and a spool of bright cord for marking routes. But know that marks may fade or the route may change. A light climbing harness, a couple of locking carabiners, and a via ferrata lanyard are worth their weight if you know how to use them; many stairs and bridges have cables but no fall protection. Trekking poles help on rough ground but can slip on metal; use rubber tips.

Shelter: a bivy sack or small tent with good ventilation, a synthetic sleeping bag—down loses warmth when damp—and an insulated pad. Bring a tarp for condensate. A small canister stove and pot let you make hot drinks and food; hot food is morale. Fire is hard in the damp, so carry a reliable lighter and storm matches. Water: carry enough for 48 hours, and a filter or purification tablets. Do not drink seep-water or industrial runoff; collect condensation from clean surfaces if you must.

Repair kit: duct tape, zip ties, wire, multi-tool, sewing awl, spare buckles, silicone sealant, spare laces. The place will tear your gear.

---

### Health and medicine

Bring altitude medication if you know you need it, plus ibuprofen, anti-nausea, and rehydration salts. The damp causes fungal rot: pack antifungal powder, barrier cream, and change your socks twice a day. Wounds go bad quickly in constant moisture; clean and cover even small cuts. Blister kit, burn gel for steam pipes, antiseptic, wound closure strips, splint, emergency blanket.

Antihistamines and insect repellent for ghost-midges and other biters; permethrin-treated clothing helps. A head net may or may not stop them, but it’s better than nothing. Bring antibiotics for GI infections, and any personal meds in waterproof containers. A small dental kit, painkillers, and caffeine tablets.

### Navigation and movement

The city is a vertical web of ledges, bridges, staircases, and cable trams. Move along built structures—paved ledges, railings, lit galleries, steam pipes—not raw rock. The city’s machinery is not just industry; it is the world insisting on a shape. Paths that are lit, noisy, warm, and maintained are more stable. If a path is silent, unlit, blurred, or warm in the wrong places, do not take it.

Mark your route with chalk or tape at every intersection. Use multiple cues: the direction of the quarry boom, the smell of the gas works, a particular crow colony. The boom arrives three times because the valley’s acoustics are strange; treat the first as warning, the second as direction, the third as proximity.

Do not rush. Time here is slow, and the fog moves in long patient waves. Rushing makes you slip or step off a ledge that is not entirely there. On stairs, keep one hand on the rail. Do not count Stair Nine; if you must use it, do not count, do not think about the count, just climb until you reach a landing or the rail warms, then stop and reassess. If the rail warms where no pipe runs, let go and use your own balance.

Never walk on blurred rock. If the edge of a cliff looks like a sketch or a heat-shimmer, it is not load-bearing. Do not step into fog that has no visible bottom or structure. If a stairway ends at a blank wall in the morning, do not wait for it to change; go back. If a new path appears that wasn’t there before, do not take it unless you see locals using it.

For exposed crossings, use a lanyard, clip in where possible, cross one at a time, and do not look down for too long. The gaps are deep and the handrails are sometimes the only real thing.

### Fauna

The animals are abundant and mostly indifferent, but not all are harmless. Cinder crows will steal small shiny objects and may mimic speech; don’t follow crow calls, and secure your gear. Cable-mites swarm on wires; avoid brushing against them. Ghost-midges bite before you see them; wear long sleeves, tuck pants into boots, use repellent. Cliff-urchins graze on mineral blooms; don’t startle them near a drop. Veil moths are beautiful and harmless but will cluster around your lamp; keep them out of your mouth. Wind-eaters drift high above; their groans are felt more than heard. Do not anchor under them. Shimmer-locusts are visible only when they land; if the air seems to shimmer over the cobbles, step carefully.

### Psychological preparation

The place will try to unnerve you with dampness, dimness, noise, and spatial uncertainty. You may feel like you are in a dream that cannot decide what it is.

Bring a small notebook and pen that writes in the wet. Keep a journal. Write down your name, why you are here, the date by your watch, and a description of your route. If you start to doubt your memory, read it aloud. Carry a personal anchor: a coin, a photograph, a familiar stone—something small and real. Establish a routine: eat, drink, dry your feet, sleep at the same time by your watch, regardless of local light. Routines are as much a reality anchor as the city’s machines.

Sleep will be hard. Use earplugs, an eye mask, and a sleeping bag liner. The damp will make everything feel clammy; a small candle lantern inside a ventilated tent can dry the air slightly and give you a warm point of reference. Do not sleep on raw rock or near blurred edges. Sleep near a maintained building, a lit gallery, or a humming pipe.

If you become confused or panicked, stop. Sit down on a built surface, drink water, eat something real, and breathe. Recite your name, your home, your route. Do not follow figures in the fog, even if they look like you. If you hear your name in crow calls, ignore it. If you see double landmarks or the same bridge twice, stay put until the feeling passes, then retrace your route using marks and sounds.

---

### Social preparation and trade

You are an outsider. The city runs on work, guilds, and unspoken rules. When you arrive, find a gatehouse, union hall, tram office, or local inn. Present yourself politely. Learn a few words: “safe,” “ledge,” “bridge,” “unfinished,” “do not enter,” “steam,” “quarry.” A local guide or fixer is worth more than any map; hire one if you can.

Pay in trade goods if money is not accepted. Bring things that are scarce in a damp, industrial, vertical city: dry socks, batteries, antibiotics, painkillers, chocolate, coffee, salt, lighters, good rope, carabiners, needles, thread, small mirrors, whiskey, tobacco. Dry socks are almost currency.

Respect the machines. Do not touch valves, switches, or fresh paint. The workers are not just maintaining infrastructure; they are helping the city hold its shape. Do not interrupt work rhythms. Do not carve your name into the rock, remove lichen, or disturb the animals. Do not take samples from blurred edges. The place may already be on edge; you are a guest in a world that is still deciding whether to exist.

---

### Arrival and extraction

Enter slowly. Acclimatize at lower ledges before climbing. On the first day, do little but observe: the rhythm of the pumps, the direction of the fog, the sound of the quarry. Establish a base landmark—an old blockhouse, a tram station, a stone stair that is heavily used—and use it as your reference. Learn the schedule of machines; they are clocks. The stamping press falls once every few seconds, then after a long interval. The trams creak on a schedule. These rhythms will orient you when the map fails.

Plan multiple exit routes and mark them with multiple cues: chalk, tape, sound, light. If you have a return mechanism or multiverse device, keep it on your body, wrapped in oilcloth, dry and warm. Test it only from stable ground. If the device fails, move toward the loudest, hottest, most electrically alive part of the city—the machines insist, and their insistence may help you stay real long enough to find the way out.

Do not leave during the deepest fog or the lowest light, because the dimness never truly ends and the fog swallows landmarks. Leave when you are rested, fed, and clear-headed.

You will leave with the smell of wet wool and cold iron in your clothes. Your boots will never fully dry. You will hear the crows in your sleep. And for weeks afterward, you will count stairs and wonder if the count was the same the second time. That is the cost of visiting a place that has not yet decided to be.
