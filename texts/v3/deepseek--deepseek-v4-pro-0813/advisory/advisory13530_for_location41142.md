Before anything else, understand this: the Sunken Table—the High Fog Sea—is not hostile in the way a desert or deep ocean is hostile. It will not attack you. It will wear you down, cut you slowly, confuse your eyes, and hold you in wet silence until you make a mistake. The place is young, mineral, still, and profoundly unfinished. You are not walking into a landscape; you are walking into a process.

Preparation is mostly protection against small injuries, disorientation, volcanic gas, and the slow leaching of heat and morale. Here is what an ordinary human multiverse traveler should know and do.

---

## 1. Understand the environmental baseline

The water comes first. You will be in or near water for most of the day. Your feet will be wet. Your gear will be damp. The air is as wet as the water, and there is no wind to dry anything. The light is green-gray, filtered through fog above and water below, so depth perception and color judgment will be subtly wrong. The terrain is black, glassy basalt, young and sharp, with no sand or rounded pebbles. The vegetation is dense, jointed, and partly translucent. The silence is almost total, broken only by drips, creaks, and the faint hiss of volcanic gas.

Expect mild to warm temperatures—perhaps 15–25°C in the air, similar in the water—but with altitude and 100% humidity, you can become chilled surprisingly fast when stationary or sleeping. At night the fog may thicken and the temperature drop several degrees. The real danger is not cold alone but perpetual wetness combined with cool, still air.

There are no biting insects, no predators, no toxic plants in the usual sense, and almost no animals. That does not make the place safe. It makes the dangers less familiar.

---

## 2. Physical and skill preparation

An ordinary human should train for this as a mixture of canyoneering, shallow-water swamp travel, and high-altitude trekking.

- **Water confidence.** You will wade, swim, and possibly paddle. Be comfortable with water up to chest level, with sudden depth changes, and with submerged vegetation. Practice swimming in clothing and with a small pack.
- **Entanglement self-rescue.** The glass-reeds grow close and their stems are as thick as a wrist. If you slip into deep water among them, you can become tangled. Practice reaching for a blunt-tipped knife on your life jacket, cutting imaginary stems, and staying calm.
- **Balance and scrambling.** The basalt is sharp, slick with algae in places, and often vertical. Practice moving over wet rock, talus, and narrow ridges with a loaded pack.
- **Altitude acclimatization.** The plateau sits high—possibly high enough to cause acute mountain sickness if you arrive directly from low elevation. If your multiverse travel allows, spend a day or two at an intermediate altitude. Know the symptoms: headache, nausea, dizziness, fatigue. Carry acetazolamide if you have used it before, and know when to descend or call for evacuation.
- **Navigation in fog.** Visibility may drop to a few meters. Practice compass bearings, pace counting, GPS trackback, and using a taut line or flagging tape to mark a route. Do not rely on visual landmarks.
- **Packraft or small-boat handling.** If you bring a packraft, practice inflating it quickly, paddling in tight spaces, and repairing punctures. The reeds and sharp rock will test it constantly.

If you can only train three things, train these: wading with a staff, self-rescue from water entanglement, and fog navigation.

## 3. Footwear and lower-body protection

Your feet and legs will take the worst abuse. The basalt is conchoidally fractured—meaning it breaks into shell-shaped, glassy edges—and the underwater felt of algae softens nothing.

**Boots.** Use high-top canyoneering or wading boots with sticky rubber soles, ankle support, and drainage holes. They should have a puncture-resistant midsole. Avoid heavy leather mountaineering boots; they stay waterlogged and dry poorly. A boot that drains and dries is better than a waterproof boot that fills and stays full.

**Socks.** Wear quick-dry synthetic or wool socks, not cotton. Carry several pairs and rotate them. Consider thin neoprene socks inside the boots if your feet get cold, but remember the water is warm, so overheating and maceration are bigger risks.

**Gaiters.** Low gaiters or homemade rock guards will keep sharp chips and plant debris out of your boots.

**Knee and shin pads.** You will kneel and crawl on sharp rock more than you expect. Lightweight neoprene or hard-shell knee pads are worth the weight.

**Pants.** Wear tough, quick-dry nylon or soft-shell pants with reinforced knees. No cotton. Long pants are essential even if warm, because the reeds and rock will cut exposed skin.

---

## 4. Upper body, hands, and head

**Torso.** A long-sleeve, abrasion-resistant shirt—sun hoodie or light soft-shell—is a good base. Over it, a very thin wetsuit top or rash guard can help if you swim. Carry a synthetic insulating layer (fleece or synthetic puffy) for rest stops and nights, because down loses warmth when wet.

**Shell.** A waterproof-breathable jacket is necessary for fog and drips, but you will sweat. Choose one with large pit zips. There is no wind-driven rain, but constant condensation will soak you.

**Gloves.** Bring two pairs. First, a cut-resistant, rubber-palmed work glove for wading and grabbing reeds or rock. Second, a heavier leather or belay glove for scrambling over sharp ridges. Your hands will otherwise be sliced to ribbons.

**Head.** A wide-brim hat or cap with a neck flap protects against filtered UV and constant drips. A buff or neck gaiter is useful. Sunglasses with polarized lenses help cut surface glare and see into the water, but in fog they may be useless; bring clear eye protection for bushwhacking.

---

## 5. Shelter and sleeping

The ground is either sharp rock or floating vegetation. There is no soil. You will camp on damp rock islands or ridges, never on the floating mats.

**Tent.** Use a freestanding tent with a tough bathtub floor. Stakes will not work in rock; bring sandbags or use heavy rocks to anchor corners. A single-wall tent may be lighter, but condensation inside will be intense; a double-wall with good ventilation is better. If you use a hammock, you must have wide tree straps to avoid damaging the emergent trunks, and a bug net is unnecessary. A tarp alone is not enough because fog will condense on everything.

**Sleeping pad.** Bring a thick, puncture-resistant closed-cell foam pad, plus an inflatable pad for comfort. The rock will find any weak spot.

**Sleeping bag.** Synthetic, not down. A 0°C to 10°C rating is probably enough for the air, but remember you may be damp. A vapor barrier liner can help keep the bag dry from your own sweat. Bring a small, waterproof bivy sack as an emergency shelter.

**Camp site selection.** Choose elevated rock, not depressions. Avoid overhanging vegetation that drips. Look for the pale high-water mark on rocks and camp above it. Clear the area of loose sharp fragments, but do not damage the living felt more than necessary.

---

## 6. Water, food, and waste

This is a closed basin. The water cycles only through evaporation and dew. It is warm, mineralized, silky with dissolved silica, and faintly salty. Do not drink it without distillation.

**Drinking water.** Carry all the water you will need, plus a reserve. In humid, warm conditions with exertion, plan for 4–6 liters per person per day. Use durable collapsible bottles or hydration bladders with bite valves. Insulated bottles are unnecessary because it is not hot, but sun can warm them.

**Purification.** If you must use local water, only a distillation method is adequate—boiling will not remove dissolved minerals or heavy metals. A small solar still or a compact distiller is heavy but could be life-saving. A high-quality filter with activated carbon and ion exchange may reduce some contaminants, but do not rely on it for silica-rich or volcanic water. Rainwater or dew collected on a clean, dedicated tarp is probably safer, but still treat it with caution: the fog may have picked up volcanic gases.

**Food.** There are no edible plants or animals you should rely on. Bring all food, high in calories and low in residue. No open fires—there is little fuel, everything is wet, and the place is pristine. Use a small stove with a reliable fuel source, or eat cold. Avoid cooking inside your tent due to condensation and gas buildup.

**Waste.** Pack out everything. Solid waste should go into wag bags or a portable toilet. Urine should be collected in a wide-mouth bottle and packed out if possible; this is a closed basin with no outflow, and even urine can alter the water chemistry. If you must urinate, do so on bare rock away from any water, and pack out the container later. Do not wash with soap in the water. Use hand sanitizer and pack out wipes. Before departure, clean your boots and gear thoroughly to avoid introducing seeds, spores, or microbes.

## 7. Mobility: wading, swimming, and boating

You will move in three modes: walking on rock ridges and islands, wading through shallow water and reeds, and possibly paddling across deeper basins.

**Wading staff.** This is your most important tool. Use a sturdy, non-conductive pole about chest-high, with a blunt tip that will not snag. Mark depth lines on it. Probe every step in water you cannot see through. The bottom is sharp, uneven, and often deeper than it appears because the layered water bends light. The staff also helps you feel the temperature boundaries in the water—they are real, stable, and can be disorienting.

**Do not walk on floating mats.** They are not ground. A mat of broad leaves may look continuous, but it will not hold your weight, and underneath is water and a forest of reeds. Treat the surface mat as thin ice over a snare. If you must cross a mat, do so only in a boat or by carefully pulling yourself along without placing full weight on it—but ideally avoid it entirely.

**Packraft.** A small, durable packraft or inflatable kayak is invaluable for crossing deep channels and open water between islands. Choose one with a reinforced or Kevlar floor to resist punctures from reeds and rock. Carry a four-piece paddle, a small pump, and a comprehensive repair kit. Always wear a personal flotation device when on or over deep water. Be aware that dense reed forests can trap a boat; you may have to push, pull, and back-paddle. Keep a knife accessible on your PFD to cut yourself free if entangled.

**Swimming.** If you swim, use slow, controlled strokes. The water layers may feel strange—a cool surface band, a warm middle, a cool lower band—like passing through rooms. Do not dive. The optical distortion can make depth and obstacles impossible to judge. Swim with a buddy and a tether in poor visibility.

## 8. Navigation and communication

This landscape actively resists navigation. There is no horizon, no distant landmark, no trails. The fog does not blow; it only thickens and thins.

**GPS.** Carry a waterproof, rugged GPS unit with preloaded topo maps, and use it to drop waypoints constantly. Track your route and set it to display a breadcrumb trail. Extra batteries or a power bank are essential; solar charging may be weak in fog.

**Compass.** A traditional compass as backup. The young basalt may have magnetic anomalies, so cross-check with GPS when possible. Learn to follow a bearing in fog, using both the compass and a line of sight to a partner or a marker.

**Altimeter/barometer.** Useful for understanding your position on ridge systems and for detecting weather changes, though the weather here is mostly stable fog.

**Marking.** Bring bright flagging tape, reflective cord, and small LED markers. Mark your route at intervals, but remove them on your way out. In dense fog, a spool of thin line can be unreeled behind you as a physical return path.

**Communication.** A satellite messenger or personal locator beacon is the only reliable way to call for help. If your multiverse travel system has an emergency recall beacon, carry it and test it before entry. A two-way VHF radio is useful for a team. Whistles and signal mirrors are backups, but fog may render mirrors useless.

**Drone.** If you have one, a small, quiet drone with collision avoidance can help reconnoiter from above when fog lifts. But humidity and lack of GPS lock may ground it; do not depend on it.

## 9. Safety gear and first aid

The three non-negotiable safety items in this environment are a **gas monitor**, a **first aid kit geared for cuts and punctures**, and a **knife accessible on your person at all times**.

**Volcanic gas monitor.** The faint hiss of bubbles is not necessarily harmless. In closed basins with no wind, heavier gases such as carbon dioxide can pool in low areas. Carry a compact 4-gas monitor that measures oxygen, carbon dioxide, hydrogen sulfide, and combustible gases. Set alarms conservatively. If it alarms, move immediately to higher, well-ventilated ground. Do not rely on smell—CO2 is odorless, and H2S may deaden your sense of smell after a moment. Treat any low basin, especially near visible fissures or areas of cloudy water, as suspect. Early symptoms of gas exposure include headache, dizziness, confusion, and breathlessness.

**First aid.** The basalt will cut and puncture skin even through clothing. Your kit should include:
- Irrigation syringe and sterile saline for wound cleaning
- Antiseptic wipes and chlorhexidine
- Wound closure strips and skin glue
- A variety of adhesive bandages, including large island dressings
- Gauze pads and roller bandages
- Waterproof tape and elastic tape
- A SAM splint or similar
- Tweezers and a magnifying lens for removing tiny glassy splinters
- Antibiotic ointment and oral antibiotics if you have a prescription
- A tourniquet and hemostatic dressing for severe bleeding from a fall onto sharp rock
- Blister care: moleskin, second-skin, tape
- Anti-fungal powder or cream for skin maceration
- Altitude sickness medications: acetazolamide, ibuprofen, dexamethasone if prescribed
- Personal medications in waterproof containers

**Eye protection and eye wash.** The water is slightly alkaline or mineralized; splashes may irritate eyes. Carry a squeeze bottle of sterile saline eye wash and use it immediately if you get water in your eyes. Goggles are useful for swimming and for close work in reeds.

**Knife and multitool.** A blunt-tipped, serrated rescue knife on your PFD or chest harness can cut you out of reeds, rope, or netting. A multitool with pliers and scissors is invaluable for gear repair.

## 10. Environmental discipline and ethics

The description says there are no structures, no tools, no made things. The only patterns are biological and mineral. This is not a place that has learned to tolerate human presence. Treat it as a greenhouse, a laboratory, a sacred garden before animals were invented.

- **Leave no trace.** Pack out everything you bring, including food scraps, tape, thread, and toilet paper. Even a single stray fiber is a made thing.
- **Do not cut plants except in a genuine life-threatening entanglement.** If you must cut a stem, use the smallest cut possible and do not break others in the process.
- **Do not walk on the living felt or the floating mats more than absolutely necessary.** Choose bare rock routes. The felt fills every crack and is part of the ecosystem; crushing it may take decades or centuries to recover in this slow-chemistry world.
- **Do not introduce soap, sunscreen, insect repellent, or fuel into the water.** Use physical sun protection (clothing, hat) rather than chemical sunscreens. Wash yourself away from any water, using a small amount of biodegradable soap only if absolutely necessary, and pack out the gray water or scatter it on rock far from basins.
- **Do not collect samples unless your travel authorization explicitly permits it.** Even a single glass snail or a shard of basalt is part of a closed system.

If you can leave the place exactly as you found it—minus your own brief presence—that is the best possible outcome.

## 11. Psychological preparation and daily rhythm

The silence will be the hardest thing to get used to. There is no wind, no birdsong, no insect hum. You will hear your own heartbeat, your own breathing, the creak of your pack. The only environmental sounds are small and irregular: a drip from a leaf, a stem creaking, a seed pod popping, the soft hiss of gas. Some travelers find this meditative; others find it unnerving.

The light is green-gray, like being inside a shallow lagoon even when you are standing on a rock island. The fog may not clear for days. There is no horizon. Distance is difficult to judge. The water’s layers bend light, so a stem may look broken at a boundary, a rock may look nearer than it is, and the bottom may seem higher than it truly is.

**Coping strategies:**

- **Trust instruments over eyes.** When your eyes tell you one thing and the GPS, compass, or staff tells you another, trust the instrument.
- **Move slowly and deliberately.** This is not a place for speed. A rushed step onto sharp rock or a false judgment of water depth can end your trip.
- **Use a buddy system.** If traveling alone, check in with a base contact at set intervals. If traveling with others, maintain voice or visual contact in fog.
- **Set small daily goals.** Covering 5 kilometers in this terrain may take all day. Accept a pace of 1–2 kilometers per day if conditions are bad.
- **Rest often.** The humidity and altitude will sap your energy. Find rock islands or ridges above the water and sit, drink, eat, and let your gear dry as much as it can.
- **Journal or record audio.** The silence can be overwhelming. Recording your thoughts can ground you.
- **Be prepared for the feeling of being watched or followed.** In a place with no animals, your own nervous system may invent presence. This is normal. Breathe, look slowly around, and remind yourself of the facts.

**Daily rhythm.** Travel during the brightest part of the day, when the fog thins. Use morning and evening for camp chores, water purification, gear repair, and navigation planning. Do not travel at night. The combination of fog, sharp rock, and open water is lethal in darkness.

## 12. Emergency scenarios and contingencies

**Gas alarm goes off.** Stop, do not rush. Look at the reading. If oxygen is below 19.5% or CO2 is above 5000 ppm, move immediately uphill and away from the low area. Do not descend into a basin to retrieve gear. If a companion is affected, do not enter the area without a respirator and tether; call for help. Symptoms of CO2 exposure include headache, dizziness, rapid breathing, confusion, and loss of consciousness. Move to a ridge or open water surface where gas cannot pool.

**Lost in fog.** Stop moving. Mark your location with a bright item. Check GPS and compass. If you have a tracklog, backtrack along it. If truly lost, find a safe rock above water, set shelter, and wait for fog to thin. Do not wander. Use whistle or radio to contact your team.

**Injury.** Clean the wound with sterile saline, not basin water. Control bleeding with direct pressure. Dress the wound with waterproof materials. If you cannot continue, activate your emergency beacon, find or make a sheltered spot, and stay put. If the injury is a puncture from rock, watch for infection; the warm, wet environment accelerates bacterial growth.

**Packraft puncture.** Get to shore or a rock island. Dry the area as best you can, apply a patch, and wait for it to cure. If the boat is unusable, you may have to travel along ridges or wade shallow areas. Do not attempt to swim across a large basin without flotation.

**Altitude sickness.** If headache, nausea, or dizziness develop, stop, rest, hydrate, and consider descending if possible. On this plateau, descending may mean moving to a lower basin or ridge, but those may still be high. Acetazolamide can help if taken early. If symptoms worsen—confusion, loss of coordination, breathlessness at rest—activate evacuation.

**Entanglement in reeds.** Do not panic. Stop moving. Take out your rescue knife. Cut the stems one at a time, keeping your face above water. If you are with a partner, they should approach slowly and cut from behind. Float on your back if possible. The reeds are not strong enough to hold you if you are calm, but thrashing will tangle you further.

## Final gear checklist for an ordinary human multiverse traveler

**Clothing and body:**
- High-top canyoneering boots with puncture-resistant soles
- Neoprene or quick-dry socks (2–3 pairs)
- Quick-dry, abrasion-resistant long pants with knee pads
- Long-sleeve sun hoodie or rash guard
- Synthetic insulating layer (fleece or synthetic puffy)
- Waterproof-breathable shell with large vents
- Wide-brim hat, buff, polarized sunglasses, clear safety glasses
- Cut-resistant gloves and heavy belay gloves
- Thin wetsuit top or shorty for swimming

**Shelter and sleep:**
- Freestanding tent with tough floor and rock anchors
- Thick closed-cell foam pad
- Inflatable sleeping pad with repair kit
- Synthetic sleeping bag rated for 0–10°C
- Bivy sack (emergency)
- Tarp for dew collection and gear cover

**Mobility:**
- Wading staff with depth markings
- Packraft or inflatable kayak with reinforced floor
- Four-piece paddle, pump, repair kit
- PFD with attached rescue knife and whistle
- 20–30m floating rope or throw bag

**Water and food:**
- 4–6 L water carrying capacity per person
- Electrolyte tablets
- Water distiller or solar still (if not carrying all water)
- High-calorie, low-residue food for entire trip plus 2 extra days
- Stove and fuel (or cold food system)

**Navigation and communication:**
- Waterproof GPS with breadcrumb tracking
- Magnetic compass
- Barometric altimeter
- Flagging tape, reflective cord, spool of line
- Satellite messenger or emergency beacon
- VHF radios for team
- Headlamp with spare batteries/power bank

**Safety and first aid:**
- 4-gas monitor (O2, CO2, H2S, combustible gases)
- Trauma and wound care kit
- Altitude illness medications
- Eye wash and goggles
- Blunt-tipped rescue knife
- Multitool with pliers and scissors
- Duct tape, zip ties, repair patches
- Personal medications in waterproof case

**Waste and hygiene:**
- Wag bags and portable toilet
- Urine container
- Hand sanitizer
- Pack-out bags for all trash
- Biodegradable soap (use sparingly, away from water)

## A final intuition

When you first step onto a black rock island in the High Fog Sea, you may feel as if you are standing inside a painting that has not yet dried. The water is too still, the air too wet, the silence too complete. The stems around you will look jointed and false, like glass tubes arranged by some unseen hand. You will reach down and feel the temperature change as your fingers pass through a layer of water that is not quite the same as the water above or below it, and for a moment the world will seem reassembled from panes of slightly misaligned glass.

That is not danger. That is the place itself.

Move slowly. Probe every step. Do not trust the mats. Listen for the hiss of the rock breathing, and check your gas monitor when you hear it. Keep your knife where your hand can find it. Drink your carried water. Leave no trace.

The Sunken Table will not remember you if you leave nothing behind. That is the best possible outcome—for it, and for you.
