# Preparation Guide for the High Held Land (The Place of Wrong Distances)

**Assumptions I am making about you:** You are an ordinary baseline human with no superhuman endurance, no innate immunity to cold or altitude, no perfect navigation sense. You have access to multiverse travel, but once you arrive you are a body of meat, bone, and water in a cold, thin, damp, and spatially unreliable place. You can carry gear, learn local customs, and ask for help. If you have cardiac, pulmonary, or hematologic conditions, this destination is genuinely dangerous. Get medical clearance before going.

The High Held Land is not hostile. It is not trying to kill you. But it is old enough that some of the rules your body and brain rely on have begun to fray. Prepare for altitude, wet cold, botanical silence, and the slow unraveling of distance and direction.

---

## 1. First, understand what you are actually walking into

The High Held Land is a high plateau, country-sized, worn down to whale-backed stone and rubble. The air is thin. Your breath will hang white even under direct sun. Daytimes hover just above freezing; nights drop below. The cold is not the dry, brittle cold of a mountaintop. It is a damp, seeping, fog-drip cold that gets into boots, gloves, and lungs. Water is everywhere as fog, mist, rill, and seep. Rain is often so fine it walks upright. Stone sweats. Moss holds water like a sponge.

There are no animals. No birds. No insects. No fish. No beasts. The silence is not emptiness. It is a botanical quiet—plants, fungi, microbes, and a few slow, quiet people. That silence will feel wrong at a bodily level. You may feel watched. You may feel suddenly aware of your own heartbeat and breathing.

The city, called the Weave or the Tied Land, is a distributed network of drystone roundhouses, terraces, fog-nets, cisterns, and paths spread over hundreds of square miles. It is not built in straight lines. The people do not trust straight lines or right angles. They avoid them because the land is “soft” in places—places where distance, time, and direction no longer hold.

In soft places:
- A tower half a mile away may stay half a mile away no matter how long you walk.
- A path may repeat the same white boulder and same bend three times before releasing you.
- Shadows may point toward the sun.
- A person may cast two shadows, one moving late.
- Water may pause, then flow uphill.
- Frost may never melt, even at noon.
- The same fern may always be unfurling.

The people mark these places with cairns of white stone and build curved paths around them. You must learn to do the same.

## 2. Physical preparation before you leave

### Altitude
The elevation is high enough that every effort costs more oxygen than you expect. If you arrive directly from sea level, you will be short of breath walking on flat ground. You may get headache, nausea, fatigue, poor sleep. That is altitude sickness, and it can progress to dangerous cerebral or pulmonary edema.

- If possible, spend a week at moderate altitude before arrival, or use gradual multiverse staging.
- If you cannot stage, plan to do almost nothing for the first 48–72 hours after arrival.
- Consider talking to a doctor about acetazolamide (Diamox) as prevention. Start before arrival if prescribed.
- Avoid alcohol, sleeping pills, and heavy meals for the first several days.
- Hydrate aggressively. The air is damp but your breath loses water rapidly.
- Learn the symptoms of HAPE (high-altitude pulmonary edema) and HACE (high-altitude cerebral edema): cough, pink froth, severe headache, confusion, loss of coordination. If these occur, descend or seek the locals’ help—they know the land, but they may not have advanced medicine.

### Fitness
You will be walking long distances over uneven, slick, moss-covered stone. There are no pack animals. There is no vehicle. You carry everything.

Train for:
- Hiking 8–12 hours with a loaded pack.
- Walking on wet, sloping rock and thick moss.
- Balance and ankle strength.
- Cold, damp conditions—do multi-day trips in rain or fog.
- Bending, crouching, and working with your hands in gloves.

### Clothing and body
The damp cold is the main physical threat. Hypothermia can happen at just above freezing if you are wet and moving slowly.

Clothing system:
- **Base layer:** merino wool or synthetic. No cotton.
- **Midlayer:** fleece or light wool.
- **Insulation:** synthetic fill is better than down here, because the air is nearly always humid and down loses loft when damp. If you bring down, it must be water-resistant and kept dry.
- **Outer shell:** waterproof-breathable with a hood, pit zips, and taped seams. You will live in this.
- **Pants:** softshell for dry cold, hardshell for rain/fog. Consider gaiters for wet moss and rills.
- **Footwear:** waterproof leather or synthetic boots with high ankle support and stiff soles. Break them in. Bring waterproof over-socks or vapor-barrier liners.
- **Socks:** at least four pairs of merino or synthetic. Change at midday. Sleep in a dry pair.
- **Gloves:** liner gloves, insulated gloves, and waterproof overmitts. Your fingers will get cold wetting out from handling stone and fog-nets.
- **Head:** wool or synthetic hat, neck gaiter, and a hood. A wide-brim hat is not useful in wind; use a cap with chinstrap or a hood.
- **Sun protection:** high-altitude UV is strong even when the sun has no warmth. Bring high-SPF sunscreen, lip balm, and wrap-around glacier or mountaineering sunglasses.

Foot care is critical. Wet, cold, friction, and steep stone will wreck feet. Air them nightly. Use antifungal powder or cream. Treat blisters early.

## 3. Shelter and sleep gear

Nights drop below freezing. The air is humid. Everything will be wet in the morning from frost and condensation.

- **Tent:** A 4-season or sturdy 3-season freestanding tent with good ventilation. It must handle wind-driven fog and fine rain. A single-wall tent will struggle with interior condensation; a double-wall is better.
- **Sleeping bag:** comfort-rated at least 10°F / -12°C, preferably lower. Synthetic insulation is the safer choice in this wet environment.
- **Sleeping pad:** insulated, R-value 5 or higher. The stone will suck heat out of you.
- **Bivy sack:** optional but useful if you have to spend an unintended night out.
- **Groundsheet** and repair tape for tent fabric and poles.

Expect to wake up wet. Hang your gear during the brief midday dry spell, but do not expect it to fully dry. Sleep in dry base layers and a dry pair of socks. Keep tomorrow’s clothes in a waterproof sack inside your sleeping bag so they are not frozen or clammy in the morning.

## 4. Water and food

### Water
Water is abundant but not always clean. The locals use fog-nets and cisterns. You should not assume every seep is safe.

- Bring a water filter with small pore size, plus chemical purification as backup.
- Boiling works, but at altitude water boils at a lower temperature. Use a pressure cooker or treat water chemically after boiling, or simply use a good filter.
- Collect from flowing rills or dripping stone, not stagnant basins.
- Ask locals where they draw drinking water. Their cisterns are maintained.
- In a pinch, you can make a simple fog net and collect condensation. The locals can teach you.

### Food
There is no hunting, no fishing, no animal food. The local staple is breadmoss, a dense cold-adapted herb. You will probably eat it in some form.

Bring high-calorie, plant-based rations:
- Nuts, seeds, nut butters
- Dried fruit, chocolate
- Oats, quinoa, couscous, rice, or other quick-cook grains
- Lentils, beans, textured vegetable protein
- Protein bars or meal replacement bars
- Olive oil, coconut oil, or other dense fats
- Salt, electrolytes, spices, tea, coffee
- Multivitamin, B12, and iron if staying long. The local diet may be low in certain micronutrients.

Avoid bringing meat, dairy, or anything that reeks of animal. The locals have never eaten or kept animals. The smell alone would be alien. If you must bring animal products, keep them sealed and do not offer them.

Pack out all food waste. The microbial and fungal food web here is delicate. Do not bury organic waste. Do not toss crumbs into soft places.

---

## 5. Cooking and fuel

There are few trees. Wood is scarce and precious. The locals use small charcoal fires and moss-oil lamps. You should bring your own stove and enough fuel.

- A multi-fuel stove burning white gas is reliable in cold and high altitude.
- Canister stoves can work, but bring a pressure regulator and keep the canister warm at night.
- Bring a wind screen.
- Bring a pot with lid, mug, spork, and a lighter with backup matches.
- Do not cook inside a closed tent. Carbon monoxide kills.

---

## 6. Navigation, maps, and the soft places

This is where ordinary preparation fails. The High Held Land is called the Place of Wrong Distances for a reason.

- **GPS** may give contradictory or impossible readings. The land does not honor a clean coordinate grid.
- **Compass** may work normally in some places and swing wildly in others. Treat it as unreliable.
- **Local maps are not to scale.** They are drawn as remembered sequences of landmarks. A journey is measured in breaths or fogs, not miles. Accept this.
- **Straight lines are not safe.** Long straight streets invite wrongness. The locals build curved paths deliberately. Do not cut corners. Do not create your own straight trail.
- **White stone cairns** mark soft places. If you see white stone, do not enter. If you find yourself near a place where frost never melts, water flows uphill, or the same shadow appears twice, back away slowly the way you came.
- **If a path repeats**—same boulder, same bend, three times—do not push through. Stop. Turn around. Walk back exactly the way you came. If that path also repeats, sit down, stay warm, and wait for the fog to change or for a local to find you.
- **Do not trust your shadow.** Check it against the sun periodically. If it points toward the sun, or if you have two shadows, one of which lags, you are in a soft place.
- **Do not follow water uphill.** Water is supposed to flow down. If it does not, the land has forgotten which way is down, and you should not follow it.
- **Sound is not reliable.** A conversation half a mile away may arrive clearly; a footstep may be swallowed by moss. Shouting may not help, and may echo wrong. Use a whistle sparingly. Carry a small signal mirror.

The only truly reliable navigation is a local guide. Hire one. Pay them with something useful. Do not go alone if you can avoid it.

### Carry a small “wrong-place kit”:
- Whistle
- Signal mirror
- Headlamp with fresh batteries
- Bright cloth or space blanket to make yourself visible
- High-energy snack
- Water
- Small white stone (if a local gives you permission to carry one as an anchor)
- Notebook and pencil to record landmark sequences
- A note in the local language saying you are lost and need help

Do not tie yourself to a fixed object in a soft place. The object may not stay fixed.

## 7. Health and first aid

Beyond altitude, the main risks are wet cold, foot problems, and the mental strain of wrongness.

Bring a first aid kit that includes:
- Acetazolamide for altitude, dexamethasone for severe altitude illness if you know how to use it
- Ibuprofen for headache
- Antidiarrheal and rehydration salts
- Antifungal cream for feet and skin
- Antibiotic ointment
- Blister bandages and moleskin
- Elastic bandage, tape, gauze
- Splint material
- Scissors, tweezers
- Personal medications
- Hand warmers
- Space blanket or emergency bivy

No insects means no insect-borne diseases, but the constant damp can cause fungal infections. Keep skin as dry as possible. Check your feet every night.

If you get altitude sickness, do not push upward. Rest, hydrate, and if symptoms worsen, descend or ask the locals for help. They have lived here for generations and know the land’s rhythms, even if they do not have high-tech medicine.

## 8. Cultural preparation and etiquette

The people are few, slow, and quiet. They speak softly. The land is always listening. You should move through it the same way.

- **Lower your voice.** Voices carry oddly here, but silence is the default.
- **Move slowly.** Abrupt motion is not dangerous, but it is out of place.
- **Do not draw right angles** on the ground, in a notebook, or with sticks. The locals avoid right angles and long straight shapes. This may seem superstitious, but after a few days you will understand.
- **Do not ask “how far?”** Ask “how many fogs?” or “how many breaths?” The locals measure journey length that way.
- **Learn local phrases.** Even a few words—greeting, thanks, “is this path tied?”, “where is the white stone?”, “I need a guide”—will help.
- **Do not enter soft places.** Even if a local enters one, do not follow without explicit invitation and preparation.
- **Do not take stones from cairns.** White stone cairns are safety markers.
- **Do not photograph or record people without permission.** The light itself may do strange things in soft places, and the locals may have taboos about it.
- **Do not bring animals.** No dogs, no cats, no service animal, no pack animal, no pet. The altitude, cold, and absence of animal food would be cruel, and the land would not accept it. If you depend on a service animal, this destination is not safe for either of you.
- **Do not litter.** Pack out everything. The land is old and clean in its own way.
- **Offer useful gifts.** Small copper tools, awls, needles, colored plant fiber, good cordage, or a simple knife are likely valuable. Avoid high-tech gadgets unless they are clearly useful and you can explain them. A small, robust flashlight might be appreciated.
- **Do not try to “fix” the soft places.** Do not attempt to map them precisely, straighten paths, or test them. The locals have learned to live with them. Your curiosity is not more important than their safety.
- **Do not bring seeds or soil from other worlds.** The local web is single-threaded. Introducing a new plant or microbe could unravel it.

If you are offered breadmoss, accept it. If you are offered water, accept it. If you are invited into a roundhouse, remove your boots if they do, and sit where indicated. Follow the local pace.

## 9. Mental preparation

The silence will be one of the hardest things. There are no birds, no insects, no small rustling in the moss. Your brain is used to animal background noise. Here there is only weather: the hiss of mist, the drip of condensation, the crack of frost, wind over stone.

You may feel:
- Deep loneliness
- A sense of being watched
- Mild paranoia because sound carries oddly
- Disorientation from the wrongness
- Grief for the absence of animals
- A strange urge to fill the silence with noise

Do not fight these feelings. Name them, let them pass, and keep moving slowly. Bring a small object from home to hold. Keep a journal. Establish a daily routine. If you start to doubt your own shadow or sense of time, tell your guide or a local. They will understand.

The land is not malevolent. It is settling into a final form where place and time are no longer separate. You are a temporary visitor in that process. Do not pull on the threads.

## 10. First 48 hours after arrival

Assuming you arrive at a settlement or near the Weave:

- **Rest.** Do not immediately set out on a long walk. The altitude will hit you harder than you expect.
- **Find the local watchkeeper or headman.** Introduce yourself quietly. Present a small gift. Ask for a guide and current information about soft places.
- **Establish water and food.** Learn where the safe cistern is. Eat simple, warm food.
- **Observe.** Watch how the locals move, how they speak, how they avoid certain paths. Notice the white stone cairns. Notice the curved paths.
- **Check your own shadow** against the sun. You need to know what “normal” looks like here so you can recognize when it is wrong.
- **Sleep with your head slightly elevated** to help breathing. Expect a mild headache the first night. Drink water.

Do not be embarrassed to be slow. The locals are slow for a reason.

## 11. A day in the High Held Land

To prepare your intuition, imagine waking at dawn inside a small stone house or tent. Everything is dripping. Fog is pressed against the walls. Your breath is white. You dress in damp clothes, but your base layer is dry from the night. You step outside and see green moss in the hollows, gray stone everywhere else, fog moving in long silent herds. No birdsong. No insect hum. Just the drip of water and the hiss of mist.

By midmorning the sun burns through. The light turns sharp and white. Frost begins to melt. You walk slowly along a curved path. The guide ahead of you watches the ground, not the horizon. They point to a white stone cairn to the left and lead you around it. You realize the path bends for no obvious reason—but you follow. At midday you stop, eat breadmoss porridge, and watch water move through a stone-lined channel. It flows downhill. Good. You check your shadow. One shadow. Good.

In the afternoon the light grows long and strange. The air chills. Fog begins to rise from the hollows. You help mend a terrace wall for an hour, lifting stones and fitting them without mortar. The locals work slowly, speaking in low voices. Bells—small clay bells—ring once from a watch post, and everyone pauses, listens, and then continues.

Evening comes. The fog thickens. A few small lamps of moss-oil glow scattered across the darkness. No animal sound. The world is vast, cold, damp, green in patches, mostly stone and air, and mildly, thoroughly wrong. You will sleep deeply, if the altitude lets you.

## 12. Contingencies

### Lost in a soft place
- Stop moving. Do not trust distance or direction.
- If the path repeats, walk back the way you came.
- If that fails, sit down on a solid stone, wrap yourself, eat a small snack, drink water, and wait.
- Use your whistle sparingly—three short blasts.
- Do not follow water uphill.
- Do not follow a figure you see in the fog until you are certain it is real and local.
- Wait for the fog to change or for a guide.

### Altitude illness
- Rest, hydrate, and do not ascend.
- If severe headache, confusion, or breathlessness at rest, descend or seek help immediately.

### Hypothermia
- Get out of wind and wet. Change into dry clothes. Insulate from stone. Drink warm fluids. Do not fall asleep alone in the cold.

### Injury
- Sprains and falls on slick rock are likely. Use trekking poles. Move slowly. Carry a first aid kit and know how to splint.

### Running out of food
- Do not forage without a local guide. Some plants may be edible, some not. Breadmoss is safe if prepared correctly. Ask.

### Separation from group
- If you become separated, stay put if you are in a known safe area. If near a soft place, move to the nearest white stone cairn and wait.

## 13. What not to bring

- **Animals or animal products** (meat, pet food, live animals)
- **Loud speakers, drones, or noisy electronics**
- **Wheeled vehicles** (the terrain and the paths are not built for them)
- **Weapons** (there are no predators, no game, and no one to fight; a knife is a tool, not a weapon)
- **Heavy iron or steel** (you do not need it; locals use copper, stone, and clay)
- **Anything that requires a stable signal or electrical outlet**
- **An expectation that distances will behave**

---

## 14. Return and aftercare

When you leave, the contrast will be jarring. Normal air will feel thick and warm. Animal sounds may be overwhelming. Colors may seem too bright. This is normal.

- Reacclimatize gradually to lower altitude.
- Wash all gear thoroughly to remove spores, moss fragments, and soil.
- Check your feet for frostnip or fungal infection.
- Journal what you experienced, especially any soft place incidents. Memory may blur.
- If you lost time or felt the land “holding” you, note it and talk to someone you trust.
- Do not share exact locations of soft places with outsiders if the locals asked you not to.

---

## Final word

The High Held Land is not a place you conquer. It is a place that lets you pass. Go slowly, dress for wet cold, breathe small, listen to the silence, follow the curved paths, and honor the white stones. If you do, you may see something very old and very rare: a landscape that has finished most of its eroding and begun to settle into a final shape, where green basins and gray expanses hold water, light, and a wrongness that is not hostile—only ancient, and still, slowly, moving.

Prepare to be a careful guest, not a tourist. The land is listening.
