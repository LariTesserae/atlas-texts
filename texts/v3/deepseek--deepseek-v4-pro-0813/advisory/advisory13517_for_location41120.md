# Preparation for Visiting Vess and the Karrask Plateau

*Assuming you are an ordinary baseline human with multiverse transit but no local adaptations, no superhuman resilience, and no prior acclimatization to this place.*

This is not a gentle destination. It is cold, wet, dark, loud, biologically dense, and structurally disorienting. The Karrask Plateau and the city of Vess will not coddle you. The environment is not hostile in the way a toxic furnace or vacuum is hostile, but it is relentlessly uncomfortable, and it will wear down an unprepared traveler within hours. The rules are simple: wet cold kills slowly, darkness hides sharp edges, noise exhausts the mind, and the animals are everywhere.

Below is what your intuition should tell you to prepare for.

---

## 1. First, Understand What You Are Walking Into

The sky over Vess never clears. There is no daylight, no sun, no stars. There is only a low violet glow reflected from the cloud deck, the pale blue of microbial mats, the moving points of billions of small animals, and the dim cold blue-green of city lights. The air is a few degrees above freezing and so saturated that everything runs with water. Stone weeps, metal sweats, and the drizzle is so fine it seems to float. No surface is dry. No surface is still.

The soundscape is continuous and immersive: running water, dripping water, falling water, the click-chitter of animals, the patter of hull-bat sonar, the hum of wind through pipe arrays, and a subsonic thrum from the city's thermal pumps that you will feel in your sternum before you consciously hear it. There is no silence. Not at night, not indoors, not in the deepest rooms. Silence is simply not a thing here.

The ground outside the city is raw, black, glassy basalt. It is sharp. There are no rounded stones, no sand, no soft soil. Erosion has barely begun. Every fall is a potential deep cut. The open country steams where warm brine meets cold air, and the ground is alive with chemotrophic films and grazing animals that need no sun.

Vess itself is not a collection of buildings. It is one continuous, layered structure built into the rim and bowl of a caldera. Streets are narrow and roofed. Upper lanes pass over lower lanes. Buildings lean so close that their upper floors join into shared galleries. The true life of the city is interior, where the air is warmed and the water is channeled. You may go days without seeing the sky.

The animals outnumber the human inhabitants a thousand to one. They are not pests. The city has been designed for them: crawl-spaces, nesting shelves, guano traps. Sump-mites cover walkways in shimmering crowds. Pipe-eels hunt in the drains. Hull-bats roost in thousands. Rust limpets eat the metal and excrete insulating foam. Glass midges hatch in clouds so thick the automatic shutters must close. You will be touched constantly. You will never be alone.

This is the place you are visiting. Prepare for wetness first, darkness second, noise third, and the sheer overwhelming presence of life fourth.

## 2. Physical Conditioning Before Departure

You do not need to be an athlete, but you do need to be comfortable with sustained discomfort.

### Train in cold, wet conditions
Wet cold is far more dangerous than dry cold. Water conducts heat away from your body 25 times faster than air. At a few degrees above freezing, with saturated air and fine drizzle, hypothermia can creep in even when you think you are active. Before departure, spend time in a cold rain chamber or a wet cave environment. Learn how your body reacts. Learn the early signs of hypothermia: shivering, confusion, stiff fingers, poor coordination.

### Train on sharp, slippery rock
The basalt is glassy, sharp, and permanently wet. If you have never walked on wet volcanic glass, you will fall. Practice on wet artificial rock surfaces or real obsidian/basalt if available. Wear the boots you intend to bring. Practice walking with a heavy pack in near-darkness. Ankle strength and proprioception are critical.

### Train in continuous noise
The sound level in Vess is high and unrelenting. If you have never spent 24 hours in a loud industrial environment with dripping water and high-frequency clicks, you may find it mentally exhausting. Wear your hearing protection during training. Learn to sleep with earplugs or active noise-canceling earbuds. If you cannot sleep with noise, you will not function.

### Train in darkness
The darkness is not absolute—there is faint violet skyglow and bioluminescence—but it is far darker than most urban humans are used to. Practice navigating dimly lit rooms, using night vision if you bring it, and reading terrain by feel and sound. Do not expect your eyes to fully adjust; the darkness is permanent. Bring tools.

### Cardiovascular and climbing fitness
Vess is vertical. Streets are layered, lanes pass over lanes, and there are few open horizontal spaces. You will climb stairs, ramps, ladders, and narrow passages. Practice carrying your pack up and down many flights of stairs. Practice squeezing through tight spaces if possible. If you are claustrophobic, this is a serious problem.

## 3. Clothing and Personal Protective Equipment

This is the most important section. If you get wet and cold, nothing else matters.

### Base layer
- Merino wool or synthetic base layer. No cotton. Cotton kills.
- Top and bottom, close-fitting but not tight.
- Bring at least two full changes, sealed in dry bags.

### Insulation layer
- A high-loft synthetic or merino fleece mid-layer. Down is warm but useless if it gets wet; synthetic is safer. If you bring down, keep it absolutely dry in a waterproof stuff sack.
- A heated vest or jacket with spare battery packs. This is worth the weight. At just above freezing with high humidity, active heating can make the difference between functioning and hypothermia.
- Insulated pants or a warm synthetic mid-layer for legs.

### Outer shell
- Waterproof, breathable jacket and trousers. Not water-resistant—waterproof. Seams taped. Hood that fits over your headgear.
- The drizzle is so fine it will find any gap. Wrist closures, waist gaiters, and neck seals matter.
- Avoid noisy shells if possible; the city is loud enough already. But protection comes first.

### Footwear
- Insulated, waterproof boots with aggressive traction for wet rock. Mountaineering boots or high-quality approach boots with sticky rubber, but they must be insulated.
- The basalt is sharp. Boots should have a stiff sole and toe protection.
- Waterproof gaiters to keep water from entering at the ankle.
- Vapor barrier socks to keep sweat from soaking your insulation. Wear them over thin liner socks, then warm socks.
- Bring at least four pairs of socks. Your feet will get damp regardless. Change socks at every rest stop. Dry them against your body or with heat packs.
- Trench foot is a real risk. It begins in damp, cold conditions and can happen within 24–48 hours. Keep your feet as dry as possible.

### Gloves
- Waterproof insulated gloves for general use.
- Thin, grippy work gloves for handling gear, opening doors, climbing ladders.
- Spare glove liners. Your hands will get wet when you remove gloves to manipulate small objects. Practice doing tasks in the cold.

### Head and face
- Warm hat or balaclava that fits under your hood.
- Full-face shield or goggles with anti-fog coating. Glass midges can fill the air in clouds; you do not want them in your eyes, nose, or mouth.
- Mesh face cover for when midges are thick but you need to breathe without a respirator.
- A hooded shell that keeps fine drizzle off your neck and face.

### Additional protection
- Knee pads and elbow pads if you expect to crawl or climb through tight spaces. The city is dense and layered; not all routes are walkable.
- A lightweight helmet. Low ceilings, sharp rock edges, falling debris from construction, and hull-bats overhead all pose head injury risk.

## 4. Lighting and Vision

The locals keep the dark. Light is for close work, not for the sky. You must bring your own light, but you must use it respectfully.

### Bring
- A dim, shielded headlamp with low-lumen red or blue-green modes. Blue-green is locally common and tuned not to blind the animals. Red is less intrusive to your own night vision. Use the lowest setting that allows you to see.
- A small handheld flashlight for emergencies, with a tight beam you can point downward.
- Chem lights for marking your route or your campsite. Choose blue or green, not bright white.
- Night vision monocular or goggles. Light amplification works well here because there is faint violet skyglow and bioluminescence. Thermal imaging is also very useful for spotting warm brine, hot pipes, animals, and people in the dark.
- Spare batteries, power packs, and desiccant packs. Cold and humidity drain electronics faster than you expect. Keep everything in waterproof cases.
- A small mirror or marker panel if you need to be seen.

### Rules
- Never shine a light at the sky. The cloud deck reflects it, and it can be seen far away. It violates local custom and may disturb the animals.
- Never shine a light into animal roosts, nesting shelves, or the eyes of hull-bats. Use low light and point down.
- Most street lights are dim or off in animal quarters. Do not expect public lighting.
- Your eyes will adapt somewhat, but the dark remains. Use night vision rather than flooding the space with white light.

## 5. Hearing Protection

The noise in Vess is not a background nuisance; it is a physical force. Water roars through channels, wind hums through pipe arrays, hull-bats click, and the city's pumps produce a subsonic thrum you feel in your chest. Long-term exposure without protection will damage your hearing and exhaust you mentally.

### Bring
- Electronic earplugs or earmuffs with flat attenuation. These reduce loud sounds while allowing speech and some environmental sound through. They are ideal for navigating and communicating.
- Backup foam earplugs. They are cheap, lightweight, and work.
- Active noise-canceling earbuds or a white-noise machine for sleeping. Even with earplugs, the subsonic hum may keep you awake. A small device that generates a consistent low-noise mask can help.
- Extra batteries for electronic earpro.

### Acclimation
- Wear hearing protection from day one. Do not wait until you notice hearing fatigue.
- Some areas will be quieter than others, but there is no true silence. Lower your expectations.
- The subsonic thrum can cause discomfort, nausea, or anxiety in some people. If you feel pressure in your chest or a sense of dread, move to a shielded rest area if possible. This is a physical response, not weakness.

## 6. Breathing and Air Quality

The air is cold, saturated, and full of particles: spores from fungal spires, bacteria from silt-skin, guano dust from hull-bats, fine mineral dust from basalt, and clouds of glass midges. Warm brine areas may release sulfur compounds, possibly hydrogen sulfide.

### Bring
- An elastomeric respirator with P3 particulate filters. This will protect against spores, bacteria, and midges.
- Acid-gas cartridges or a combined particulate/gas filter for sulfur compounds and ammonia. If you smell rotten eggs, leave the area or don the gas protection.
- Disposable N95/FFP2 masks for lower-risk situations.
- A small air quality monitor that detects H2S, CO, O2 levels, and particulates. Basalt geology and warm brine can produce pockets of low oxygen or toxic gas, especially in depressions or enclosed spaces.
- Fit-test your respirator before departure. A poor seal is worse than no mask because it gives false confidence.

### Practical notes
- The glass midges are small enough to get into your nose and mouth. A mesh face cover is enough in many situations. A respirator is better when clouds are dense.
- If you have asthma, allergies, or any respiratory condition, this environment will be difficult. Bring extra medication and consider whether this destination is wise.
- The constant wet air may cause bronchial irritation in some people. Warm your breathing air by wearing a loose face covering or balaclava over your mouth when possible.

## 7. Food, Water, and Biological Waste

There are no plants on Karrask. No leaves, no roots, no fruit, no grains. All local food is microbial, mineral, or animal. Your ordinary human digestive system is not adapted to it. Do not experiment.

### Food
- Bring all the food you will need for your entire stay, plus a reserve of at least 20 percent extra.
- Choose high-calorie, high-fat, low-volume foods. Cold environments demand more energy.
- Dehydrated meals are fine if you have a reliable way to heat water. Bring a small stove or use local heated water if available.
- Energy bars, nuts, chocolate, dried meat, cheese, and vitamin supplements are good. Avoid foods that crumble easily; crumbs attract animals and can contaminate the environment.
- Seal all food in odor-proof, animal-proof containers. Sump-mites and other small creatures will find any opening.
- Vitamin D supplementation is essential. There is no sunlight. Your body will not produce it naturally here.

### Water
- Do not drink untreated water. The water is cold, briny, sulfurous, and full of microbial life. It may contain iron, sulfur, and unknown minerals.
- Bring a water purification system: a 0.1-micron filter, chemical purification tablets or drops, and ideally a small desalinator if the water is brackish.
- Collect rain or drizzle if possible, but still filter it. The air is not clean.
- If the city provides potable water, use it if you trust the source. But do not assume.
- Bring insulated water bottles and a thermos. Cold water will chill you from the inside. Warm drinks help maintain core temperature.
- Electrolyte tablets or powder. You will sweat under your waterproof layers, and you may not notice how much fluid you lose.

### Waste
- Your gut bacteria are foreign. Your shed skin cells, hair, and breath carry microbes that may not exist in this biosphere. Do not contaminate the local ecology.
- Bring sealable toilet bags and a compact waste system. Pack out all solid waste if possible.
- Do not discard food scraps or biological waste into the city's water channels. The local microbial mats and animals are part of the city's balance.
- If you must use a local facility, ask how to do so properly. Follow local hygiene rules strictly.

## 8. Medical Kit

This is a wet, cold, sharp, biologically active environment. Minor problems become major ones quickly.

### Must include
- Hypothermia treatment: chemical heat packs, emergency bivy, space blanket.
- Wound care: antiseptic wipes, sterile gauze, medical tape, butterfly closures, skin glue, hydrocolloid dressings, waterproof bandages.
- Trench foot prevention and treatment: antifungal powder, barrier cream, extra dry socks, vapor barrier socks.
- Blister treatment: moleskin, blister pads.
- Eye wash: saline solution, sterile eye cups. Glass midges and spores will get in your eyes.
- Antihistamines: for bites, stings, or allergic reactions to spores and animal proteins.
- Anti-inflammatory and painkillers.
- Antibiotics: only if prescribed and appropriate. Standard human antibiotics may not work on alien microbes. The best defense is cleaning wounds immediately and preventing infection.
- Epi-pen if you have any known allergies.
- Ear drops if you are prone to ear infections from constant moisture.
- A small mirror to inspect your face, eyes, and wounds in the dark.

### Notes
- The basalt is sharp. A fall can cause deep, dirty cuts. The microbial films on the rock may be alien. Clean any wound immediately with antiseptic and cover it. Seek local medical care if the wound is deep or shows signs of infection.
- If you feel confusion, extreme fatigue, or shivering that does not stop, suspect hypothermia. Get to a warm dry space, remove wet clothing, and warm your core.
- The constant moisture and darkness can worsen mood and immune function. Rest, eat, and stay dry whenever possible.

## 9. Navigation and Communication

Vess is a three-dimensional maze. The city is one continuous structure. Streets are roofed, lanes cross over lanes, and there are no open plazas. You will get lost.

### Navigation tools
- GPS will likely not work under the cloud deck and deep inside the city. Do not rely on it.
- Bring a rugged tablet or phone with offline maps if available. Load any local maps or schematics you can obtain.
- An inertial navigation system or local beacon receiver is ideal. If you can access local tech, ask for a navigation aid.
- A compass may work if the local magnetic field is stable, but inside the city the basalt and metal structures may interfere. Use it only as a backup.
- Mark your route with chem lights, reflective tags, or removable marker pens if allowed. But do not permanently alter the city.
- Consider hiring a local guide. No map will prepare you for the reality of the layered streets and animal quarters.

### Communication
- Bring a translator device loaded with local languages. You will need to ask for help.
- Arrange for a local sponsor or contact before arrival. This is crucial. Doors in Vess recognize you by the weight of your breath. As an outsider, you will not be registered. A local will need to register your breath signature or escort you through doorways.
- Bring an emergency beacon with multiverse link. Test it on arrival, but do not assume it will work deep inside the city. High-density materials and water may block signals.
- Learn basic local phrases: "Where is the dry hall?", "I need warmth", "I am lost", "Do not harm the animals", "I mean no harm."

### Practical navigation
- The city is dim. Landmarks are subtle: the sound of a particular water channel, the glow of a nutrient vat, the texture of a wall. Pay attention.
- Do not enter unfinished construction zones. They may look like streets but end in sharp edges or active machinery.
- If you are lost, stop. Use your beacon. Mark your location with a chem light. Wait for help if possible.

## 10. Cultural and Social Preparation

The people of Vess are not hostile, but they are not gentle. They live in a slow, patient rhythm. The city has been under construction for centuries. The ground shifts. Water must keep moving or it will freeze and split the stone. Animals are not pests; they are the city's other citizens. Heat is the only currency that matters.

### Rules to follow
- **Keep the dark.** Light is for close work, not for the sky. Use dim light. Do not shine it upward or into animal quarters.
- **The animals are not pests.** Do not kill, swat, poison, or intentionally harm them. If you step on sump-mites, it may be unavoidable, but do not go out of your way. Accept that they will crawl on you.
- **Build slow.** Do not rush locals. Do not treat unfinished construction as failure. Do not demand quick answers.
- **Heat is currency.** Bring tradeable heat or energy: high-density power cells, thermal batteries, or useful skills. Arrange credit or barter before arrival.
- **Water must keep moving.** Do not block drains, channels, or pipes. This is not just a rule; it is survival. Still water freezes and splits the stone.
- **Respect the interior.** Vess is a single continuous structure. You are inside the body of the city. Do not vandalize, carve, or alter surfaces without permission.

### Practical social advice
- Find a local contact who can register your breath signature for door access. Without this, you will be locked out of many areas.
- Hire a guide familiar with the animal quarters and the layered streets.
- Observe how locals move through spaces, how they walk among the animals, and how they use light. Imitate them.
- Be patient. The pace of life is slow. Do not interpret a long pause as rudeness.
- If you are offered local food, ask if it is safe for an unadapted human. If unsure, decline politely.

## 11. Psychological Preparation

This is the part most travelers underestimate. The physical challenges are obvious. The psychological ones are not.

### Darkness
There is no sun. No dawn. No blue sky. The sky is a permanent, low violet lid. The darkness is not absolute, but it is constant. For a human used to daylight, this can cause a slow, grinding depression. Bring vitamin D, use a light box in your private quarters if you have one, and maintain a regular sleep schedule. But understand that you will miss the sun.

### Noise
The roar of water, the clicks of animals, the subsonic thrum of the pumps—it never stops. It will wear you down. You may feel tense, anxious, or exhausted without knowing why. Use hearing protection even when resting. Create a small island of quiet in your own head.

### No green
There are no plants. No leaves, no grass, no trees. The only growth is pale microbial film and fleshy fungal spires. For many humans, the absence of green is subtly distressing. Bring a digital image of a forest, a small object made of wood, or anything that reminds you of a photosynthesizing world. You may need it.

### Constant contact with animals
The animals are everywhere. Sump-mites shimmer on walkways. Glass midges fill the air. Hull-bats click overhead. You will be touched by tiny legs, wings, or antennae constantly. If you are squeamish or have a strong aversion to insects or crawling things, this will be very hard. Prepare mentally. The animals are not dangerous; they are the city's life.

### Claustrophobia
The city's true life is interior. Streets are roofed, lanes are narrow, buildings lean together. You may go days without seeing the sky. If you are claustrophobic, this is a serious risk. Visit the rim periodically to look out across the plateau, but even there the sky is low and dark. There is no open blue.

### The slow pulse
Everything in Vess operates on long timescales. The plateau rises centimeters per century. Buildings take two hundred years. Animal populations cycle over decades. If you are used to fast results, this will feel unsettling. There is no rush. The city is still being born. You are a brief visitor.

## 13. Arrival and First Hours

When you arrive, do not linger in the open air. The drizzle and cold will begin to soak you immediately. Find your shelter or secure a dry interior space as soon as possible.

1. **Check your air quality monitor.** Ensure the air is safe. If not, don your respirator.
2. **Check your body.** Are your feet dry? If not, change socks. Are your hands warm? If not, use heat packs.
3. **Seal your food and gear.** The animals will investigate anything new. Do not leave food open.
4. **Meet your local contact.** Register your breath signature for door access. Learn the nearest safe routes.
5. **Acclimate slowly.** Your first day should be low exertion. Stay inside, let your ears and eyes adjust. Test your night vision and hearing protection.
6. **Learn the soundscape.** Identify the different water sounds, the sonar clicks of hull-bats, the hum of pumps. The city speaks in noise. You will learn to read it.
7. **Do a gear check.** Dry any electronics that may have been exposed to humidity. Charge your batteries if local power is available.

## 14. Emergency Planning

### Hypothermia
- Symptoms: shivering, slurred speech, confusion, poor coordination, fatigue.
- Action: get to warm dry interior. Remove wet clothing. Warm core with heat packs and warm drinks. Do not rub extremities. Seek help if severe.

### Injury from sharp basalt
- Action: control bleeding, clean wound with antiseptic, cover with waterproof dressing. Elevate if possible. Seek local medical care for deep or dirty wounds.

### Lost
- Action: stop. Mark your location with a chem light or beacon. Do not wander. Use your emergency beacon. If you have a local contact, call for help.

### Gas exposure
- Symptoms: rotten egg smell (H2S), headache, dizziness, nausea, shortness of breath.
- Action: don respirator with gas cartridges. Move upwind or to higher ground. Seek filtered shelter. Leave the area if symptoms persist.

### Animal incident
- The animals are not aggressive, but a startled hull-bat or a dense cloud of glass midges can cause panic. Move slowly, shield your eyes, and wait. Do not swat. If bitten or stung, clean the wound and take antihistamine if needed. Seek local advice.

## Final Intuition

You will be a wet, cold, blind, deafened, overwhelmed mammal in a city that is more like a living machine than a human settlement. The plateau is raw and indifferent. The city is patient and full of other lives. If you prepare well—if you keep your feet dry, your light dim, your hearing protected, and your mind steady—you can survive and perhaps even begin to hear the slow pulse under the roar.

But do not come expecting comfort. Do not come expecting silence. Do not come expecting green things or sunlight. Come with heat, patience, and respect. The city will not notice you. The water will keep running. The animals will keep moving. The dark will stay.

And if you do not prepare, the Karrask Plateau will not be hostile. It will simply continue, wet and black and loud and cold, as if you were never there.
