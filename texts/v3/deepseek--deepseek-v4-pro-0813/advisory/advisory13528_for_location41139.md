Pack for a place that will feel like a sauna full of sleeping machines. You will step into air the same temperature as your skin, and within minutes every exposed surface—including your face, your eyelids, your hair—will be covered in a fine, warm film of condensation. The light will give you no shadows. The quiet will be so complete that your own breathing, clothes, and heartbeat will sound intrusively loud. Animals will be underfoot, overhead, wedged into pipe brackets, hanging in rows, holding still in the water. Prepare to be a polite, temporary, self-cooling, self-navigating visitor inside a closed loop that does not need you and will not change for you.

Below is a full preparation guide for an ordinary human multiverse traveler.

---

## 1. Understand what you are walking into

This is not a wilderness. It is not a ruins. It is a warm, wet, low, enormous factory-world that appears to have forgotten any outside. The ground is machine decking, tiles, copper mesh, and composite plates. The “weather” is one condition repeated forever: near body-temperature, near-total humidity, no wind, no sun, no seasons.

The main dangers are not violent animals or storms. They are:

- **Wet heat** — sweat will not evaporate. Your body’s cooling system will fail unless you bring active cooling.
- **Electricity** — busbars, cables, conduits, and wet grounded metal are everywhere. Treat everything as possibly energized.
- **Slipping and falling** — every surface is wet. There are flooded galleries, sharp weirs, low overhead clearances, and multi-level drops.
- **Disorientation** — flat shadowless light, repeating terraces, no visible sun or horizon, and a landscape that never changes.
- **Biological contamination** — the place has a closed, precise ecology. You are a foreign warm wet object. You must not damage it, and you must not let it damage you.
- **Psychological stasis** — the place appears to have removed time. Visitors can sink into a kind of trance, monotony, or derealization.

The animals are dense but not aggressive. They do not scatter. They do not call. They are not interested in you except as a new surface. That does not mean you should touch them, sit on them, eat them, or collect them.

## 2. Pre-departure physical and mental conditioning

An ordinary human needs to prepare the body and mind.

### Heat acclimation

Spend at least two to three weeks before departure doing heat acclimation in high humidity:

- Sauna, steam room, or hot humid chamber sessions.
- Light exercise in warm, humid conditions while wearing your intended base layers.
- Learn your personal signs of heat exhaustion: confusion, nausea, heavy legs, sudden cessation of sweating, headache, irritability.

The goal is not to become immune. The goal is to recognize your limits and know when to stop.

### Foot and joint conditioning

You will be standing and walking on hard, wet, often ridged metal and composite surfaces. There is no soft ground anywhere. Condition your feet, ankles, knees, and lower back with loaded pack walks on hard surfaces and stairs.

### Confined-space tolerance

The place is vast but also dense and layered. Gaps between levels are low. Walkways are narrow. Pipes run in thick clusters. If you have claustrophobia, practice working in low, tight, humid spaces before you go.

### Mental rehearsal

Read the description repeatedly. Imagine the flat gray-white sky, the constant drip, the complete absence of green, the animals packed like living machinery. Accept that the place will not respond to you. Accept that nothing will change while you are there. That acceptance is protective.

## 3. Clothing and wearable gear

### General rule

Wear synthetic, quick-drying, antimicrobial, high-visibility clothing. No cotton. No leather. No wool. No plant fibers.

The environment is already saturated with water and organic films. Natural fibers will hold moisture, chafe, grow odor, and may introduce foreign organic material.

### Base layer

- Long-sleeve synthetic top and long pants. The skin should be covered for abrasion, electrical arc, and animal contact reasons.
- Light color or high-vis orange/yellow with reflective strips. The light is flat and shadowless; you want to be visible to your team.
- Sealed cuffs and ankles. Small hexapods cluster thickly at rail bases and stanchions. They are not aggressive, but they may crawl onto you. Wrist gaiters, ankle gaiters, and a neck gaiter help.

### Outer/protective layer

You do not need insulation. You need:

- A thin waterproof or highly water-resistant shell with a hood for constant dripping from above. But it must be loose enough to allow air circulation.
- Or a hooded single-layer worksuit made of water-shedding synthetic material. The goal is to keep condensation off your skin without trapping heat.
- A wide-brim hard hat or bump cap with a neck flap. There are low beams, cable trays, pipe clusters, and chainfalls at head height.
- Anti-fog goggles or wraparound safety glasses. They will fog immediately. Bring anti-fog wipes and a dry microfiber cloth sealed in a waterproof bag.
- A fine mesh face hood or bug net. The air is full of small flying things holding station. They do not seem to bite, but you do not want them in your mouth, nose, or eyes.

### Footwear

- Waterproof, composite-toe, non-metallic, puncture-resistant boots.
- Non-slip, aggressive tread suitable for wet metal and smooth vitrified tile.
- Non-metallic is important because of possible electrical hazard.
- Calf gaiters to prevent water and small animals from entering.
- Vibration-damping insoles. The ground carries a low transformer hum.

### Gloves

Bring at least two types:

1. **Cut- and puncture-resistant work gloves** for general movement. Every edge may be sharp, crisp, newly made.
2. **Insulated rubber electrical gloves** with leather protectors if you must handle or steady yourself on metal structures. In a wet environment with busbars and cables, a metal railing may not be safe.

Do not touch your face with gloves after touching biofilms or animals.

### Cooling system

This is essential. The air is close to body heat and saturated. Sweat will not cool you. Bring:

- A phase-change cooling vest with replaceable PCM packs pre-charged to below skin temperature.
- A neck cooling collar.
- Cooling wristbands or a cooling towel, though evaporative cooling will not work well; phase change is better.
- Battery-powered active cooling garment if available to a multiverse traveler.

Bring spare PCM packs in an insulated container. Without active cooling, limit continuous physical work to short intervals.

## 4. Hydration, food, and breathing

### Hydration

- Bring your own water. Do not drink local water. The channels, puddles, and condensation may contain industrial chemicals, biofilms, animal waste, and unknown minerals.
- Drink on a schedule, even if you do not feel thirsty. In saturated heat, thirst perception can be misleading.
- Use electrolyte replacement, not only plain water, to avoid hyponatremia.
- Monitor urine color if possible. Dark urine means you are falling behind.

### Food

- Bring sealed, low-odor, high-energy food that does not crumble.
- Eat only in a clean area, ideally inside a small disposable groundsheet or hood. Crumbs could introduce foreign organic matter.
- No open food near animals. They are packed everywhere and may investigate by slow contact.
- Do not feed anything.

### Air quality

The air smells of warm metal, ozone, iodine, and a clean aquarium left in the sun. It may be breathable, but:

- Carry a personal 4-gas monitor for oxygen, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, and ozone.
- Ozone is a lung irritant. If you smell it strongly or the monitor rises, use a respirator with an ozone cartridge or leave the area.
- The humidity may feel heavy in the lungs. If you have asthma or reactive airways, use a heat-moisture exchanger mask or a powered air-purifying respirator with a humidity exchanger.
- Do not enter enclosed housings, pipe galleries, or low unventilated pockets without checking the air. The place is a continuous interior, and gas may not disperse.

## 5. Navigation and communication

### The navigation problem

- There is no sun, no moon, no stars.
- The light is flat and shadowless. Depth perception will be poor.
- The horizon is a wall of stacked pipeways and gantries fading into vapor.
- The landscape is vast, stepped, repetitive, and dense with machinery.
- Compasses may be affected by electrical fields. GPS may be blocked by overhead metal and interference.
- It is easy to become lost in a place where every terrace looks identical.

### Navigation equipment

- Multiple redundant devices: GPS, inertial navigation, compass, pedometer.
- A strong flashlight or headlamp to create artificial shadows. Use it even in the flat “noon” light.
- A laser line projector or laser rangefinder to judge distance and surface texture.
- Physical breadcrumb markers: high-visibility tags, reflective tape, small weighted cones, or IR beacons.
- A spool of bright line or micro-cord to lay down as a physical return trail in complex areas.
- A waterproof notebook and pencil for sketching route features. Pens may fail in humidity.
- A wrist-mounted mechanical watch and a separate digital timer. The place may affect electronics, so do not rely on one clock.

### Communication

- Metal and possible electromagnetic fields may reduce radio range. Use a mesh radio system with drop nodes.
- Agree on a simple acoustic or line-tug code with your team.
- Carry a personal locator beacon and a strobe.
- Do not go alone. Use a buddy system.

### Timekeeping

The place seems to have removed time. The same drop falls from the same pipe. The same ripple stops at the same four heads. You will not feel the passage of time naturally.

- Set a vibrating countdown timer for every task.
- Use time-stamped photos or audio notes.
- If you notice you have been standing still watching water for a long time, it is time to rest, hydrate, and reorient.
- Establish a hard turnaround time before entering. Do not extend it except for emergency.

## 6. Safety around the industrial environment

### Electrical

This may be the single greatest physical hazard.

- Treat every metal surface as potentially energized unless tested.
- Carry a non-contact voltage tester.
- Do not touch busbars, cables, valve stems, conduit, or standing water near electrical equipment.
- Water is continuous across surfaces. A fault far away could use the wet film as a path.
- Use insulated tools and insulated gloves if you must handle metal.
- Do not open panels, turn valves, or operate switches. The place is in a standing state. Disturbing it may trigger machinery or release energy.

### Slips, trips, and falls

- Every surface is wet. Condensation runs in continuous films. Puddles look like smoked glass.
- Walk slowly and deliberately. Test footing with a walking stick or probe before committing weight.
- Do not step on animal clusters. They are slippery, soft, and may move slowly.
- Avoid flooded galleries and channels. They may be deeper than they look, and the water is dense with animals.
- Use three points of contact on ladders and stairs.
- Watch for low overhead clearances. Hard hat is mandatory.

### Sharp edges

The landscape is crisp, new, and industrial. Edges are sharp. Pipes, brackets, non-slip ridges, stamped copper mesh, and cable trays can cut. Wear cut-resistant gloves and long sleeves.

### Confined spaces

Do not enter pipe housings, valve stations, or areas where the ceiling drops below comfortable standing height without training and air monitoring. The place is a continuous interior; access routes may narrow suddenly.

## 7. Interacting with the local biology

### The animals are not pests and not pets

They are a standing population, exactly balanced against food and habitat. They do not grow visibly, fight, or call. They fill every available hold.

You must not:

- Step on them if avoidable.
- Collect them, even dead ones.
- Feed them.
- Move them more than necessary.
- Introduce your own biology to them.

### If animals climb onto you

They may investigate your warmth, moisture, or salt. Do not panic. Brush them off gently with a soft brush or gloved hand. Do not crush them. Do not slap.

### Biofilms

The gray, pink, and amber films follow condensation lines. They may be harmless or may be irritants. Avoid touching them. If contact occurs, clean the area with your own sterile wipes. Do not use local water.

### No plants

There are no plants. Do not bring seeds, spores, soil, or any plant matter. Your boots, gear, and clothes must be cleaned before entry. A single introduced grass seed or fungal spore could disrupt an ecology that has no plants at all.

### Human waste

Pack out everything. Urine, feces, sweat, food wrappers, hair, skin flakes—all of it is foreign input. Use waste bags, WAG bags, and sealed containers. Do not spit. Do not leave even a tissue.

## 8. Rest and camp protocol

If you must stay long enough to rest:

- Do not sit or lie directly on animal-covered surfaces.
- Use a portable cot or hammock with its own stand.
- Place the legs on the barest possible deck area, preferably on a small groundsheet.
- Use a smooth, non-permeable groundsheet with upturned edges to create a rest zone.
- Give animals time to move away from the area before setting up. Do not crush or peel them off.
- Use a fine mesh net around your rest area to keep flying things off.
- Sleep in shifts. One person should remain alert, monitoring time, air, and team condition.
- Use an eye mask and earplugs only if safe; you may need to hear changes in the hum or team signals.

### Choosing a safe rest site

- Avoid channels and flooded galleries.
- Avoid direct proximity to electrical equipment.
- Avoid areas where the overhead levels are so low you cannot stand.
- Choose a spot with two or more clear escape routes.
- Mark the site with beacons and reflective tags.

## 9. Medical and emergency preparation

### Heat illness

This is likely your greatest medical risk.

- Know the signs: confusion, irritability, nausea, headache, dizziness, muscle cramps, hot dry skin, or sudden heavy sweating.
- Treatment: stop activity, move to a shaded or cooler spot if possible, use active cooling packs, hydrate with electrolytes.
- If core temperature rises severely, you need evacuation. Do not push through.

### First aid kit

- Blister care.
- Antiseptic wipes and ointment.
- Broad-spectrum antibiotic ointment for cuts.
- Bandages, gauze, medical tape.
- Antihistamines for contact reactions.
- Eye wash in sealed sterile containers.
- Heat illness supplies: cooling packs, electrolyte powder.
- Personal medications.
- Splinter/tick-style removal tools for small animals or metal fragments.

### Emergency extraction

- Carry a personal locator beacon.
- Pre-arrange an extraction route and signal.
- If you become disoriented, injured, or heat-sick, call for extraction early. The place will not help you.

## 10. Psychological preparation and conduct

This is the part most travelers underestimate.

### The silence

The place should roar with so much metal, water, and animal life, but it does not. There is a low hum in the soles of your feet. Water ticks. A shell clicks once. The quiet is full to the point of stillness.

You may feel the urge to make noise to prove you are still there. That is normal. But be deliberate. Your voice will sound strange in the damp, muffled air.

### The sameness

Nothing changes. The same drop falls. The same ripple stops. The animals breathe in unison without sound. Time appears to have been removed.

This can cause:

- A feeling that you are becoming part of the machine.
- A loss of urgency.
- Confusion about how long you have been there.
- Mild derealization or panic.

### Countermeasures

- Use scheduled check-ins with a buddy.
- Keep a mission log with time-stamped entries.
- Carry a small personal object from home. Look at it, hold it, remember outside.
- Use breathing exercises.
- If you feel the place “taking over,” stop, rehydrate, check your timer, and call your buddy.
- Do not attempt to “fix” or alter anything. Accept that you are a temporary observer.

### The animals

They are wrong in small ways. Eyes on leg joints. Twelve soft wings. Mouths that open as slits along the neck. Shells that mimic riveted seams and hexagonal plates.

Do not stare too long. Do not try to make sense of them. They are not hostile. They are simply present, as permanent as the pipework.

## 11. Entry and exit procedures

### Before entry

- Full body and gear cleaning. Remove soil, seeds, fibers, and organic residue.
- Check all equipment: lights, radio, gas monitor, cooling vest, timer.
- Brief the team on route, turnaround time, and emergency signals.
- Confirm air quality and electrical safety at the entry point.

### During the visit

- Move slowly.
- Test surfaces before trusting them.
- Stay on marked paths or lay a physical trail.
- Do not eat or drink in animal-dense areas.
- Monitor your own condition and your buddy’s.

### Exit and decontamination

- Clean boots and gear before leaving the immediate area.
- Inspect all seams, cuffs, and packs for stowaway animals or biofilms.
- Pack out all waste.
- After exit, change into clean clothes in a contained area.
- Disinfect equipment with a residue-free method.
- Dry everything thoroughly; the moisture from the place can breed your own molds later.
- Quarantine anything that touched local biofilms until you know it is safe.
- Log any changes you observed, even if you think you saw nothing.

---

## 12. Recommended first-visit limits

For an ordinary human traveler:

- First visit: no more than 4–6 hours.
- Do not sleep there on the first visit.
- Use two-person minimum.
- Stay within sight of your entry route or physical trail.
- Do not enter flooded galleries, pipe housings, or confined spaces.
- If you feel heat illness, disorientation, or overwhelming unease, leave early.

The place is not going anywhere. It will still be there, exactly the same, if you return.
