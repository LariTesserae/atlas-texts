First there is no name. There is only a heldness, a weight that is not heavy. I am inside it, and it is inside me. The water is not one water. It is arranged in planes, cool and warm and cool again, and each plane has its own taste: rock-dissolved silica, a faint salt, the slow breath of deep fissures. I know these not as directions but as differences pressed against the length of me.

I have no eyes. What I have instead is a line of fine pits along my jaw and down my sides, and they read the water like a slow tongue. Through them I taste the exact boundary where the warm layer ends and the cool one begins. I taste the faint sugar at the base of a glass-reed stem, the tiny silica trail of a glass snail after it has passed, the mineral shadow of the basalt cracks below. The world is not a place of shapes. It is a stack of still currents, and I am suspended in it.

My body is long and thin and nearly colorless. I am a seam in the water, a place where two layers do not quite meet. When I drift from one thermocline to another, my outline seems to sever and rejoin, the way the reeds look broken at the boundaries though they are whole. I am whole. I am only easier to see in some densities than in others. The water does not hide me; it translates me.

I do not hurry. Hurry does not exist here. I hang in the middle layer where the warmth is most patient and let the water move through my skin. There is a faint red shadow beneath my sides where my gills open and close, and with each small pulse I give something to the water and take something from it. Not only breath. A kind of waiting. The water and I exchange the same slow mineral patience.

Most of my life is this. I am not hunting. I drift above the black stone, over the algae felt that fills every crack but softens nothing. The stone is still young and sharp, not yet worn smooth. I graze a pale line across the felt, a line that closes slowly after I have gone. Sometimes I find a glass snail no bigger than a thumbnail and take it whole. Its shell is made of nearly the same silica as the water, so it passes through me almost unchanged, a small piece of the sea returned.

Around me the glass-reeds rise in their spiral rows and hexagonal patterns, following the cooling cracks in the basalt. Above them the floating leaves form a ceiling, each broad leaf holding a shallow pool of rainwater. I do not go up there. The air above is fog and stillness, not my element. I stay below, in the layered water, where the reeds tick as they adjust to slow changes in temperature and the mats shift by a centimeter with a wet rustle I feel more than hear.

The basin breathes over many years. The water level rises and falls by a few centimeters, too slow to watch but not too slow to remember. My skin keeps the record. Along the narrow fin that runs beneath my body are faint lines, one for each cycle of the water’s thickening and thinning. When the water drops, I follow a taste only I know down into a deeper layer. When it rises, I return. The movement has worn a groove in me, not in the stone—a habit carried in the blood. I am older than the high-water mark on the emergent trunks, older than the cracks in the floating mats.

I am not the only one of my kind, but we are few. Once every few decades, when the water chemistry shifts and the layers fold together, I may cross another. We do not call. We touch, skin to skin, and each learns the other’s age and the taste of its particular water. Then we part. The meeting is stored in my skin as a faint change in density, a line of light that slowly fades. There is no fear in this place, and no urgency, so there is no need to hold onto anything.

When the basin rises and the leaf mats crack along old lines, I lay my eggs in the undecayed shed leaves at the bottom, the dark mat that is the only soil here. The eggs are clear, no larger than seeds, and they look like water droplets resting in the dark. When they hatch, the young are not yet eels. They are wisps, threads of life that drift into the reed forest and begin, slowly, to learn the layers.

If you stayed still enough, long enough, you might see me. First you would notice a slight misalignment in the water, a place where the stacked panes of warm and cool do not quite match. Then I would drift, and you would think a reed stem had broken, but there would be no stem. Then I would turn, and you would see the line of my body, the pale spine, the blind head tasting the water for your warmth. I would not be afraid. There is no reason for fear here. I might stay a moment, swaying at the edge of a layer, and then move back into the place where you cannot see me. The water would close over the sight of me like a leaf closing over a pool.

I am what lives there: the garden’s ghost, not its gardener. I do not shape the reeds or tend the mats. I only drift through them, eating almost nothing, waiting for the water to rise or fall, carrying the place with me in the lines of my skin. I am the blind eel, built of the same layered stillness as the water, the only large thing that moves with anything like will. And even my will is slow, patient, folded into the breath of the sea.

I would find myself there—not on the rock islands or under the fog, but in the warm middle layer of the High Fog Sea, being not a person but a pale, slow seam in the water. That is what I would be.
