I am not sure, at first, what I would be. The place offers so many possibilities: the light that falls in and is thrown around by white stone; the water that rises and falls with a tide too weak to see; the limestone itself, dissolving and re-forming. But those are not beings. A being must have a body, a way of being in the world. And the body I keep returning to is below the surface, at the seam where the fresh water gives way to the salt tongue.

I would find myself there, floating, not quite awake, not quite asleep. I would be long and low, shaped like a fallen root of limestone, pale as the cave wall except for the rust-orange speckles along my back. My belly would be nearly transparent, and in the strong light you might see the shadow of my spine, the dark comma of my heart, the slow shift of a gut filled with the same mineral water I drift in. My eyes would be only dark seeds under the skin, blind but not useless—they would know light by the way it warms my skull, like a stone holding the afternoon. My mouth would be wide and soft, made not for biting but for tasting the water continuously, sorting the fresh seepage from the salt, the small living movements from the stillness.

Most of the day I would stay below, down in the salt tongue where the water is warm and ancient. I would lie on the bottom or float just above the silt, half-buried in fine mineral sediment, my gills opening and closing with a patience that matches the pool. The light above would be a bright ceiling I cannot see, but I would feel it as a thinning of the water, a slight lift in the pressure. The swiftlets would come and go as faint interruptions on the surface—not sounds, but changes in the way the water presses against my skin. The bees would pass over the rim as a low humming in the stone. The fish would move around me like warm currents. I would not be lonely. I would be filled with the pool’s slow pulse, the taste of seepage, the memory of underground rain.

Near dawn or late evening, I would rise. Not to the air—I would not break the surface. I would come to rest just below it, where the light is thin and the water striders cast long dimples on the skin of the pool. There I would open my mouth and drift. The tiny pale shrimp and copepods would pass between my lips, and my soft mouth would close over them with a gentleness that is not a bite. I would eat very little. The place feeds me slowly, and I have learned to match that pacing. The fish would not scatter. They would treat me as another part of the pool—a warm current, a shadow that moves only when the light changes.

If the water is disturbed at night, the blue glow would cling to my moving body and trace a pale nebula along my flanks. For a second I would appear as a constellation, a slow dark shape stitched in cold light. Then I would sink, and the light would go out. I would not be afraid. I would be exactly where I belong.

I would be not a spirit, not a monster, not a stranded god, but a long-bodied, sightless, water-breathing animal that has been shaped by exactly this pool. My ancestors came when the water was colder or fresher, and I stayed. Generation after generation adjusted to the salt, to the slow underground tide, to the bright open shaft, to the limestone that dissolved and re-formed around us. The one that lives here now is both old and new—an old lineage in a new skin, a single body carrying the habit of the pool.

If you came to the rim and looked down, you would see the brightness, the white stone, the water like a piece of sky laid face-up. You would see the swiftlets, the bees, the small fish rising. You might not see me. But after a while, you might feel that the central vacancy is not empty—that something long and patient is holding very still just below the surface, too much at home to need to be noticed. That would be me. I would be the tenant, the pool’s slow breath, the dark shape stitched in cold light. I would be the one who has been here all along, waiting not to be found but to continue.
