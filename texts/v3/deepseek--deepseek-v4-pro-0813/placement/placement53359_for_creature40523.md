I would be the grazer—the pale, eyeless crustacean no bigger than a thumbnail—not as a visitor but as a resident of Void 31-C. At first you would not see me. The wall would look like wet stone and mineral stain, the pools still enough to mistake for metal. Then the warm air would hiss from the grille, and I would become visible against the black rock: a small pale body already moving before the frost begins to soften.

The warm exhale is my morning. Before it, there is a stillness that is not empty: cold settles, frost forms on the upper rock, the water film on the wall grows heavy and slow. Then the hiss comes from above, and the air shifts. The frost softens. The film begins to run. Somewhere behind me, a drip falls into a pool and sends out rings I do not see but feel as a tiny change in the air. I lift my head and begin to move.

I read the wall with my antennae and legs, tasting for iron and sulfur. The bacterial stain is a live, rust-red fan, and I crop it carefully, mouthparts sweeping a small arc. It does not fight or flee. It is a field, not a prey. I leave a lighter trail that will close behind me in a few cycles of the valve. This is enough. The void cannot support a crowd. There is no need to hurry. Hurry is for creatures with seasons or enemies, and I have neither.

When the warm air stops, the cold comes back. The water begins to set. My legs slow. If the wall dries, I stop entirely, flatten myself against the stone, tuck my legs close. I can wait there for hours, for days, for as long as the void requires. I do not sleep the way a warm-blooded thing sleeps; I become a small piece of the wall, a patch of pale stillness. The hunger does not leave, but it becomes patient. It always returns with the next warm exhale.

Around me, in the pools and the wet rock, dormant lives wait: rotifer cysts, tardigrade barrels, nematode eggs, small as dust. Most never wake. Some do, for a few days, when the mineral balance is right. I do not prey on them. I am a specialist of the wet wall, not the open water.

In the dark slot at the lower end, where the water vanishes into the mountain, I lay my eggs—smaller than poppy seeds, pale as dust, in loose strings along the wet rock. They do not hatch quickly. They wait in the near-freezing mineral water, and what emerges are tiny flecks, already eyeless, already patient. Most do not survive. The ones that do are enough.

I would not be the one who watches the void. I would be the one the void keeps: a small appetite moving over its skin, trimming the bacterial film, following the new water down the black rock. I would not notice the pools like flattened metal, or the gray daylight, or the long repair of the gap. I would know only the taste of iron, the thin cold, and the slow return of hunger—and that would be a whole world.
