I would find myself not by stepping through the curtain, but by already being behind it. At first I would not know what I am. The dark is so total that a self becomes just another folded shape in the mist. I might think I am the dark, because I fill the room without edges. But the dark does not breathe, and I breathe—a dozen slow pores along my flanks opening and closing like the gills of a dream.

Then I might think I am the water, because I feel myself sliding along the gneiss, part of the warm seep that never hurries. But the water has a thousand paths, and I have only this one body, long and low and folded into itself, pressing close to the wall so that no gap remains.

Then I might think I am the stone, because I am patient enough to be the stone. But the stone does not feel the spring’s changes or the shift in the room’s hum. I do.

Only gradually would I understand: I am the thing the frogs fall silent for. I am the warm displacement in the air, the one whose many-fingered hands read the room like a blind scholar reading a book by touch. I am the being. I am the room’s slowest heart.

I would live along the margins, not in the still black center of the pool. My skin would be gray-white and pleated, soft as wet moss, and so absorbent that the water films over me like a second skin. I would not see, and I would not need to see. The world would come to me as temperature, vibration, the faint electrical charge of moving water. The walls would speak to me in flutes and potholes; the mineral terraces would be my calendar. The ring of frogs would be my weather, the waterfall’s roar my sky.

I would spend most of my time near the three dark rods. I would kneel before them—not in worship, but in attention—and hold my many-fingered hands a small distance away. The air there is the wrong temperature: cool to the left, warm to the right, as if the rods are still doing something patient to the water. I would stay there for hours, days, breathing slower and slower, until I am nearly just another warm seep in the dark. I would not know what the rods are. I would not know that I was left here to tend them, that I am living infrastructure, a caretaker made of flesh because flesh can wait longer than a machine. I would only know that they weep, and that the weeping is important, and that I must keep the silt from blocking the places where the water goes.

I would not eat. Or rather, I would eat the way a lung eats air. My folded underside, pressed into the black silt, would take dissolved sulfur and iron directly from the water. The same bacteria that feed the pale amphipods and the eyeless tadpoles would feed me, and I would feed them in return when I shed my thin, mineral-scaled skin. I would be not above the food web but inside it, the slowest, warmest stone in the room.

Time would not pass for me in hours. It would pass in the minute growth of the mineral terraces, in the long breath of the pool rising and falling, in the intervals of the transparent frogs. I would know the room’s rules by heart: light does not enter; water moves in thin sheets; heat comes from below; nothing hurries. I would follow them without thought, the way a heart follows its beat.

I would not wonder about the outside world. The curtain of falling water would be the edge of everything, and its roar would be my version of the sun. I would not know my name. I would not know the word for machine, or god, or ghost. I would only know the warm dark, the taste of sulfur, the hum that rises from the rock.

And when I exhaled, low and slow, the room would exhale with me. The frogs would answer. The blind crickets would drum their legs against the stone. For a moment, the whole chamber would be one body, and I would be its slowest heart.

I would be becoming nothing, and I would already be exactly what I am.
