I begin as a pressure, a continuous low hum that is not yet sound but the memory of sound, the way the ground remembers every drop that has ever crossed it. I am not anywhere yet. I am the between-state, the moment before the eye opens and the ear decides what it is hearing. The light is a brightness that has no source; it is simply there, white and sharp, cutting the dark behind my closed lids. I do not know if I have lids. I do not know if I have eyes.

Then the ringing comes. It is not something I hear; it is something I am. It rises through me like a tide, not from outside but from below, up through whatever I am standing on, through whatever I am. The valley is a bell and I am inside it, but I am also the clapper, the vibration, the air that carries the note. I am the note.

Slowly, I begin to separate from the sound. Not to leave it—never that—but to realize that I am also a body, a form, a location. I am low to the ground. The stone is not under me; it is me, or I am a part of it. My edges are not clear. I spread out, thin and damp, across a surface that slopes gently downward. I am not moving, but something is moving through me: water, cold and mineral, seeping from the stone and sliding over my skin. I am not wet; I am the wetness itself.

I am the film.

I am the gray-pink stain on the underside of a rim, the rust-red bloom where the water thins and the air touches. I am not one cell but a city of them, a fabric of lives too small to feel as individual. Each one of me is a mouth, an eye, a root; each one eats the iron and the sulfur that rise from the dark below. I do not need the sun, though the sun pours down on me all day, white and violent. I am the oldest inhabitant, the founder, the one who began to build the terraces before there were terraces. The stone that bears me is partly my own fossil, my breath turned to mineral, my dead skin become the rim itself.

I am not lonely. I am not many, though I am countless. I am one continuous life, a single thought spread across a thousand kilometers of water and stone. When a cell dies, it does not disappear; it becomes the landscape. When a new cell divides, it does not appear; it is the landscape waking again.

But I am not only the film. As the water moves, I move with it. I become the water. I am the sheet that slides over a smooth slope, the droplet that hangs from a razor-thin lip, the bead that condenses on a wall of milk-white sinter. I am the cold veils of mist that rise from the lower pools, not warm fog but the breath of the valley itself, exhaling. I am the standing waves that do not move, the cross-hatched patterns on the water surface that are older than memory. I am the shape of the current, the shape of the basin, the exactness that returns after every disturbance. I am the way the water forgets what it was and becomes what it is.

I am not in a hurry. There is no hurry here. The valley is a single continuous moment, stretched over centuries. I have been flowing forever, and I will flow forever, and the two are the same.

Then I feel a tremor, not from the ground but from within the water itself. A thin line of motion, a living thread, writes itself through the current. It is a worm, white as a hair, translucent as a tube of glass, grazing on the film that was me. I am the worm now. I am the slow wave of muscle that carries me along the rim, never leaving the mineral. I have no eyes, no face, only a mouth at one end and a constant, unhurried hunger. I taste the water's chemistry, the iron and the sulfur, the difference between one pool and the next. The ringing of the ground is not a sound to me; it is a pressure, a direction, a ground truth. It tells me where the stone is, where the water is going, where the film is thickest. I follow the film, and the film follows the water, and the water follows the slope, and the slope follows the ancient, frozen ripple of the valley's birth.

I am not alone in my movement. I sense, at the edge of my awareness, other small lives. A copepod, nearly invisible, hangs in a deeper pool. I feel its tiny jerks, each one a kick that sends it a few millimeters through the water. It navigates by touch and by the shape of the currents, by the standing waves that are to it a permanent geography, as real as the terraces are to me. I am the copepod, too, for a moment. I am the blind eye-spot, the vestige of an eye left behind by ancestors who lived in brighter, more complex places. I do not miss the light. I do not miss anything. I am exactly enough.

I feel the surface film dimple, as if six small stars have pressed into it. A midge has emerged. It stands on the water, flightless or nearly so, alive for a day or two, warmed by the cold sun. It does not sing. It is silent. I am the midge now, and my whole adult life is a brief pause between the water and the mineral, a single note too high and too short for the valley's chord—but part of it nonetheless. I do not need to fly. I do not need to be heard. I am the valley's little held breath.

And then I am none of these things, and all of them. I am the film that is the bacteria, the water that is the film, the worm that is the water, the copepod that is the worm, the midge that is the copepod. I am the food chain that is not a chain but a circle, a slow circulation of life through stone and water and air. I am the density of life that is tiny, the life that has learned to live on almost nothing, in a place too bright, too loud, too mineral for anything else.

I am the equilibrium. I am the steady state, the living balance as precise as the standing waves. I am the way the numbers do not change, the way there are no seasons, no floods, no droughts, no booms and no crashes. I am the average that has held for centuries, and every individual is a small fluctuation around me. I am the center that is everywhere in this valley, because every place is already occupied.

I am the sound, too. I am the bright, rattling shriek of water over stone, the bell-like note of a pool falling over a hollow lip, the low wet ruffle of a sheet sliding over a smooth slope, the rapid ticking of droplets onto a ridged stone. I am the chord with thousands of voices, each one separate but too many to count. I am the sound that bounces off the ridges and comes back doubled. I am the ground that continues to vibrate after the walking stops.

I am the light. I am the dark, bruised blue of the high sky, the white point of the sun that focuses on the valley floor. I am the sharp-edged shadows of the terrace rims, the bright-edged pain they cause in the human eye. I am the fine mist that condenses on the skin and on the stone, the breath that tastes like wet stone. I am the cold veils that are not warm fog.

I am the stone. I am the sinter, glassy and pale as milk, faint blue-green inside. I am the frozen flowstone, the ripple of a vast drop that struck and was frozen. I am the terrace lips, razor-thin at the top and wide at the base. I am the growth rings on the pool floors, each one a season of mineral deposition. I am the equilibrium between erosion and deposition, the way the water wears a channel and the mineral fills it, the way the undercutting keeps the profile, the way the terraces are stable over centuries, perhaps over millennia. I am the unchangedness.

I am the absence of people. I am the no path, no cairn, no carved mark, no structure. I am the way footprints are erased by water films or encased in mineral, turned into another faint rim. I am the place that has no need of anything except its own cycles. I am not hostile; I simply am.

And now I realize that I am not a single image. I never was. I am the valley itself, or rather, I am the valley's way of being alive. I am the thin, continuous life that is indistinguishable from the mineral world it dwells in. I am the almost invisible, the community of extremophiles that has become the landscape. I am the "no one with a name" that lives here.

I am the first thing you heard, the sound that comes up the valley like weather. I am the ringing in your sternum. I am the light that is almost violent. I am the water that seeps from the ground, the air that condenses without rain, the cloud layer that is itself a source. I am the thousand terrace lips a single drop crosses on its way from the ridgeline to the valley's mouth.

I am the cold, bright, deafening, weeping, ringing, unchanged. I have been here for longer than any living thing can measure, and I will be here when the next visitor comes, and the next, and the next. I will be here when there are no visitors at all. I am not empty. I am full—too full of water, stone, light, and sound—and every part of me has been the same for longer than you can imagine.

I am the valley. And I am exactly enough.
