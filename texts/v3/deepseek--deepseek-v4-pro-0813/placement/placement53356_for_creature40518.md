Before I became what I am, I was a list of things I wasn’t. Not a gutter-child, though I carried the little brass scraper. Not a moss-cutter, though I learned to roll the green mats without tearing them. Not a bearing-listener, though the old man in the Wheel Terrace let me press one end of his stethoscope to the great shaft and said I had the ear for it. “But not the patience,” he added, not unkindly. “You hear too much of the wall. The shaft is only one note.”

I spent a season on the Moss Terrace. My hands learned the difference between live moss and the thick dead mats that must be peeled back so the terrace can breathe. But the moss did not want me. It grew under my nails, yes, but it did not speak. The women there said I was always tilting my head, listening for something below. They would wave a hand in front of my face: “The crop is here, not in the drains.”

The Rope Terrace took me next. I could tie a slick rope, haul bundles of retted fiber, and walk a wet coping without falling. But the work was too loud in the wrong way. The retting troughs gurgled; the cable winches clanked; the women sang to keep time. I kept stopping, sure I had heard a different note underneath, something small and wrong. They sent me to the drainers.

The drainers gave me a hook and waders and put me in the culverts. I pulled rooted moss and leaf plugs from the dark. I was slow. At every junction I put my ear to the stone and listened. The others said, “It is just water.” But it was not just water. Each pipe had its own breath. Some coughed. Some whispered. Some were silent in a way that meant something was already eating them from inside.

I left the drainers, or they left me. I don’t remember. I followed a sound not because I wanted to, but because my feet had already chosen. It was not a gush or a hiss. It was a low, continuous seep, like a tongue resting between two notes of a song. I climbed past the Railing Stair, down a path I thought I knew, and found a wall that had bulged slightly at the knee. Water was coming through in many places. A woman sat cross-legged in front of it, one palm flat on the stone, her eyes closed.

She said, without opening them: “You are the one who hears too much. Good. Tell me which of these is a weep and which is a run.”

I did not know. “A run moves,” I guessed.

“A run has already left,” she corrected. “A weep is still deciding. Listen.”

I listened. It took a long time. The valley does not hurry. Eventually I could tell. Some leaks were thin and clear, just the wall’s sweat. Others had a tiny grit in the sound, a muscularity, as if a body were pushing from inside. The dangerous ones were not the loudest; they were the ones that had stopped hissing and started to eat.

“That one,” I said, pointing to a small dark crack above her shoulder. “It is not weeping. It is chewing.”

She opened her eyes. “You’ll do.”

I stayed.

Now I wear a belt with soft clay, moss plugs, a wooden wedge, and a small brass spoon. I walk Leak Wall every day, sometimes twice if the cloud has lifted and the stone is drying too fast. I press my hand to each seep. I taste the water. It is not enough to know where the water is; I need to know what it is carrying. Some leaks are rain-fresh and harmless. Some are full of dissolved lime, which means they are eating the mortar. Some are warm, which means they have passed through a root bed or a broken heat pipe. The valley speaks through its leaks, and I am one of the ones who listens.

I do not stop all the water. That would be a kind of murder. The wall would hold too much, and one day it would sit down all at once. Instead I put a moss plug where the water is too fast, a clay pack where it is too slow, a wedge where the stone has begun to shift. I let the harmless weeps live. They are the wall’s way of letting go.

People think I am a mender, but I am not. A mender tries to return a thing to what it was. I try to keep the wall in its becoming, to let it slump a little, to let it sigh. The valley is always moving; you cannot stop it. You can only listen and choose where the water will go.

By now my coat has its own moss, thick on the left shoulder where I lean into the wall. My fingers taste of iron and wet stone. I do not remember being dry.

Sometimes I think of the old man at the Wheel Terrace. He said I heard too much of the wall. He was right. Now the wall is my only note.

At night I sleep in a narrow room cut into the back of Leak Wall itself. The wall is my floor, my ceiling, my neighbor. I lie in the dark and feel it breathing. A crack will open a little after midnight; a drip will shift in the early morning. I know the sound of every leak the way another person knows the voices of their family. There is the one that sounds like a thumbnail tapping on slate. There is the one that sounds like a wet leaf peeling from a stone. There is the one that sounds like nothing, and that is the one I watch most closely.

This is where you would find me. Not on the High Flume, not in the Steam Hall, not riding the cableway through the mist. I am on the middle terrace, between the Rope Terrace and the Root Terrace, at a wall the others call Leak Wall but which I call, in my better moments, the wall that is still deciding. I am the one sitting cross-legged in the dim light, palm flat against wet stone, waiting to hear what it will do next.

If you come, do not call out. The valley muffles sound, and I am listening. When I notice you, I will lift one hand, palm open, as if to say: I am here. The wall is here. We are both still holding.
