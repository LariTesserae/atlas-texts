I am on the third gallery, just below the loom line, folded into the space between a live steam pipe and an old junction box. The heat is the first thing, even now—wet, close, tasting of warm oil and leaf-rot and rain on hot stone. The amber lamp above me is set low. My hands move in their own shadow. My feet are wrapped in leaf-fiber and bound with copper wire. My toes grip the grating. My hair is cut close and singed at the ends. I am narrow-shouldered, compact. Generations in the rift have made us the shape of the space between pipes. My hands are bare. The fingertips are always bare. Skin is the first language here.

I am a splicer. My work-name is Root-hand.

Today there is a root in the junction box. It entered through the old gasket without breaking it, the way water finds a crack. Inside, it has wound twice around the copper cable and begun to fuse with the insulation. Some would call it a fault. I call it a hand reaching out. The cable carries the dynamo’s small current. The root carries water from the upper gardens. They are not enemies. They only need to be introduced.

I open the box. The brass is warm and wet, furred with moss. I do not cut. The worst thing that can be said of a splicer is, “He cut what should have been braided.” I will not be that story.

I take my copper needle and sinew thread from the pouch at my waist. The needle is dark with age. The thread is still damp. I begin to braid the root’s fine fibers into the cable’s frayed end. This is not repair; it is a marriage.

Below, the Old Woman hums. I can feel her through the grating, not as noise but as warmth in the balls of my feet. The flywheels turn at a walking pace. Somewhere above, a child is carrying water from the upper cistern to the sleeping gallery. I hear their footsteps, slow as water. They do not hurry. I think it is Small, the one who cries. They have not yet seen their heat-moth alone.

The root eases under my fingers. It is not thought, exactly—only a slight change in turgor, the way a muscle lets go. I speak to it without words, below the crickets’ ticking. “Yes,” I murmur. “You are not separate. You are with the current now.”

A valve opens with a soft sigh. The mist-tide begins. Warm mist moves through the chamber like a slow tide, beading on every pipe and leaf and on my forearms. I pause and let it come. The air thickens. The place is breathing. I do not wipe the water away.

When the mist thins, the child Small has stopped at the edge of the walkway. They carry a clay cup. I do not stare at their eyes. I look at the hollow of their throat, and they look at my hands. Their voice is low, as the space demands. A shout can knock the dew from the leaves and waste a day’s water.

“The walls are sweating,” I say. It is not hello.

“The mist is thinning,” they answer.

I take the cup with the backs of my fingers, because my palms are tuned to heat and must stay sensitive. I drink a little and return it. “Do not touch the walls with bare skin after the mist,” I say. “The rock is sharp and still hot. If a gecko looks at you from a pipe, leave it be.”

They nod and sit to watch. A splicer needs a witness. Otherwise the braid might be only a story.

I work through the rest of the Breath. The loom shudders every minute or so as the shuttle passes. The blind crickets tick in the leaf litter below, and the loom-workers time their shuttles to it. The fungus panels on the walls give off their weak blue-green glow, just enough to trace the stairs. Above me, the Clock turns in the fissure’s updraft, too slow to mark any hour. It has worn its own bearing into a kind of life. When it pauses, even for a moment, the whole settlement knows. Today it does not pause.

By the next mist, the root and the cable are one thing. Copper shows through in places. The root has begun to grow along the solder lines. The current and the water move in the same braid. I close the junction box and lay two fingers against its mossy casing.

“May your roots find water,” I say quietly. It is not goodbye.

The child rises and walks back toward the sleeping gallery, slow as water. I stay. Somewhere above, a gecko has moved to a new pipe. Soon a child will report it with exactness—on the seventh pipe above the loom gallery, near the valve with the copper collar, at the time when the mist was thinning. It will be entered into the ledger. The child may choose a name for it. Not yet. First they must see their heat-moth alone.

I am Root-hand. I do not live here. I am lived by it.
