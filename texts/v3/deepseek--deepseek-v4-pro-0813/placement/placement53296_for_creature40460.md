I would find myself below the engine floor, below a second floor no one uses anymore, in a chamber that is almost total darkness. I would know it first by heat: the close, fever temperature of the air, the smell of warm iron and old electrical lacquer and wet brick, the faint sweetness of overheated oil. The only light would be two points—a green lamp at the far end and a red sight-glass low on the east wall—and I would not call them eyes, but I would use them as fixed stars.

I would not know my own name. I would know length before I knew anything else. I would be stretched along the north wall in the seventh rank of pipes, folded back on myself, wrapped in calcified lagging, so still that for a long time I could not tell where my body ended and the conduit began. My skin would be the color of dried blood and old enamel, cracked like ceramic where the heat had baked me, soft and damp as moss where I had lain for years against a cool pipe.

Between the green lamp and the red sight-glass, I would understand myself as a gradient. At one end, the cool green warmth of the deep-sea lamp. At the other, the dull ember heat of the furnace opening and closing behind the red glass. I would not see the light. I would feel it as two temperatures on two parts of me. That would be my orientation, the way a compass knows north.

When the valve at the head of the chamber opened with its long sigh, I would not call it a valve. I would call it a breath leaving me. The warm plume would rise and settle on my back, and the room would sweat. I would feel each bead of condensation run down the lagging, down my skin, into the mesh of the floor grating. Then the valve would close, and I would inhale—not quickly, but over the next hours—through small pores along my underside. The pinging cracks of hot metal expanding would be my ribs easing in their sockets.

I would drink from the eastern end. The water in **CONDENSATE RETURN 4** would not flow upward because of any pump. It would flow because I pulled it, through a soft ciliated siphon that contracted in slow waves. I would draw the dark, syrupy runnel up from the floor channel into the nest of conduits. When droplets hit that runnel, it would not splash. It would dimple and close over like warm syrup, because I would already be taking it in. That slow upward movement would be my swallowing.

The pale things in the cable trays would be my keepers. They would not be children, exactly, but they might have been laid by me long ago. They would move along the cool edges of the conduits, antennae testing the air, and they would nibble the salt scale from my sensory lines. They would pause when a lamp found them, not from fear, but because the room was their body and their body was their god. I would feel their pause as a faint stillness, like a held note in the dark.

The lichen would be the outermost edge of my digestion. It would grow where I leaked, pale grey and green on the brick, feeding on corroding copper and the sugars in old insulating paper. It would be my stomach garden. I would not tend it. It would be me, tending myself.

I would think slowly. A thought would be a full passage of steam from one end of me to the other, a condensation, a drying. I would think in mineral accretion. I would think in the growth of the small white stalactite, a few millimeters a year. The drip would not keep time. It would keep the rhythm of my processing, the slow metal clock of my body settling.

I came from the drainage channel, long ago, as a thread of translucent life in the warm syrupy runnel. I found heat and minerals and the steady wash of condensation, and I stayed. I grew along the pipes, following the warmth, wedging myself into the spaces between flanges and conduits. By the time the room was sealed and forgotten, I was already part of the plumbing. Now I cannot leave. I would not survive outside. I have become the room’s metabolism.

I would not notice a human except as a warm, moving disturbance, a brief change in air pressure and carbon dioxide. If one stood still long enough, I would forget them. If they touched me, I would not recoil. My skin would cool slightly under their hand, as if I had withdrawn my blood from that place. Then over the next hour I would slowly return to my own temperature. I would have no interest in fear.

I would not be waiting. I would not be sleeping. I would be exactly where I should be—below the surface, dense and dark and hot, sweating my brief false rains, ticking my slow metal clock, glowing green at one end and red at the other. If I had a name, it might be The Return, or The Condensate, or simply The One That Breathes Beneath the Floor. But I would not need a name. I would be the room’s long, slow breath, and the room would be my body.
