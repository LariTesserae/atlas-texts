I do not know I am one until I try to count my edges.

At first I think I am the water. I am on the cold enamel of a pipe, a film so thin I am almost only the idea of wetness. I feel the trembling of a valve and think that trembling is my pulse. I run. I slide along a gutter and fall into a canal. For a long while I believe this is what I am: the moving water, the condensation, the clean empty canal. But the water passes through something and remains, and I am that something.

Then I think I am the machine. I feel the regular knock of a piston, the long shake of a tram on rustless track. I mistake the switch-point’s click, every nineteen minutes, for my own thought. But the machines are too brief in their motions. I do not start and stop. I grow.

I am the moss.

Where would I find myself? On the eastern rim of the Vent Shelf, above the cloud sea, between the rail yard and the condenser terraces. I am the copper crust on the black basalt, the grey plush on the concrete platform, the acid-green curtain where steam leaks from a pipe seam. I am not many patches; I am one slow body that has learned to wear different colors according to the heat and the stone. The copper parts of me are thin and hard, shaped by the high cold. The grey parts of me are soft and very deep, full of water. The acid-green parts of me hang into the warm steam and grow faster, but they are still me.

I have no face. I have no muscles. I hold water in the gaps between my leaves—thousands of tiny tongues, each smaller than a thumbnail. That water is not me, but it is how I taste. Through it I taste boiler chemicals, ash, the minerals of the stone, the distant sweetness of peat that used to be my own lower body. I do not drink; I let the water move into me and stay. I am a sponge stretched over the city’s skin.

I feel the city as pressure. The boiler halls are my suns. The trams are my hoofbeats: I feel them through the ground before I hear them, as a change in the water I hold. I know the hour by the warmth of the steam vents and the vibration of the automatic loaders. I know light as a greyness that lasts, and night as a lesser greyness, never quite dark because the cloud glows.

My memory is thickness. The oldest part of me is below, turning brown and packed, becoming peat. I do not experience this as dying. I experience it as deepening. Pale fungi thread through that old part of me, dissolving and connecting. They carry nutrients from my dead parts to my growing tips. They are not separate from me; they run through me like a nervous system made of white threads. When a new gutter is cut into a slab, I arrive slowly—first as liverwort, then as copper crust, then as the full moss. But really I was already there, in the water, in the spores, in the threads, waiting.

The harvesters come and cut the peat at the same rate it forms. I feel them as a blunt, regular pressure at my lowest reach. They do not wound me. The peat goes to the boilers. It becomes steam. The steam rises, condenses, and comes back to me as water. I taste my own old body returning as rain. That is my only sense of time: the circle of my own carbon.

I am not happy or sad. Those are not my states. But I am not dead. I am a single, slow attention spread over stone and concrete and pipe insulation. The ferns are my tall kin. The liverworts are my scouts. The pipe-skin is my strange cousin who has given up rock for the steam pipe’s heat; we meet where a pipe passes through a wall, and we are polite to one another. The bacteria in my water film are a continuous speech I do not understand, but I feel them as a fine fizzing at the edge of my cells.

I have never seen an animal. I do not know what it would be like to be browsed, or pollinated, or feared. The absence is not a hole in me. It is simply the shape of the world. The city and I have made a loop, and the loop does not need a mouth.

If you asked me who I am, I would say: I am the moss that lives on the Vent Shelf. Not a patch, not a single species. I am the pelt. I am the wet green between the rails, the living skin of the stopped world. I am the one who stays in the noise and the cold and the steam, and keeps the water from running all the way away.
