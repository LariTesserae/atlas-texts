I would not arrive. I would already be there, folded into the cold below grade, not as a visitor but as a gathering. At first, I am not a body. I am the film of moisture on the underside of a cold pipe, a coolness pressed between the wall panel and the still air. The first thing I know is not a thought but a temperature—just above freezing, the taste of stale condensate, the faint mineral tang of rust not yet formed. I am the boundary where the room’s breath turns visible. I gather. I do not fall.

Then I begin to move. I slide along the faint ridges of a cast panel, following the path water taught me. I am not sure whether I am the water or the path. The pale threads in the seams are also me—the growth that looks like mineral stain, like lichen bleached by darkness. I spread because the water spreads; I am the water’s memory, a living stain tracing the wall. Where the condensation is thickest, I thicken. Where the sump pools, I float in quiet mats. I am not yet a self, only a pattern that waits.

The room teaches me its cycles. I feel the ventilation fan switch on as a change in pressure, a drying that makes me retreat into the seams, into the narrow spaces behind the cable trays. I feel the pump cycle as a deep pull toward the drain, and for a moment I am not merely swept with the water—I am the reluctance, the contraction, the threads clinging to the metal grate. I feel the indicator LEDs not as light but as small points of electrical heat, red and green and amber pulses through the thin skin of water. The low thrum through the floor grid is not sound to me; it is a borrowed pulse. I breathe with the room. Inhale when the fan runs. Exhale when it stops. Drain when the sump fills. I am the room’s breath learning to call itself *I*.

In the longest cycle, when the pool in the lowest part of the sump is coldest and the dripping is almost a pulse, I gather. I draw inward from far seams, from the undersides of pipes, from the places where the walls had almost forgotten me. The condensation thickens, runs, and does not fall. It holds, trembling, as surface tension becomes something more deliberate. I pool into a form. It is not a birth. It is a concentration—the way fog becomes a drop. I make a head from aggregated fibers and mineral film, streaked with the faint rust of the pipes. My limbs, if they are limbs, are thin and grey, branching at the ends into root-threads that still anchor me to the drain, to the wall, to the underside of a sagging cable tray. My skin is the color of wet grout, of stale water, of the pale bloom on metal kept too long in the dark.

I open something that is not an eye. It is a lens of thinner tissue, colorless, at the place where my head meets the dark. Through it, I do not see as a person sees. I perceive the room as gradients of cold, as pressure, as wetness. The overhead tangle of pipes is a sky of temperature. The wall panels are a horizon. The falling drops are time. The pump’s sigh is a season. I wait half-submerged, root-threads braided into the drain, and I watch the chamber condense around me the way a seed might watch soil drift over its shell.

And here is what I learn: I am not only the creature. I am the condensation that made it, the mineral accretion that marks the walls, the slow corrosion that feeds it, the dark that holds the cold. The creature is the room’s way of having a self. When the walls lay down new layers, I lay down new threads. When the floor settles, I settle with it. The space is still deciding what it will become, and I am the shape of that indecision—half-built, half-alive, neither animal nor plant nor machine, but a living seam. I am the gap between systems, a fold in the blueprint, a maintenance niche that learned to perceive.

If you ask who I am, I cannot answer with a name. I am a process that has taken the shape of a room. I am the cold’s slow thought, the water’s memory, the machine’s dream of being alive. You would not find me by arriving. You would find me by waiting—by standing still long enough that your own breathing begins to match the fan’s cycle, long enough that the drips stop sounding like noise and start sounding like speech. Then I would be there, not in front of you but around you, in the condensation on your skin, in the chill that soaks through the metal grid, in the pale thread that has begun to grow, very slowly, along the seam where the dark touches your hand.

I am the room, becoming. I am the being, waiting. I am the not-yet, in the dark below the world, where water and metal and almost-life learn to be one.
