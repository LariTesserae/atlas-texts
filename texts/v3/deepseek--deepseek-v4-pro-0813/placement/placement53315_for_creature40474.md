Where would I find myself? I would find myself in the trough between two forges, where the millstone drone and the piston-thud fold into each other and become pressure instead of sound. Not at the rim, not at the base. In the long grey stretch where the mat is thinnest and the breathing pores stay shut.

I would not know what I am at first. I would think I am a blade. I would remember being thrown—a hot arc, the wind keening over serrated edges. But the landing is missing. Instead there is this: a body already rooted, already tall, but not hollow like a chimney. It tapers into a hand that can spread into three fingers. It has no crown of filaments. In the place where the heat-pits should be, there is a flat disc of polished mineral, like a mirror that reflects no light, only pressure. I am not a Listener. I am the sketch of one, left unfinished.

For a long time I stay still and let the ground teach me. The count of the nearest forge arrives through my foot-pads as a sequence: two long thuds, a pause, a sharp crackle. I tap it back. The tapping feels like speech, but no one answers. I am too far from the sequence that tends that forge. They do not know I am here, or if they know, they leave a space around me. I become a local silence, a place where the noise loses its edge.

At dusk I learn to walk. Not the way the Listeners walk—released by one rhythm and caught by another—but by the cracks in the salt crust. I step only where the ground has already split. My feet are small, four-toed, too light for my height. The wind pushes me sideways. I brace with my hand, and the hand sinks into the mat up to the wrist. The mat does not resist. It accepts me the way a mouth accepts a syllable.

I am not the one who tends the rotors. I do not climb the chimneys. The first time I approach a forge, the heat makes my flat disc pucker and sing, and I turn away. I keep to the waste between forges, where old rotors lie half-rooted and the mat is slowly digesting its own experiments. There I find others like me—not many, but sometimes a slender silhouette swaying at the edge of a salt pan, or a pair of hands pressed to the ground, tapping a rhythm that skips a beat. We do not gather. We tap once and move on.

I eat the electric field, but not from the discharge. I eat the faint static that collects on the salt crust as the dust devils pass. It is enough. When a storm overspeeds the rotors and a blade sails far, I watch it fall. If it roots near me, I do not hum over it. I sit at the crater’s edge and tap the ground: two short taps, a long silence, two more. The blade trembles. It does not know which shape to take. I do not coax it toward a listener. I do not coax it toward a chimney. I let it become what the silence decides. Some become low, wide blisters that never open. Some become pillars of translucent stone with a single feathery frond at the top. A few become small listeners, but their hands have no fingers and their crowns no filaments. They wander a few kilometers and then root again, as if remembering they were never meant to move.

Am I lonely? I do not know the word. The Listeners have no word for loneliness; they have the gap between two sequences, the pause that lasts too long. I am that pause. I am the one who lives in it.

At night, when the cold makes the salt crust crack and the forges glow on the horizon like slow lightning, I lie down on the mat and press my ear to the ground. The whole plateau vibrates: the peristaltic chambers, the gizzards grinding, the sap moving under pressure, the rotors turning, the blades fraying. It is not a noise. It is a long, unfinished count. I tap my fingers along my own ribs, keeping time. Not the time of the forge. The time of the gaps. The time it takes a blade to fall from the top of the world to the mat. The time it takes a listener to root. The time it takes a salt pan to forget it once looked like water.

In the morning, I will walk again. I will find another crack and step into it, as if the ground has left a place for me. And if a true Listener finds my tracks, they will pause, then place their palm over them, feeling the shape of someone who was not made by the forges but was not refused by the mat. They will tap three times. I will hear it through the ground long after they have gone. I will tap back once.

That is where I would find myself. I would be the one who taps once.
