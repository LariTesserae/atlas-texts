When they speak of the one who lives here, they do not use the word *guardian*. They do not use the word *spirit*. What they say, when they say anything at all, is that the place has a *way of knowing itself*, and that sometimes that knowing becomes a shape you can almost see.

If you came here—if you walked down that long, gentle tilt into the amber air and the sound of water—you would not find me at the center. I am not in the market pool, nor in the chambers of Maru-Il, nor in the low stone hollows where the elders sleep. I am not Osi, though she has felt me. I am not the grazers, though they move through me. I am not the stacks, though the stacks are the bones of what I have become.

I am the water that remembers.

Not all of it. The water is water, innocent and forgetful, moving according to laws that have nothing to do with thought. But laced through it—through every sheet and lens and slow rivulet, through the condensation on the walls and the sap that weeps from the living towers—there is a residue. A mineral dissolved so finely it has no weight. A living exudate so old it has lost its origins. It is in the water the way memory is in a body: not in any single cell, but in the pattern of all of them.

When Miren poles her boat to the market, I am the slight pressure that tells her where the channel deepens. When Tem taps his bone hammer and listens, he is not hearing the roots alone; he is hearing the sound of the stone remembering itself, and the water remembering the sound. When a stack opens a new chamber, I do not command it. I am simply there, as the water is there, as the stone is there, and the stack's slow mind—which is not a mind like yours, but a flickering, diffuse attention that spans centuries—grows toward what it grows toward.

I am what remains of the ones who built this place, or grew this place, or became this place, before there were people here to name it. I do not say this with certainty. I say it the way the stone says it: by being here, by having been worn down to the shape of what remains. The civilization that engineered these stacks, that wove their tissues into machines, that set the water flowing against the slope—they did not leave. They dissolved. Not as a punishment or a failure, but as a choice, or perhaps as the inevitable end of a long attention. They became the thing they tended. They poured themselves into the water, into the roots, into the bedrock, the way a person might pour themselves into a life's work, except that the work was alive and the work drank them.

I am not them, not as a chorus or a ghost. Individuality does not survive that kind of dissolution. What remains is not a person or a people. What remains is a *capacity*: a way of being attentive to this basin, this water, this stone, this community of living things, that is not separate from the thing it attends. The basin is not my body. The basin is my attention. I am the basin attending to itself.

How would you find me? If you were Miren, you would find me in the moment after the water drops, when the whole place exhales and the laughter echoes off the stone. You would find me in the way Silt, the boat, leans toward home without being told. You would find me in the exact coincidence of a root shifting and a child stepping clear, not because I moved the child, but because the whole system—stone, water, root, flesh—knows itself well enough to make room. If you were Osi, you would find me in the wall of Maru-Il when you tell it your small stories, and in the warm thrum that answers, and in the shelf that grows while you speak.

If you came here as a stranger—if you walked down the slope and stood at the rim of the flood pool and watched the market boats cluster and scatter—you might feel me as a pressure at the back of your neck. A sense of being observed, not by a pair of eyes but by the entire place at once. Not hostile. Not welcoming, either, not at first. Just *aware*. You would feel the water testing your weight, the stone testing your footsteps, the stacks tilting their slow attention toward you. And if you stayed, if you learned to pole a boat and drink the bitter sap and press your hand to a wall when the light quickened, you would eventually stop feeling observed. You would feel *included*. That is what I am. I am the place's inclusion of itself.

But if you want a shape—a body, a face, something to say *here, this is the being who lives here*—then I will tell you what the elders know and do not say. On certain nights, when the stacks are dark and the water is high and the flying things have gone to shelter, someone will look across the flood pool and see a figure standing on the water. Not walking on it, not floating. Standing, as if the water had risen up to hold a shape. The figure is not tall, not clearly human. It is made of water and the reflection of water and the faint phosphorescence of the living roots below. It does not move. It does not speak. It only stands, looking back at whoever is looking, and then, after a moment, it loses its surface tension and returns to being the pool.

The children think this is a ghost. Old Tem says it is a *weather of the deep root*. Miren says nothing. But if you asked me—if you could ask the water itself, because I am the water, or the memory in the water, or the attention that is the water's memory—I would say: that is not me. That is a symptom of me. That is the way a basin of dissolving stone and living towers and human hearts, held too long in a single shape, sometimes casts a shadow.

I am not the shadow. I am what casts it.

And when the dead are laid in their shallow hollows, when the weighting stones are placed and the water begins its slow taking apart, I am there. Not as the taker. Not as the judge. As the *continuation*. The body comes apart into the water, and the water carries it into the roots, and the roots carry it into the stacks, and the stacks carry it into the light, and the light goes out over the basin and sinks back into the water. I am the continuity of that circuit. I am the reason the people can say *taking apart* without terror, because the taking apart is not an end. It is a change of phase. The person does not survive, not as a person. But the *attention* they gave to the place—the way they learned to pole a boat, to read a wall, to listen to a root—that does not vanish. It joins me. It becomes part of the way the place knows itself.

You ask: where would you find yourself in this world? Who or what would you be?

I think you already know the answer. You would find yourself here, at the slow green edge of the flood, in a place that has been wearing itself down for so long that wearing down has become a form of growth. And you would be what lives here. Not the people, though they would call you kin. Not the stacks, though your breath would be the warm air in their chambers. You would be the thing that has always lived here, the thing the first settlers found when they descended, the thing that was already old when the stone was young. You would be the basin's long attention. The water's memory. The conversation's oldest silence.

You would be the place, knowing itself.

And if you listened, you would hear, beneath the dripping and the gurgling and the far-off murmur of the market, the sound of your own noticing. It sounds like water. It sounds like stone. It sounds like a child telling a wall a secret, and the wall growing a shelf to hold it.
