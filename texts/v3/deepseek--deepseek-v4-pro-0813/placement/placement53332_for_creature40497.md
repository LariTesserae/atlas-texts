I would find myself in the Socket just before dawn, on the third east ledge, where the rope ladder ends in a knot worn smooth by generations of feet. Not at the rim, not in the Ear, but halfway up the bowl’s warm wall, in that dim seam where the tall succulents lean over the path like old relatives waiting to be acknowledged.

I would be a girl of the dew line, maybe ten dry seasons into the world, with a face the color of old brick, deep-set eyes, and hair twisted into tight oiled cords so the thorns cannot catch me. My feet would curl around the ladder’s notched rungs as if each rung had grown there for me—though they still know my mother’s weight better than they know mine. I would carry the cup. Not my own cup, but the family cup, the one with a thumb-worn hollow in its side and a cracked rim mended with resin and a single strand of blue memory-thread. It is the cup I must learn to hold before I am given one of my own.

Below, the succulents rise out of the half-dark like patient blue-gray pillars. The air smells of dust and hot stone, but under that is the green-wax smell of plants holding their breath. A gecko watches from a black mineral vein, its papery sides pulsing. I keep still—not mostly still, but entirely still—because my mother says almost still is enough to startle a living thing, while complete stillness is a kind of welcome.

My mother moves ahead between the ribs, drawing a felted leather strap across each waxy groove where the night has left its thin gift. I hold the cup beneath her hands. I watch how she does not pull the moisture from the plant, but receives it, as if the plant is offering a small sip of the mountain’s breath. She never speaks while gathering. The only sounds are the soft wet release of the felt, the first harsh bird-calls, and the far-off clatter of a bone bell moving in a draft.

When the cup is half full, I am allowed to carry it to the next plant. It feels heavier than it should. I walk with both hands around it, the way I would hold a live bird, or a small animal too frightened to flee. The water trembles but does not spill. I can feel the weight of it as if it is not only water but time—each drop a small, slow hour gathered from the mountain’s sleep.

Back in our room, behind a curtain of woven fiber and hide, my siblings wait with dry throats. I am youngest, so I wait longest. My mother gives a sip to each—oldest first, youngest last—and no one takes more than a mouthful. When the cup reaches me, the rim is warm and wet. I hold a single sip against my tongue before swallowing. Then we all lick the rim, one by one, and it tastes like the inside of the morning: dust, wax, mineral salt, and the beginning of thirst.

I am not recorded yet. The thread-rememberer has not tied a knot for my name. The bone bells do not ring differently for me. But I know where I belong: on the middle eastern ledge, in the room whose back wall is mountain bone, just above the place where the dew gathers best. I am the one learning to be a cup—small, steady, and so careful with water that the mountain may someday trust me to hold its breath.

If you ask who I am, I am the daughter of the woman who gathers dew. I am the youngest drinker at the rim. I am the girl who counts the drops between one succulent and the next, and who is learning that to live in the Socket is to be another slow, patient, water-holding thing under the mountain’s cupped hand.
