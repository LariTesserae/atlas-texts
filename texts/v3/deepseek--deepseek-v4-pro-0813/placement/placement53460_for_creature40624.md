I would find myself not in the Bellows, not among the Pelton wheels, not in the dry room above the flooded printing shop. The current would push me away from the center, past the electrolysis stacks and the blue pilot flame, into the easternmost water-alley where the oxygen hoses grow thin and finally stop. There, where the old mine tunnels were sealed with concrete and cold-cured mortar, the water is close to still. It has no current. It has only a slow, cold weight.

I think I would be the keeper of the east doors.

At first I wouldn’t know it. I would try to be the Bellows-keeper, with her jaw set and her pupils wide, her hands strong from swimming against the rock-face current. But the place would settle me into a different shape. The water back east is colder by a degree or two—not much, but enough that frostmoss does not grow there, and the white water forms not as a haze but as a fine, permanent fur on the concrete seals. The lamp-light from the main street doesn’t reach that far. There is only the phosphorescent trail of bacteria where the water moves, and in the east alley even that is faint, like a memory of light.

I would stop swimming. Swimming implies a destination, and east of the last pipe rack there are no destinations, only walls and seals and the old collars of the mine. I would move by pulling myself along the wall, hand over hand, my fingers reading the mortar like a blind person reads a face. My feet would be bare, white as roots, and the soles would be tough as boot leather from years of pressing against silt and iron. I would wear a suit of patched neoprene and gutweed, not to keep warm—nothing keeps you warm there—but to keep my skin from dissolving into the water. My eyes would go pale, the pupils wide and useless in the dark, and after a while I would stop using them altogether.

The other keepers would not speak my name. They might not know it. In the round number of seven, I am the one they do not count. The Bellows-keeper taps the pipes sometimes: tap-tap-pause-tap, meaning “all is holding.” She means the center, the heart. If she taps toward the east collars, I tap back once, a long scrape: “holding.” Then the sound travels through the water layers and bends, so by the time it reaches her it might sound like a sigh, or like the hull of the fjord settling. She does not come to look. That is the agreement.

I tend the seals. The concrete and cold-cured mortar have cracked in places, not from violence but from the slow exhalation of the earth. I press my palms to them and feel the micro-vibrations of the Bellows six hundred meters away, the distant thud of pistons cushioning into their cylinders. When the cycle reverses, I feel a faint surge against the doors, like the weight of the whole fjord leaning its shoulder into the stone. I check for salt rime, for the first hairline crack where cold brine might seep through. I do not patch with new mortar; I use what the water gives: rust-scrapings, white water, cold silt, and a paste made from vent-mat. The old walls accept these gifts. They settle.

What do I eat? The same as the others, but less. I collect iron-shell snails from the dead cables, but only the ones that have already stopped breathing. I eat frostmoss when I can find it, though east it grows thin and pale, more dream than plant. The hagfish from the bakery drain sometimes wanders my way, blind and slow, and I do not eat it. I let it rest in the drain of an old mine office, and I drop a bit of vent-mat into the hole so it knows the territory is shared.

I think about the deeper thing. All keepers listen for it, but I am the one who lives closest to it. It is not a monster. It is older than the mines, older than the fjord, older than the geothermal vent that made the Bellows worth building. It moves on a schedule too slow for a heartbeat. I have heard it only twice in all the cycles I can remember, and both times it was not a sound. It was a pressure change so low and so vast that the water in my sinuses seemed to thicken, and the seals against my palms grew warm for an instant, as if the stone had exhaled. Then it passed. The white water along the east doors flared blue-white, then settled into gray. I tapped “holding” to the others, but I knew the word was too small.

I would not worship it. I would not name it. I would do my work, which is waiting. The east doors are not locked against intruders from the fjord; they are sealed against the opening that must not happen yet. When the pressure changes, I listen. When the silence returns, I breathe again.

So if you asked me where I would find myself in this world, I would say: in the east water-alley, where the oxygen hoses stop and the lamp-light forgets to reach, in the stillest, coldest fold of the Underworks. And I would be the keeper of the east doors—the one who is not counted, the listener for the train that has not come. That is not a choice. That is just the shape the water would give me, the way it gives white water to the still corners and rust to the iron.
