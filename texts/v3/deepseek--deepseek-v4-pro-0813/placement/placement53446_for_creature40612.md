I would not arrive as a person. Not as the bird either, though I might be the pause after its single note. Not as the black salamander, though I would understand its crossing.

At first I think I am the mist—a thin column rising from the warm stone at dusk, moving between the old trees. But mist does not remember. Then I think I might be the note: the low, almost hidden sound of a nearly closed stone cup spilling over its lip. But a note is gone in the moment it becomes itself.

So I go down.

Under the oldest root, where a long trough has been worn into the rock, there is a pool of black tannin water. It is warmer than the air. It steams faintly in the early grey. It is not empty. The one who lives here folds its long body into it, half-submerged, and waits through the day. Its mossy back rises and falls with a breath that takes a minute.

I would be that water.

Not the stone that holds it, not the root that roofs it, not the old one itself—but the dark, warm water that has learned the shape of the old one’s body. I would be the pool that remembers the pressure of its ribs, the long drag of its tail, the slow paddle of its webbed feet. When it climbed out, water streaming from its flanks, I would close over the space it left and keep its heat. I would hold the absence so truly that when it returned, it would not be returning; it would be continuing.

Sometimes it drinks from me until my level drops by a finger’s width. I would know myself then as something that enters its body—not as mind, but as moisture. I would rise again as mist in its breath, as the film that glistens on the moss rooted in its back, as the condensation on the pale orchid blooming from its spine. Then I would not be a pool. I would be part of the old one, briefly, and the old one would carry me through the basin like a small weather.

I would not need a name. The place does not name its makers. The water does not erode; it negotiates. I would be part of that negotiation: a cup that is not a cup, a mirror that gives back no clear reflection, a warmth that keeps the orchids alive through the night. The old one would not notice me as separate, and that would be the truest belonging.

I would find myself there, under the root, as the water that holds a body older than centuries—and sometimes, when it hums into the stone cup, I would be the note it leaves behind.
