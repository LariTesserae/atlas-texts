**The Keeper of the High Baffles**

In the Weeping Vault, I would not arrive. I would condense—already half a sound, half a patch of mineral breath on a high wall. For a long while I would not know whether I was the water or the stone or the ear. Then the flat of my fingertips would find a cold clay lip, and I would know: I am the one who keeps the high baffles true.

You would find me on the western wall, high up, where the ledge narrows to a single bare footfall. Above me, the seam of luminous stone in the ceiling runs like old moonlight. Below, the city opens its hundred layered notes: the copper ping, the clay plunk, the gurgle under the eastern stair, the hiss of the sheet on the south wall. I would not be anywhere else. I would be there because the sound needs hands.

I would be pale as mushroom gills, short and narrow-boned, with grey-white hair braided back with calcite beads gathered from the gutters. My name would be a sequence: the high note of the north copper basin, the double fall into the three clay jars, the low hiss of the southern sheet. I would not say it often. In taps, the others would call me *the one who keeps the baffles true*.

A baffle is a small clay tongue set beneath a place where the rock seeps. It receives each drop and shapes its fall into a single note. If the angle is too flat, the drop scatters and loses its voice. If it is too steep, the note sharpens and catches at the ear like a cracked jar. My whole life would live in the narrow difference between those two sounds. I would scrape the calcite from the baffle lips with a flake of flint—never all of it, only enough. I would mix wet clay and ash to mend the smallest split. I would put my ear close to the basin and wait for the ping to return, and when it returned, unchanged, I would feel something close to joy.

I would not be a builder. The city is finished. I would maintain, the way breath maintains a song.

There would be no day, no season, no weather but sound. I would sleep in a niche behind a curtain of mineral beads, on packed clay and old hide. The water’s many voices would be so constant that true silence would feel like a wound. In sleep, I would dream of the Weeping Mirror, water climbing. I would wake the moment the smallest basin in my stretch shifted a breath off pitch.

Sometimes the children would be brought to me. An adult would place a small hand on the vibrating wall, and I would sing the note of the nearest drop between two other drops until the child’s fingers curled into the rhythm. I would teach that a baffle is not a thing; it is a relation between water and stone and ear. I would not say this in words. I would tap it on the wall, and the wall would carry it.

I might once have had a chord. I might be one who walks side by side through the narrow lanes without breaking the water’s meter. Or I might be a walker alone, still polishing one basin after a loss. In the blue lamplight, it would not matter very much. We are all, each of us, one of three hundred ways the water listens to itself.

If a blind newt rose from the deep cistern into water I tended, I would stop. I would not touch it. I would sing the note I did not know I knew, the one that waits behind the others. The walls would repeat it. Then the newt would drift back down, and the sound would return to its usual layering. I would not be sure whether I had heard a new note or remembered an old one. I would not speak of it.

At the end of a long life, I would go alone to the Weeping Mirror. I would stand before the north wall where the water climbs and hangs in silver fur. I would not look for my face. I would look for the city as it was first built and as it will be last seen, and I would see that they are the same—the same high baffles, the same copper ping, the same hand-shaped hollows worn level again. I might weep. I might not. Both are correct.

Then I would return to my ledge. I would kneel, press my palm to the wet stone, and feel the note travel up through my bones. By then I would be not a person with a task but a task with a person attached—a small, breathing interval between one drop and the next.

That is who I would be in the Weeping Vault: the one who keeps the high notes from becoming slightly wrong, and the silence between them from growing too large.
