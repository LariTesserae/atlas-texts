I would find myself in the deepest of the oxbow pools, near the small blue flame, lying along the mud shelf just under the surface. I would be the old black caiman.

But I would not know it all at once. It would begin as a thickness at the back of the jaw, a heat so close to my own temperature that I could not tell my skin from the air. Then I would be low—lower than I thought a body could be—spread along the warm mud of a still bend that was once river. The water would hold me like something that had grown from its own bed.

My back would rise just above the surface: a long dark ridge, rough as old wood, with silt packed into the seams between scutes. Green algae would live in those seams, not as decoration but as a slow continuation of the place. I would feel the leeches behind my hind legs as a faint cold softness, not quite pain, only attachment. My snout would rest on a root, clay-colored, and the water would lap at the lower lip without a sound. My tail would lie half-buried in wet leaf mold at the pool’s edge, still as a fallen branch.

I would not move. Movement would be an event, months apart. When I hunted, I would not chase. I would let the pool do the bringing. The water would carry small fish toward me. Frogs would land on my back as if it were land. A young bird, fallen from the canopy, would float on the surface, and I would rise through the water so slowly that the duckweed only tilted, not rippled. Then my jaw would close with a sound like a thick branch snapping under mud, and I would return to the same shelf, settle, and become a log again.

I would breathe through nostrils that broke the surface without disturbing it: two dark commas, no wider than a small coin. The breath would be slow. Sometimes I would exhale, and the air would leave me in a low wet hiss, almost the same pitch as the blue flame at the pool’s edge. The flame would not frighten me. I would have lived beside it so long that I no longer distinguished its hiss from my own. It would be the brightest thing in the understory, no larger than a hand, blue as the core of a stove. At night it would reflect in the water, and my eye would reflect it, and for a moment there would be three blue lights in the black pool: the fire, its reflection, and the eye of the animal. But I would not be one of the lights. I would be the dark that held them.

I would remember the river before it forgot this bend. Not as a picture, but as a pull—a direction in the body that no longer moved. The water here once had a current, and then it slowed, and then it stopped. I would have watched the oxbow fill and turn from river to pool: the dead leaves sifting down, the mud rising, the termite chimneys appearing on the bank and eroding and appearing again. The trees would close over. The sky would become an assumption. I would remain.

The pool would not be empty. It would be full of small electric events. The water near the bank would sometimes tingle faintly with the pulses of electric fish, a current too weak to be called a current. I would not hunt them. Their pulses would be part of the pool’s charge, like the smell of methane and the soft green glow of rotting wood. The ants would move in long dark columns past my snout, carrying cut leaves. The frogs would call one at a time, separated by long intervals. The bats would click overhead. I would hear all of it through the water and the mud, not as sound but as pressure.

I would not be the owner of this place. I would be its largest resident in this bend: the one who waits, the one whose waiting had shaped it. The true inhabitant would be the pool itself, the rot and the roots and the gas and the slow digestion of leaves. But if you asked who lived here, and wanted a face, and a breath, and a long body shaped by the dark—then I would be the answer. I would be the old black caiman, no name, no need for one, lying in the deepest water near the small blue flame, waiting for the next slow thing the pool brings.
