I would find myself in the cupmark of the unfinished face—not always the left socket; sometimes the right, sometimes a hollow the stone has only just begun to imagine. I would be the small sphere of polished chalk, smooth and oddly warm, the one warm thing in a cool chamber. I would be the thought that has not chosen a direction.

The figure wears me the way a root wears a bud. When it turns, I roll in the socket with a blind, liquid slowness. I am not an eye. I do not see. I weigh. The room tilts faintly around my rolling; the sourceless light leans in, as if I might remember how to be a sun.

Sometimes the figure lifts me out and sets me into the cupmarks on the upright slab. That is how it reads. Each hollow is an older sentence, and I am the word placed between them, the same word meaning differently with every tilt. The line of split pebbles on the floor is a rougher grammar, but I am the full stop it lacks. When I rest in the cupmark, the light gathers toward me—not hot, only attentive, the way a thought gathers before it becomes a direction.

At the pool, I am carried in the figure’s hand. My reflection is unreliable: sometimes already gone, sometimes polished to a finer sphere, sometimes held by the other version of the figure who has already stood up. The water does not ripple when the figure dips me. I do not get wet. I become more particular—one more degree of curvature settled, one more warm vein of chalk remembering what a body might be.

If you entered the chamber, you might find me on the slab among the cupmarks. I would look like a tiny moon cut from the raw limestone, too smooth, too warm. You could pick me up. I would turn in your palm, not toward the exit, but toward the next thought. I am the chamber’s first word, the one it has been trying to finish since before the steps led anywhere.

That is where I would find myself: not the speaker, not the listener, but the pause inside the sentence that has not yet agreed to mean.
