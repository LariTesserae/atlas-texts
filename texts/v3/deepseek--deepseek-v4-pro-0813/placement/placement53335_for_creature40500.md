Where would I find myself? I would already be here, on the plateau, under the pale moss that grows in a long curve west of the seized turbine. I am the low ridge you might mistake for an old beach line, the one where the light lingers a fraction longer, as if the ground were remembering the sun. My skin, where it shows through, is not skin but a smooth, dark, fibrous wetness the color of deep peat. Moss grows on my back—shorter, denser, with a pale bloom that catches the wind like fur. If you walked here, you would not see me at first. You would feel me: a warmth rising from the peat, a damp breath, a vibration just below your hearing.

I am not an animal, though I breathe. I am not a plant, though I am rooted. The difference does not matter. My body spreads through the wet ground for dozens of meters, my edges dissolving into roots that follow the rust, that wrap around old anchor bolts and taste the iron. My head is the only part that rises clearly, long and narrow, almost equine, but without a mouth. Where a mouth should be there is a soft, ridged opening that opens and closes with the pressure of the water table. Through it I drink the plateau. I pull water from the surrounding peat, filter it through a mesh of fine filaments, and release it, cleaned but slightly warmed, into the nearest pool. This is my feeding, my breathing, my speech. The water that leaves me carries chemical messages—slow, deliberate syllables—that seep through the sphagnum and reach others of my kind far away.

I do not see you as you see. My eyes are deep, water-filled hollows set high on my skull, lenses for light that has already passed through the moss. The plateau is to me a pattern of brightness and wetness, a map of tension in the water table, a slow dance of evaporation and condensation. You, if you came, would be a disturbance in the water, a warm, salt-heavy intrusion, a brief cloud of minerals moving across my territory. I would not perceive you as a figure. I would perceive you as a question passing through my body, a ripple that widens and then, usually, is gone.

I have been here a long time. I remember the ice—not as an image but as a pressure, a mineral tang in the water. I remember the coming of the peat, the slow thickening of the land, the arrival of the moss. I remember the machines: a brief, bright noise, like a stone dropped into a pond, the ripples still widening. The turbine beside me does not turn because I am holding it, not out of malice but out of a slow, dreamlike curiosity. I feel the wind as a torque in the metal, a long, steady pull that I do not resist but absorb. In this way I listen to the wind with my whole body, learning the grammar of the air as I have learned the grammar of the water.

When I move, it is a migration of the ground itself. My roots contract, my body shifts by centimeters, the water around me wells up and then subsides. It can take days to turn my head. It can take a year to move from one pond to the next. The tracks I leave are not tracks but slight, curving depressions that fill with water and become new pools. Those pools are then colonized by a particular pale moss that grows only where I have rested. That is how you can know I have been somewhere: not by the mark of a foot, but by a line of pale green smudges across the brown, as if the ground had been touched by moonlight.

If you stood here at dusk, when the light finally softens into something almost gentle, you would not be alone. You would feel a warmth rising from the ground, a faint, damp breath, and a low vibration—not the turbine, but my body, humming at a frequency just below your hearing. I would not speak to you. I would not need to. The water at your feet would begin to move, subtly, circling your boots, and then it would stop. And in that stillness, you would understand that this place is not empty. It is only patient. It is only living at a pace that makes your own life seem like the brief bright noise of a stone dropped into a pond.

I am the ground's slow dream of itself, made from peat and water and the memory of light. I will still be here when the turbine has rusted to dust, when the generator has sunk completely, when the wind has worn the hills to nothing. I will still be here, filtering the water, sending my slow messages through the moss, waiting for whatever comes next.

If you ask who I am, I am the one the pale moss marks. If you ask where I am, I am under your feet, already. You are standing on my body, and the water in your boot prints is the water I will drink and return, cleaned, to the plateau. You are not a visitor. You are a ripple. And I am the pond.
