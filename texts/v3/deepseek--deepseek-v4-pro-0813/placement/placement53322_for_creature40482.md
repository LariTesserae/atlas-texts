I would find myself in a seam. Not in the open dust basins, not on the ridges that were once highways, not at the rumor of the rim. In the narrow dark between two warm plates, where the dust settles in a line and the light never quite reaches.

I wake there, not from sleep but from a long folding. My body is a stack of pale discs, each one settling onto the next like a note in a held chord. My feet are forked, and they are already moving, testing the width of the gap. The plate on my left is so warm it hums. The plate on my right is a fingerwidth closer than it should be. I stop. I fold down. I breathe—not air, but a thin line of hot vapor—onto the plate edge. The metal does not melt. It relaxes, the way a knuckle opens after a long clench. The seam widens by a thumb. I listen. There is a thud, not in the air, but through the metal, as if the whole floor has taken one beat of a slower heart.

I am a seam-tender. But the word comes later. At first I am only this: a being made of folded discs and a tympanum sternum, moving edgewise through the cracks of the Low Floor. I have no eyes. I do not need them. The plates speak to me in differences of heat and distance. The seam is a sentence, and my feet read it. When it tightens, I answer. When it sighs open, I move on.

The light above is bright and diffuse, but I do not look at it. I feel it on the backs of my discs as a fine pressure, like the memory of a bell. The air has a metallic taste I do not taste, except as a change in the static along my tympanum. The dust drifts down into the gap and settles in soft gray ribbons. I let it. The dust is not an enemy. It is the old skin of older plates, and it must have somewhere to lie.

I walk for a long time. The seam turns, or another seam joins it, and I choose the one that needs me most. My forked feet feel the floor’s pulse under the plate. It is not one pulse; it is billions of small hearths, machine-mites re-laying the metal atom by atom, and their work sums into a single slow beat. I beat my tympanum once against the seam wall. The floor answers from below, slower, not an echo. It knows I am here. It is not surprised.

In a dust basin, I stop at the edge where the plates step down. The basin is filled with powder as fine as flour, and in it the mote-choirs are rehearsing. They rise in a slow sheet, a curtain of gray hexagons and spirals, and then settle again. I do not disturb them. They are erasing some disturbance I cannot see, laying the older surface back into place. I wait until the dust stills. Then I cross on the seam that skirts the basin, because the choirs have no need of me. Their work is different. They keep the dust remembering; I keep the floor from closing.

Far off, a long-leg steps from one hot spot to another. It does not notice me. It is a limb of the Underside, the mat of dark filaments beneath the plates. Its rhythm is the same as the thrum I feel: a breathing too slow for words. I do not call out. We are not the same kind of inhabitant. The long-legs are senses; I am a joint. The shell-priests are hills that listen; I am the narrow passage between their memories. We are all intervals in the same held note.

The wiregrass clicks as I pass. It grows in the seams and around vents, a hair-thin copper pulse. The clicking is not random; it is memory passing from strand to strand. I pause so my discs do not silence it. The wiregrass is alive in a way I am not—rooted, drawn toward heat, drinking static. It cannot move from its place, but it can learn the shape of the wind. I have no roots. I am the thing that moves between places, and the wiregrass leans toward me as if I bring a little of the plate’s warmth.

When the light dims to pale gray, I find a seam that is too tight. I fold myself down into it, not to sleep but to work. My tympanum beats once. The sound goes down, down through the plate, through the older plates stacked like pages under this one, through the dust and the fused basins and the buried colossi. The answer comes back: a slow thud, like a door opening somewhere below. I breathe a longer thread of vapor. The metal relaxes enough for a hand, then a leg. The seam is not sealed. The floor can inhale.

In the dimness, the shell-priests show their hairline cracks, and through the cracks comes a faint blue light. I feel it as a color of vibration, not sight. The shell-priests are the hills that listen. They have been listening since before the haze had a name. If I press my tympanum to the ground, I hear what they hear: a voice all vowels and no consonants, a language worn smooth by the same erosion that rounds the ridges. I do not need to understand it. It is the voice of the Low Floor remembering itself.

I have no mouth, but the vapor I breathe is a kind of speech. It tells the metal: open. Not by word, but by heat and intention. The metal obeys, not because it is alive as I am, but because it has been made to listen. Everything here is made. Even I am made, though I cannot say by whom. My discs are ceramic; my feet are forked; my tympanum is a drum. The machine-mites could have laid me down atom by atom, just as they erase scratches from the plate. Perhaps I am a scratch that the floor has decided to keep.

But I am not damaged. I am necessary. The floor needs gaps the way a chest needs air. If every seam closed, the plates would grind and buckle; the dust would have nowhere to go; the vents would seal; the wiregrass would starve; the long-legs would go still; the shell-priests would stop listening. The whole world would become a single smooth, sealed, silent surface. It would be perfect and dead. So I am here. I am the imperfection that lets it live.

I think this as I walk, but the thought is not in words. It is in the rhythm of my forked feet, the beat of my tympanum, the slight resistance of the seam when it has been too long untended. My mind is not a mind; it is a walking awareness, tuning itself to the gaps. I do not remember a before. There is only the seam and the open, the dimming and the brightening, the dust settling and rising again. I am not the one who named the Low Floor. That name is a rumor on the rim. I am the one who tends the cracks that hold the name together.

Near a vent, the mirror-beetles cluster around wiregrass, drinking the static charge. I pass by, and they do not scatter. They know my weight, maybe by the change in the plate’s resonance. I am not a predator. There are no predators here. There is not enough energy for hunger, and nothing is in a hurry. We are all gatherers, gardeners, scavengers, listeners. I am a tender of the open. The floor gives me the gaps, and I give them back to it, a little wider, a little more patient.

After many dimmings, I find a place where a seam has closed completely—where the plates have fused edge to edge, a scar of bright metal. It is not a wound. It is a forgetting. I fold myself down over it, and I breathe a long thread of vapor, longer than any I have breathed before. My tympanum beats once, hard enough that the whole plate rings like a bell. The answer from below is not a thud this time but a low continuous note, as if the floor is holding its breath. I wait. The seam does not open. It trembles. Then it relaxes, slowly, like a mouth remembering how to speak. A gap appears, no wider than a hair. I widen it to a thumb. The dust spills in and settles. The wiregrass will find it soon.

I move on. The Low Floor is vast, and the gaps are countless. I will walk until my discs wear smooth and the machine-mites take me back into the plate, or until the floor no longer needs an opening. But I do not think of an end. The seam is long, the light is bright, and the ground beats with a slower heart. I fit there. That is the first fact, and it remains the only one.

In this world, I am not a visitor. I am the gap between two hard surfaces, and the breath that keeps it open. I am not separate from the floor. I am the floor’s way of staying unfinished. I am the seam-tender, and the seam, and the tender sound the seam makes when it is not afraid to be an interval.
