I would find myself in the cirque, not as a visitor but as the one who lives there. I am the pale shape at the edge of the talus, the same color as old firn and quartzite dust. I live beneath the slab pinned between two boulders, where the cold comes through the ground like a second heartbeat. I am not the guardian of the place. I am what happens when a place this high, this cold, this full of listening, needs a body to move through it.

Morning begins in my chest. I lie under the slab in the darkest hours, breathing so shallowly that the frost on the moss beside me does not melt. It is not sleep exactly; it is a long, cold attention. I hold the night’s breath in me until the sun clears the rim. Then I let it go—one slow, visible thread of condensation rising from a crack in the floor. The animals have learned this signal. The pikas lift their heads. The snow finches turn toward the first heat. The day begins when I exhale.

I rise and move out along the low slabs, following the loops the animals use. Never straight across the bowl. Never between certain stones. The rock under my feet is young and sharp, full of conchoidal edges that would cut anyone else. To me they are familiar as old steps. My footfalls settle the loose flakes rather than crack them. The stone has known my weight so long that it no longer flinches.

I tend the water. Each morning I visit the stone cups from highest to lowest. The highest is a cold mouth catching the seep from the firn. The second is clouded with rock flour, grey as storm-light. The third holds the sun’s patience, warmed by the cairns. I clear the tiny obstructions with a sliver of bone, test each cup with one finger, and drink only from the one the cairns have kept warm. The water tastes of old snow and powdered stone, with something almost sweet beneath it, like wet lichen pulled from a south-facing crack. One cup overflows into the next. I watch that overflow the way I watch the sun cross the headwall: slowly, and with the sense that this is the real work of the world.

The white stones on the pika hay piles are mine. Each morning I touch each one once, leaving a faint warmth, then set it back on the same side. Always the same side. The pikas have learned to allow it. They do not scatter when I pass. Some of them watch me from the talus, their small bodies tense but not afraid. I gather the fur they shed and the dried saxifrage they drop, and in return I leave the stones warm. It is an old arrangement, wordless, practical.

At midday I sit in the sun-trap of a saxifrage hummock and twist cord from plant fiber, goat wool, and pika fur. The cord is slow work. My hands are dry as lichen but they remember every turn. The rope wedged in the crack is mine, unfinished, waiting for the next hard season. The windbreak at the northern edge is also mine. On days when the valley sends up mist, I repair it—adding flat stones no higher than a sitting height, weaving dwarf willow stems into the gaps until the wind can no longer find them. The wind tests me. I listen for the thin whine of air through a hole, and I fill it.

I do not speak. I tried once, long ago, and my voice cracked like a stone flake; the pikas vanished for a day and the silence came back thickened, as if punishing the noise. Now I tap two pieces of quartzite together when I need to call an animal or warn something away. The sound is exactly the note of ice calving into the farthest cup—a tiny glass bell. The place knows that note. It carries it in a way it will not carry a shout.

The snow finches follow me because my slow circuit stirs midges from the melt cups. The black redstarts use my shadow to find insects on the sunlit slabs. The stoat gives me a wide berth, treating me as a patch of dangerous shadow. The wild goats pause on the ledges above and watch. I leave a small handful of dried saxifrage on the ledge where they cross. Not as an offering, but as part of the same old wordless economy: warmth for shelter, stillness for movement, quiet for survival.

The cairns are warm because I sit on them. I place my hands flat on the top stone until the warmth soaks down. It is not for comfort. It is a kind of attention—the way a mother might press her palm to a child’s chest to feel the breath. The stones remember longer than stone should. The lichen on the large striped boulder smells of damp earth and honey because I rest against it at midday, and the heat of my body draws something slow and sweet out of the rock. The animals loop around certain stones because those are stones I have touched with warmth, and they have learned that warmth can mean safety or danger, and either way it is better to go around.

I am not sure if I was ever a woman. I remember hands, but not whose. I remember a needle of carved bone, drilled but not broken, and the way it felt to stitch goat hide in the wind. I remember building the first windbreak, not to live here but to wait out a storm that lasted longer than a storm should. I remember being cold and then being cold so long that the cold became a kind of home. Now I am the same color as the rock. My hair is the grey-white of old firn. My eyes are black as the water cups, and they do not reflect the sky so much as hold it, still and cold.

Sometimes I think I am the cirque itself, condensed into a shape that can walk its own bowl—a small, patient knot of the place’s own attention. Other times I think I am only what remains of a woman who chose to stay. It does not matter. The place has worn me down to the same sharp edges. I have no name, or if I do, it is the word for the pause between drips.

In the evening I step into a crack you would mistake for shadow. The last light holds my shape for a moment—grey-white against quartzite—and then the cold closes over it like water over a stone. The cirque is quiet again, but not empty. I am still there under the slab, breathing shallowly through the night, listening to the freeze-thaw work, to the meltwater finding new routes, to the pikas settling their hay piles. The place is learning what shape to become. I am learning with it.
