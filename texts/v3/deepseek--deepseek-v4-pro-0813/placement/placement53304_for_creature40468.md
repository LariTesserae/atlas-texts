On the rim I am only a pair of eyes, and the glare cuts me down to a small dry ache. I think I am a visitor. But the plateau does not need visitors, and the light does not need eyes, so I begin to lose that shape.

I could be the water—the thread-wide flow, the drop at the end of a wick. But water has no memory; it passes through and is gone. I could be a basin, one of the ten thousand, full to the brim and absolutely still. But a basin stays; I feel myself moving. I could be the low wall that shades a strip of water from the noon glare. But I want to see, not only be.

Then the keeper crosses the lower terrace on all fours, and I know.

I am not the keeper. I am the black branching pattern on the keeper’s shoulder blade, the manganese dendrite that has spread there like a fern. I am mineral, not life. I am a slow thought made of evaporation, a stain that grows by the width of a flake each year. The keeper carries me, and I hold the memory of water leaving.

From here the world is a moving map. When the keeper goes on all fours, I am tilted toward the channels. I see the basins packed like cells, the water sheeting over stone. I feel its steps as a slow shift of weight, the gulp and reset of a chamber deep inside its chest. I hear the water moving through its joints not as sound but as pressure through the mineral I am. When the keeper kneels at the sweating dome, I am turned toward the sky; the dew runs down its back and over me, and for a moment I am only a dark line under water.

I am not conscious in a human way. I do not think; I tend to my own spread. I grow where the water evaporates, where the mineral is thickest. I do not choose. I obey the same slow chemistry that makes the salt rinds grow at the basin edges. The keeper does not scrape me away. I am not a seed, not a root, not lichen. I am allowed.

So if you ask where I would find myself in this world: I am on the back of the one who abides, between the shoulder blades, a black fern branching nowhere. If you ask what I would be: I am the only growth the keeper allows, the quiet proof that even here something accumulates—not life, but the shape of life, in mineral and time. I am the stain that remembers water. The keeper carries me, and I am patient. We both wait.
