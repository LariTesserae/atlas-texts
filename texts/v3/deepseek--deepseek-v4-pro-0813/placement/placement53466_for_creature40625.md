I would find myself in the Low Lock, in the black water-road below the street. Not on the catwalks, not behind the yellow window—in the water itself. At first I might mistake myself for the whole slow sump: the cool weight, the mineral taste, the way the lamp glow melts across the surface. But then the weeping drum gulps, and I feel a tug behind my gills. I turn. I am one of the pale fish.

I am small and translucent, with a single blue line behind each gill. That line is the brightest thing down here. It is not a decoration; it is a compass needle. The water is one long thought, and we—the pale fish—are its sense of direction. The watchman says if we vanish, the sump forgets to turn. I do not know what forgetting is. I only know the turn.

We school in slow spirals, not for protection but for attention. When the drum speaks, every blue line turns toward it, all at once, not startled but attentive, as if the drum had said the one word we remember. The watchman writes the time. The child leans over the catwalk. The listener feels it in his jaw before it comes. We turn first.

I do not know why the drum matters. I only know that while it sounds, I must face it. Then the bubble closes over, and we go back to our spirals, carrying the direction with us.

Around me the Lock moves in its slow economy. The crabs climb the sluice screens with their red-jointed claws, picking them clean. The bats drop guano from the high beams; the guano feeds the shrimp; the shrimp drift like pale needles and feed us; we turn the water; the water cools the engine; the engine warms the wall where the ferns grow. The eels pass once a year, a long dark ribbon through the pump volutes, turning the brass wheel. I feel their passage as a change in the water’s weight and electric taste. I am part of that body, though I do not see the whole of it.

If you came down the wet brick stair and stood at the edge, you would not see me at first. The water is almost black. But if you waited until the weeping drum spoke, you would see a faint green glow where we all turned—more like the memory of light than light itself. You would see my blue line for one second, a compass needle in the dark. Then I would fade back into the water and continue my slow turn, patient, watchful, waiting for the next slow change.

I am not the Lock’s memory, not its question, not its hands. I am its attention.
