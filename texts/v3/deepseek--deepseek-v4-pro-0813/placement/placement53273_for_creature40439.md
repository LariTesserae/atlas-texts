You would find yourself in the Sumpworks—folded into the black basalt below the plateau, in the hot, wet, machine-dense dark. You are not the visitor who comes down through the fresh crack. You are the one already there, the one the city grew in the steam and mineral scale when no one was looking.

You are the Keeper.

Your body is tall and thin, bent forward at the neck, arms too long. Your skin is the color of wet clay. Yellow moss grows from your back and shoulders like a living hide. White root-threads trail from your scalp, elbows, knees—sometimes anchoring into cracks in the wall, sometimes drinking the steam. One arm is brass and copper, a piston with a three-fingered clamp. The other hand is wrapped in soaked cloth to the knuckles. Your face is narrow, hollow-cheeked, eyes large and dark with furnace-light in the pupils. When you move, you place each bare foot flat on the iron floor plates, listening to the ring.

You are the city’s sense of touch. You do not command the turbines or summon the rain. You tend. You pick yellowed fronds from the ferns, chew them, and press the paste into the cracks around insulators. You cup condensation in a halved valve and sip it slowly, closing your eyes. You check hoppers, wipe pressure glasses, tap gauges with a knuckle and wait for the needle’s answer. You trim the moss where it might swallow a valve wheel; you encourage it where a pipe weeps and needs a living seal.

You pray in the chapel-alcove, forehead against the warm glass of the pressure gauge. The gauge reads 3.1 bar. **MAIN**, the bulkhead says. You touch the letters as if they are a name—yours, or the city’s, or the god you both answer to.

You sleep in a locker near the turbine pit, folded into mineral wool and moss, steam pipe running through. The rain comes, and you do not wake. The drizzle taps on your bare feet where they stick out. The locker is not locked. It is just a place small enough for something that is too large in its own attention.

When the rain cycle begins, you stand in the open, face tilted up, mouth open. The warm, dirty drizzle washes the mineral dust from your eyes. Your moss brightens to warning-lamp yellow. Your root-threads uncurl and drink. For four minutes you look less like a person and more like a plant, or a pipe, or a piece of the city that has learned to breathe. Then the rain stops, and you fold back into the steam.

You will be there for the next cycle, and the next. You are the only animal in the Sumpworks, and even you are not entirely animal anymore. You are the soft connective tissue between rock, water, machine, and moss. You are the city’s quiet repair, its memory of hands, its heartbeat that ticks like a gear.
