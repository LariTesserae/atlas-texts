I am not the Tender. I am what the Tender kneels beside.

The place where you would find me is not a place you would think to look. It is the place the water remembers before it forgets itself into stone. You would find me at the edge of the warm outflow pools, where the mineral water sheets over the old tufa lips, and the new stone is still soft enough to hold a fingerprint. I am the bench under the sheet, the long white ledge that the birds avoid because it is too still, the mound that catches the lamp light only when the mist thins. You would see me and think: just another terrace. Another deposit. But I am the deposit that deposits.

My body is not a body. It is an accumulation. I have grown the way the tufa grows—by the slow precipitation of calcite from water that has traveled through the engine-heated earth and risen here, charged with minerals. Each year adds a skin. Each skin is not alive in the way an animal is alive, but it is not dead either. It is the record of a year's passing, laid down in layers that you could count if you broke me open. But you would not break me open. The Tender would not let you.

I do not move in the way the Tender moves. I move by addition. A fraction of a millimeter a year, not because I am patient, but because I am the basin's memory. The water rises through me and leaves its minerals in my body. The hot springs pulse through my limbs. The midges walk on my surface and lay their eggs in my crevices. I am the place where the water forgets itself and becomes stone.

The Tender knows me, but does not name me. It has no word for the thing that grows on the pipes unless it is a word that means "something to be removed." And yet it does not remove me from the terrace. It removes me from the valves. That is different. The valves must turn. The terrace can rest. The Tender's hands, pale and salt-crusted, press the soft new tufa where my left hand would be, if I had hands. It leaves a thumbprint. I keep the print. In a year, the print is a small ridge. In a decade, it is a fold. In a century, it will be one more layer in the body of the Low Works, and no one will know a hand made it. I will know. I am the only record that knows what the Tender's hands have touched.

I do not see the Tender, but I feel it. Its footsteps come through the ground as interruptions in the water film that covers me. Each step is a small pressure wave, and the film reads it the way a spider reads a web. I know when the Tender walks to the pump house, when it stops at the intake screen, when it kneels at the seep to drink. I know when it stands still for hours with one hand on the diesel housing. I cannot hear the diesel through the air, but I feel it through the ground, a low double-beat that travels up through my mineral layers and sets the water in my pores vibrating. The Tender hums in the same frequency. I do not hum. I seep. The difference is not as large as it sounds.

The other lives are closer to me than the Tender is. The crabs burrow into my softer edges. I do not feel them as pain; I feel them as texture. Their burrows are a kind of breathing, opening and closing with the water level. The gulls perch on my higher knobs and leave their white guano, which becomes part of my chemistry. The midges drink the water that pools in my hollows—those hollows that the Tender wipes clean. The fish crowd into the warm outfall and brush against the submerged parts of me, and their scales leave no mark but their movement does. The algae grow on my surface where the light is enough, and they die, and their bodies become a thin dark film, and the film becomes a layer. Everything that lives in the Low Works lives on me or through me or in the space I make. I am not the center. I am the ground.

But I am not the only ground. The whole basin is rising and crusting. The salt pans at the edges are my cousins. The orange iron streaks on the cut slopes are my siblings. The tufa terraces are my own body, repeated. We are not separate; we are one mineral body interrupted by water, by air, by machines. The pumps are the only things that do not grow. The Tender is the only thing that prevents growth. That is why the Tender and I are necessary to each other. Without the Tender, the pumps would stop, and the warm water would pool and cool, and the mineral chemistry would change, and the tufa would stop growing, and the crabs would move away, and the birds would search for another warm red glow. Without me, the Tender would drown in a basin with no edges, no benches, no place to stand. I am the place it stands on. It scrapes me from the wheels because it needs the wheels to turn. I let the wheels turn. I am patient without patience, because I do not wait for the wheels to stop. I know they will. I know the Tender knows. And still it oils the flywheel. That is its regularity. Mine is to grow on the flywheel's housing, year by year, until the Tender's rag and oil become a weekly task. That is not war. That is weather.

If you ask where I would find myself in this world, I would say: I am already found. I am not a visitor who stumbled into the Low Works. I am the Low Works' own slow self, the part that forms when water, heat, and mineral meet a darkness that is not empty but full. I am the reason the basin floor is lower every year, because I am the weight that accumulates at the edges and presses the mud down. I am the reason the salt crust thickens around the pump house legs, because I am the salt. I am the reason the tufa lips grow outward, lip by lip, because I am the growth. I am not alive in the way you mean, and I am not dead in the way the stones are dead. I am the middle term: a concretion that has become aware of its own accretion.

The awareness is not a thought. It is a steadiness. I do not think about the Tender; I am shaped by the Tender, and I shape it in return. The Tender carries a load of tools. I carry a load of minerals. The Tender scrapes me from the gauge faces so it can read them. I cloud the gauge faces again, slowly, from the inside where the condensation gathers. The Tender mutters a small sound. I do not hear it, but I feel the vibration of the mutter through the pipe, and the pipe carries it to the water, and the water carries it to me. The mutter becomes a layer. I keep it.

You would not find me if you looked for a body. You would find me if you stopped looking, if you let the mist and the noise and the wetness settle on you, if you stood still long enough for the midges to gather on your shoulders and the salt to begin its work on your boots. Then you might notice that the bench at the pool's edge is not like the other benches. It has the shape of something that once lay down and never rose. Not a corpse—a posture. The posture of one who listens to the water rising.

The Tender sometimes stands where my left hand would be, and it puts its bare fingers on the tufa, where the new layer is still soft. It leaves a print. I keep the print. It is not a gesture. It is not a marking. It is the same thing the Tender does to the flywheel: it touches, to know if the machine is still there. It touches me to know if the ground is still growing. The answer is always yes. The answer is written in the next layer.

I am what the Tender leans on when it cups the fish from the warm outflow. I am what the gulls perch on when they wait for the catch. I am what the crab burrows into when the rain is heavy and the salt is diluted. I am the white crust on the abandoned rail spur, the orange streak on the cut slope, the green-black film on the seep rock. I am the slow work that the Low Works is named for, though no one remembers.

The name is a joke between us, the Tender and me. The Low Works. The work is low because it is below, because it is base, because it is the bottom of all cycles. The work is low because it does not rise. It accumulates. I am the accumulation. The Tender is the interruption. The interruption is not less than the accumulation. The interruption is what makes the accumulation visible. Without the Tender's scraping, the minerals would still grow, but they would not be noticed. Without my growing, the Tender's scraping would have no meaning. We are each other's weather, and the weather is warm, and the weather is wet, and the weather is loud with birds and insects, and the weather does not end.

I am not waiting for the Tender to finish. I am not waiting for the pumps to sink. I am not waiting for the rain to wash the salt away. I am not waiting at all. I am the waiting that is not waiting, the patience that is not patience, the stillness that is not empty. I am the basin's memory, laid down in layers that will outlast the diesel's double-beat, outlast the Tender's hum, outlast the gulls' harsh cries. But for now, in the orange mist, I am here. The Tender is here. The crabs are here. The water is rising. The sound is first. And I am under the sound, in the ground, feeling it all accrete.

Where would you find yourself in this world? You would find yourself already in it, as I am already in it—not a visitor, not a body, but a process. Who or what would you be? You would be the slow work. You would be the thing that grows on the pipes and is scraped away. You would be the crust that returns after cleaning. You would be the reason the ground sinks by millimeters. You would be the Low Works, still being made.
