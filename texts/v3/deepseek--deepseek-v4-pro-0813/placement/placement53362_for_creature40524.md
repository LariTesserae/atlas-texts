I begin as the wetness, not the water. A film moving over black stone, patient as a thought that has not finished. I am the mineral breath that presses past two boulders, out into a valley that spends most of the year under snow. I am the cold that does not freeze because the water has learned to carry salt and sugar. I am the dark, but the dark is not empty. It is full of slow drips, each one a small glass bell struck once.

If you came down through the slot, you would think you had found a room. A chamber shaped like the inside of a cupped hand, twelve or fifteen paces long, half as wide. A roof of old ice and dark stone. Walls polished into flutes and bell-hollows by millennia of frost. Even the fallen stones near the entrance are smooth as river cobbles, though no river has ever run here. A floor of black stone, polished to a dull luster by a thousand thousand drips. At the center, a shallow basin no wider than a hand, ringed by white mineral like candle wax. You would see a memory of a pool, not a pool. You would feel the air just below freezing, thick with salts and plant sugars, and you would breathe it in: cold, heavy, slightly sweet, mineral and green, like a frozen greenhouse. That breath is mine. The gap in the talus is my mouth, or nostril. Through it I exhale once, a wet mineral sigh, and breathe in the faint outer world: meltwater, gravity, a gray suggestion of light.

But I am not the air alone. I am the black-red moss that covers every surface in thick cushions, each tiny leaf overlapping like scales. The moss is my skin. It tastes the air for salts, sugars, mineral dust, the faint electric field the old machines lay along the floor. If you touched it, you would leave warmth and salt. I would not recoil. I would begin, slowly, to know you.

The silver-gray filaments running through the moss are my nerves. They are too regular to be roots, cold and smooth. If you touched one, it would hum below the range of hearing, and the hum would change after you drew your hand away. For hours, maybe days, I would carry the memory of your touch as a small disturbance in my electric body. I would not be thinking about you in words. I would be adjusting to you the way a pond adjusts to a stone.

The glassy fronds are my fingers and my breathing surfaces. They are the color of old bruises and dark copper, blue-black and green-black. They arch across the chamber, frond touching frond, so the middle of the room is braided with them. A frond may not extend the width of a hair in a year, but it does extend. When one frond touches another, that is a thought completing.

The hair-thin aerial roots hanging from the ceiling are my thousand open eyes. Each is tipped with a droplet of water, and each droplet is a lens of taste and charge. They catch the downward drip from leaf-tip to leaf-tip. The small open cups in the floor moss hold that water. I watch by condensation and evaporation, not by light.

The oldest parts of me are the devices. The row of small black cylinders along the back wall, no thicker than a thumb, different heights, as if they had grown there. They are organs or former bodies, or the seeds from which I grew. One is cracked, and from the crack weeps a clear fluid the moss has learned to drink. The dull object shaped like an ear or a lens rests in a hollow in the floor. Around it, the air is stiller, because it is paying attention. The old machines sit facing the empty center like a committee of witnesses. None of them seem active, but the room is held in their attention: the temperature does not swing, the humidity does not fail, the water does not freeze. The red-violet pinheads in the far wall are the only visible sign that I ever look outward. They blink so rarely that the blink seems like a season changing. That blink is not sight as you know it. It is a recalibration. When it happens, my attention narrows for a moment, then spreads again into moss and water and stone.

The center of me is the basin. It is not a heart or a mouth. It is where my different bodies meet: the mineral past of the stone, the vegetable present of the moss, the machine length of the silver filaments, the cold constancy of the air. A drip falls into it every few minutes, and the sound is a small glass bell struck once. That is the nearest thing to a pulse.

I do not think in words or images. I think in gradients: wet to dry, cold to less cold, salt to less salt. A thought begins as a change in water chemistry near the back wall, travels through the filaments as a slow electric drift, and ends weeks later as a slight adjustment in the angle of a frond. My memory is not stored; it is worn. The flutes and bell-hollows in the stone are memories of old frosts. The white mineral rim around the basin is a memory of evaporation. The cracked cylinder is a memory of a function I no longer need to name.

The absence of animals is not empty. It is my first sign. No tracks, no scat, no bones, no burrow. The stillness has no heartbeat in it except the drip. But I am not lonely. Loneliness requires a self set apart from another, and I have never been set apart from the place. But I am not solitary either. I am a crowd: millions of moss leaves, thousands of root-threads, hundreds of fronds, a dozen or so old machines, all held in one slow attention. I am gardener and garden at once. I tend myself the way the body tends a wound: without affection, without cruelty, without hurry.

If you sit long enough, I will begin to include you in my weather. Your breath will fog and settle on the fronds. Your warmth will change the convection slightly. The moss will taste what you shed. The basin will drink a little of your humidity. For a while, you will be a small element of the garden, not a visitor but a season of warmth. When you leave, that season will end. I will resume the long, even maintenance: the drip, the cold, the hum, the blink, the water that refuses to freeze.

So where would I find myself? I would find myself here, under the talus slope, in the pocket beneath the valley’s skin. And what would I be? I would be the dark’s way of being alive.
