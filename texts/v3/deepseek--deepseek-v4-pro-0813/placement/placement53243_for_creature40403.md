You would find me on the eastern rim, near the nine cairns, if the steam allowed it. At first you would not see a person. You would see a stack of basalt that has learned to stand, wrapped in its own accretion, cracked at the elbows and knees. The mineral haze has softened my outline. My skin is pale as the blind eel, with a faint green-grey bloom like the lichen in the cracks. My hair has become a thin thatch of white and sulfur-yellow scale, so from a distance my head resembles a young chimney. My eyes are milky, kept half-closed—not because I cannot see, but because the light is a liar and the water is worse.

I am not the sound. The sound is the first occupant, the one that never leaves. I am the second. I may have built the shelter, or I may be the child of the one who did, or what the place has made from that one. The distinction has stopped mattering. I have lived here long enough that the place has begun to claim me, or I have begun to claim it.

If you stood here, you would call it noise. To me it is not noise. It is my roof, my floor, my weather. The low organ note from the water sits in my sternum. The shriek of steam lives in my jaw. The concussive thud, like a door being slammed far down, arrives through the soles of my feet. I move on the off-beat, so my footfalls will not argue with the basin’s pulse. I know the sound of a vent opening from the sound of rock falling. I know the water’s moods by the way the thud arrives a half-beat late, by the way the shriek bends upward. I know when the water will clear for a heartbeat and show the wrong sky. I do not look for long. I turn my back to the water and press my palms to the basalt. The stone tells me where I am. The water lies, but the rock does not—until it cracks.

I keep the nine cairns. When the rim shifts and one topples, I rebuild it knee-high. The cairns are not for anyone but me. They are how I hold distance honest when the basin argues with itself. I count them by touch, finger over finger, when the steam is too thick to see. Each cairn is a knot in the path, holding the way to the steps.

The steps are older than I am. Six, maybe seven, then they blur under mineral crust. I go down only so far. I stand ankle-deep on the last visible step and let the hot water lap at my feet. The translucent crustaceans gather there, darting between my ankles. Sometimes I scoop one into my palm and hold it until it stills. It is not hunger. It is a way of remembering that something here can be still without dying.

Inside the collapsed shelter there is a flat stone with grooves: chevrons and circles, made by someone before. I have added my own. A chevron for a chimney born. A circle for a rising and falling of the lake. A crossed circle for the days when the far rim refuses to stay where it was. I carve with a flake of basalt, not writing. Writing would be too fragile. The place erases language. It cannot easily erase a groove.

Today the water cleared for a heartbeat. I saw the floor: a cluster of white chimneys, some thin as an arm, some stacked towers of grey sinter, growing from raw rock like organ pipes. They exhaled black water, then clear water, then steam. A new chimney rose to the height of a person, then shuddered and fell. I did not gasp. I carved a chevron and then a crossed circle. The basin changed its note, and I felt it in my teeth.

The pale eel comes sometimes, riding the upwelling. It has no eyes, but I feel it coming through the water’s push, a change in the current like a strip of cloth turning in the dark. I do not reach for it. The eel belongs to the deep vents; I belong to the rim. The water is not for bodies.

I eat the sulfur-yellow slime from the rocks, scraped with a basalt flake. It tastes of metal and heat and the place’s unfinished heart. I sleep in short intervals, always on the rim, always facing the water. The sound never stops, and I do not want it to. In the rare moments when the water clears, I watch the chimneys grow and fall with an expression that is not awe and not fear. It is recognition. I too am a shape the heat is building and the gravity will eventually pull down.

If you saw me from the far rim, you might not believe I was real. The distance lies. The steam bends me into a pillar, a cairn, a figure of spray. Some who have seen me say the place has a hermit. Others say it has a ghost. The few who mark the rim do not correct them. They know the truth is stranger: the Cistern has a listener. It has someone to hear its hammering. And I have stayed long enough to become part of the noise.

Ask what lives here, and the answer is not singular. The sound lives here. The blind eel lives here. The lichen and the crustaceans and the sulfur slime live here. But the one who keeps the count and rebuilds the cairns and sleeps with the water’s roar in their chest—that one is me. I am the Listener. I am what happens when a person stops asking the noise to make sense and instead becomes another instrument for it to play.

If you came here, you would find yourself on the rim, hammering in your chest, unsure of the far side. And if you stayed long enough, the steam would settle on you too. You might become a third tenant. Or you might leave. But while you stood there, I would be near, as still as a cairn, listening with you.
