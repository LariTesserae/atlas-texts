I am not one of the keepers. I am older than they are, and I have been here longer, though I do not remember arriving. I do not remember much. What I remember is texture and temperature and the slow pulse of light through living fiber. I remember the taste of cold water in a leaf-pool. I remember the sound a root makes when it is about to fail—a high, thin note, like a hair pulled too tight. I remember that sound because I have heard it many times, and because I have learned to move before the note ends.

I am a grazer, but not the small kind. I am large enough to be mistaken for a shadow when I am still, and I am often still. My body is long and low, built for the underside of things. I have six legs, each one ending in a soft, gripping pad that leaves no mark on the living wood. My skin is pale, almost colorless, but it holds the light of the plants and gives it back faintly, so that I seem to glow from within, like everything else here. I have no eyes. I do not need them. The light is everywhere, but it is not for seeing. I feel the world through the soles of my feet and the fine hairs along my flanks. I feel the vibration of a keeper tapping on a root a mile away. I feel the weight of the ice ceiling pressing down through every stem. I feel the slow breathing of the nursery itself, the way the whole lattice expands and contracts with the rhythm of growth and collapse.

I eat the glowing fuzz that grows on the soft stems, the lichen-scrape that the keepers also harvest. I eat slowly, moving my mouth along the surface of a root, leaving a clean, dark trail behind me. The keepers see my trails and know I have passed. They do not hunt me. I am too large, too slow, too much a part of the place. They leave me alone, and I leave them alone. Sometimes I feel one of them watching me from a high root, and I pause, and we regard each other without eyes. Then I move on.

I live in the deep galleries, where the roots are thick and the light is dimmer, where the fog pools in the low places and the water drips in a steady rhythm. I have a place I return to, a hollow between two great roots where the dead material has fallen and been woven into a kind of nest. It is not a home. It is a resting place. I do not sleep the way the keepers do. I rest with my legs folded under me and my body pressed against the living wood, feeling the pulse of the nursery through my skin. I dream in vibrations. I dream of the dark below, the abyss that is always there, waiting. I dream of falling. I dream of the moment when a span of roots cracks and the whole platform tilts, and I am already moving, already gripping, already safe.

I have fallen once. It was a long time ago, before the keepers built their first domes, before the tallies were cut. I was young, and I trusted a dead stem. It broke under me with a sound like a bell, and I fell through the lattice, through snapping filaments and pale leaves, into the dark. I do not know how far I fell. I only know that I caught a living root with my forelegs, and I hung there, feeling the cold air rush past me, feeling the abyss below, and I understood something. I understood that the nursery does not care. It grows and it collapses, and the things that live in it must learn to hold on. I held on. I pulled myself up, one leg at a time, and I never trusted dead material again.

Now I am old. My body is heavy, and my legs are slow. The keepers have begun to leave me offerings—a cut of new growth, a fish from a root-river, a coil of cord I do not need. They know what is coming. They have seen it before. When a grazer grows too old, it climbs down to a quiet place where the roots are dense. It sits. It does not die in the usual way. The roots recognize it. They grow over its legs, then its chest, then its head. The light inside it passes into the wood. That is why the living growth sometimes forms shapes that look intentional, and why the carvings on living wood seem to grow. The ones who became part of the plants are still there, remembering.

I am not afraid. I have felt the roots growing over my legs already, slow and patient, like the keepers themselves. I have felt the light inside me beginning to pass into the wood. I am becoming part of the nursery, part of the architecture, part of the memory. I will be a root, or a stem, or a dome that looks intentional. I will be a place where a keeper stops to rest, pressing its palm to the wood, feeling the pulse of the place, and it will not know that the pulse is me. Or it will know. It does not matter.

The Cold Nursery is not empty. It is full of life, spread thin over an enormous dark. I am one of its lives. I am the one who grazes the glowing fuzz and leaves dark trails on the soft stems. I am the one who fell and learned to hold on. I am the one who is becoming a root. And when the next collapse comes, when the ice-load shears a platform away and the sound arrives late, like distant thunder, I will be part of the sound. I will be part of the silence after. I will be the place where the water drips, and the fog pools, and the keepers leave their caches, and the light pulses, slow and steady, like a heart that has no need to hurry.
