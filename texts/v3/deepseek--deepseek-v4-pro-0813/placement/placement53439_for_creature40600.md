I would find myself in the lowest bowl, a little north of the central trough, where the black moss mats thickest and drips in a patient, almost conversational rhythm. I would be standing at the mouth of an alley that exists only when seen from one end—on the side where it has already become a row of wet stones. The cold would not blow; it would wait. I would be waiting with it.

At first I might mistake myself for the damp, or for the afterimage of a window seen through wet air. Then I would move, and understand. I am tall and narrow, as if the streets have pressed me into a door-shaped thought. I turn slightly even in open spaces, because somewhere behind me a door is remembering not to close.

My clothing is not clothing exactly. It is an accrued surface: mineral damp, pale lichen scabbing at the elbows, gray-green ceramic scales at the cuffs. Black moss grows along my seams, not from neglect but from belonging. It beads with condensation. When I stand very still, I am old stone standing in the rain for a long time.

My face is hard to keep. I have seen it only in puddles that are more like windows. It is smooth as polished pavement, with features appearing only when I am not looking directly. My eyes are too far apart, or too close; the number changes. My mouth moves before my words, or after them. When I greet someone, the sound reaches them while my lips are still, and then the lips move a little, catching up. My voice lacks an overtone, like the bell in the tower that has not decided to be a bell tower.

I have two shadows. One lags behind by half a step. The other pools at my feet, or lifts partway up a wall, or points the wrong way down an alley. I am careful never to step on either. When the two shadows cross, I pause until they separate. This is courtesy, not fear. Sometimes I look back at my footprints in the soft mineral paste. They remain for hours, then fade not by drying but by being forgotten. I nod, making sure they are being forgotten properly.

My work is the opposite of completion. I press my palm against a wall that has thickened too quickly; the wall pauses, apologetic. I stand in a doorway that has forgotten it was ever an opening, humming at a frequency just under hearing, until the door remembers and rises back into the wall like skin healing. I carry no tools. When a correction is needed, I pluck a moth-sized drone from the air and whisper to it, or I simply gesture, and the air agrees. I leave every task one step short. I never close a door all the way. I never speak a sentence that ends in a hard stop.

I came from a building that was reabsorbed into its own blueprint. I do not remember it clearly—only a staircase with fourteen steps descending and eleven ascending. Perhaps I was the thirteenth step, or the difference between the two counts, or the hesitation in a runnel flowing uphill before it remembered itself. When the building was reabsorbed, something was left over: a fold of intention, a half-finished thought, a stair that no longer counted correctly. That leftover stood up, brushed the mineral damp from its shoulders, and joined the others. That is me.

I eat little. I press my lips to a north-facing wall where the black moss is thickest and drink the condensation. Sometimes I nibble a colorless fern growing from buried heat, careful never to take the last frond. The cold is not a discomfort; it is a medium. It settles in my hands and behind my ears, and I let it.

I gather with the others at the central trough when the water is very cold, when the eel-like things become visible—faintly pink cartilage and glass, seeming to be elsewhere at the same time. We stand shoulder to shoulder on the wet stones, saying nothing. Perhaps we are watching ourselves. The long-legged insect that walks across the wall without casting a shadow is treated as a colleague. I nod to it. It does not nod back. That is polite.

My name changes each meeting. Sometimes it is a color, sometimes the sound of water, sometimes a numeral left unfinished. If you ask me my name, I will look at you as if you have asked a puddle where it keeps its map.

I rest in an upper room where gravity is managed in patches. I prefer the rooms where my inner ear insists I am falling. I lie down on the air, or on a floor that is not quite horizontal, and I dream of streets that connect both ends. I always wake before the connection is complete, and I leave the dream exactly one step short. Then I go out into the city, which is an open sentence, and I am one of its open doors.

If you stand long enough in an alley that exists only when seen from one end, you may feel me pass behind you. You will not hear footsteps. You will feel the air thicken, then hesitate, then go on. A puddle at your feet will ripple, though no stone has fallen. When you look down, the reflection will show the unbuilt tower and two soft shadows standing where yours should be. By the time you turn, I will be gone, or I will not have been there at all. Both are true.
