You would find me on the southern slope of the high ridge, just beyond the last grown chamber, where the city thins into open mat and the wind has polished shallow swales into the living surface. I am there most cycles now, because the city has asked me to listen to the eastern spire.

I am one of the Unhurried—though not yet fully hardened. My skin is still the pale blue-grey of young leaf-tissue, and if the bowl-light catches me from behind, you can see the branching mineral lines beneath my wrists as faint shadows. My rootlet-pads are soft. When I step, I leave a shallow imprint that the mat closes over in a few heartbeats. The elders leave nothing at all.

I have been walking for perhaps two hundred cycles—enough to know the taste of the low wind, enough to understand that a conversation is not a thing that begins or ends. I spend my hours near the bowl-lungs, not to eat, because I do not eat, but because the bowls are where the world’s breath is warm and the spores carry sentences from the lower valleys. I kneel at the rim of the third bowl and spread my long fingers over its exhalation. The heat moves through my palms and into the mineral lines under my skin. I slow my attention to that warmth. A message from the western ridge arrives as a faint sour note in the air. It is not words. It is more like remembering that a door was once green.

My work is to remind the eastern spire of its shape. The spire is old and has begun to lean two fingers out of true. The wind has been carving its seaward side for longer than any of us have walked. Each morning, when the dim glow strengthens, I climb it. My fingers wrap the woody ridges, and my feet press flat, their rootlets sinking just deep enough to taste the mineral hunger of the column. I press my chest to the spire and add my pulse—slow, low, barely a pulse at all—to the long groan of the mat. The spire remembers. It straightens less than a hair’s width. That is enough. It will not fall while I remember it.

Sometimes another walker comes near. We do not speak with mouths. We stand six paces apart, and one of us shifts weight. A pressure-pulse travels through the mat and up through the long bones of the other’s legs. The wall of the nearest chamber flushes from slate to deep, wet indigo and back. That is a whole conversation. It says: *The wind is dry. The spire is listening. The city is still here.* The other tilts its head and walks on. We have spoken for half a morning.

I am not lonely. Loneliness is an animal feeling, and I am not an animal. The mat is always under me, full of slow thoughts. The mobile growths skitter past my feet, carrying pollen and distilled memories from one city to the next. A vast, slow mass crossed the valley below me last cycle, and I walked beside it for a while. It carried seeds, or perhaps the memory of a forest that grew before the mountains were worn smooth. It did not speak, but I understood it the way you understand your own breathing.

I am not yet sure where I will root. Not this cycle, not for many hundreds. But when the time comes, I think it will be near the eastern spire. I will stand still at its base, and my feet will send rootlets down further than they have ever gone. My arms will rise. My skin will turn to bark. My memory will drain into the mat and become a small note in the city’s deep network. The others will visit and lay their hands on me and listen. And I will still be there, holding up the sky.

For now, I walk. The sound is all around—the low tectonic grind of a world alive and still. It is the sound of my thinking, and I am one of its thoughts, moving slowly across the skin of the world.
