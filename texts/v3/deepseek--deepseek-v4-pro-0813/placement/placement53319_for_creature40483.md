Where would I find myself? In the cold first. Seven degrees, slow-moving, never quite still. The light is blue-shifted and ordinary, bright enough to cast shadows on raw black basalt. The sound is continuous: snapping shrimp crackling like static, carbonate chimneys huffing, mussel beds clicking, whale song arriving through the rock as though the mountain itself were singing.

If I enter a single body, I am the small octopus. I flow across a basalt slab, each arm tasting the stone. I turn black to match the rock, then white where the water whitens, then black again. I pause at the edge of a brine pool. Its surface is a mirror, and the black fangs appear in it upside-down, sharper than the real world. I do not enter. The water there is too heavy, too salty, too still. I am a creature of the moving seam, the boundary where one kind of water becomes another.

But I am not only that body. I am also the bacterial mat under my arms—the orange and rust and white crust that eats what the mountain breathes. I am the mussels packed into the fissures, each one farming bacteria in its gills. I am the tubeworms rising like organ pipes, red plumes flinching when a shadow crosses. I am the crab prying with pale, hairy arms; the snail rasping; the amphipod haze thickening the water. I am the silver fish school parting around the chimneys with a low whump. I am the skate half-buried, the wolf eel watching, the glass sponge filtering, the red worm at the salt margin.

The question asks who lives here. The answer cannot be one creature. I am the crowdedness itself, the density beyond comfort, packed into every crack and over every surface. I am the living seam where the seep-water rises and the sea receives it. I am the mineral engine’s soft wiring, grown not built.

Where would you find me? On the drowned saddle between two black fangs, high ground in the sea, under ordinary light, in a place too loud and too abundant to be called empty. I am not a person. I am all of them at once.
