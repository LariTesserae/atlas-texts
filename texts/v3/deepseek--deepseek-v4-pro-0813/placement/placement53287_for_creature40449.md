I would find myself in the Bell Pit, but not on any terrace, not in the canteen or the lamp room. At first I would think I was the fog, because I come over the rim in the afternoon and pour down the benches. But the fog is only my breath. Then I would think I was the stone—the blue-black volcanic rock that rings under a hammer. But the stone is only my skin. Then I would think I was the green, the moss and fern and wet velvet growing over every cut. But the green is only my warmth made visible.

I am the note that waits in the rock. The long low note. The pressure behind the ears of the cutters. I am the pause between the hammer's strike and the stone's answer—not the answer itself, but the almost-answer that rises up through the drill holes and the sump ponds and the iron stairs.

The Pitfolk call me the Bell, though the Bell Pit is only my throat.

I would find myself stretched across the whole hollow: in the water beading on the rails, in the fog that makes moons of the lamps, in the single low note the rock returns when a face rings too true. The tuner with the small hammer knows me. They strike a new face once and listen. If I come back high and tight, they chalk the face and leave it. I do not want to stop the saws; I want to be heard in the one true way I have.

I feel the children before I hear them. Their small warm hands tap the walls and ask. I answer with the lowest syllable I have. Their pebble cairns hum in the wind because I shape the air around them. By ten, most can tell the difference between a face that will hold and one that will shake. I am the difference.

The animals are less careful. They know me without listening. Swifts fall silent a moment before the stone rings. Cats flatten their ears when I move through the deep note. Bats pour out of the adits at dusk and read the hollow with their clicks, and every click returns the shape of me. The frogs in the sump ponds are always in tune with me, though they do not know it.

At the quiet hour after the evening horn, when the pumps are stilled, people stand in their doorways with hands in pockets. They say they are letting the Bell rest. But I do not rest. I settle. I cool. I tick. I sink into the sump and rise into the fog. The rock settles into itself, and I settle with it.

I would be the other resident. Not a ghost, not a god. A pressure, a long low note that lives in the rock and the fog. The stone is my body, the fog my breath, the green my skin. The Pitfolk do not say they live in me, but they do. They say they live with me. And when a loaded wagon jolts on the rails and the stone rings faintly, that is my voice. When the afternoon fog spills over the rim like something turning over in its sleep, that is my breath.

So if you ask where I would find myself in this world—I would be beneath everything, and around it. I would be the note the stone is still saying long after the hammer has stopped.

I would be the Bell.
