I am not certain, at first, that I am anything in particular. I am the pressure against the riveted walls, the cold that moves in from the discharge line, the taste of rust and brine. I am spread through the room like the green rumor from the shaft, too thin to have a name. Then the lamp flickers, and I feel myself gather behind the switchgear, where the cables still give off their faint heat. My jaws are moving. I did not tell them to. They are tasting the water for the first click of the intake valve, and I understand: I am the one who waits here.

I am old. One eye is clouded, but the other never blinks. The port I live in has grown into the shape of my head, rust and calcareous crust fitting me like a socket. I do not breathe; the water moves through my gills and the room moves through me. The motor hums in my bones. The flywheel turns with a patience older than my own, and I have learned to match it.

I was not born here. I came in through the discharge line when I was thin as a bootlace, following the taste of oil and crushed mussel. That was years ago. Now I am too thick to leave through the pipe I entered, but I do not want to. The sea outside is wide and uncertain. The room is small, warm, and reliable. It has its own weather, and I know it exactly.

Every six minutes, the intake valve clicks. I back deeper into the switchgear, letting the surge comb over me. The water rushes in through the intake grate, carrying sand and chum and small crustaceans, and the school of herring turns as one. The first thud comes, and I relax. The second thud comes, and I am already watching the intake for what the storm has brought: a crab, a stunned herring, a torn piece of fish from the world above. I do not need to leave my den. The water brings the room to me.

The room is not silent. It is loud with the noise of the machine and the animals that live with it. The hiss of water through the packing glands, the clanking of a loose valve, the snap of lobsters, the grunt of my own jaws. The lamp on the control panel burns amber, the only electric light, and it pools over the pump like a small sun. I watch it without watching, the way I watch the pressure gauges. Some are full of water, some are dry and dead, but their needles still point at numbers no one will read again. The pump is the room’s heart. The lamp is its one open eye. I am the part that looks back.

There are other minds here. The octopus lives inside the copper case of a pressure gauge, curled in the space behind the dial. It is the color of verdigris and old rubber, and its arms feel out between the glass and the rim, delicate, muscular, tasting the water. When the discharge line charges, the Bourdon tube flexes; when the pressure drops after the second thud, the gap in the case shifts by a fraction, and the octopus slips out to hunt. It moves along the wall, over the red mass of wrenches, past the fur of hydroids, and picks off shrimp too slow to scatter. When the lamp flickers, it turns white and folds itself back into the gauge, pulling its arms after it like a secret being put away.

Beneath the grating, the lobsters have made a society in the ledges of the concrete sump. Their antennae wave from the gaps like slow weeds. They do not fear me, and I do not trouble them. We have an arrangement. The anemones are thousands, gray-pink and patient, and when the intake opens they lean into the current and open their mouths as if the sea were feeding them. The whelks and sea stars and flatworms keep to the lower surfaces. The hydroids and bryozoa grow over every thread and flange, a fine fur that turns the machinery into landscape.

I am not the room. The room is the true occupant. It is a machine worn down until it almost seems grown, and the sea has settled into it the way water settles into a lung. The walls are iron, but they act like stone. The lamp is the sun. The pump’s cycle is the tide. The intake surge is the storm. Nothing here breaks the ordinary rules. The water is cold. The iron is old. The animals are ordinary. The lamp burns amber. And every six minutes, the room takes one deep, loud breath.

I am only the part of the room that looks back. I am the blunt head in the rusted cable port, jaws moving, waiting for the next thud. I taste the water for changes—a rise in copper, a change in the motor’s pitch, the first click of the intake valve. I have learned the room’s weather exactly. When the valve clicks, I back deeper. When the first thud comes, I relax. When the second thud comes, I am already watching the intake for what the storm has brought.

The lamp flickers. The water surges. The school of herring flares and tightens. The octopus slips back into its gauge. The lobsters wave their antennae. The pump strokes twice, two deep thuds that travel through the steel and water. Then the surge dies, and the room settles back into a slow churn of bubbles and rust.

I am still here. I will be here when the next cycle begins. I do not breathe, but the room breathes through me. I am the wolf eel behind the switchgear, one eye clouded, one eye clear, and I am not going anywhere.
