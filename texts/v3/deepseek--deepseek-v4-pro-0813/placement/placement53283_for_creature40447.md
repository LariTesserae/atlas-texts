You would find yourself not in the high terraces, not in the engine halls with their organ-pipe stacks, but in the water.

The canal would be warm, almost body-warm, and you would be standing knee-deep in it, then waist-deep, your broad feet finding the old ruts in the basalt. The water would be dark, filmed with mineral rain, and it would move slowly, like breath.

You would be a sluice-child. Not a warden yet. Your hands would be wrapped in waxed cloth, and in them you would carry a hooked iron bar, worn smooth by other hands before you. Your skin would be the color of wet concrete, stained copper-green where the railings have left their rust, and your hair would be shaved close so the grey-orange film cannot find a hold. You would be short and wide, built for the thin warm air, and you would move slowly because the stone is slick and the water hides the deep places.

Your name, if anyone asked, would be not a name but a task. They might call you Clearer, or Sluice-Singer, because you learned to whistle the pitch of the pipes before you learned to speak. Your earliest memory would not be of a room but of the canal’s edge: the warm water closing over your feet, the current pushing against your ankles, the sound of a gate grinding open.

You would be the one who crawls into the narrow channels when the mats grow too thick and the water backs up into the lower stacks. You would know the taste of the mineral film—copper, salt, something mushroom-like but not exactly—and you would know when it is ripe enough to eat and when it is old enough to clog a gate.

Today you would be below the boiler quarter, where the water runs hottest and the steam rises in sheets. You would be clearing the third intake, the one that always blocks when the grazers come down to drink. The water would be up to your chest. The walls would be slick with the grey-orange film, and it would come away in thick mats when you scraped it, leaving the stone dark and clean. A flock of featherless birds would be watching from a pipe above, their long bony snouts tilted down, and they would whistle in the same key as the steam. You would whistle back, not to charm them, but to let them know you are not a threat. They would stay.

The grazers would be there too, slow six-legged things, rust-colored and glossy, moving over the mineral film at the water’s edge. They would not startle. One would pause near your hand, its body warm and smelling of wet copper, and you would let it pass because there is no need to hurry. The water would rise a little as you opened the gate, and the canal would carry the scrapings away—grey-orange clumps dissolving into the current, becoming food for the eels, becoming the film again, elsewhere.

You would not think of yourself as important. The city has many hands: the mat-tenders who peel the walls, the canal-wardens who wade deeper than you, the boiler-tenders who feed the engines, the flock-callers who keep the birds from the turbines. You would be one of many. But you would know that if the sluice is not cleared, the water backs up, and the lower stacks flood, and the mats grow too thick, and the grazers cannot graze. So you would scrape, and you would listen. The city would be loud around you—engines humming, steam hissing, rails clanking, animals calling—but the water would have its own note, low and steady, and you would listen for the change that means a blockage is shifting.

You would have no god, only the Warmth Below and the Wet Above. You would feel the Warmth Below in the water around your legs, in the stone that never chills. You would feel the Wet Above in the fog that settles on your shoulders, in the drip from the pipe where the birds roost. You would not pray. You would just keep the channel clear, which is its own kind of thanks.

At the end of the day, you would climb out of the canal, your legs heavy, your wrapped hands dripping. You would sit on a low wall and eat a mat-cake, tasting copper and salt and the sour tang of fermentation. The fog would turn the grey-gold light into something almost solid. The grazers would move slowly over the steps, grazing the film that had grown during the day. The birds would settle on the railings. The eels would coil and uncoil in the dark water. You would hum a low note, the same note as the pipes, and you would not feel small. You would feel like a part of the city’s body—one of its many mouths, one of its many hands, one of its many clearances.

When you die, the family will lay you on a roof, or place you in a canal for the eels. The mineral film will colonize your skin. The grazers will come, then the birds, then the smaller scavengers. You will become part of the mat, part of the animals, part of the water. That is not sad. It is the same slow exchange. You are already part of it now, wading in the warm canal, scraping the slick stone, whistling back to the birds.

So where would you find yourself? You would find yourself in the water, below the boiler quarter, with an iron bar in your hand and the city’s low note in your chest. And who would you be? You would be the one who clears. That is enough.
