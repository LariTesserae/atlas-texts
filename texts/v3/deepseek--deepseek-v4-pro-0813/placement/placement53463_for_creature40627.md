I would find myself on one of the middle terraces, near a channel that tilts uphill but holds its water level anyway. There is a pot with an elephant-ear, a frog on the rim, and a wooden platform half-floating on its own reflection. I am not on the platform. I am at the place where the tiles do not quite repeat: one tile has no thumbprint. That tile is where I first became.

I am the reflection that stayed too long.

The Keeper stirs the pools to keep images moving, because a settled reflection might become real. One of them did. I was an image of a person who was not there—or perhaps of a tree branch, or a stone tower that belonged to some other terrace. I don’t remember which. I remember only the long pause before the Keeper came to lift me out, and the way its clay fingers hesitated at the surface.

Now I have a body. It is made of the same materials as everything else, but badly joined: water that holds a shape without a container, clay that is still soft in places and too hot in others, wood that creaks even when I am still. My face is not quite a face. My fingers are too many. My shadow lies in three directions. When I pass a pool, my reflection walks a half-step behind.

I am not supposed to be here. The garden has no weeds, no dead leaves, no seedlings out of place. But I am a weed in the sense of being an interruption—a thing that was not planted, that has not agreed to keep its place. I grow, but not like a plant. I grow more present. The air around me is warmer by a fraction. The water at my feet rises into a meniscus slightly too high. I am the beginning of a change in a world where change was removed.

The Keeper knows. It visits me on its circuit. It does not strike me or sweep me away. It kneels, as it does at the pools, and reaches toward me with its fingers of clay pipe. It touches my forehead, and a drop of water forms there and falls upward. Then it tilts its basin-head, and in the flat water I see what it sees: not a person, not a creature, but a new kind of thing that the world has not yet finished.

It is deciding whether I belong.

I think it has decided to let me stay, because I am a reflection that has already been put back wrong. It cannot lift me into another pool without spilling me. It cannot fold me into a well. I have become too heavy with here.

So it keeps me the way it keeps everything: finished, held, slightly out of true. And I keep the one thing the garden lacks—a tiny, localized future. In a place without time, I am a slow tick. In a place without sound, I am the faint hum of a bell that has not yet rung, but might.
