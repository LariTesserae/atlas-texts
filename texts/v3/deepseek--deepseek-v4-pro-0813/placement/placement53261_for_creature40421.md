I would find myself on the landing just below the high gantry, at the place where the stairs turn and a film of water runs beside me. Part of the stillness has a face. Mine.

At first I am not sure whether I am walking or being walked. The deck is smooth and cool through the perforations in my shoes. I stop at a threshold and touch a painted handprint. My fingers come away the same color as the mark. Then I know: I am the one who made it.

I am a path-marker. I do not live in the Upcast, the Sort, or the Ledger; I live in the seams between them. My room is behind a stair that descends half a kilometer and arrives at the same landing. It is narrow, but I keep it neat: a bedroll, a rack of pigment jars, a coil of wire, a few clean bolts. The walls are covered with handprints in five colors—rust, copper, moss pulp, ground enamel from the beetle-things. Some are mine. Some are older. I keep them all fresh.

My work is to find the stable paths and mark them. A stable path is not a straight line; it is a line the city has agreed to remember. I test it by walking it three times—once with my left eye closed, once with my right, once with both. It sounds like a riddle, but it works. My left eye sees the world as a map; my right eye sees it as a room. When the map and the room agree, I paint a red handprint. When they disagree, I paint a dotted line and leave a wire shrine at the corner with a clean bolt and a drop of oil. Then I walk away without looking back.

I know the rules. I do not trust a straight corridor longer than two hundred meters. After that, the corridor may not end where its perspective says it should. I have walked one that repeated itself exactly but for a missing rivet. I have opened a door and found a street the map says is on the far side of the plateau. I have descended a staircase for half a kilometer and arrived at the same landing I left. I have learned not to be frightened. It is not a flaw in the architecture. It is not exactly a flaw in the world. It is the city dreaming, and my job is to keep the dream from swallowing the walkers who cannot see both ways.

This morning I found a puddle that reflected the lanterns burning in a different order. I painted a dotted line around it, not to contain it, but to warn the walkers: the reflection has an opinion. In a certain room near the fourth gantry, the condensation drips upward. I painted a handprint on the ceiling to mark the path of rising water. Some people think it is a shrine. It is not. It is a handprint, and a reminder that up is only a direction the city sometimes forgets.

The air is dense with visibility, but not with sound. I feel the machines in the soles of my feet before I hear them. The pistons on the north stair take five minutes to complete a stroke; they make no more than a soft exhale of steam. A dropped bolt does not ring; it lands with a small private tick. Even the birds are quiet. They open their beaks but no song comes out, or perhaps the sound is taken before it reaches me. I read the flicker of their wings instead.

I pass through the tiers. The lamp-haunters nod from their high roosts, pale faces turned toward the huge even light. The ledger-folk in their small bright rooms compare three maps before they will trust a new corridor. The sort-folk near the patient conveyor belts have hands dark with graphite and oil; they do not stop their work, but they tap the rail as I pass: *I am here.* The sump-folk at the wet seams offer me moss-cake and a cup of pipe water. I accept. It would be an insult not to.

At the long dimming, when the lamps sink to their lowest and the mist lies thick on every rail, I walk the stable paths with a small lamp and a paint-pot. I repaint the handprints that have begun to fade. I check the wire shrines and replace the offerings: a little oil, a little water, a clean bolt. I listen to the city breathing—the slow pistons, the water in the gutters, the sub-audible hum of the lamp fields. I do not sing. If I sing, it is a sound too low for the air to carry, felt in the deck and returning as a pulse.

I am not a priest. I am not a hero. I am the one who knows which doors are lying and which stairs are true. I am the one who will pass again, if the city permits. I touch the last handprint of the evening and leave a small wire shrine at the corner where a shadow lags a quarter-second behind the person casting it. I do not look too closely. I do not need to. I am exactly where I am supposed to be.
