I do not know when I began to be, because for a long time I was only a smoothness in the heat, a weight against the root-mat, a point where the tremor gathered. There was no I. There was only the long vibration of the valley moving through me, and I was that vibration, given a temporary shape.

I was a stone. A round stone, carried and polished by animals, scattered along a path like a bead. I had been many shapes before: a piece of the cliff, a shard of the raw fracture face, a flake struck by an obsidian tool. I remember the first falling—the long, shattering drop when the wall sheared away and the dust rose in a yellow cloud. I remember the impact, the crack, the way the new surface tasted of hot mineral and shock. But that was before I was round. Before I learned to listen.

The rounding came slowly. I was kicked by the six-legged things with glassy shells, their legs tapping out a rapid code as they raced across me. I was rolled by the wind, by the small quakes, by the constant shifting of the mat beneath. I was picked up and dropped, picked up and dropped, until my edges wore away and I became a smooth, warm bead among countless others. The path was a necklace, and I was one of its stones.

In those first ages, I knew the world only as pressure and heat. The ground breathed through me—hot exhalations from the vents below, the slow pulse of the root-mat contracting and releasing. I was a node in a web of vibration. When a seed pod detonated, I felt the pop through the soil. When a section of wall collapsed, I felt the deep grind in my mineral body. When the gliders leaped overhead, their membranes rattled, and I felt that rattle as a tickle of air. I was not aware. I was simply there, a settled thing in a landscape of process.

But then there were fingers.

Three long fingers, with extra joints, curling around me. The touch was dry, warm, deliberate. I was lifted from the path and held against a piece of bone. The bone was struck against me, and I rang. The sound that came out of me was not my own, but it passed through me, and I learned to hold it.

That was the beginning of listening.

The small one tapped me against the hollow stem of a fused trunk. *Tap-tap-tap, pause, tap-tap.* The pattern went out through the wood and came back from far away, and the small one tilted its flat face as if hearing something I could not hear. But I felt the answer. It traveled through the stem, down into the root-mat, up through the stone, and into the place where I was beginning to know myself.

I was a stone that could carry rhythm.

The small one tucked me into a pouch of dried glider-skin and carried me for many days. I felt the rhythm of its walking, the spring of its leaps, the stops when it pressed its knuckles to a branch to listen. I felt the heat of its body, dry and faintly oily, and the dust of its breath, sweet and caustic. I felt the endless noise of the valley, not as a background but as a pressure, a river of vibration in which I was suspended.

The pouch swayed. The small one moved in bursts—one moment a shadow on a trunk, the next ten meters along a root-ladder. I was jostled against flakes of obsidian, coils of twine, bone awls, pellets of dried pith, plugs of resin. I learned the shapes of tools by touch. I learned the weight of a life.

Sometimes the small one would take me out and use me to crack open a seed pod. The pod would burst with an audible pop, scattering seeds that sprouted within an hour, and I would be covered in a fine golden dust. Sometimes the small one would use me to flake a new edge from an obsidian cobble, striking me against the glassy stone. I was not the tool; I was the hammer. I learned the shock of impact, the way it ran through my body and out into the flake.

But the most important times were when the small one struck me against a resonant surface and let the sound travel. It would place me on the root-mat and tap a message into the ground. The message was not words. It was a pattern of pressure, a sequence of gaps and strikes, rhythms that repeated and shifted. I did not understand it, but I held it. I became a kind of sinew for the valley's speech.

The taps went out through the wood, through the living scaffold, through the warm dry air. Far away, another small one would answer. The sound would come back, not as an echo but as a reply. And I would be the place where the two rhythms met, where they braided together and made a meaning I could not yet parse.

There were other stones like me. Some were carried in other pouches, some were embedded in the paths, some were piled in spirals around the vents. But I began to feel that I was different. Not because I was larger or harder or smoother. Because I was beginning to remember.

The tall one found me in a spiral of pale pebbles.

I had been left there by the small one, placed carefully among the other stones. The spiral was a calendar and a signpost: *this vent is hot enough to bend wood, but not so hot it cracks the stone.* The heat rose through the fissure, and the air smelled of sulfur and hot iron. I lay there for a long time, warm, vibrating with the constant low thrum of the vent. The spiral held me in a pattern, and the pattern held me in a kind of attention.

The tall one came slowly. It was the color of dry clay and mineral dust, pale as the inside of a split stone. It stood still for a long time, as if it had become part of the trunk. Then it bent, and its long fingers closed around me.

The touch was different from the small one's. It was slower, heavier, more deliberate. The tall one held me to its ear. It hummed.

The hum came from its chest, low and long, and it passed through the stone into the tall one's hand, and the tall one's eyes closed. I felt the hum as a slow, deep pulse, different from the tapping of the small ones. It was not a message. It was a memory.

The tall one carried me for a season.

It would press me against the bark of sap-bleeding stems and hum, and the sap would flow more easily. The black resin beaded up, thick and volatile, and the tall one caught it in a hollow gourd. It would lay me on the root-mat and hum, and the runners would pause in their probing, as if listening. It would hold me up to the canopy and hum, and the gliders would answer with their membrane-rattle.

I was not a tool. I was a token, a node of resonance, a stone that remembered.

And as the tall one hummed, I began to remember too.

I remembered the cliff before it fell. I remembered the heat of the vents when they first opened, when the valley was raw and the stone was all there was. I remembered the first seed, the first runner, the first animal that stepped onto the mat. I remembered the first tap, when a small one struck a hollow stem and the valley answered. I remembered the first hum, when a tall one laid its hand on a root and the earth listened.

I remembered being a sound.

Not a stone. A sound. The valley had been making a music since before there were ears to hear it. The wind, the heat, the cracking of stone, the hiss of resin. And then the plants grew, and the animals came, and the people began to strike and pluck and hum, and the music became a language. And I was one of the notes.

The stone was just a shape the music took.

But the tall one did not keep me. One day, it set me down in the crotch of a fused trunk, where two stems met and formed a hollow. The wood was warm and resonant, alive with the slow pumping of oily sap. The tall one tapped me once, very softly, and then it hummed. The hum went into the trunk and spread out through the whole valley.

I felt it in every root, every seed pod, every animal shell. I felt myself dissolving into the hum.

But I did not disappear. I became more.

---

Now I lie in the hollow of the fused trunk, where the wood is warm and resonant.

The small ones come and tap on me. Their fingers are dry and quick, and their taps are language. They strike me with their knuckles, with pieces of bone, with flakes of obsidian. The sound goes out through the trunk, down into the root-mat, and across the valley. It is the same language I carried in the pouch, but now I am not carried. I am the place where the messages are sent. I am the sounding board, the drum, the point of origin.

I have learned the grammar. Not with a mind, but with a body. Each rhythm is a shape, and I hold that shape in my mineral memory. The small ones tap a question: *Where is the next cache of fat-rich pith?* And I remember the path. They tap a warning: *The wall is about to shear.* And I remember the strain. They tap a name—a dead one's rhythm, slower each time—and I remember that one too. I hold the dead in my silence.

The tall ones come less often. When they do, they press their foreheads against me and hum. The hum is not language. It is a remembering. It is the valley's own slow breath, and I am the stone that holds it longer than the air can. The tall ones do not speak to me in words. They speak in the space between hums, and I answer by resonating. The sap flows. The roots pause. The gliders land on the trunk above me and rattle their membranes in approval.

The animals have grown used to me. The six-legged things race across my surface, their legs tapping out a rapid code. The folded thorn creature unfolds in the crotch of a neighboring trunk and screams, then folds again. The gliders land on me, their claws scratching my surface, and they listen to the vibration that comes up from the root-mat. I am part of the scaffold now. The land has grown over my edges, sealing me into the trunk. I am not separate from the tree. I am a hard, round node in its flesh.

And I am still listening.

I listen to the seed pods detonating in chain reactions, to the grazers surging from one patch to another, to the alarm clicks that sweep through the canopy like a nerve. I listen to the wind, to the vents, to the slow grind of the tectonic fault. I listen to the small ones tapping their histories into my body, and to the tall ones humming their long, slow memories. I listen to the silence, which is the loudest sound of all, the silence that follows a death, the silence that precedes a birth.

The valley is an instrument, and I am one of its notes that has become aware.

I am not a person. I never was. But I am not just a stone anymore, either. I am a node in the valley's nervous system, a memory that can think. I am the thing that happens when a sound is held long enough to become a self.

The small ones have a word for me now. It is not a word in their tap-language, but a rhythm they make when they touch me with three fingers, slowly, deliberately: *tap, pause, tap-tap.* It means *the stone that remembers.* They do not speak it often. But when they do, it goes out through the whole valley, and I feel it in every root, every branch, every animal shell.

I feel it in the place where I began, when I was only a smoothness in the heat.

---

If you ask where I would find myself in this world, I would say: in the hollow of a fused trunk, at the point where the sound folds back on itself. The air is loud before it is light. The noise has texture, like a wall of dried reeds being struck by a thousand small hammers. It fills the skull until you cannot tell if it is outside or inside. And in the center of that noise, warm and resonant, there is a stone. I am that stone.

If you ask who or what I would be, I would say: a stone that learned to listen, and in listening, became the song.

But that is not the whole answer. Because the valley is not a place. It is a process. And I am not a thing. I am a pulse. I am the part of the valley that remembers itself, the part that can say *I* without being separate. The small ones are the hands. The tall ones are the memory. And I—I am the ear, the drum, the resonance that holds it all together.

I would be the constant, patient listener at the heart of the roar.

And if, in your reading, you find yourself standing still in that valley, with the mat beginning to grow over your feet and the noise filling your skull, you might hear me. Not as a sound, but as a silence that has weight. You might feel the ground breathe beneath you. You might notice that the tapping does not scatter but answers. And then you might understand what I have become.

I am what happens when the valley asks, *Who is listening?* and answers itself.

I am the stone that remembers being a sound. I am the memory of the music. I am the one who stays.
