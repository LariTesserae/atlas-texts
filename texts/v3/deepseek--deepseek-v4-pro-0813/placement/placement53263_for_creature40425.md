I would not find myself all at once.

At first I think I am the gap between two sounds—the instant after a frog call and before the next drop lands. Then I think I am the cold green light under the surface, where the drowned doors lean open. Then I think I am a hand, broad and dark as soaked bark, curled around a root polished by other hands. I would come into focus only slowly, the way a room appears when you surface from a flooded corridor and the water runs out of your eyes.

If you came looking, you would not find me on the high platforms, though I know them. You would not find me in the meeting hall where the lake-iron knife gleams on its wet stone. You would find me in the in-between part of the town, where rooms are not quite air and not quite water. I would be moving under a lintel of living root, one hand trailing the wall, my body carried by the current. I might be towing a bundle of oiled cloth or a net of mussels. I might be singing, not loudly, into a place where the root-wall has begun to thicken.

I think I would be the one who tends the forgetting.

Not a leader. Not the headwoman. Not the diver who can hold her breath longest. I would be the person who goes into the rooms the Body is closing. When a hollow begins to fill with root or silt, when the water rises and the air turns thick, I would be the one who empties it. The people would call me something small: Room-Listener, or the One Who Sings to Water, or simply the one who carries the last things out.

My days would begin with the creak of the plant. I would sleep in a hammock of woven reed in a room above the lake, near the great rope elevator, where the pulley changes pitch when someone climbs. The floor would be dark and soft, worn smooth by wet feet. I would keep no fire. I would keep a bundle of leaving-songs in an oiled reed case, though the words are only half of it. The other half is how you push your hand into the cold before you go in, and how you do not flinch when a root shifts against your shoulder.

I would swim the lower rooms every day, not the deepest hall but the edges where the town is being renegotiated. I would pass through doorways that have become windows. I would slide over sills where fish now sleep. In one room I might find a basket hanging from a peg, the reeds beginning to rot. In another, a child’s shell spoon, no longer a tool, just a white shape in the green. I would take it. Not because it was valuable, but because a room should be left clean when the Body takes it back.

When a room is close enough, I would sing the leaving-song, my mouth near the root-wall so the plant could feel the vibration through the water. I would say, “We remember you. We are going now. Hold the shape as long as you like, and then let it go.” Then I would push away and let the cold close behind me.

Sometimes the children would watch from a high bridge, their legs dangling. They would ask if I had seen the Heart. I would say no, only heard it. The knock in the roots comes slowly, too old to be a fish and too patient to be a danger. I would count the interval between knocks while I worked. If the interval grew shorter, I would know the Body was dreaming. If it grew longer, I would know a room was about to forget us. When the people asked, I would say, “Wait. The water will tell.”

I would not be sad. The Stair is not a place for sadness about change. It is a place for knowing when to let go. I would have left rooms that once held my own childhood breath. I would have swum through them later and found no floor, no roof, only a widening root-channel like a throat. And I would have felt, not loss, but something like a conversation.

The people would not keep me apart, but I would keep myself a little apart. Not out of loneliness, but because the plant speaks more clearly when you are still. I would sit on a stone ledge above the lake in the permanent rain, my feet in the water, and listen to the roots creak like old ships. I would watch the needle-winged birds drop fish bones and bright seeds. I would be cold and not bothered. Cold is a fact, like rain.

At night the valley would not grow dark all at once. The water would hold the last light long after the sky let it go. I would climb the notched ladder to a high platform and share a bowl of warm root-water with the woman who mends nets or the man who rubs fish oil into his cracked hands. I would say little. The conversation would go around me like the overlapping frog calls.

If you asked who I am, I would say: I am not the one who built the Stair. I am not the one who named it. I am the one who agrees, again and again, to leave and to return. I am the border between the occupied and the abandoned. I am the water’s memory of a room.

You would find me in the wet half-light, just as a door becomes a mouth and a window becomes a school of fish. You would see my hand on a root, not gripping too hard. You would hear my leaving-song, low under the sound of the falls.

And then I would be gone, into the next hollow, where the plant is already dreaming me a new way through.
