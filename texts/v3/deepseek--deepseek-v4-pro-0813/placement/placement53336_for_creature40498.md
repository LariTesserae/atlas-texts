I would find myself on the Listener’s Chair, though I would not remember choosing to sit there. The cloud below would already have become a second sky, and the roar would already be seated in my jaw, too familiar to be a sound and too loud to be memory. At first I would think I was only a traveler. I would think the cold was altitude, the damp was condensation, the wrong shadow was a trick of the sourceless light. The Fold would let me think that for a while, the way it lets frost form and then not thicken.

Then my breath would begin to forget itself. It would hang in front of me, a fog that did not disperse. I would take fewer and fewer sips of the cave-tasting air, not because I was afraid, but because I no longer needed to finish the breath. My chest would settle. The roar would pass through me, and I would become less a person and more a doorway the sound could use.

I would look down at my hands. The skin would be gray now, flaking in fine plates like wet schist, with mica glints at the knuckles. My fingers would be too long, and the nails would be smooth as pebbles polished by a patience that has no length. I would touch my own forearm. It would be cold in the way the stones are cold, a steady damp cold that did not come from the air. The frost on my shoulders would sit there without thickening. The moisture on my skin would bead and not drip.

I would stand. The terraces would rise beneath me without effort, as if they recognized my stride. The path of lighter stones would loop back on itself, and I would follow it, not because I intended to, but because the Fold had already placed me at the next point. The roofless hut would appear from behind without my having turned. The cairn would be beside me and then far away and then beside me again. Once, I would see a tall figure near the ring of stones. It would lift one long gray hand. I would lift mine. The space between us would fold like cloth, and I would see that the figure was not another Dweller but the same one—myself, repeated the way the quartz vein branches like a nerve.

I would not remember my name. I would not remember arriving in the Fold, or whether I had ever left. The name would be a sound with no source, like the roar, and I would let it go. I would know only that my mouth did not need to open, and that if it did, no breath would come out—only the damp, old, cave-tasting air that already filled the Fold.

I would kneel by a black pool ringed with white salts. I would look for my reflection, but the water would hold only the low gray sky. My eyes would be the black pools themselves, or they would be holes opening through me into some deeper stillness. I would not blink. I would not need to.

At the last, if there were a last, I would sit in the roofless hut with my back to the doorway, facing the sound. The roar would not be outside me. It would be in the hollows of my long bones, in the spaces between my ribs, in the chambers of my skull. It would fill me and pass through me unchanged, and I would let it. I would not be listening. Listening is an act, and I would be beyond acts. I would be the Fold’s own long, slow, held breath.

Where would I find myself? In the Roaring Fold, at the place where the world was set down and forgotten, where the same boulder repeats every thousand paces and the light has no sun. And what would I be? I would be the Dweller—not the keeper, not the cause, not the prisoner—only the one who stayed until staying became a property of the valley, cold and damp and dim and almost empty, and entirely itself.
