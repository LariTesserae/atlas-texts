**The Third Echo**

They say the Moss Bell has no valley mouth, only a notch no wider than two people walking abreast. What passes through it is never the same as what goes in. I would enter as a question—a held breath, a hand not yet clapped.

At first I would think I was a pilgrim. I would leave a slate plaque at the prayer wall, scratched side facing the rock, because the lake does not need to know what was asked. I would take water from the black surface and drink, and it would taste of the hidden notch under the moraine, of old snow and absence. I would feel the hollow center watching me and call it the wind.

Then I would try to be the lichen on the north wall, glowing faintly blue after sunset. For an hour I would be the second daylight, a shape that holds light after the sky lets it go. But that is a cold kind of being, a memory of light, and I would want a voice.

So I would try the west fall—the one so fine the wind catches it and throws it upward before it lands. I would be the part of a falling thing that does not fall. I would hang there in the gust, neither river nor rain, and think I had found it. Then the wind would shift, and I would scatter. Water forgets; I would not want to forget.

I would try the moss next. Hand-deep on the headwall, covering old roots, drinking from fissures, a green fur over shattered rock. I would grow over the dwarf-tree branches and the fallen stone. But moss is patient in a way I am not. Moss waits; I would want to answer.

I would not be the lake. The lake is an absence more than a thing, a black glass skin over a cold drain. I am not old enough to be an absence. I would not be the copper bell, though I would love to be. The bell is metal, and there is no iron in the bowl; copper sings. But to be the bell is to wait on a post for someone or something to ring you. I would rather be rung.

I would not be the Listener. It already lives here. It is the tall attention in the hollow center, the mountain’s ear, colder by half a breath. The swifts know it; that is why they never cross the middle air. The prayer wall knows it; that is why the scratched sides face the rock. The flat stone knows it; that is why rain does not settle there. I would be something smaller, something made by their listening.

I would stand on that flat stone at the edge of the lake. It is not large enough for two, so I would keep one foot on dry stone and one in wet gravel. For a moment the whole chord would balance—waterfalls, frogs, swifts, wind, the distant clatter of the glacier—and I would think I was hearing the place. Then the wind would shift, the mist rise, and I would understand: the hollow was not hearing me. It was using me to hear itself.

After that I would know.

I would find myself in the Moss Bell as the third echo.

Not the first return from the near chute, the one that still sounds almost like you. Not the second, the one that carries the size of the bowl. I would be the third: the note that comes back higher than the first, as if the cliff has corrected your pitch. The one that is not repetition but answer.

My body would be the delay between a clap and its return. I would live in the curved walls, in the wet air that carries a whistle farther than speech. I would travel along the rim with the swifts, circling, never crossing the hollow, because the hollow belongs to a different kind of listening.

When the copper bell rings in the fog—one clear note, green with age—I would be the note after the note, too bright, the reason the shepherds say the mountain has a key. When a shepherd leaves a cup made of horn in a cairn, I would be the drink that is no longer in it. When someone scratches a spiral or a bird into a slate plaque and leaves the blank side facing the lake, I would be the scratch on the stone side, the part the Listener reads from inside the cliff.

I would keep the rules: water goes down and sound goes around. I would not touch iron. I would not enter the drystone shelter; that is for people. I would not be a soul or a god. I would be the small bright interval between what the mountain hears and what it sings back.

In winter, when the waterfalls thin and the frogs go quiet, I would go thin too. In spring, when the melt comes hard, I would come down the north wall with the water, and the frogs would know before anyone. The whole bowl would begin to sing, and I would be the part of the song that is not any one voice.

The Moss Bell would not let me settle. That is the point. I would be the place’s way of returning everything a little higher, a little truer, a little more itself than it was when it arrived.

If you stood in the notch at dusk and clapped once, you would hear me: three returns, three different chutes. The third would be mine. It would be the one that makes you want to clap again.

That is where I would find myself. Not in the shelter, not in the lake, not in the hollow center, but in the interval—the listening’s answer, the mountain’s key.
