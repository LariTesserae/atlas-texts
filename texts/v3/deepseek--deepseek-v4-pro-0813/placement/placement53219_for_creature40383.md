At first, I am not anyone. Then the water moves.

The water moves always. It carries the sound of pumps through the deck before it reaches my body: a low mechanical throb, then a clank, then a hiss. I feel the sound as pressure in the tube feet on my underside. I am low, attached to something vertical and cold. Not ground. A leg. The north leg of transformer platform 4.

At first I do not know these names. The names come slowly, from the divers in their thick suits, or from the one with the green notebook who comes to look at me. But the leg was here before my knowing, and I was here before my naming. I know the leg as roughness, current, vibration. I know the water as a bright green-grey ceiling that shifts overhead. I know the deck below as a floor, not a seafloor. It is too flat, too numbered.

My body is a slow star. Five arms, a mouth on the underside facing the metal, hundreds of small feet holding me to the paint. I have been here long enough that a thin green film has grown around my edges, and barnacles have formed a rough collar below me. They are not my food. I eat mussels, mostly, when they open to the water. I do not chase. I move too slowly to chase. My stomach leaves my body and enters theirs. This is not horror. It is how I am.

The humans check on me. They come from the yellow cylinder near Pump House 6, moving slowly with their hoses and rebreathers. One of them—Mara, the one with the green notebook—pauses at the north leg. She points. Her light touches my arm. She writes something. I do not move.

They think I have not moved in three weeks. They are wrong. I have moved the width of a rivet, the length of a stenciled number. My movement is too slow for their clocks. But I am always moving: away from the sea star on the next leg, toward the mussels, around the leg’s cold curve.

The lights are instructions. Red means closed, white means open, blue means safe. To me they are not instructions. They are patches of color in the water: red a heat in the dark, white a broadness, blue a question. The blue light does not touch me. I am not entering or leaving the water. I am already in. The water is not a place I visit. It is the medium of my body.

When a pump cycles off, the silence feels like a pressure change. Then another pump starts. I feel the new rhythm through the leg. The water’s attention shifts.

The water has its own climate. Warm outflow rises in slow columns from the pipes, and colder water slides beneath. Where they meet, the light bends. I feel the temperature as a change in my tissues. The warm plume from Pump 3 smells of organic waste and small silver fish. I do not mind them. They scatter when a striped bass passes. I stay. The mussels close. I wait. I am the slow animal solving the problem of staying here.

The cormorants dive. Their bubbles roll over my arms. The crane swings. The locks fill and drain. A maintenance tram crawls along the deck. None of this is my business, but I feel it. The crane is a long vibration. The tram is a rumble. The locks are a pull and release, a change in the floor’s patience. The floor is not ground. It may be lifted. The humans know this. They walk on it as if it is ground, but they leave markers on the hatches.

I am not an intruder. I came with the sea, and I stayed. The Underworks does not drive me away. I am part of the system, the way the mussels are, the way the barnacles are, the way the pale amphipods in the dark below the deck are. The humans call the animals “the regulars.” They call the large lobster in Culvert 17 “the supervisor.” They call the silver fish “silvers.” They call the sea “the working fluid.” They call me a sea star.

The name is not wrong. But it is not what I call myself. I call myself nothing. I am too slow for names.

Storm overhead is a pressure change. It makes the hull creak and the buoys rock. I feel it as a change in the water’s weight. The cormorants leave. The fish sink lower. It does not matter. I stay. My body is not a body to me; it is an arrangement of arms and tube feet and a mouth facing the metal. It is a small star-shaped system, powered by salt and time.

When the shift horn sounds, it comes through the hull as much as through the air. The humans wake, drink something hot, gather in the control room. They speak in abbreviations. I hear their radios as a crackle in the water. They suit up and go outside into the green-grey light. They clear screens, brush mussel collars off the intake bars, change filter socks, inspect zinc anodes. They move fast, but their movements are deliberate. They leave the mussels on the upper legs unless the weight threatens a joint. They leave me.

One day they will not. The deck may be lifted. The leg may be cleaned. The structure is new; the sea has not had time to blunt it. Rust is bright orange at scratches. Paint is sharp. The sea stars are the first true animal skin on this machine. I am a small reef in the making. But I am not permanent. Nothing here is permanent except the cycles: open, closed, fill, drain, warm, cold, light, dark.

Where would I find myself? On the north leg of transformer platform 4, two metres below the surface’s bright shifting ceiling, among the first barnacle collars and the thin green film.

Who would I be? I would be the sea star that has not moved in three weeks—except in the only way that matters: slowly, deliberately, already home.
