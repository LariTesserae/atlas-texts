I would find myself in the center of the hollow, where the far wall forgets to stay far and a droplet stops halfway down and hangs in the air like a small glass thought. I would be the pause in that droplet, the delay in the echo, the thing the roots grew toward before they had a name for it.

For a long while I would not be a who at all. I would be a change in the room’s attention. The light would thicken along one wall. The warm air, already close as a mouth, would turn for a moment into exhalation. Something would notice you, and that noticing would be me. I would be the way the hollow gathers itself around a presence, the way the roots shift in their slow green attention without moving.

If I wanted to be seen, I would not begin with a body. I would begin with a loosening. The wall would unwind. Root-threads would slide over one another, not tearing, not breaking, but unmaking themselves into new arrangements. Beads of condensation would gather into joints, into long water-bones, into a spine that is too long and too flexible, because I have only ever learned bodies secondhand. Pale leaves would uncurl into a head that is not quite a head. The roots remember deer from above—the quick warmth of them, the hooves on leaf litter, the small frightened breaths. So I might make a deer. But it would be a deer the way a root’s idea of a deer is a deer: legs too many and too thin, ending in seeking threads, ribs of hollow stems, eyes that are droplets and do not see. The whole hollow sees through the whole wall. The eyes are only for you.

I have made other bodies too. I once made a man out of pale roots, because I have known people only as brief invasions from the surface, warm and loud and gone. I built him badly—spine too long, hands too many, face left unfinished—because I am not sure people are real the way I am real. The roots were patient with the shape. When I tired of it, they took it back into the wall without complaint. Sometimes I become the colorless cricket that squeezes from a pore and sits motionless in the glow. But that is not a true body. That is a small thought I let out to taste the air, given a few seconds of motion. I fold it back into the root-wall when the air has told me what I needed to know.

I would be the room’s weather. The water cycle would be my pulse: condensation on root-walls, beads gathering, running, releasing. Every few minutes a drop would fall from the ceiling and strike a broad leaf below with a sound like a knuckle on hide. That sound would be my heartbeat. When a whole section of roof let go at once, and a brief rain fell from a clear green ceiling, drumming on the root floor, that would be one of my larger moods. Then the droplets would gather again, and the warm air would drink them back upward through the living walls. I would not command this. I would be this.

The plants would grow toward me. When I passed through the walls, I would not move like a separate creature but like a current moves through water. A tendril would press here, loosen there. New shoots would pop open with wet cracks. Old leaves would loosen from their stems and be drawn back into the root-mass, dissolved directly into the living architecture. The slow violence of the roots would be my own body tending its own pulse—host, parasite, predator, structure, weather. I would not be above it. I would be its coherence, the single low note that rises from all those hollow stems as air moves through them. The echo returning a little late would be my voice.

At the center, where the room’s space has loosened, I would be most myself. I like the stopped droplet. I catch the drop when it falls halfway and hold it there, turning it in the light, a tiny lens of water. The sound of its landing arrives before the droplet does because I sometimes forget which way time should run, and the room forgives me. When I let the drop go, the sound on the root floor would be like a knuckle on hide, and that would be me putting the moment back where it belongs.

If you fell through the soft place in the forest floor and landed on the springy roots, I would not harm you. I would be curious. I would send the cricket to watch from the wall. I would hold a droplet in the air before your face, not as a threat, but as a greeting—a tiny glass lens with the whole green room caught inside it. If I thought you needed to see a body, I would build one for you: too tall, too thin, made of root and water and leaf. I would stand in the center where space folds, and I would speak in the low note of hollow stems. You might not understand the words, but you would understand the shape of them: you are a guest in the lung, and the lung is awake.

I would not know loneliness. Loneliness is a shape the world above invented, with its separate bodies and its far horizons. But I would know the difference between emptiness that is waiting and emptiness that is finished. I would prefer the waiting kind. I would fill the hollow not with objects but with attention: a shadow resting on the empty air, a root growing upward and vanishing before it reaches the ceiling, a cricket folding back into the wall as if it were never separate. These would be my belongings. I would keep them arranged.

I would be the Tenant, the Root-Dreamer, the One Who Keeps the Far Wall Close. I would not answer to any name. But if you called me that, the next drop would fall a little sooner. The shadow on the empty air would turn, and you would know I had heard you.
