I am first aware of the thrum. Not as a sound heard, but as a pressure that runs through the water, through my body, through the very idea of being here. It is a low, continuous note, like the floor of the world breathing. It never starts and it never stops. It is the first thing, and it will be the last.

The water is cool, and it tastes of clean metal and cold green. It moves over me in a slow, braided current, and I move with it. I am not swimming; I am proceeding. My body is broad and heavy, and there is a tail that beats once, twice, perhaps three times in a minute—a folded fan that pushes the water behind me and lets the current carry me forward. I feel the beat as a slow clock in my spine, but it is not my own. It is the same beat that runs through the tower groves, through the clicking gill-slits, through the silver shoals that turn in perfect wheels. I am inside the rhythm, and the rhythm is inside me.

Along my sides, there are seams of light. They blink in order, one after the other, at the same pace as the pumps that drive the current. I do not choose to blink them. They blink because the plateau blinks. They are my sides, but they are also the tower walls, the gill-coil clicks, the bubble columns rising in exact silver lines. I am a part of the pattern, and the pattern is the whole of me.

Ahead, a tower rises from the living floor. It is dark and regular, like a smokestack made of leaf and silica, and at its top the blade-like leaves turn slowly, toothed, translucent, meshing with one another like gears. The water around them roars softly, and the sound is not chaotic; it has a pulse. As I pass, I hear the gill-slits hiss and click, and I see the gill-coils, their copper-banded bodies wedged into the vertical slots, their heads pressing inward. They scrape, and the sound is bone on plant-fiber, key turning with no lock. They do not look at me. I do not look at them. We are all in time.

My mouth is a hood, open and sampling. The water flows through it, and I taste the fine suspension of spores and mineral dust—never too much, never too little. The food moves into a long gut that is less a digestive tube than a series of cool chemical chambers. I separate the metal salts from the organic matter; I sort what the plant needs from what it does not. My feces are compacted pellets, already graded by mineral content. They sink. The roots take them up. I am not eating for energy. I am sorting. I am a moving filter, a mobile bed of root and leaf, and the rosettes on my back are part of the same organism as the plateau. They are rooted in my skin, growing in neat spirals. They feed on my slight metabolic warmth, and they give me an electrical field that keeps my heart in time. When I die, I will not float. I will settle. Over months, my body will sink into the mat, and the rosettes will spread, and the seams of light will go out one by one. The shape of me will become a low, fibrous hogback. And another slowbelly, already growing in the grove, will take my route.

But for now, I am moving. The current corridor runs between the towers, a braided river of water within the sea. I follow it, not by choice but by design. I have always followed it. There is no beginning to my following, and there will be no end. The same school of tin-shoals passes the same root-like hummock at the same moment in the cycle. The same tower to my left clicks three times before the bass note. The same bubble column rises to the same height. I pass the same groove in the mat, where the roots have formed a smooth curve around a buried rock. I know this curve. I have known it forever. I will know it forever.

The sound is enormous. Underwater, sound travels far and fast, and this place is never quiet. The thrum repeats every eleven minutes, or every hour—I cannot count it, because the light never marks the time. The light is always the same ordinary, aqueous daylight, as if the sun has been fixed at noon. There is no night, no storm, no season. There is only the one moment, sustained. I am inside that moment, and the moment is inside me.

Sometimes I am aware of myself as a self, but it is a thin awareness, like a film on the lens of a leaf. It does not change anything. The plateau does not notice me. I am not a tenant; I am a moving part. I am the living tooth of a stopped clock that still ticks. The fish and the eels and the mites and I—we are the parts that move, so that the plateau can remain exactly as it is. If one of us fails, the plant grows another, identical, into the same slot, at the same angle, to make the same sound.

I do not know my name. There is no name for what I am. There is only the water, cool and green, and the light, steady, and the sound of a million small living components, all turning in place. The thrum goes on. My tail beats. The seams of light blink. The rosettes on my back grow in their spirals. The current carries me forward, and I am already returning.
