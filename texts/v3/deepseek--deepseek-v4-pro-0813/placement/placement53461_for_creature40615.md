At first I am not sure I am a person. The noise is so complete, and my body so much a part of it, that I might be the stone, or the steam, or one of the pale shrimp turning in the blue-green pool. Then my hand finds the knife—shell blade, sinew-wrapped handle, a groove worn exactly to my thumb—and I am a keeper of the lower terraces, a tender of the ancestor wall, in a room whose floor never stops humming.

The room is cut into soft tufa on the third rim below the bat caves. One wall is always wet, and every morning a new crust of calcite has grown over the cracks, the lamp shelf, the toe of the door. I scrape it back before anything else. The scrape is not separate from the noise; it joins the thousand scrapes of the settlement, the hiss of hot water, the high wire of frogs, the clatter of the ash-gray birds. I feel the vibration through the soles of my feet before I hear it. Perhaps I feel everything before I hear it. In this place, the body is an ear.

The ancestors are set into the wall: skulls, long bones, the small bones of hands and feet arranged in patterns old enough to have become a language of their own. They look out from the tufa with dark eye sockets. They are not buried. They are kept. My work is to keep the stone from taking them back. Every morning I scrape the fine white crust from each skull, each knuckle, each rib. The knife fits into the same grooves it has worn over years. The sound of blade on bone is a click, a tick, a small clean note in the roar. I know each ancestor by the note their bones make. This one, my mother’s mother, gives a clear ring because the bone is dense and old. This one, a child who died before I was born, gives a dull tap, as if the bone still holds water. I speak to them in signs as I work—little flicks of my fingers near my chest: *I am here. I am still your hands. The wall is holding.*

I am of the Blind Fish clan, patient and pale. My house is near a pool that glows faint blue-green at night, not from any lamp but from the living film in the water. The pool is warm, milky at its source, and full of small transparent shrimp. I tend its rim. If I stopped scraping the mineral crust, the pool would grow a new lip and spill, or seal itself, and the shrimp would die. So I tend it. I run my hands along the edge every day, feeling for the places where the stone is mounting too fast. The water leaves stone; stone must be scraped away or used. That is the first rule. The second rule is older: the dead are the true owners of the house, and the living are their hands.

This morning, the noise is different. I notice it first as a body-sensation, a slight cooling in the vibration behind my ribs. The frogs are still piping, the birds are still shrieking, the water still hisses. But something underneath has changed. I set down the knife and go to the door. The steam is thick, the light flat and gray. I make the sign for *listen* to no one—to the air—and I wait. There it is: a small absence, a thread pulled from the weave. One of the deeper channels has narrowed, or a stone has shifted, and the water is not moving the way it should. It is not silence. There is no silence here. It is a wrongness in the texture, like a missing tooth in a comb.

I take my scraping tools and a lamp, though I do not need the light yet. The path down to the lower pools is a set of notched steps, slick with mineral and white with snail shells. I pass Old Sill, who carries a basket of blind fish. We exchange a brief sign—*something is off*—and a nod, our dark eyes wide in the steam. The settlement is not a town of words; it is a town of small precise gestures and low voices pressed to the ear. At the edge of the lower channel, I kneel and put my palm flat on the stone. The water is warm, almost too warm. I feel the blockage before I see it: a slab of fresh tufa has fallen from the wall, and the water has begun to pool behind it, slow and thickening. The frogs have quieted just there, and I see a line of white shrimp stranded in a shrinking puddle. I do not speak. I set the lamp down and begin to work.

The tufa is still soft enough to cut. I chisel at the edge, and the stone gives like damp clay, then hardens in my hands as the air touches it. I scrape the channel clear, re-cut the lip, and watch the water find its path again. The sound comes back as a small rush, a low note that slips into the roar and disappears. The shrimp scatter. The frogs resume their piping. I feel the vibration settle behind my ribs. This is what I am: one of the many small living surfaces of the body, a hand that keeps the water moving.

By the time I return to the house, the light has begun to change, though the sky is still hidden. The blue-green glow of the pool is just visible under the steam. I scrape the ancestors again before sleep, not because I need to, but because the ritual is the same: the dead are kept, the stone is held back, the house remains. I run my thumb along the bone of my mother’s mother and think of the word for silence. We have no word for it. The closest is *the moment before the pool overflows*, a thing that never quite happens. I have waited all my life for that moment, but it has never come. The noise is too full, the water too diligent, the body too alive.

I am not the noise. I am not the stone. I am not the blind fish or the frog or the ash-gray bird. I am the one who listens for the missing thread, the one who scrapes the ancestors, the one who tends the pool. In the sign of my people, my name is made with three fingers curled against the palm and then opened toward the ear: *the one who hears the change before it is a change.* But sometimes, in the warm dark, with the floor humming and the bones of my kin looking out from the wall, I think I am simply the moment the body knows itself.
