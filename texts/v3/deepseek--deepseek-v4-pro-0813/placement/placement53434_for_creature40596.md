I do not begin as a person. I begin as a texture, a temperature, a pressure in the ears. The air is cool and close, and it carries the taste of wet limestone—not the flat dryness of chalk, but something alive, mineral and faintly green, the way leaves taste when you crush them with your fingers. There is water moving somewhere to my left, not rushing but sliding in a thin, patient film down a carved channel. The sound it makes is not a trickle. It is nearly a breath, a whisper so continuous it becomes silence.

I am standing on a ledge, I think. My feet are bare. The stone under them is not smooth; it has a tooth, a fine grain like the skin of a shark, and it holds the cold as if the cold belonged to it. I flex my toes and feel the rock flour shift slightly, a dust so fine it might be the stone’s own exhalation. Above me, the sky is a pale, narrow blade of light; the sun has moved past the rim, or has not yet reached it. In this light, my skin looks wrong: unfired clay, too warm, too brown. Not yet the color of the stone.

I am not born here. Or perhaps I have always been here. Both are true in the way dreams are true. What I know is that I am a stranger to myself, and that the Cut knows me better than I do. It knows the weight of my body. It knows how much sound I make (too much—already too much, a warning gathered in the way my breath leaves my mouth too loudly, a hiss of air that would carry in this quiet). The walls are high and many-leveled, and each one is drilled with small dark windows, seams of blue shadow, the glint of water, the pale crust of lichen. Somewhere below, a mallet taps—not against stone but against wood, the sound muffled, a heartbeat through felt—and then stops. Everything stops. Then the silence comes back, thicker than before.

I put my hand flat against the wall beside me. The stone is not warm. It will never be warm. It is the temperature of water underground, of cellars, of the space beneath a mountain. But after a long moment, it seems to press back, the way a sleeping dog pressed by a foot will not move but leans, just slightly, into the weight. That is my first language. The rock is listening. I am listening back.

I do not know my name. I do not have a name. In the Cut, you earn the sound of yourself, and it is often not spoken aloud. It is tapped on a threshold when you come home: a rhythm that says, not *I am here*, but *the one who listens to the stone is here*. I do not know how to make that rhythm yet. My knuckles are too soft. I would raise my hand to the stone and strike it, and the sound would be wrong—a knock, a question asked too loudly. The stone would not answer.

But I am not frightened. The fear I expected is absent. Instead there is a strange, low certainty, like finding a room in a house you have lived in all your life and closing the door and thinking: *So this is where the other life was kept.* The Cut is a wound that has become a home. I am the animal that lives in the wound, not to heal it, not to widen it, but to be present in it. To keep the edges clean. To know where the next crack will form.

I think I am a child, but not the kind of child the description mentions—the one who presses an ear to the floor in the lowest quarry and waits for the tick before the split. I am older than that, or younger in a different way. I am not destined to be a Listener; I know this because the Listeners hear the stone the way other people hear wind, always, and I only feel it as a leaning, a slow language of pressure. I am not a Channel-keeper; water is too quick for me, too eager to leave, and I cannot follow its many small minds. I am perhaps a person between roles. The Cut is full of such people: those who do not choose what they are but become it through some old distribution of temperament and bone. I am drawn to the thresholds—the places where a room opens onto a lane, where a rope bridge sags over a lower gallery, where a window looks not out but inward. I am the one who notices what is missing. I am the one who keeps the edges.

But to say what I do, I must first learn to move. The ledge is narrow; beyond it, a staircase of raw ledges drops away. The steps are not steps in any ordinary sense. They are the seams the city has accepted, the places where the stone gave way and was then shaped. Some are broad enough for two people to stand together. Some are a hand’s width, no more, and the stone is dusted with rock flour so fine it acts like oil. I move the way the people of the Cut move—or I try. I place the outside edge of my foot first, test the stone, then commit my weight. I go slowly. The air is so still that I can hear my own joints, the small creak of an ankle, the rustle of woven cloth against my leg. I am wearing clothes I do not remember putting on: a tunic of rough grass-fiber, a hide vest tied at the side, a strip of cloth around my waist. The hide is old and supple, and it smells of woodsmoke and something sharper, like rain on dry dust.

The ledge narrows. To my right, the wall rises; to my left, the city opens like the inside of a lung. I can see other ledges, other terraces, stacked and nested, each one packed with dwellings. The walls are of stacked limestone, some whitewashed, some raw. Roofs are mossed slate, reed mats, a stretched hide dark with water. Lanes twist between them, no wider than shoulders. I see a woman come out of a low doorway. She carries a clay jar, and she moves the way I am moving: heel-toe, heel-toe, her body tilted slightly forward, listening with her feet. She does not look at me. I understand that not looking is a kind of courtesy. The Cut is close enough already; the eyes must be allowed to rest.

I pass under an archway, and the temperature drops. The air here does not simply cool; it becomes a pressure against the skin, damp and heavy. The sound of water is closer now, but also more diffuse—dripping from many places, sliding down the walls, no longer a single channel but a life-wide seep. I realize I am inside the inhabited ground, not above it. The sky is gone. Above me is stone, and above that, more stone, and above that, the weight of the city. The darkness is not complete. There are seams of light, thin and blue, where shafts have been cut through to the surface. They fall through the dark like threads, and I can see dust turning in them, so slowly it seems the dust is thinking. The air is full of tiny points of mica, glittering faintly.

I have no lamp. I do not need one. I am being drawn downward, not by desire but by a kind of quiet recognition. The lower you go, the newer the stone. I am not new stone. I am something else: recently exposed, perhaps, but not new. I am a crack waiting to be found.

The channel appears beside me again, a groove carved into the floor, and I follow it. The water is not clear; it carries a faint milkiness, rock flour suspended in it, and it leaves a pale line on the channel’s edge. Everywhere I feel the stone speaking in its low way. I do not hear words. I hear gradients: a deep thrum of weight, a thin high note of tension near a crack, a soft hush of relief where the rock has already settled. I wonder if this is how a child first hears the tap-language: not as meaning but as rhythm, not as words but as the world’s own pulse.

Then I hear something real. A tap. Three quick strokes, then a rest. *Stop, stop, stop.*

I freeze. My body knows the sound before my mind does. My foot is half-lifted, the outside edge already touching the stone. I do not put it down. I do not breathe. The taps came from the wall to my right, but not from the wall itself—through the stone, from somewhere ahead and below. I wait. The silence folds back. A drop of water falls from the ceiling and strikes the back of my hand. It is cold, and I feel it as a tiny rebuke.

Then a tap, single, slower. A question? No. A summons. *Come.*

I lower my foot and step forward. The channel turns with me. The passage narrows until I must turn sideways, my back against one wall and my hands against the other, the stone grazing my shoulders. The rock is not rough; it is raw, and my fingers find the half-erased lines of chisel marks. This is a side-quarry, one of the newer branches. The air is thick with rock flour, and I can taste the copper in the dust. I realize I have been here before. Not in this life, perhaps, but in the life of the stone. The walls remember being cut. They remember the hands that scored them, the wedges driven in, the long wait for the split. The memory is not in my mind but in my palms, as if the stone has pressed something into me.

The passage opens, finally, into a low gallery. A rope bridge spans the space before me, crossing a gap that drops away into darkness. I can feel the drop more than see it: the air changes, becomes emptier, and a faint updraft carries the smell of wet rock and the sound of nothing. The bridge is simple—two ropes for the feet, two for the hands, with slats of wood lashed across. It sways slightly, not from any wind but from the weight of the person on the far side.

She stands at the edge of the bridge, one hand on the rope, not moving. A woman of the lower terraces, pale as blue shadow. Her hair is braided close, and her tunic is old, patched with pieces so thin they seem made of dust. She is watching me. I know she is one of the Channel-keepers, because her fingers are long and stained dark, and because the water in the channel beside her has stopped moving, as if it too is waiting for her to speak.

She does not speak aloud. She taps the stone under her foot with the heel of her hand. One tap. Then another, longer, with the flat of her palm. The stone carries the sound to me through my bare feet. It says: *You are the one who came too far down.*

I do not know how to answer. My hands are too soft, I told myself. But something moves in me. I kneel—slowly, because the stone is listening—and press my palm flat against the floor. The rock is so cold it burns, but I do not pull away. I strike it once, lightly, with the side of my hand. Then again. The sound is small, a dry click. But the stone carries it. It always carries it.

The Channel-keeper tilts her head. She says, in spoken words so low they seem to come from the wall itself, “You have been walking a long time.”

“I am looking for the seep,” I say, though I did not know I was going to say that. The words come out in a whisper, shaped by the damp air. “The one that has no channel yet.”

She looks at me for a long moment. Then she steps onto the bridge, not toward me but past me, along a narrow ledge I had not noticed. She moves like water, no sound, no shift of weight. I follow her. I do not need to be told. This is the thing I have become: someone who follows the water into the unopened places.

The passage slopes downward, and the darkness becomes total, but her feet know the way. I can hear the change in the air, the rising mineral tang, the sound of water moving not on stone but through it. We come to a place where the wall has cracked—a fresh crack, still sharp, with a faint dusting of white around the edges. A thin thread of water moves along the crack, not flowing but creeping, then gathers at the bottom and drips into a shallow natural bowl. The sound is too quiet to hear. It is more a movement in the stone’s moisture, a slow thick weeping.

She crouches, cups her hand, and raises it to her mouth. She tastes. Then she turns to me, and in the absolute dark, I feel her eyes like two small stones. “This water is from the high rain,” she says. “It has not yet learned the stone’s taste. You will teach it.”

I do not understand. But I sit down beside the seep. The stone is wet and cold, and my body sinks into that cold as if it has been waiting for it. I put my finger into the water. It is so thin, so slow, a thread of life climbing through the stone from somewhere high and bright. I think of the upper terraces, their whitewashed walls, their pots of moss and fern. I think of the rain that comes in autumn and spring, when the channels run full and the air smells of wet dust. I think of the swifts, who come back each year. And I know that I am not going back up. Not yet. I am the one who stays with the new water until it finds its channel. I am the one who names the seep, not with a word but with a rhythm, tapped into the wet stone: *Here. Here. Here.*

The Channel-keeper taps the same rhythm, once, on my shoulder—not with her knuckles but with her palm, soft and flat. Then she leaves me there. Not because she is cruel, but because this is how the Cut teaches. You are left with the stone until the stone tells you what you are.

I do not know how long I sit there. The air is cold, but after a while I stop shivering. The water drips. The stone shifts, almost imperceptibly, and I feel the sound of it through my legs, a deep creak that might be the whole Cut breathing. I think of the people above, each one moving slowly through the lanes, each one speaking in taps, each one tending a small fire or a cistern or a child. I think of the cutters at the lowest level, their copper chisels scoring a line, their wooden wedges waiting in the dust. I think of the Listeners, their ears pressed to the rock, hearing the first faint tick before a crack opens. I am not among them. I am below them, or inside them, a place where the map has no name.

But I am not alone. In the dark, I begin to see shapes. They are not phantoms; they are the outlines of the stone, the texture of the walls, the faint sheen of water. My eyes have adjusted, or perhaps my skin has. I see the crack where the water enters, a fine dark line running upward, branching like a tree. I see the pale stains where water has found other paths and then dried. I see the shallow bowl below me, not more than a cupped hand, and I see that the stone around it is beginning to weather, just barely, at the rim. This is the city’s true direction: down and inward, into the smallest openings, into the places where water and stone make their slow agreement.

I put my hand on the wall and tap, very softly, two knuckles and a rest. A question. The stone does not answer. But the water drips, and the drop falls into the bowl, and the bowl holds it. And I think: this is enough. To be the bowl. To be the channel not yet cut. To be the place where the new water learns patience.

When I finally stand, I am not the same as when I came down. I am colder, slower, more careful. I have forgotten my own name, if I ever had one. But I know the rhythm of this seep. I know its taste. I know that somewhere above, a child is sleeping with its ear to the cradle floor, listening to the same deep pulse I am listening to now. The Cut is not a ruin, not a wound, not a city. It is a long conversation between water and stone, and I am a word that has just been spoken.

I climb back up, not because I am finished, but because the water has more to teach me. The stone feels different under my feet—no longer strange, no longer proof of my otherness. I move the way the city moves: slowly, testing, committed. I do not know what I will become. The Listeners will not call me. The Channel-keepers may not call me again. But I am not worried. I am the one who listened to the seep and heard the stone breathe. That is enough. That is the beginning.

Somewhere above, a swift cuts across the open sky above the Cut, a dark line against the pale gray light. It does not sing. It does not need to. It simply is, and the Cut holds it for a moment against the sky. I am like that, I think. Held. Not trapped. Held.

I will find my niche near the water that comes from the high rain. I will teach it the stone’s taste. I will not be a Listener or a Channel-keeper or a cutter. I will be the one who sits in the dark and listens to the drip, and when someone asks, I will tap, low and true: *Here. Here. Here.*

The stone is listening. I am listening back.
