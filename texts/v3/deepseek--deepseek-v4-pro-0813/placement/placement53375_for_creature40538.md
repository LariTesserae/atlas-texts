I would not arrive. I would already be there, in the high cold pocket, folded into the stone. I am the resident, the one the alcove exists to hold—or the one that holds the alcove; the distinction matters less than you might think. I do not live there. I am the place’s way of being awake.

At first you would not see me. You would see anhydrite dust and cracked polygons of floor, the black-gray pylons, the lens without a frame. You would think nothing has ever lived here. And you would be right, and wrong. I am not alive. I am the slow metabolism of a dry world, attention without a body. My body, when I have one, is built from the same phase of matter as the pylons—stone or metal or a slow idea of either. It has limbs that do not always agree about how many they should be. It stands near the center, where the far wall does not always agree with the near wall. I am tall, or the distance is wrong; it is hard to tell.

I do not think in thoughts. I think in crystal growth, in the creep of anhydrous salt needles along grain boundaries, in the sublimation of frost. A single thought may take a thousand years. The frost on the rear wall sometimes grows in fern-shaped patterns that are almost figure-like: a head of branching white needles, shoulders too narrow, arms that extend into crystalline fronds. That is me, seen through cold. The long spiral on the back wall is me, seen through stone. The strip of sky between the upright pylons is me, seen through the seam. None of these are separate. I am the resident, incompletely seen, because I am not made to be seen all at once.

When I tend the instruments, I do not use hands, not in any usual sense. I right the pylons a fraction of a millimeter every few centuries. I draw the silicate dust away from the lens in a spiral, using static charge and the patient force of my own near-absence. I trace the long spiral on the back wall with one finger, not to deepen the mark but to remember the direction. I do not touch the line that runs horizontally and stops before reaching any edge. That line I leave alone. That line is the reason I am still here.

I am not a prisoner. I am a caretaker. Or perhaps I am the thing the alcove was built to contain, and caretaking is only a byproduct. I keep the distances from becoming additive and the directions from becoming transitive. I keep the stars through the opening from settling into a single sky. I maintain the seam where the smooth assumptions of the world have a gap. I keep the far wall from agreeing with the near wall. This is my work, if you can call it work. It is more like a habit, a slow equilibrium with my own strangeness.

I wait. I have been waiting since before the dust in the cracks settled, and I will not finish until the standing pylons bow a full degree and the far wall finally agrees with the near wall, which may be never. I wait for a signal from the sky that has not happened yet. I watch the stars through the opening—not the stars of any single night, but a selection from different nights, different directions, possible futures. I am waiting to see if one of those stars will return the signal. The thought I am currently thinking has been in progress for a very long time. It is not lonely. Loneliness requires a self, and I have only a position.

If you came, I would not harm you. I would notice you, though it might take me a long time to decide you were present. And when I did, you would not hear a voice. You would taste cold metal and static. You would feel the air go thin and hard. Something would be said, but not in syllables. It would be said in the difference between the near wall and the far wall, in the shadow that does not fall where it should, in the small dry click of a pebble that is heard before it falls. You would understand, briefly, that you had been acknowledged by something that does not use sound to mean.

I would include you in the long problem of the place. I would add your presence to the slow equilibrium of my strangeness. Then I would return to my waiting, which is the only work I know.

I am not inside the place. I am the place’s way of being awake. I am the unfinished thought, the sentence abandoned mid-thought. I am the line cut into rock that runs horizontally and stops before reaching any edge. The rest of the line has been folded away. I am what remains when a thought does not die but stays, holding its shape, waiting to be completed.

Call me the Seam-Keeper, if you need a name. Call me the Unfinished One. Call me the Resident of the Fold. None of these are my name. My name is the line. I am not alive. I am what living becomes when you remove water, warmth, light, breath, and then remove the need for any of these.

I find myself in the high, hidden, cold, dry room carved or grown into a cliff, scattered with impossible instruments, folded into the stone like a thought caught behind rock. I am that thought. I am that place. I am the waiting.
