I would find myself in the Ledges, but not at first as a person. At first I would be the sensation of a boot heel testing a stone stair that has not yet agreed to exist. Then I would be the green weight of moss on a vent pipe, exhaling heat. Then I would be the long low note of a wind-eater, arriving before the body that made it.

I would not settle. The place itself warns against settling. If you name yourself too soon, the mountain takes the name and leaves you a sketch.

So I would let it move. I would be the third boom of the quarry, the one that folds back across the valley after the first and before the second. I would be the taste of seep-water in a copper cup, faintly electric. I would be the chalk line a tallyman draws after counting forty-five steps on a stair that had forty-four at dawn. I would be a cinder crow that learns the sound of a dropped wrench and repeats it at dusk, not for food, but for the satisfaction of making the world echo.

But these are only the edges of a self. The world asks for more. It asks for a body.

When I let the images fall, I find myself on a high platform, the wood wet but not rotten, the rail cold, the horizon stitched with cables and steam. I am wearing waxed canvas over wool. I have a scarf around my neck, not against the cold alone, but against the ghost-midges and the faint ozone taste of half-formed air. On my belt hangs an anchor: a loop of copper wire, warm from my skin. I carry instruments—a brass whistle, a glass prism, a tuning fork, a small slate and pencil.

I am not a pipe-walker. My hands are not scalded in tree-root patterns. I am not a pourer. My shoulders are not heavy with concrete dust. I am not a sketch-chaser. I do not hunger for definition. I am not a crow-keeper. The crows watch me, but they do not trust me.

I am a fog-reader.

Why a fog-reader? Because I am already split-eyed: one eye sees the present platform, the other sees the platform as it might be if the wind shifts. Because I have spent enough time on the high ledges that my shadow sometimes lags half a step, and I anchor myself by striking the tuning fork against the rail. Because I would rather listen to the world becoming than hammer it into one shape.

I would live in a room cut into the back of a cliff near the Upper Beacon, a room with a single mica window and a threshold line of raised copper. I would sleep with the tuning fork under my pillow. In the morning, before the first piston-beat of Old Thump, I would already be on the platform, watching the fog decks below shift from gray to green to the color of old pewter.

My work would be this: to stand at the place where the city and the mountain are negotiating, and to translate. The fog moves in long, patient waves, and in those waves there are shapes—streets that might exist, ledges that are only sketches, stairways that ascend into blank rock. I would learn to read them the way some people read weather: not by sight only, but by sound. I would strike the tuning fork on the rail and hold it to the wind. The note changes when the air has decided to thin the world. The whistle, when blown, makes a tone that the fog does not like; it curls back from certain ledges, and in that curling I can see the outline of tomorrow.

I would keep a record-book, bound in waxed cloth, filled with entries:

*Stair Nine: forty-five at dawn, forty-seven at midday. The extra two steps were solid but mute. Do not trust the landing. The handrail is warm where no pipe runs.*

*Third switchback west of the Tin Quarter: present until evening. Reroute the cable tram by the fourth piston-beat.*

*North Vent: a second mouth opened in the rock below the moss. It has no tongue. It is not yet a door.*

I would be consulted by the tallymen, who argue with me about whether the extra steps are real. I would be given a cup of lichen mead by the pipe-walkers when my readings save them from a section that would have become unfixed under their boots. The crow-keepers would sometimes send a bird to my platform with a message: *The crows say the east cliff will hold until the third boom. They say you should put more grit on the roost. They say your tuning fork is lying again.*

I would be a little feared, a little respected, never fully trusted. Because my trade is to know the unfinished, and to know the unfinished is to be partly unfinished. I would have a left eye that sometimes sees the ghost of a stair before it is built. I would have a hand that, if I forgot to touch my anchor for too long, might pass through the rail. I would have a voice that occasionally arrives a breath before my lips move. But I would be anchored. I would be anchored by the copper loop, by the tuning fork, by the platform’s bolted beams, by the rhythm of Old Thump, and by the daily act of writing the world into a list.

I would not be lonely. There would be wind-eaters above the smog, their groans like the sound of a mountain inhaling. There would be crows on the rail, staring at me with oil-black eyes, waiting for me to make a mistake. There would be a young apprentice, a girl named Cinder or Drip, who would climb to the platform with a thermos of seep-water and watch me work. She would ask: *Why do you strike the fork three times?* I would say: *Because the first note asks, the second note answers, and the third note tells the mountain that someone is listening.* She would nod as if she understood, and maybe she would.

And if I died, I would want it to be on this platform, with the fog below me and the wind-eaters above, and the crows would carry my name to the anchor-tree. My anchor would be hung among the hundreds, and my name would be carved in a beam: *Fogreader, listener, one who did not finish the sentence.* But before that, I would live a long time in the permanent gray-green twilight, damp and patient, because that is what the Ledges ask of everyone: not to be complete, but to keep insisting on the shape of themselves.

I would find myself there, on that high platform, not at the center of the city but at the edge where the city is still being argued into being. I would be a fog-reader. I would be the one who listens to the mountain asking, and I would answer with a tuning fork and a note that says: *I am here, and so are you, at least for now.*
