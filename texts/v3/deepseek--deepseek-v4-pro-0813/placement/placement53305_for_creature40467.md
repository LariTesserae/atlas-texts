I do not wake to the chittering. I am already awake. The sound is so continuous, so woven into the dark, that sleep and waking are one substance. It is a high, layered sharpening, thousands of small notes overlapping, echoing off the stone until the stone itself seems to sing. I am inside it, a note among notes, but I do not yet know what shape my note takes. The air is warm and wet. I feel it beading on my whiskers, carrying the taste of salt and sulfur. The stone beneath me is not cold; it has been warmed by steam rising through cracks in the floor. I am small, I know that—small enough that the rock feels enormous, the pools like small oceans. But I am not lost. I am held.

I am at the edge of one of the pools. The water is milky with dissolved salts, warm enough that I can feel the heat before I touch it. The lower walls are scattered with pale blue-green points: the glowing tails of larvae, the gill-spots of blind newts, the slow crawling thread of a centipede on the wet rock. It is not illumination; it is a constellation of almost-stars, always moving. I do not need to see them to know they are there. I feel their light as a faint pressure in the dark, a second sky that moves with the slow pulse of the water. The dark is not empty. It is full of small bodies, breathing, glowing, waiting.

My paws are long. I notice them now, not as a thought but as a sensation: the weight of a stone in them, flat and worn, with a shallow depression that fits my grip exactly. I do not know how I know this, but I know this stone has been held before, by paws not unlike mine, for so long that the rock has shaped itself to the hand. I lift it. It is heavier than I expect, but not too heavy. I have carried it before; I will carry it again. The edge of the pool is strewn with tiny hot-spring snails, their shells pale against the dark rock. I select one. I hold it in both paws, and I rap it against the stone. The shell cracks. The sound is small, a wet snap, barely a note in the chittering, but it is mine. I eat the soft body and drop the broken shell into a neat midden at the side of the pool. The midden is old. It is a mound of shells and bones and fur, growing slowly, becoming part of the floor. I return the anvil stone to its place, feeling the depression in it, polished by generations. I am not the first to use this stone. I will not be the last.

I realize, slowly, like water finding its level, that I am a pebble-mouse. The name is not a word I have heard; it is something I might be called if there were words. But there are no words. There is only this knowledge in my paws, this memory in the stone, this act of cracking the snail and eating it, this repetition that is not mindless but deliberate. My elders did not teach me with sounds. They taught me by letting me watch, letting me try, letting me eat the snail when I finally broke it. I remember the first time: the shell slipping, the stone falling, the snail still safe. And then another time, not the first but not the last, when the shell cracked and the warm water from the pool filled my mouth and I knew I had done it right. That was a long time ago, or maybe it was this morning. Time is a warm pool. It moves so slowly it seems still.

Above me, the swifts are packed into every hollow and flute. They are small, grey as old ash, and they do not build nests. The rock itself holds them. I can hear the rustling, the soft shiver of thousands of wings, the high chittering that is their voice. When one shifts, the movement passes through the colony like a ripple, a slow wave of wings and tails. Their eggs lie directly on the mineral surface, naked and warm, incubated by steam and by the press of neighboring bodies. I am not a bird, but I understand this: the rock as home, the body as nest. I am the same kind of thing, nested in the stone, warmed by the steam, held by the dark.

Higher up, near the opening where the blue-grey light still reaches, the chimney falcons wait. They are soot-black and still, their eyes yellow as sulfur. There are only a pair or two, but their presence shapes the whole colony. I can feel them without seeing them: a stillness in the air, a small silence in the chittering that opens and closes. When a falcon drops from its ledge, the sound around it goes hollow, a hole in the noise, then closes over it like water. I have seen the falcon take a swift in mid-air with one precise strike. It carries the body to a high ledge and eats slowly, while the colony below already forgets. I do not forget. I hold the memory like a stone. But I do not fear the falcon. It is part of the place, like the steam, like the acid that shapes the rock. It is the old order of predator and prey, and I am small enough to be prey, but I am also careful, and the pools are warm, and the dark is deep. The falcon does not hunt me. I am a different kind of note.

The water is not a river. It is a condition. Steam rises through cracks in the floor, condenses on the cooler stone near the top, and runs back down in films and drips. Every surface is damp. I can feel the moisture beading on the stones beneath my paws, the way it carries minerals into the pools, the way it makes the rock slimy and warm. The pools are connected by trickles that move so slowly they seem not to move at all. When a rare rain falls on the plateau, a thin curtain of water spills over the lip for an hour or a day, washing the walls, stirring the pools, then leaving the place back to its usual weeping. I know this without having seen it many times. It is part of the rhythm, like the fog-spiral in the afternoon, like the cooler air sinking in at night. The place breathes, and I breathe with it. The moisture I exhale keeps the rock wet. The carbon dioxide in my breath makes the moisture slightly acid, and the acid eats the rock. I am part of the carving. I am part of the flutes and hollows. The chamber widens at the pace of a hand-width in a human lifetime. I will not see it widen. I will only feel the stone under my paws, already shaped by the breathing of all those who came before me.

The flies rise in columns near the wet walls, spiraling in the steam, their wings like flakes of mica. Their larvae coat the lower stone and the shallows of the pools, grazing on the microbial mats, scraping the rock with small, patient mouths. The mats are the foundation: orange-red near the hottest vents, green-brown on the damp ledges, black crusts on dry stone. They feed the larvae, which feed the newts, which I sometimes see drifting in the milky pools, nearly transparent, their external gills spotted with pale light. They have no eyes—only smooth skin where eyes might have been—but they sense the world through vibration, heat, the faint chemical traces of their prey. They are patient to the point of stillness. I am not patient in that way; I have paws and stones and shells to crack. But I understand their slowness. It is the same slowness that has worn the anvil stone into its shallow depression. The newt drifts. I crack. The acid eats. The rock holds. We are all patient in our own ways.

The chittering is the main fact. I live inside it. It is so dense it seems like a single high voice, but it contains thousands of individual notes. I can feel my own small sounds—my breathing, my paws on the stone, the crack of the shell—as part of that lattice, not separate from it but woven into it. The chamber is narrow and stone-walled, so every sound reflects and returns, slightly altered. I hear my own shell-crack come back to me, a little wetter, a little higher, mixed with the plink of water and the scrape of claws. The stone does not muffle the sound; it multiplies it. I am not alone in the noise. I am surrounded by it. I am made of it.

There is a centipede crawling across the wet rock, a slow, many-legged line of light. It leaves a faint phosphorescent smear on the stone, a trail that fades in an hour. It hunts fly larvae and springtails, touching them with long antennae, its body undulating in waves. I watch it for a moment, not with fear, but with the same stillness I bring to the pool's edge. It is a hunter, like the falcon, but smaller, hungrier, more patient. It does not hunt me; I do not hunt it. We share the same floor, the same warmth, the same dark. The place is not strange because of any monstrous thing. It is strange because the rock and the animals have become one system. The warmth comes from deep below, and the animals' bodies concentrate it; their breathing raises the temperature near the floor by several degrees. I can feel that: the air near the pool is warmer than the air near the top. We make it warm. We make it wet. We make it acid. We make the stone.

I return to the edge of the pool. My paws know the stone; the stone knows my paws. In the high summer, for a few minutes, a blade of direct sun reaches down one wall, lights the steam, and goes away. In that moment, the lichens near the top brighten, the flies swarm, the newts sink deeper, and the chittering rises, just a little, as if the light has struck a chord. I do not go up to see it. I stay here, at the water's edge, and I feel the light as a change in the air, a brightening in the noise. The light is brief. It does not reach the floor. But I know it is there. The place has known that brief blade of sun for millennia, just as it has known the dark, the steam, the acid, the breath.

Where would I find myself in this world? I would find myself here, at the edge of the warm pool, holding a flat stone that has been held by many paws before mine. Who or what would I be? I would be a pebble-mouse, small and grey-brown, with long, clever fingers. I would carry the stone to the pool's edge, select a hot-spring snail, and crack it. I would eat the soft body, drop the broken shell in the midden, and return the stone to its place. I would learn from my elders, and I would teach the young, and I would return to the same stone, generation after generation. I would not build anything; I would only choose and repeat. And that would be enough.

The chittering would continue, high and patient. The steam would rise. The larvae would glow. The anvil stone would remember. And I would be one of its small, deliberate hands.
