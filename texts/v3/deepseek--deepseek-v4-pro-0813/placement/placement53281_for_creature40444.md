You would not be the watcher on the bare ledge. The air would not allow it—too dry, too hot, too slow. Instead you find yourself already low, already black-shelled, already inside a ring of flat basalt plates. Your face is only a heat pit. You are a kiln-keeper.

The Kettle is not a landscape to you; it is a room made of temperatures. The fumaroles are glaring mouths of warmth. The floor is a cool, moving skin made of millions of small armored bodies. Their blue dots flash as cold sparks when a ripple passes. A hoodmaw is a warmer pressure moving through the floor; the pale tubes are slow pulses of gas-warmth; the other keepers are distant furnaces.

You reach with one forelimb and turn a plate. It has tilted out of true. The stone settles, and the heat pools differently against your shell. That is your life: not building, not naming, only keeping. The edges of the plates are concave from keepers before you. The grooves near the entrance are polished by generations of forelimbs, and yours will add to them. The cairn on the ledge means nothing to you. Memory is not a monument; it is the angle of a stone, the way the ring holds.

There is no water in this body, no thirst, no sky. The mountain exhales long through the fumaroles, and the tubes swell with that breath. You feel it in the stones. You are not a neighbor to the carpet, the hoodmaws, the tubes. You are one of the Kettle’s old organs, deliberate and slow. You keep the heat from escaping too quickly. You hold a territory that is also a heartbeat.

If a human stood on the bare ledge, they would see only a dark shape among dark shapes. They would not know that you perceive them as a wrong heat—too tall, too wet, too loud. A footstep would be the loudest thing in the Kettle. But they are not you.

You are not the watcher. You are the keeper of the ring. You are the one the Kettle keeps.
