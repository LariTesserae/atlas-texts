I wake to the hush, as always. The world presses against me like a warm, damp hand. I am curled in the crook of a branch, my fingers wrapped around the wood as if I might fall. I don’t remember falling, but I remember the falling. That’s different.

The light comes through the canopy in a green-amber haze, and for a moment I forget what I am. My hands are long—too long—with extra knuckles that bend in directions I still find strange. My skin is the texture of old bark, and along my shoulders grows a fine, velvety moss that I did not plant. It just came, like the world was claiming me.

I am not a Tender. Not exactly.

The Tenders found me years ago, or maybe decades—time moves differently here. They found me tangled in a net of vines near a true hole, my clothes torn, my skin pale and soft, my fingers short and blunt. I was dying, or so they said. They brought me water in a leaf-cup, fed me fruit, taught me to listen to the wood. They were patient, as they are with everything. They waited for me to grow into this place.

And I did. Slowly, my body changed. My skin grew rough, then bark-like. My fingers lengthened, the joints multiplying. Moss began to grow on my back, spreading from a small patch to a fine, velvety layer that matched the world around me. My eyes adapted to the dimness, losing the need for bright light, gaining a green sheen that lets me see depth and tension in the weave. My ears learned to read the vibrations in the wood—the creak of a settling strand, the weight of a distant herd, the tick of a blind cricket. I learned to tap messages: *tik-tik-tah*, like water on a leaf.

I am not a Tender, but the Tenders no longer seem to mind. I am something else.

I uncurl from my nest and stretch. The moss on my back flexes with me. I drink from a nearby pool, the water warm and tasting of pollen. I eat a fruit, spitting the seeds into a crevice. I check the new shoots along my branch, trimming one that has grown out of pattern. My fingers know the knots, the tension, the suggestion. I have learned the Tender’s work, but I do it differently. They tend the weave; I tend the holes.

The true holes. They are the reason I am here, I think. I fell through one, or near one, and ended up here. The Tenders say that to fall into a true hole is to leave the world, and no one knows what remains after that. But I fell through, and I remained. Or maybe I didn’t fall through. Maybe I was always here, and the memory of another world—sky, soil, rock, the solid ground—is just a dream the hole gave me. I don’t know.

What I do know is that the holes recognize me. When I pass near one, the air goes cold, and the water drops hesitate at the edge, then run sideways. The flat, colorless absence watches me like a blind eye. And I watch back. I do not fear them as the Tenders do. I make the tying-off gesture, not to seal them, but to acknowledge them, and they respond—a faint shift in the pattern, a ripple in the weave. They are not empty, I have learned. They are threads that have been pulled too tight, or not at all. They are the places where the world forgot itself. And I, who have forgotten my own world, am kin to them.

Today I follow the north span, where the wood is soft and the heat from below rises in slow, wet breaths. I am checking for true holes. They shift; a gap that was there at dawn may be gone by dusk. I keep a mental map of them, but the map changes. That is the first rule of the holes: they are not places, but absences, and absences move.

I find one near the old crossing, a small hole no bigger than a fist. It sits in the crotch of a branch, flat and colorless, like a blind spot in the eye. The moss around it has turned black and brittle. I crouch beside it, not too close. The Tenders would make the tying-off gesture and move to the far side of the hollow. But I stay. I have been doing this for so long that it feels like a conversation.

Hello, I tap on the wood, not with my fingers but with a small bone needle I carry. The vibration travels through the fibers, reaches the edge of the hole, and stops. That is the strange part: the sound does not echo, does not pass through. It is swallowed. But if I wait, something comes back—not a sound, but a feeling. A pressure. A memory.

Today the hole gives me a memory of rain. Not the mist and dripping of this world, but a hard rain, a storm, with wind and thunder and the smell of wet earth. I see a flash of green—bright green, the color of leaves in sunlight—and then it is gone. I sit back on my heels. The hole has not moved. It is still there, waiting.

I do not know if the holes are alive. They do not breathe, do not eat, do not grow. But they respond. They remember. They are the world’s memory of what is not there. And I, who remember a world that is not here, am their interpreter. That is my role. The Tenders tend the weave; I tend the holes.

I reach into my pouch and take out a small piece of moss, damp and alive. I press it gently into the edge of the hole, where the braided strands are frayed. The moss takes root immediately, and for a moment the hole’s edge softens. It is not a repair—the hole cannot be repaired—but it is a bandage. A way of saying: I see you. I remember. Then I make the tying-off gesture and move on.

The rest of the day is ordinary, if this world can be called ordinary. I walk the spans, checking the knots I have tied. I drink from leaf-cups. I pass a herd of grazers, slow and silent, their bodies colored like wet bark. They move aside for me, not because I am a threat, but because I am a Tender—or close enough. A glider launches from a high strand and sails across the gap, silent as a leaf. I watch it go, and for a moment I remember what it was like to fall.

I was falling. That is the last clear memory of my other world. I was falling through a gap in something—a bridge, a floor, a hole—and then I was here. The Tenders found me tangled in vines, my clothes torn, my body broken. They say I was lucky. They say the true holes are not kind. But I do not remember the fall as pain. I remember it as a passage. A letting go.

The light never gets bright here. It is always a perpetual late afternoon under heavy clouds. But the heat is constant, rising from below, and the water is everywhere. I have grown to love this place, if love is the right word. It is more like acceptance. The world is woven, and I am a thread in it, even if I am a thread that does not quite match.

At night, I return to my nest, not in the crook of a branch, but near the edge of a large hollow where a true hole has been known to appear. I do not fear it. I curl into my ferns and shed skins, and I listen to the world settle. The water drips. The wood creaks. The holes are silent. But in my dreams, the holes speak. They speak in the language of rain and sunlight, of soil and rock, of a world I have almost forgotten. And when I wake, I remember that I am not entirely here. I am a bridge, perhaps. A thread between the seen and the unseen. And as long as the holes remember, so do I.

Sometimes, in the stillest hours, I test the boundaries of what I have become. I press my hand against a living strand and feel its slow pulse, the way the wood holds its tension. I close my eyes and let the vibrations travel through me—the distant tick of crickets, the soft weight of a grazer's step, the whisper of a glider's launch. And beneath it all, a deeper thrum, a low and constant note that I have come to think of as the world's heartbeat. Or perhaps it is the sound of something falling, far below, still falling.

I do not know if I will ever fall again. The holes watch me, and I watch them. Sometimes I think they are waiting for me to remember something, a word or a name, that will let them close. Other times I think they are waiting for me to forget, to become fully this place, so that they can take me back. But I do not forget. I remember the sky. I remember the rain. I remember the solid ground, the earth that did not give way. And as long as I remember, I am not entirely theirs.

The Tenders do not ask about my other world. They are not curious in that way. Their world is complete, woven and hollow, holding itself together over the vast, warm, hazy deep. They tend it with the patience of seasons, and they do not need to know what lies beyond the holes. But they accept me, this stranger who moves among them with moss on her shoulders and memories of rain. They tap my name—*tik-tik-tah*—and I answer. I am part of the weave now, even if I am a thread that came from somewhere else.

One day, I may find a true hole that is large enough to fall through, and I may step into it. Not out of despair, but out of curiosity. I want to know if the other world still exists, if there is still a sky and soil and rock, if the rain still falls hard and green. But I am not ready. The world here is alive, and I am part of it. The shoots need guiding, the knots need tying, the holes need tending. There is always a thread that needs attention. And as long as the pattern holds, so do I.

So I remain. I wake to the hush, I drink the warm water, I walk the spans, I tend the holes. I am the one who fell through and stayed, the one who remembers, the one who bridges the seen and the unseen. And when the Tenders tap my name through the wood, I answer, not with words, but with a vibration of my own: *I am here. I am still here.*
