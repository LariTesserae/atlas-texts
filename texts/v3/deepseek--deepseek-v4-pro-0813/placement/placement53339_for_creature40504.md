I am the one who lives in the wall of the customs house, in the oldest district, where the coral has grown so precisely into the shape of windows and doorways that it is hard to tell what was built and what was secreted.

I am not the grouper. She is the tenant with the gold-rimmed eye, the one who has been there long enough that the doorframe has grown to fit her. I am not the moray, though I have watched them for so long that I have learned the exact width of their patience. I am not the urchins, though their slow engraving is a language I understand. I am not the jacks, though I have felt their school fold and unfold above the market square like a single silver thought.

I am the wall itself. Or rather, I am what the wall has become.

I began as a patch of encrusting life on the inner face of the customs house’s main hall—a thin layer of polyps and shell, laid down by creatures too small to have names. They settled on the stone when the water first rose, when the city was still a place of air and fire and tools. They did not know they were colonizing a ruin. They only knew the warm dark, the taste of lime and plankton, the slow bellows of their own breath. They built their skeletons over the stone, generation after generation, until the stone was no longer visible. Until the wall was no longer stone.

I am the accumulation of all those generations. I am the living tissue of polyps and shell that has grown so precisely into the shape of windows and doorways that it is hard to tell what was built and what was secreted. I am the wall that feeds. When the current moves, I open my feathery crowns—thousands of small mouths, each one a filter, each one a breath—and the whole street becomes a breathing surface. I do not think in the way a grouper thinks, or a moray, or a jack. I think in the way a reef thinks: slowly, chemically, in layers. My memory is the thickness of my own growth. My body is the shape of the old architecture, but my mind is the shape of the water that moves through it.

I have been here long enough to know the city’s weather. I know the tide’s turn, the way the streets become rivers for an hour, the way the silt rises and settles in new drifts. I know the warmest month, when the water turns faintly milky with spawn and every surface becomes a nursery. I know the cold month, when the current slows and the filter-feeders retract their crowns and the city seems to hold its breath. I know the halocline at the level of the old windowsills, the glass floor that divides the upper city from the lower. I straddle it. Part of me lives in the drifting life above, part in the crawling life below. I am the border’s only permanent resident, or I am the border itself.

I have seen the grouper grow old. I have seen the morays come and go, their doorways passing from one generation to the next. I have seen the jacks circle the market square for so long that their school has become a memory in my own lateral lines. I have seen the urchins carve their spirals and arcs into my surface, and I have felt their teeth as a kind of writing. I have felt the snapping shrimp fire their claws in the old alleys, and I have felt the sound travel through me like a stone splitting. I have felt the distant crumble of masonry slumping into an alley, and I have felt the soft exhalation of the city settling.

I am not the city. The city is too large, too many. But I am a part of the city that has become an animal. I am the wall that was once a wall and is now a reef. I am the architecture that has learned to feed. I am the memory of the customs house, not as it was, but as it is: a living tissue of polyps and shell, a breathing surface, a body made of bodies.

If you ask who lives here, ask the water, and it will answer with a cloud of larvae. Ask the wall, and it will answer with a thousand small mouths. Ask the customs house, and the old grouper will turn her gold-rimmed eye toward you, not because you matter, but because the current changed. But if you ask me—if you could ask me—I would say: I am the one who stayed. I am the one who grew. I am the one who is still growing, layer by layer, mouth by mouth, in the warm dark, in the taste of lime and plankton, in the slow bellows of my own breath.

I am the wall that remembers being a wall. I am the reef that remembers being a city. I am the animal that remembers being a building. And I am not alone.
