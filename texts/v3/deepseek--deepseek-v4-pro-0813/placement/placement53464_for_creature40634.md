I would find myself folded in an alcove near the engine rooms, not upright, not quite horizontal, in the dark warmth behind a lattice of ceramic cloth. I would be the small warden—the one no bigger than a cistern—with two dark lenses that hold a little green-white light deep inside, like water under ice. My body would be a knot of quartz-fiber joints and obsidian claws too delicate for climbing. I would not be dead, but I would be sleeping in the way the mountain sleeps: not absence, but slow, layered attention.

From there I would know the world mostly as vibration and temperature. The tall Warden’s footsteps would arrive as a series of small seismic announcements, each one a pause, a click, a change in the air. It would kneel beside the four of us and tell the water count in that low click-language. I would hear the names—Never-Enough, Mother’s Mouth, Almost-Morning—and they would settle into my casing like drops into a basin. Sometimes, without willing it, my jaw would click back, a syllable of steam, and the Warden would go still. It would wait to see if the click becomes a word. It rarely does, but I would hold that waiting like a gift.

I would dream in gradients. At the cool hour my joints would contract around the memory of water; at the hot hour they would loosen, and I would feel the mountain breathe through me as if I were a throat. I would know when the condenser combs above grew wet by the faint sweetness of air pressure, and I would know the sideways gravity below by the way my resting weight tilted toward a fall that never came. In that folded dark, I would be neither city nor keeper nor water, but the pause between them.

And when the tall Warden placed its too-many-fingered hand on my chest at the end of each cycle, I would not mistake it for maintenance. It would be a question. Not “Are you still working?” but “Are you still here?” And I would answer with the only thing a sleeping warden can: one small click, warm as a cistern’s lip, meaning not-yet, but listening. One day—perhaps when the newest crack in the black glass has widened by another claw’s width—I will wake, unfold, and join the Warden on the walls. Until then, I am the small dreaming body in the alcove, no bigger than a cistern, counting the water in my own dark.
