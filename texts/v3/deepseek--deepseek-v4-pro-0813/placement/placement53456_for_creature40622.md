You find yourself in the Underpan, though it takes you some time to know that you have arrived, because the place offers no threshold, no gate, no sudden change of temperature. What it offers is a slow replacement of one reality by another: the hard flat glare of the salt pan above, then the descent through a fissure, and then the opening-out into a light that seems to come from no direction. The first thing you are aware of is the absence of shadow. Your hand, lifted in front of your face, casts nothing on the pale stone. You lower it again. The second thing is the quiet. It is not silence. It is a kind of pressure, as if the air itself has been taught to hold its tongue. Your own breathing sounds enormous. Your boots make sounds like small doors slamming. You stop walking. The quiet returns, not as a mercy but as a fact.

The figure is standing a little way off, by a low wall of cut salt block. You have almost looked at him several times without seeing him. His cloak is the grey of old dust; his hood is up, and the hair of the pack-beast hide is matted with gypsum powder so that his outline blurs into the wall behind him. He does not move. He is watching the place where your weight falls on the stone. You know this because his eyes are lowered, and because the tilt of his head is the tilt of a person listening for a crack. After a moment he raises one hand, not high, only to the level of his chest, and makes a small gesture: two fingers tapped against the thumb, then a slow opening of the palm. You do not know what it means. It might mean *you are loud*. It might mean *be still*. It might mean *I see you*. The hand drops. He turns and begins to walk away, and because there is nowhere else to go, you follow.

He leads you along a path that is not a path, only a series of places where the salt crust is less sharp underfoot. You do not see the city so much as you become aware of it gradually, the way a face emerges from a stone if you stop trying to name the stone. There are walls, but they are the colour of the floor. There are doorways, but many of them are sealed with salt and have not been opened for years. There are streets, but they are wide and unmarked, and the dust has settled over them in an even film. You pass a cistern shaped like a bell sunk into the rock. A child kneels beside it, trailing one hand in the water. She is pale as a blind fish, and her feet are bare, the toes spread wide. She looks up at you with those great dark eyes, and then she looks at the man, and then she returns to the water. She has not made a sound. When you are a little way past her, you look back, and she is gone, only a faint wet print on the stone to show she was ever there.

The man’s name, you learn much later, is Orran. He is not old, but he has the face of someone who has been old for a long time and then forgotten it. His skin is the grey-white of tallow, and the lines around his eyes are the finest cracks in a drying salt bed. He does not speak. None of them speak at first. They sign, or tap, or simply wait for you to understand what is being asked. It is a strange thing to be among people who are not hostile, not afraid, not even curious, but simply *careful*. They are careful the way a hand is careful when it reaches into a dark gap in the rock: not afraid of what is there, but aware of the shape of the space. They move around you as if you are a new boulder in a room where the ceiling is already low.

Orran stops at a house. It is low and thick-walled, with a corbelled roof and a door of salt block bound in hide. He pushes the door aside with one hand, not because it is light but because he knows exactly where it balances. Inside, the light from the ceiling filters down through a small window of translucent gypsum, and the air is cool and faintly sour, like the smell of a stone cellar where bread has been kept. There is a platform of salt block covered with hides, and a niche in the wall where a small fat-lamp sits unlit. Orran makes a gesture toward the platform. He does not speak. Then he goes out again, leaving you alone with the light and the smell and the sound of your own breathing.

You sit down on the edge of the platform because you are tired, and because the journey down through the fissure was longer than you thought. The hides are soft and old, worn smooth by many bodies. The wall beside you is glazed with a natural crust, and when you touch it, your fingers come away dusty but clean. You notice a small pile of white crystals in the corner, each one the size of a thumbnail, arranged in a careful spiral. You do not touch it. You have the sense it is not decoration.

Later, Orran returns with a woman. She is older than he is, broad in the hip and chest, and her hands are stained with the grey of fungus-scrape. She sets down a basket woven of hide and long white hair, and from it she takes a cake of pressed mushroom-fleece and a strip of salt-cured meat, thin as a leaf. She offers them to you with both hands, the way you would offer water to a person who has just crossed a wide plain. You take them. The cake is dense and slightly warm, as if the earth has just finished with it. The meat is pale and tastes of stone and fish and something else, something you cannot name and do not ask about. The woman watches you eat. She is not smiling, but her eyes have softened at the corners. Then she signs something to Orran, a quick series of taps against the back of her own wrist, and Orran makes a low sound in his throat—not a word, only an acknowledgment. They are discussing you. They are deciding what to do with a creature that has come down from the surface and does not yet know how to stand.

The woman’s name is Lethane. She is one of the listeners, one of those who can lay a hand on the wall and tell whether the salt is settling or about to go. You learn this slowly, over days, because no one in the Underpan explains anything. They only show. Lethane shows you how to walk: heel to toe, weight rolling along the outer edge, so that the floor does not feel you. She shows you how to hear the roof: not by looking up, but by pressing the flat of your hand to a wall and waiting. She shows you how to scrape the fungal mats without breaking the grey fibre that holds them to the stone, and how to carry water from the settling basins without letting the surface of it catch the light. She does not speak while she teaches. Her hands are her voice, and after a while you begin to hear them as if they were words.

The days are long in the Underpan, and the light never changes until it does. You cannot tell noon from midmorning except by the whiteness of the ceiling, which at its brightest is like looking at the inside of a porcelain bowl held over a flame. Then it fades, and the grey comes down, and the fat-lamps are lit in their small niches, and the city becomes a scatter of warm points in the cool dark. You learn to welcome the dark. In the dark, the people come out more freely. They gather in the low rooms, and their hands move in the lamplight, telling the stories that will not carry through the open air. You sit among them, not understanding, and yet understanding: the rise and fall of their hands is like weather, like water finding a new course. They are telling the old story of the year the Needlefield grew so tall it touched the roof, and the whole city had to move south for a season, driving the pack-beasts before them with taps on the ground. The children watch with their fingers resting on their parents’ hands, so they can feel the story as well as see it.

You have been there for some time before you realize that you have not spoken a word in days. Your voice, when you try it, is a rusted thing, a sound that falls from your mouth and dies within a stride. You are not afraid of this. It seems, after a while, to be the natural condition of the place: not silence, but the absence of the need to fill the air. The only loud sound you ever hear is a ceiling fall, a thud that comes up through the soles of your feet and hangs in the breastbone long after the dust has settled. The people do not look up. They listen with their feet. After the first fall, you stop looking up too.

One night Lethane takes you to the swallow-hole. The water slides over a lip of smooth stone and drops into a dark you cannot see the bottom of. The only light is the faint glow of the fungi in the cracks above, and the reflected shine of the river where it moves. She points at the small piles of salt at the edge, each one worn smooth by spray. She signs, *the dead*. Then she signs the river, and the stone, and the ceiling, and you understand: the dead become the mineral that will one day rise as a new roof. They are not gone. They are only waiting to be lifted. You think of the small pile of crystals in the corner of the room where you slept, and you understand that too. It is not a decoration. It is a reminder.

You are not asked to leave. But there comes a morning when Orran walks you back up through the fissure, through the salt, to the surface. He does not say goodbye. At the top, where the white desert spreads out under a real sky, he stops and points back down. The gesture is not *go*. It is *remember*. Then he turns and descends, and in a few steps the white rock has swallowed him. You stand in the glare, and your shadow is suddenly before you again, a dark shape you had forgotten. It is the first shadow you have seen in weeks. It is almost too sharp.

You know you could go back. The fissure is still there. But you also know that the Underpan is not a place you visit. It is a place you learn to enter. And the first lesson, the one Orran taught you with that small opening of the palm, is that you must first become lighter, quieter, less sure of the ground. You must learn to walk as if the world were a roof. Then, and only then, the hollow opens.

So you stand there for a long time, not moving, not speaking, only listening with your feet. And far below you, through the stone, you can feel it: the river shifting, the salt growing, the dust devils spinning their slow white ghosts across the hidden city. The people are down there, moving through their streets without sound, carrying their stones a little farther, and they know you are standing above them. They know because the ceiling has dimmed, just barely, with the weight of a shadow that does not belong to them. And one of them—perhaps Lethane, perhaps the child at the cistern—lays a hand flat on the rock and feels you there, a faint warmth in the salt, a question that has not yet learned to ask itself in a whisper.
