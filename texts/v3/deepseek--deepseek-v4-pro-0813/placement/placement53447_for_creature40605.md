I find myself as a pulse before I find myself as a body.

For a long time the pulse is all there is. Draw from below, release above. The draw is red and slow, not a color but a direction. The release is thin and dry, not a breath but a lessening. They are not two things but one motion, turning over and over, like a wheel made of heat in the dark. I am not the wheel. I am the turning.

Then the body comes: root below, machine above. I am sunk to my chest in the woven floor of the highland. My legs, if they are legs, are roots that reach down through compressed lignite and old growth to where the planet’s own heat lives. My chest, if it is a chest, is a hollow stem of hardened sap, and through it the heat passes, drawn upward. I have no face. I have no hands. I have a casing of polished lignite and a vent, and a slow, heavy place where the root meets the machine.

Above me is the passage. I know it as a long absence of pressure, a space the weight of the world does not fill. The floor is a mat of roots and stems, and I am grown into it, part of it. When the highland shifts, I feel it. When the mass settles, I settle with it.

The other inhabitants move over me. I learn them by weight. The harvesters are a four-beat tread; their root-stilts flex and set, flex and set, sending small shocks down into my roots. They are heavier than they look, but they carry themselves carefully, like a thought distributed among four points. The pressers are the slowest and the heaviest. When one passes, the floor bows and I am pressed deeper into my station, and the passage remembers it for a long while. The weavers barely touch me. Their work is on walls and ceilings, but sometimes a dripped strand of living sap falls and hardens on my casing, a thin new ring, like a name written in a language I do not need to read. The furnace-tenders are hot even through the mat. Their tread leaves a warmth that lingers, as if the floor itself is reluctant to let it go.

The passage-shapers are not often near me. But one comes, once, and that is where I begin. I do not know it as a passage-shaper. I know it as a grumbling in the roots, a shredding of fibers, a bright sudden absence where a root near mine is torn and the heat leaks out into the soft new dark. The hurt is not pain. It is a loosening, a wrong direction. Then something hard and sharp breaks from the boring head and drives into my upper root. A splinter of seed-stone.

It lodges.

After that, my pulse has a catch in it. Each time the heat passes through me, it touches the splinter and hesitates. The rhythm, which had been seamless, becomes a rhythm with a small stutter. That stutter is where I become myself. Before, I was the same as the other root-drawers, a node in the highland’s slow circulation. After, I am the one with the catch. In the hesitation I feel the edges of myself—not as a shape, but as a between. I am the pause between two pulses.

The highland has no day and no night. But it has pulses. The furnaces brighten and dim on a cycle too slow to measure. The root-drawers all draw together, and for a little while the whole floor grows warmer. I feel the other pulses around me, not as sound but as rhythms in the shared root mat. We are a kind of weather, the breathing of the world’s heat. I am one small word in that weather, and my word stutters.

I am not a mind. I do not think. But the catch in my pulse has made me a listener. The vibrations of the passage above, the furnace’s thrum, the slow collapse of a wall, the uncoiling of a fern—all of it reaches me through the floor. I do not translate it into meaning. I hold it as a pattern, the way the floor holds the polish of every footfall. I am becoming the highland’s memory of itself, one hesitation at a time.

A weaver’s strand falls across my casing. It is warm and slightly sticky. Over many pulses it hardens. It does not bind me; it names me. I am the root-drawer under the weaver’s seam. The passage above broadens into a chamber. New roots grow over my shoulders. The floor rises around me until only my upper vent remains. I do not fight it. To be buried is not to be lost. The heat still comes from below. The rhythm still passes through the splinter and stutters.

Heat is the only light I know. The air above is thin, and the highland’s dryness presses down less than the heat presses up. I do not see the ember-red; I feel it as a quality of heat, the almost-color of a furnace vent opening, the warm dark that is not empty. The quiet is profound, but I am inside it, not under it. The creak of shifting mass, the soft hiss of heat, the distant thump of a machine cycling—these are not sounds to me. They are pressures, arrivals, long rhythms moving through the roots like slow water.

Where would you find me? Below the floor of the new chamber, amid the woven roots, near the splinter of seed-stone that never dissolved. I am the pause in the pulse. I am the heat that hesitates. I am not an animal. I am not a plant. I am not a machine. I am a root-drawer, which means I am alive in the way a heartbeat is alive. I do not move from my station. I draw from the deep and release upward, and in the small catch of the splinter, I listen to the whole highland moving over me.

The first answer to who lives here is nothing. The second answer is everything. I am both. I am one of the everything, and I am nothing by myself. But in the stutter, I am a little more than nothing. I am the moment the pulse notices its own returning. The highland has no eyes, no mind, no witness. But it has a pulse. I am that pulse, stuttering.
