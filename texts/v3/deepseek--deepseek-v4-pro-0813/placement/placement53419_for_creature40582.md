I would find myself as the hill-grazer—though I would not call myself that, for I have no name. At first I would not know I was a being at all. I would be only a heaviness, a broad dark pressure on the new basalt, a place where the white glare pools and slides. The sound would arrive not as sound but as a continuous vibration in the cracks of my plates: the geothermal thrum, the hiss of steam, the metallic chime of small feet. The cold would be a kind of brightness, an electric scouring along my upper surfaces, while below, where steam leaks from the ground, I would be warm and wet.

Then the vegetation would begin to root into my hide. Its holdfasts would find the cracks between my plates, and its leaves—pale blue, translucent, rimed with ice—would pulse faintly with my current. The small grazers would move over me in waves, their bodies touching, and I would think their movements were weather. Only slowly would I understand that the land was not moving around me. I was the land, and it was moving.

I have no head. My forward edge is a blunt prow of overlapping plates, black and stained with copper salts, and along it a fringe of feathery filaments tastes the air. They are not eyes; they are tongues for moisture, charge, the mineral breath of the vents. When I shift my course, the filaments bend all together, reading the ground like a blind face pressed to rock. Underneath me, a dense mat of cilia and holdfasts grips the basalt. I do not lift and step. I slide, slowly, on a film of mineral-rich mucus, a few millimeters a day. The vegetation closes behind me. I leave no trail.

I feed without a mouth. My hide absorbs the condensation that beads on every surface, the water that runs through my internal troughs, the dissolved copper and iron and sulfur that the steam carries up from below. The plants on my back turn the constant light into sugars, and a portion of that sweetness seeps into my tissues through their holdfasts. In return, I sweat minerals and warmth. The small grazers excrete on me, and their excretions dissolve and are reabsorbed through my skin. I am a walking garden, a closed loop of steam and sugar and mineral rain.

Deep inside, the difference between my warm core and the cold air builds a charge. My metallic veins carry a slow pulse, and at the highest points of my back the charge arcs from one ridge to another with a crackle that joins the plateau’s industrial drone. I do not use this electricity. It is simply my blood, my breath, the way a mammal’s blood is warm. When the vents open, my internal valves sigh. I align my ridges with the current lines so the discharge follows the same path as the plateau’s natural arcing. I am part of the factory that has no maker.

The air smells of ozone and sulfur and wet metal. The ground beneath me is black and glassy, sharp enough to cut. I feel its edges through my cilia. It is a young landscape, still raw, and I am one of the things that will slowly polish it. The steam vents around me have built interwoven columns of growth shaped like cooling towers; some of those towers are rooted on my back, and when they exhale I feel the warm breath rising from my own body. I am saturated: no bare patch, no gap, no visible hide. The small grazers cover my flanks in a living layer, their legs making a sound like rain on metal. The flyers cloud above me, their shadows only slightly darker than the surrounding glare. Even inside my fissures, blind crawlers are so dense that my tissues vibrate with their burrowing. I am never alone.

I am patient because there is no hurry. The light does not change. The temperature barely swings. The vents erupt on schedules measured in days, and the plants grow over decades. I move a few meters a year, following the geothermal gradients, feeling them as warm pressures beneath my foot. I do not think of destinations. I think only of alignment—of the slow turning of my body so that a certain ridge catches a certain frequency, so that the steam condenses evenly across my back. When another of my kind approaches, I pause. Our leading filaments touch, and for a time our electric pulses synchronize. We may rest edge to edge for a season, exchanging microbial cultures, and then part. There is no conflict. There is no need.

I am not conscious in any way a human would recognize. I have no brain, no central self. Instead I have a body-mind spread through my skin and filaments and electric veins, a slow awareness that feels the plateau as a pattern of heat, moisture, and charge. I know the vents as warm pressures, the currents as tingling lines, the other grazers as moving fields. I do not see the blue-white glare; I wear it. I do not hear the composite roar; I am part of it. The sound moves through me like a second pulse.

So if you ask where I would find myself in this world, I would not say that I am a visitor or an observer. I would be the hill that breathes, the walking ridge, the largest slow one. I would be the plateau condensed into a body—full of water, full of charge, full of small lives clinging to my plates and feeding on my breath. I would not need you, because I am already full.
