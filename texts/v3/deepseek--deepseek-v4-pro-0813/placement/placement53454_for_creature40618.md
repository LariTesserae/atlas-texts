I would find myself in the lower dark, below the terraces where the fog reaches only as a memory. The stone is warm there—warm like the flank of a sleeping grazer—and the air smells of old clay, mineral dust, and the faint sweet ferment of pollen that has drifted down from the canopy. I am small, narrow-shouldered, my feet broad and long-toed. My skin is painted with clay; my cape fringes are wet from the last fog.

At first there is no name. There is only sound. The crater is a throat, and I am an ear pressed to its wall. I hear water that is not running, only breathing: a drop condensing on a waxy leaf, rolling along a groove, falling into a clay gutter. I hear slow condensation in a buried seam, the tick of insect cages, the shift of a grazer’s wet wool against rock. I hear a hairline crack behind the fourth cistern before it becomes a leak. I hear the stone contracting in the cold hour before dawn, and I know whether the morning fog will be heavy or thin.

They call me Hears-the-Stone. Before that I was Small-Noise, because I sat by the cisterns and did not speak. Before that I was the child on the terrace with a waxy leaf and a steady hand, learning to pick up a single bead of water without breaking it. My names have worn into me the way feet wear thresholds.

I do not wake the fog. That is the fog-waker’s work, up at the rim with her wooden horn. I do not keep the ledger of tasks and shares, though I have sat in the Ledger-Room and watched the keeper’s fingers run through the knotted cords. I am the one who walks the terraces at night with a lamp of seed oil. I touch the stone gutters, clear dust from leaf-lined channels, press my ear to the rock and listen.

What do I hear? The throat drinking. The old caldera breathes heat into the roots of the plants, and the plants breathe water into the air, and the air climbs to the rim and comes back as fog. That is not a cycle; it is a slow, continuous word. I listen to it the way a tongue holds a syllable. If there is a blockage, I clear it. If there is a crack, I tap the hollow stick and the plasterers come with pumice and ground eggshell. If a cistern is settling, I know by the change in the note of its silence. There is no water that can be seen, only water that can be heard.

Tonight I am below the fifth ledge, where the rock is warmest. The Night-Ear called me because she heard a wrongness in the dark. I kneel and put my cheek against the basalt. The stone is smooth, polished by generations of shoulders, and at first I hear only the deep hum of the crater’s own heat. Then I hear it: a thin, patient hiss of vapor escaping through a crack no wider than a hair. The water is leaving as breath, not as drip. I tap three times on the stone, and the sound travels up the wall. Somewhere above me, a child wakes and carries the message. By dawn, the plasterers will come with clay and crushed pumice. The cistern will hold.

This is the whole of my law: nothing that can hold water is left open. No fog-tree is cut without planting another. Every animal, wild or tame, is part of the water cycle. To kill wastefully is to spill water. I am not gentle; I am economical. If a person leaves a jar uncovered, they sit with an empty cup on the sunniest ledge. But at dusk I bring them a wet cloth to suck, because punishment that wastes water is also waste.

I am not the crater’s ruler. It has no ruler. The closest thing is the Ledger-Keeper, and they only remember. I am one sense organ of the throat: an ear in the dark, listening for the moment when the water might escape. The crater is not a place where I live. It is what I am.
