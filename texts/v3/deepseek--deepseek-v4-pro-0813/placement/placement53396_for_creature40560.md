I am not sure what I am. I have lived in the High Ladders all my life, and still the place will not decide for me. Some mornings I wake as a pair of hands. Some mornings I wake as a pair of ears. Today I wake as a pair of feet, already walking.

The room is an old boiler. Its firebox is a cold hearth now; the walls curve around me like a held breath. I lie on a mat of woven banana fiber, and the first sound is water finding its way down a chain. Not a loud sound—more like the building clearing its throat. I get up. I do not hurry. The floor is a steel plate, ribbed and warm. The hum of the geothermal mains comes through my heels, up through my shins, into the base of my skull. It is the oldest voice I know.

Outside, the terraces are already bright. The lower mill floors glow with furnace-light, and the middle garden terraces are a green tangle. The air smells of steam, compost, and something mineral—wet stone after a pipe cools, or the taste of a coin held in the mouth. I climb. My toes grip the rungs. My fingertips are gray pads; they know the difference between a healthy bearing and a dying one by the thrum alone. That is not magic. It is just living long in a place that never stops talking.

I walk the condensate channels. That is my lean, though the older tenders still call me a child. I carry a basket with a ball of hemp cloth, a lump of resin, a wooden plug, and a banana. The garden-wrights push fruit on me when I pass. I accept, because to refuse would be a kind of imbalance. The banana is warm from the high sun.

The water is everywhere. It runs down chains, along fluted concrete, into cisterns. It does not rain often; instead the whole structure sweats. Steam leaks from valve clusters and cools on ceramic tiles, then drips into gutters. I walk through steam and come out wet. Ferns brush my arms. Watercress mats underfoot. At a cistern I stop and dip a fingertip in. The water is warm and mineral-stained, crowded with frog eggs. It tastes of old iron, brick dust, compost, and a clean note at the end. I know this tank is good. I move on.

At a drain mouth, scale has thickened. I kneel and scrape it away with a tile shard. The water runs clearer. This is not repair; it is listening. The water is the blood of this place. If I let the scale grow, the blood slows. I scrape until my knees ache, and then I keep scraping.
