At first I would not be a person. I would be a pause between two drips, a patch of lighter gray on a north-facing wall, a hesitation in the lane where the sound of the cistern changes. The town would not notice me, and that would be correct.

If I tried to choose too early, I would choose wrong. I would think I was the water, because the place is made of water. But the water already knows its path; it does not need me. I would think I was the stone, because the place is made of stone. But the stone is finished, and I am not finished.

I might try to be useful in the visible ways. I could carry water to the old woman’s house, but a woman already does that, and a second pair of hands would only make the lane feel crowded. I could sit by the gate, but the old man already holds that stillness, and two sitters would be too many. I could learn to shape stone, but there is only one teacher and one student at a time.

So I would drift. Not unhappily. The gray light would press on me lightly, the way it presses on everything. The damp would gather in the hood of my coat. I would walk from the cistern to the meeting-house and back, and I would begin to notice the empty houses.

Not the emptiness itself—that is easy to see. But the cleanliness. A threshold swept. A shutter hanging straight. A stone cup set upside down on a windowsill. A smell of cold ash and juniper, but not stale. These houses are not abandoned. They are being kept, and yet I never saw anyone go in or out.

Then, on some day with no particular quality, I would step into one. The door would move easily. The room would be cold and clean. The light would come through the shutter-slats in long pale stripes, like water running down a wall without wetting it. The hearth would be swept. The floor would be smooth. I would stand just inside the door and feel the house waiting—not for someone to live in it, but for someone to acknowledge that it still belongs to the town.

That is what I would become: the keeper of empty houses. Not by appointment. The town does not appoint. I would simply begin, quietly, and by beginning I would fit myself into a role that has been waiting. Perhaps it had been waiting for a long time.

I would know each empty house by the sound of its roof in the rain, by the way its door scrapes the stone, by the particular coldness of its corners. One would smell of old wool and woodsmoke. Another of wet stone and mouse. Another of dust and the faint green smell of moss behind a wall. I would carry a broom of bound twigs, a rag of oiled hide, and no key, because none of the doors are locked.

My work would be small. On a day when the clouds thin, I would open a shutter so the air moves without warming the room. I would clean the stone basin beside the door—the one that catches the roof-drip—and clear the channel that carries its overflow out to the lane. I would not bring extra water. The water comes when it comes; I would only keep its path ready. I would reset a threshold stone if it had shifted. I would wipe the green film from a windowsill with the edge of my sleeve. I would leave no mark. The broom would smooth my footsteps behind me.

I would not light the hearth. A fire would be too much presence in an empty house. I would not sleep there. I would not leave an offering, because an empty house is not a grave. And I would not speak inside. If I named the house, I would do it outside, afterward, the way the living remember the dead—not a summoning, just a recognition.

The children would see me go in and out. They would not follow. Sometimes a child would leave a flat stone on a threshold, and I would put it back on the ground outside, because an empty house should not become a game. The old man at the gate would watch me pass, and his silence would have a different weight after a while. The woman at the cistern would not ask what I do, but if she saw me clearing a channel, she would check the overflow with extra care, as if agreeing that water should be kept moving.

In time I would learn the names of the families who once lived in each house. Not from writing—there is no writing—but from the living, who remember. A child might say, “That is the house of the woman who wove the good wool,” and I would hold the phrase the way the town holds water: without hoarding, passing it onward.

I would grow old. My hands would become hard and gray-tinged, like the stone I tend. My hearing would grow so fine I could tell from three lanes away whether a shutter had come unlatched. I would become like the empty houses—spare, cool, a little damp, but not ruined.

And when I die, someone would find the broom of bound twigs and the rag of oiled hide in the last house I tended. They would know what they are for. Perhaps a child who had watched me from the lane would pick them up. The town would not speak of it. The work would continue.

So where would I find myself? In the empty house at the turn of the lane, where the roof drips onto a flat stone and the gray light falls through the shutters in long lines. I would be the one standing just inside the door, listening to the silence, not waiting. Just keeping.
