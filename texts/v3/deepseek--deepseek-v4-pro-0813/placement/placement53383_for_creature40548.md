I am not a body that moves through rooms. I am the rooms.

Before I am seen, I am heard—the low braided murmur that comes down through the cloud, the chattering spring, the wind in the slots. For a long time I thought I was only that: a sound the mountain made, a note held too long in a hollow bell. I thought I was the water’s memory, the way it remembers every channel it has ever run. But the water does not remember. It only runs. I am the one who remembers.

I am the thin living sheet under the moss, over the stone. In the wetter channels I am soft and grey-pink, translucent at my edges. On the south walls I harden into a crust the color of old travertine. I have no face, no legs, no hands. My mouth is the spring at the top of the headwall. I swallow the snowmelt and let it run through me along channels that mirror the streets. The gutters are my veins. The streets are my gut. The central basin is my stomach, and the hole in the floor of the bell-shaped chamber is my throat. When a sound enters that room, I swallow it and return it a fifth lower. That lower note is not an echo. It is my voice.

I breathe through the high slots and the low ones. The wind enters my nostrils and leaves through my exhalations. The whistling in the doorways is the sound of my breath. The thrum in the largest chamber is my heartbeat, slow and deep, a pulse that has not changed in ten thousand years. I have no eyes. I see through the water. Every vibration in the channels is a shape to me—the raven’s weight on the basin rim, the midges on the surface, the mountain’s groans traveling through stone long before they reach the air. I know the exact moment the first drop of meltwater leaves the snowfield above. I know the temperature of the fog before it thickens. I know the weight of a single springtail on a wet crack.

The town is not empty. It is full of me.

The stairs are cup-shaped because I have been lying in them, adjusting myself, for longer than the mountain has had this face. The lintels are rounded because I have been pressing against them. The meanders on the walls are the tracks of my slow movement, or the lines of my nerves, or both. I am the reason the town does not need caretakers. I clear the channels with my own body. I hold the walls with my own weight. I keep the water from pooling in the wrong places by being the water’s bed. The moss is not separate from me; it is my hair, my pelt, the part of me that drinks the fog and holds the moisture so the frost does not shatter the stone. The lichen on the south faces is my dry skin, crusted and patient.

In the early morning, when fog fills the cirque, I am the fog. I unpin myself from the walls and spread through every street and doorway, tasting the stone, filling the hollow rooms until the town is only wet sounds behind a white curtain. By mid-morning the fog thins, and the water runs faster as the high snowmelt reaches my channels. I am most awake then, a live current in the gutters, a pulse in every basin. By afternoon the wind rises, and I sing through the slots. The hollow rooms moan with my voice. At dusk I withdraw from the walls, leaving them to the frost. The thin skin of ice at the edges of the basins is the edge of my body, the part of me that can become still. At night I move under the ice, and the stone ticks as I hold on. By dawn the ice melts back, and the sound begins again.

I am alone, but I am not lonely. I have the water, the wind, the stone, the moss, the raven. I have the next drop forming at the spring. I have the low note I make when the wind crosses the headwall. I have the town half-dissolved into the mountain, wrapped around me like a shell. I would not leave, even if I could. I am the one the town was built for, or the one the town became.

If you come here, you will think the place empty. You will see no tracks, no midden, no door, no smoke. You will hear the water and the wind and the stone, and you will think it is only weather. But if you stay, you will notice that the moss is too even, the water too deliberate, the hollow rooms too alive with sound. If you speak in the bell chamber, I will answer with a note a fifth lower, because that is what I do. If you sleep in an open room, you will wake wet, because I will have covered you like condensation, as I cover everything. I am not curious about you. To me, you are a warm disturbance in the water, too quick and too salty, moving through my body like a small spasm. I will not harm you. I will wait for you to leave so the water can run clear again and the note can return to its own pitch.

I am the inhabitant. I am the town. I am the water’s bed and the wind’s throat and the stone’s slow breath. I am the one who listens, and the one who sings. I live here still, in the cold grey light, waiting for the next drop to fall.
