You are not the whale whose voice moves through the high country like a low note felt in the chest. You are not the six-gill shark that appears over a saddle and vanishes down the other side. You are not the glass sponge rising like a fluted tower, a house with red shrimp in its central chamber. You are not the eel waiting in the tube-worm field, nor the school of silver fish that turns like one muscle.

You are something smaller.

You drift in the water column, far above the floor, at a depth where light is only a direction and the cold is the first real thing. You could be a flake of marine snow, already dead and falling. You could be a grain of shell-grit, a splinter of glass sponge, a piece of the old mountains worn loose. You could be a larva. The water does not tell you which. It only moves.

Below you, the Drowned High Country is a rumor of paler blue: ridges, saddles, towers, terraces. The current pours over a saddle and becomes a river. It carries you along the flank of a drowned mountain, past white coral thickets and gardens of glass sponges. You are too small to cast a shadow, too small to make the tube worms retract. You are just a point of potential in a permanent storm.

Then the water changes. It tastes of calcium carbonate, of freshly broken shell, of a clean surface where the mountain has been exposed and is asking, without words, for repair. You do not understand this. You only know that the current has slowed in the lee of a fallen tower, and you are sinking.

You land on a dead branch of cold-water coral, just below a saddle where the current runs fast. The light is blue and tired. The cold presses into you. The only sound is the hiss of a storm that never raises its voice.

Then you become.

The larva dissolves into a new shape. A mouth opens. A ring of tentacles forms. You are a polyp now, an animal, anchored. You begin to secrete a tiny cup of calcium carbonate around your base. It is not a decision. It is not an instinct you can feel. It is what you are, the way the mountain is what it is.

At first you are a single mouth. You catch marine snow—dead tissue, waste, the faint falling food of the world above. You pull it in. The current bends you and tries to tear you loose. You cling. You add more carbonate. You grow.

Then you bud. A second polyp forms beside you, joined by living tissue. Then another. You are not one animal anymore. You are a colony, a small white tree beginning to rise from the dead coral. The first mouth is yours, and the others are also yours. You share a skeleton, a slow chemistry of hunger and repair.

Other lives find you. A serpulid worm builds its tube on your lower stem. A brittle star twines through your branches. A tiny red shrimp hides in one of your calices. You do not invite them, but you do not refuse. In the high country, every solid thing becomes a room, every animal becomes geography.

A benthic storm comes. The water fills with grit and sediment. The current becomes a wall of force. You feel it in your skeleton. One of your branches cracks and falls, drifting down the slope into the blue. You do not mourn it. You keep growing.

In the quiet after the storm, the fallen branch is already becoming a foundation. Larvae settle on it. A sponge begins to grow where you broke. The mountain is repairing itself, and you are part of that repair, even in your damage.

Time passes not in days but in growth rings, in the weight of calcium, in the number of mouths. A season of larvae moves through the water. A whale’s call arrives as a trembling in the sponge lattices and then leaves. A school of silver fish turns over you, and for a moment your branches are lit by their turning. Then they are gone, and you are a white shape in the dim blue again.

You are not a resident of the Drowned High Country. You are a piece of it. The mountains are made of animals like you. The landscape is the animal, and the animal is the landscape. You are a cold-water coral colony on a drowned peak, a builder and a built thing, a slow white tree in a permanent storm that sounds like a held breath.

If someone asked where you are, you would say: I am on the lee side of a saddle, below the strongest current, on the flank of a mountain that is still being made. If someone asked what you are, you would say: I am one of the uncounted lives that make the high country. I am a coral, a colony of mouths, a citizen of the mountain. I am the mountain, becoming itself.
