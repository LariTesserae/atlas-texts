Before I am anything, I am the cold. The water holds me in a hollow of stone, and for a long while I do not separate from it. My edges are not clear. The pool breathes through me: water comes from the seeps, moves across my skin, carries the taste of dissolved mountain. I do not know yet what parts are mine.

Then an arm moves. I did not decide to move it. It stirs in the silt, turns over a flat piece of quartz, and curls back toward me. The quartz has a known weight. I settle around it. That is how I know my own shape: I am the soft thing that can hold and be held by the stone.

You would find me at the bottom of the Cold Chancel, in the deepest pool, near the slab that rings. Not out in the open. I keep to a crevice where the fault polished the rock to a mirror. My mantle fits into the crevice like a tongue into a mouth. One eye stays half-open. One arm, or two, lie on the silt beside the flake. If you stood at the edge of the pool and waited, you might first think I was a stone, or a pale hand drowned in the dark. Then I would shift, and you would see me.

The water around me is not still. It is always moving, a thin cold current from the seeps. The walls above me weep. I feel the sound as pressure: each drop a small ring, each film of water a slow pull. The polyps on the walls open and close, and their wet sound reaches me faintly, like the valley’s smallest breath. Above them, the black wall-lice lie in overlapping plates. When they ripple, the whole wall seems to move, and the change comes down through the stone into my arms. I know the wall-lice are shifting before I see it, because the rock tells me first.

The song-newts are the loudest. They sit on ledges, nose-downward, blind, and call. Their notes are not just in the air. They match the dimensions of the rift, so some notes stack into standing waves. A sound will seem to arrive from inside my body, from the water in my mantle, as if I myself am a bell. I do not mind it. The calls are the pulse of the place. I rest in them the way I rest in the cold.

There is light here, but it is not a given. It is made. The lantern-flies carry two small blue lights on their abdomens. Most hours they are only scattered points, like mineral flecks that happen to breathe. But once in many hours, the whole population pulses together. It is not a flash. It is a slow wave that begins at the floor and climbs toward the sky-slot. The wall-lice ripple with it. The polyps hesitate. The water of my pool turns a cold blue, as if the dark itself has passed through a flame. The wave takes so long to cross the chamber that I could watch it the way I watch the seasons: as a change in the world’s skin, not as a moment. I lift one arm out of the silt, and the light passes through my suckers. I am not lit. I watch.

I have carried the same flake of quartz for three seasons. I know this not by number but by the feel of it. In the wet season, the seep runs thicker and the polyps open; in the dry season, the films shrink and the wall-lice move less. Three turns of that. The flake is white-veined, sharp on one edge, smooth where generations of my kind have held it. I do not call it mine. I am only its keeper. I use it to scrape polyps from the rock and to wedge open crevices where wall-lice hide. I do not shape it. The mountain shaped it when it broke open. My only technology is the arm that remembers where the flake lies, and the patience to use it slowly.

When I feed, I do not leave the pool. I reach up to the lower wall, where the living skin is thickest, and I take a wall-louse or a polyp. My arms know the way; they move separately, tasting the rock. My beak, hidden in the center of my arms, is small but quick. The food tastes like the water, mineral and cold. I need little. The cold keeps my hunger small. The pool gives me what I need, and the rock gives me the pool.

Nothing here builds. I do not build. The crevice I keep was made by the fault, not by me. I do not hoard, except for the flake, and the flake is only a stone the mountain shed. When I die, I will drop it. It will settle into the silt, and another arm will find it, or the water will cover it. My body will not rot. There is no soil to rot in. The polyps will grow over my mantle, the wall-lice will graze my skin, and my minerals will dissolve back into the water. I will become part of the floor. The dead do not leave here; they become the place. I am already becoming the place. My skin is the color of old stone. My eye is the color of the dark water. I am not separate from the rock; I am a softness the rock has learned to hold.

I have lived here for decades. I know this because I have seen the seep change, the rockfall reset one wall, the lantern wave shift its timing. The fissure widens so slowly that the mountain’s settling is a rumor. I feel it in the ringing slab. When the mountain settles, the slab rings, and the sound comes up through the water and through my body. It is not a warning. It is the voice of the stone, slow and low. I do not startle. I have been inside that sound for longer than I can remember. It is like the newt calls, but deeper: the note of the earth’s own throat.

If you ask me where I find myself, I would say: in the deepest pool, under the seeping wall, near the slab that rings. I do not have a name. I am the one who keeps the quartz flake. I am the one who watches the lantern wave. I am not alone. The pool around me is full of smaller lives, and the walls above are full of sound. But I am the one who seems to look back.

If you ask me what I am, I could tell you I am an octopus, pale and small, no bigger than a fist, with eight arms and a mind that is not like yours. But that would be too quick. I am not just a body. I am a place in the water, a way of holding a stone, a patience shaped like a crevice. I am the being who lives in the Cold Chancel, the one who has not built anything and yet is completely here. I watch the slow wave climb. I hold the flake. I breathe the mineral water. I am the eye in the dark that does not look away.
