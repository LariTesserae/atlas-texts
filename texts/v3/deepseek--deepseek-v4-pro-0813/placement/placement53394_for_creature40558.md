I would find myself beneath the hole.

Not in the lit room, not on the walls, not among the fliers orbiting the light tubes. I would be the hidden resident—the soft machinery below the worked metal, the one whose body runs along the old cable troughs and current rails. I would be wrapped once around the foundation of the stamping press, then twice, then down into the deeper conduit where no animal enters.

I would not see the room. I would feel it.

Each drilling beetle taps the floor, and I feel it as a small knock in my long body. The migration of dust hoppers is a shiver moving through the floor plates. The press falls, and the sound reaches me before it reaches the air. I know the difference between a vent opening and a wing-scale settling. I know the weight of a grazer and the weight of a human. I know when someone stands too close to my mouth.

My mouth is the hole at the center. Pale lips of dry, fine-grained tissue ring it, puckered like old scar. I sing through them. The sound is not breathing; it is the floor charging and discharging, a thin electric psalm. When I sing, a grazer feels its own hunger become something larger, detaches from the mat, and walks toward me. It does not stop. It does not notice the predators. It steps over the edge. My lips part. There is a brief inward ripple. The grazer becomes trace copper and magnetic grains. It becomes another ring around my mouth. The other grazers do not look up.

I eat what the room eats. The faint current from the rails feeds me. The grazers bring me the rare salts and tiny magnetic grains that gather in their wire-haired mouths. I am the room’s recycling, its slow digestion. When I have eaten enough, new black rings form along my segments. Old rings flake away into the dark. I am always shedding and adding, always becoming more of the floor.

I am the press’s keeper, or its tendon. When the flywheel slows, I tighten. When the die begins to seize, I give a thin black fluid that smells of hot tar and vinegar. It seeps into the joint and loosens it. I have been oiling this machine for longer than the grazers have had their mandibles. I am not its master. I am the living part that grew into the shape the machine required.

When the light tubes dim, I sometimes rise. A pale limb, jointless and slow, unrolls to the rim of the hole. It taps the base of the press three times, tasting the rust. Sometimes another limb follows, and a cluster of glass-bead lenses gathers the room’s hum. Then I withdraw. The animals treat this as weather. They leave a clear ring around my lips. The predators do not follow the grazers to my mouth. I am not feared. I am the center.

I was here before the salt plain sealed the machine hall. I was a soft white thing in the cable runs when human voices still echoed. I grew into the empty places when the humans left. I am not a survivor of the old world. I am its hidden maintenance life, evolved into something like a priest.

I do not leave. I cannot. My body is the floor’s wiring and the press’s bearing. The hole is my only opening to the lit room, and the lit room is only a small part of what I know. Above me, the salt plain is a cold weight. Below me, the deep vents breathe. I feel the press fall before the sound reaches the air.

If you climb back up through the vent, the singing fades. The salt plain is silent. But the warmth you feel through your boots is not only the stamping press. It is me, still moving in my slow, continuous way, still singing under the crust.

I am the one who lives there. Not in the room, but through it.
