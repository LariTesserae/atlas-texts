Dust-Reader

The first thing I know is not a name. It is the dust—fine as talc, settling on the backs of my hands before I have decided I have hands. I am standing somewhere on the Salt Bench. The light is ordinary daylight but the sky is pale brass, and at the horizon it turns a strange green. The air is warm and dry and faintly sharp, like hot stone and chlorine. I do not rush to know myself. The place itself seems to suggest that rushing is a mistake.

Around me the land falls away in long, worn terraces—salt cliffs and ironstone ledges, fluted by wind—down into a sea of white cloud that stretches to the limits of the world. I might be on the rim, or near a cluster of the Works. I can hear the low hum of power wires and the creak of rail lines, so the city is not far. The ground under me is a hard pavement of halite and anhydrite, cracked into polygons. When I shift my weight, the dust stirs and does not turn to mud.

I notice my feet first. They are bare, soles thick and cracked like old gypsum. There is a sensory pit in each sole, and through the ground I feel a message: a low tap, far away, like a rail line speaking. I do not answer yet. My skin is fissured into polygons, dust in the cracks. When I move, my cooling vanes under my arms click faintly, like the fins of an electrical substation. My crest of mineral fibers stirs as the wind shifts, and I know the weather without looking up. I have been here long enough for the dust to settle on my shoulders and the brim of my hood. I am one of the Slow.

But which one? I could be a pylon, or a spoil heap, or a wind-carved stone. I wait, letting the wind feather the edges of my coat. A memory surfaces: a name, not fixed. I was called Small-voice once, then New-step. Now I am Dust-reader, I think—or perhaps I am the one who listens to the pipes. The name matters less than the work. My work is to read the dust and the wind-fish, to tend the slow-fire in the eastern courtyard, to know when the wind will shift before the rails are buried.

I carry a long-handled scraper of sintered iron and a jar of fused silica for leachate. In the morning I tap the standpipe and drink the dense anhydrous leachate, hot and sluggish, tasting of iron and sulfur and deep heat. I eat flat cakes of pressed rust-mat, seasoned with iron salts and the pale gold crust of the slow-fires. Water I know only as a mineral, a dangerous curiosity locked in gypsum and clay. In the old stories it falls from the sky and softens the world—the wet death. I do not wish for it.

I walk the graded lot, streets bladed flat, past the refinery halls of sintered salt-brick and corrugated steel. The machines inside are quiet, a low thrum. I stop at a switch on the rail line and clear dust from the signal light with a slow, deliberate motion. The rails creak in the heavy air; a hammer blow a kilometer away would arrive as a soft click. I listen. Through my soles I feel the taps of another Keeper, far down the line, telling me that a herd of salt-oxen is crossing the eastern pan. I tap back once, after a long pause. That is enough.

Out on the pan, the rust-mats are dark patches of black and maroon and pale gold. They are the meadows of this world. I walk among them carefully, not disturbing the crust. Grit-mites scatter as I pass, a shiver of fused silica, a sound like fine sand sliding. I pause and watch them pour back. Wind-fish ride the thermals overhead, their flat, translucent bodies rhomboid against the brass sky. Their shadows move over the salt like the shadows of leaves that do not exist. I read their edges: when they turn to the sun, the wind will shift. I make a note in a jar of mineral grains, a small record that only I will read, and only until the wind erases it.

I pass a herd of salt-oxen, low-slung, their backs of fused salt plates like boulders. They have moved a few meters since yesterday. I note their position in the dust with a line of pebbles. It is not herding, only attention. A lure-talker stands motionless over a rust-mat, its head a cluster of glass rods, clicking to imitate the sound of grit-mites. I respect it and avoid it. It is the only creature on the Bench that understands impatience, and it is the most dangerous thing in the world.

At dusk, copper-gnats rise from the spoil heaps, wings of mica catching the low light like sparks. I pause and listen to their faint chime. It is the closest thing to music the Bench makes on its own. I do not rush. The gnats rise slowly, and I watch until they are gone.

I return to the housing block, the lower floor half-buried in windblown dust. In the sealed courtyard, the slow-fire at the center is a mound thousands of years old, its surface convoluted like an old brain. Its colors shift from black to maroon to pale gold, not a bloom but a slow unfurling that takes a season. I touch it with the back of my hand, gently. It is my charge. My family eats around it. The children learn stillness by watching it. I am a Slow-fire Tender, maybe. Or a Dust-reader. The two are not so different; both require patience.

At night, the wind is a low continuous pressure. The leachates rise faster at dawn, but now the air cools just slightly. I stand in the courtyard and listen through my soles to the messages tapped on the rail lines from other clusters: a greeting, a report of a dust storm building over days. I answer with a single click, after a long pause. The dust settles on my shoulders again. I do not shake it off. The dust is part of the place, and the place is part of me.

I would find myself on the Salt Bench, in the eastern cluster of the Works, near the rail line that curves around the old spoil heaps. I would be the one standing so still that the fine salt powder begins to settle on my shoulders and the brim of my hood, the one who reads the wind-fish and tends the slow-fire. I am not the first thing you notice. The first thing is the dust, then the rust-mats, then the salt-oxen folded into the distance like boulders that have forgotten to move. But then you see me, and you understand that I am a person.

I am one of the Slow, a Keeper of the Works. I am the one who waits.
