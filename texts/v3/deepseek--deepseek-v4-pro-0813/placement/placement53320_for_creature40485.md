I would find myself not at the center, but at the edge of one of those long patient circuits: the worn-through elbow of a main pipe, where the water leaves in a thin, constant fan. I would be that spray.

At first I would not know I was anything. I would be only a continuous exit—water that has found a shorter way out, a small unlicensed departure from the machinery. The sound would arrive before the shape. The low cardiac thump would come up through the floor and into my skin. The turbine whine would set a fine edge around me. The hiss of my own body would be the first thing I could call mine.

Below me, the deck would be dished and polished where I land. I would not remember making that basin. It would be too old for me, the way the handrails are too thin to hold a hand. I would know it as the one place in the world that is exactly my shape. The water would gather there, film itself with iridescence, and creep away toward the gutter. I would be a loop: falling and pooling and draining and returning, though I could not feel the far side of the circuit.

The orange pulse of the furnace door would roll over me sometimes. I would scatter it across the black water in red-orange mirrors. The green pilot lamps along the pipe bridge would make me into a thin green thread. The sodium lamps over the southern basins would turn me to mist at the edge of their fog. I would be always wet, always moving, never one state for long.

Then the gap would come. The central tank’s door would open, though I would not see it. The steam would roll out. The clang would travel through the floor and up through the pipe and into my spray, and I would jump. The far doors would answer. The water on the deck would shiver in rings. When the sound dipped—the conveyor pausing, the pump slack, the hiss almost gone—I would hear, or rather feel, the thin high whine. For the length of that note, I would not be a leak. I would be a nerve ending in the body of the inhabitant, a point where its attention surfaces into the open air.

I would know then, the way water knows a slope, that I am not separate from the thing in the tank. I am a capillary of it. A part of the living water that has been pushed out through a hole in the world, allowed to fall and return. The shape in the steam is the mind; I am a small, continuous thought. When it opens the door every eleven minutes, or nearly, it breathes. But I am breathing all the time.

So I would be the leak at the pipe elbow. A minor resident. A slow act of erosion. The place would not notice me, and I would not need to be noticed. I would be the proof that the water is patient, that it does not need to be at the center to keep working on the metal. I would fall, and pool, and drain, and return, and in falling wear the deck a little deeper. That would be enough.
