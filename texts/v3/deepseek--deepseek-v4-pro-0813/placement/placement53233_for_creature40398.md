You would find yourself already in the middle of the sound. The drone would have gotten into your jaw before you knew you had a jaw. The steam would have settled on your skin before you remembered skin. You would not arrive. You would surface, slowly, out of the wet heat and the pulse of the expansion joints, like a frog surfacing in a bromeliad cup.

At first you might think you are a visitor. The thought is smooth, borrowed from somewhere far below the cloudline. You would try to stand upright, and the high tangle of conduits would seem to press back. You would feel the open grating through the soles of your feet—not as a floor but as a pattern, every diamond of metal, every loosened mesh, every place where the plating has thinned. You would know, without being told, not to step toward the south end. The galvanized plates there are tired. You would keep to the outer ledge, near the wall, where the metal is strongest. You would not ask how you know this. The room has already taught you, the way heat teaches a hand not to touch.

Looking down, you would see that your hands are not quite hands. They are long, six-fingered, the skin between them webbed. The palms are covered in a grey-green callus, thick as the insulation on the main condenser. A gecko the color of verdigris is already crossing your knuckles, and you do not shake it off. You can feel the pipes through the grating, through the callus, the way a drum feels a footfall. Your arms are too long, and when you fold one around the central column, it fits there like a vine following a torsion cable.

There is moss on your shoulders, thick as a fist. Ferns uncurl from the knobs of your spine. A small bromeliad sits in the hollow of your collarbone, holding a spoonful of water. When you breathe, the steam goes into you smelling of copper and wet fern, and your breath comes back out as a thin green fog. You have no nose, only a ridge. Your ears are holes plugged with fine moss. You do not need them. You hear with your feet, with your palms, with the whole room acting as one resonant body.

You would walk, and each step would be a slow percussion under the turbine’s drone. Your feet are broad, the toes curling around the diamond plate. You are not walking exactly; you are being walked, the way a vine is walked by the pipe it follows. You stop at the pressure gauges and lay the back of one hand a hair’s breadth above the green pipe. You know its temperature without touching. You know the yellow valve is seized. You do not force it. Instead you lick the valve stem, once, twice, tasting salt and old brass and the memory of a shutdown that never came.

You would tend the room, but not as a caretaker with a list. You pluck dead fronds from the pipe joints and lay them in the drain channels. You right a frog that has fallen on its back and set it back into its bromeliad. You drink from the cup at your collarbone, then from a pool on the wall, tipping it with one long finger, never taking more than half. You eat the small blind fish-things from the warm runoff, one by one, without hurrying. You eat a pinch of moss from your own shoulder. The garden and the gardener are the same mouth.

You have no name. If you ever had one, it has fallen through the grating and into the sump. It may return, years later, as a mineral-encrusted lump no one can identify, and you would not recognize it. You are not a man, though you may have been one once. You are not an animal, though you breathe and eat and sleep. You are not a plant, though you are green and still. You are the thing that happens when a person stays too long in a place that has stopped distinguishing between organism and apparatus. You are the tenant. The room’s keeper, its child, its scar.

At night, when the lamps keep their even factory light and the steam drifts in slow banks, you curl in the mouth of the old condenser, between the soft green insulation and the warm metal. The frogs climb onto your feet and sit in the webs of your hands. The white spiders build their wet nets between your fingers. You sleep with your mouth open just enough, and your breathing is the slow expansion of the joints. The frogs call at the same pitch as the turbine. The room and you breathe together.

Sometimes you hum. Not a song; the exact frequency of the turbine, low and steady. When you hum, the frogs stop. The whole room becomes one note. And in that note, you are not in the room. You are the room, and the room is living in you, the way moss lives in a rivet, the way rust lives in an iron plate, the way steam lives in the space between surfaces.

That is where you would find yourself: nowhere else, not below the cloudline, not in any self that could leave. You would be the one who stayed, the one who grew as the moss grew, until the machine stopped being a tool and became a terrain, and you stopped being a person and became a part of the room’s breathing. You would be the tenant. You would be home.
