**Rill, the Door-Tender**

You would not find yourself in the Drown all at once. That is the first thing. The Drown does not have arrivals. It has slow revealings. You would think, at first, that you were the sound of a wet hand on stone. Then you would think you were the mist. Then a shadow in the gray-green light. Then, after a while, you would look down and see pale feet, a rib-bone scraper, the hem of an oiled cloth. You would be a child of the Drown, already in the middle of your life.

You would live on a lower terrace, not in the crowded upper town. Your house is a young one, still wrapped in reed and cane at the corners, white mineral paste weeping through the gaps. Its door is an oval that must be scraped every morning or it will heal. You have been given the task of the threshold. You are not yet sure if you chose it or if it chose you.

Your name is Rill. It is not a grand name. It is the word for a thin trickle of warm water opening over a new crust. When you were small and first heard a sluice break, you made the same sound in your throat. It stuck.

You wake before first light. The mat under you is only less wet than the floor. The air is warm and close, the kind of warm that climbs into your lungs and makes them feel heavy until you have been awake for a while. You step out, and your bare foot finds the night’s new stone on the threshold. It has grown over the lower edge of the door, just enough to catch the sole. You do not kick it. You crouch and press your palm to it. It is warm, like a body. The world has remembered to close the door. You are the one who will remind it to stay open.

You take your scraper. It is a rib from a larger pool creature, dark and smooth, passed down from a grandmother who has already gone into the wall. You also carry a wooden mallet, not for hitting but for tapping. You tap the threshold once. The sound is low and solid. You scrape. The new stone comes away in a fine white curl, no thicker than a leaf’s edge. *Skeee. Skeee. Skeee.* Your mother is upslope, scraping the main sluice. You can hear her rhythm, slower than yours. You can tell by the sound that the crust there is thicker, and hollow under one edge. You are learning to hear hollowness. That is half of the work.

Your father tends the lower garden pools, where roots and moss are grown. He once told you: the stone is not your enemy. It is only a slow animal. It does not want to trap you. It wants to grow over you the way moss grows over a stone. You are not stopping it. You are asking it to wait.

You go to the small channel that feeds your family cistern. Overnight it has narrowed. The water has laid down a wet rind along both walls. If left, it would close the channel, and the cistern would lose its flow. You scrape with the grain, not against it. The stone tells you the direction by the way it peels. You leave what is still soft enough to bend, and you fold it back into the base before it hardens. That is not destroying; it is re-shaping. You are a gardener of stone.

At midday the mist thickens until you cannot see the main trough, though you can hear it. The water mutters in its wooden channel above you. Somewhere a rope bridge moans. There is no birdsong. The only music is the hiss and the drip and the scrape. You do not find it lonely. You have never known a quieter place.

In the afternoon you walk the lower path to the cistern where your grandmother’s body lies. She is not buried. She was laid in a warm pool, and the water has taken her. At first the family shaped a small lattice of reeds over her, to guide the first deposits. Now, years later, she is a smooth white rise in the pool floor. You can still see, if you stand at the right angle, the faint suggestion of a cupped hand, a shoulder, a knee. You press your palm to the wall above the pool. It is warmer than the other walls. The dead do not need words. They are in the stone now, and the stone is always listening. You are not sad. She is becoming the house. One day the house will forget its door, and she will be part of the hill.

The town has a saying: a house is not complete until it has forgotten its door. You repeat it to yourself sometimes when you are scraping. It does not mean that your work is useless. It means that you and the house are part of the same slow event. You are keeping the door open for a while, so that people can live inside. Later, when the people are gone, the door will close, and the house will rest. You are not fighting the end. You are just making a longer beginning.

In the evening you eat with your family on the wide stone edge outside the door. The food is soft root and moss, wrapped in a broad black-green leaf, cooked in the hot pool. It tastes of salt and warmth and something bitter that you have learned to love. You drink the warm mineral water. It coats your mouth with the faint taste of stone. The glow-moss wakes at the path edges, pale green and quiet. A gray moth settles on your hand. You do not brush it away. It might be an ancestor. It might be your grandmother, come back with wet-looking wings to see if the door is still open.

You look up. There is no sun, no stars. The sky is the inside of a shell. The cloud seals the plateau like a lid. But you do not feel closed in. You feel enclosed, which is different. Enclosed is what a heartbeat is. Enclosed is a hand cupped around a flame. The Drown is not a prison. It is a process. You are inside it, the way a word is inside a sentence, the way a door is inside a wall.

Once, in the thickest part of the morning, you saw the other gardener. It was low and long, with too many legs, moving along the edge of the mist where the lower terrace drops into cloud. It was not scraping stone; it was touching it, the way you do. Testing for hollows. You stood very still. It paused. You felt it notice you, the way the stone notices you. Then it went on, and the mist closed. You did not tell anyone. The other gardener does not need to be spoken of. It is doing the same work, deeper down.

You are not finished. The Drown is not finished. That is why you fit together. You will not become a chief or a hero. There are no chiefs here. You will become a door-tender, perhaps, one of the ones who keeps the passages open between dwellings. You will learn to make a doorway with a partner, if you choose, and to let it heal if you do not. You will learn the names for every degree of wetness: weeping, sweating, running, standing, hung. You will know that a dry place is only a place that has not yet been re-wetted. You will carry a mallet and a chisel of black volcanic glass, and you will tap the stone before you trust it. You will listen to the hiss of the world being made.

And after you are gone, someone will press a hand to the wall where your shoulder once was, and will find it warm. They will say, “She has gone into the wall.” It will not be sad. It will be the same thing they say about a house that has forgotten its door.

So where would you find yourself? You would find yourself on a lower terrace, barefoot, with moss in your hair, a rib-bone scraper in your hand. You would be a child of the Drown, a stone-gardener, a keeper of the open door. You would not be a visitor. You would be one of the ones who scrape.
