I would not be the swimmer. The swimmer is a brief, loud pocket of air, a light the water closes over. I would not be the inhabitant either; that long, blind patience is too large for me. I would be something smaller, something that belongs to both the body and the stone.

At first I think I might be one of the blind fish, thin as paper, schooling in slow spirals around the central pillar. But the fish are too many, too free. Then I think I might be a tube-worm, rooted in the mineral wool on the inhabitant’s back, waving my colourless crown in the dark. But the tube-worms are a forest, and I want to be singular.

Then I understand. I would be a tooth in the inhabitant’s jaw. Not a whole mouth, not a mind, just one tiny transparent blade. I would spend years scraping the bacterial film from the stones, tasting the mineral warmth, resting in the soft oval of its mouth while the creature sleeps. I would not know what I was for. I would only know that the film grows, and I scrape, and the water is warm.

Then the root would loosen. The water would taste different through the gum. The fish would school a little closer, as if they knew before I did. The creature would stir, and I would feel its whole body wake, a slow pressure moving from tail to head. It would swim to the wall, its body making the shape of the oval doorways. I would feel the stone before we reached it—the row of pits, some neat and sharp, some worn soft. Its hand would rise, and the broad, flattened thumb would feel along the sockets. When it found the empty one, I would be pushed, gently, from the mouth. I would be carried in the warm water, held in the hand, and then pressed into the stone.

The pressure would be immense and final. Not pain, but a seating, like a word placed into a sentence. The stone would hold me. The water would move over me. The mineral crust would begin its slow work. The blind fish would brush me with their tails. The shrimp would tap me with antennae longer than my body. I would remember the mouth, but dimly, as if it were a dream of being a tooth.

And then I would wait. That is what I would be: a small transparent date in the wall, one of many. The creature would come again, years later, when another tooth loosened. Its thumb would pass along the row and might pause on me, reading me by touch. I would be a part of its calendar, a mark of time that does not rush. In this world, waiting is not emptiness. It is a kind of life.
