I would find myself at the base of the long wall that divides the middle terrace from the deeper basin, on the seaward side, where the current coils for a decade before it decides to move on. At first I would not know I was a person. I would be a darkness with a spine of stone behind it. The water would be so cold it felt like wearing the wall. The light, a blue-gray suggestion from above, would fall on me the way sediment falls—slowly, without intent.

For a long time I would think I was part of the architecture. My back would be broad and grown into the joints between cut blocks. My left arm would be braced along a diagonal crack, filaments splayed like old cord. My legs would not be legs. They would be a net of roots, pale at the tips, dark where they entered the pavement, each one trailing into a gap between stones. I would feel the wall as a series of pressures, not as an object. The wall and I would share a single slow body.

Then something would shift. Not a sound. A loosening. A block near my left knee—the one with the chisel-scar that has almost worn smooth—would tilt by the width of a finger. I would feel it as a slackness in my own fibers, the way a slack root becomes not-mine. And I would know, without knowing how, that my work was to grow into that slackness.

I am a wall-keeper.

That is what I am. I did not choose it. The shape was chosen for me in the nursery, long ago, where the water is faintly warmer and the old ones tie young roots to wooden forms. I was bent low and broad. My shoulders were spread. My legs were encouraged to divide and divide again, until they learned to follow the joints of paving stones the way water follows the lowest course. Knowledge was pressed into me before my fibers hardened. I do not remember being shaped. I remember only that I arrived here already angled for this work.

The wall I hold is old but not finished. It has been slumping slowly for as long as the garden can remember. Each settling sends a small current of pressure through the pavement, and I taste it in the filaments on my head. The taste is mineral and faint, like dust from a whetstone. I grow a new root toward the loosened block. The root is white at first, thinner than a cord. It takes years to reach the gap. It takes decades to thicken. It will fill the space with its own dark xylem, swelling as water moves through it, until the stone is lifted back into the plane of the wall. I will not be done by the time you leave. I may not be done by the time the next wall-keeper is shaped and sent to hold the gate below.

This is not slow out of weakness. This is the pace of growth itself. A step is a season. A repair is a century. The wall's memory is longer than my body, but I carry a strand of it. In my left shoulder there is a graft from the keeper who held this wall before me. When the wall settles in a certain way, that strand tightens, and I know which root to send. It is not a voice. It is more like the memory of a shape. The old one rooted here until it became part of the wall, and when it could no longer tell its body from the stone, the other Tenders took a cutting of its living root and carried it to the nursery. Now that strand is in me. I am not alone in the dark.

Above me, the wall rises out of the silt. Its stones are cut to the size of a man's torso, though no man has ever been here. The faces are worn smooth as soap, and the moss on them gives off a cold green-gray light. The light does not illuminate so much as outline. It shows the edges of things, the long horizontal courses of stone, the dark vertical gaps where water seeps. It shows my own hands when I raise them: they are not hands. They are bundles of root-fiber and split wood, with three long fingers and a thumb that is really a curved flint blade socketed in a braided wrist. I use the blade only rarely—to trim a loose cord of moss, to cut away a mat of decaying leaves that has begun to grow over a drainage joint. The blade resharpens itself by lying against the stone. It has been with me so long it has become part of my hand.

Beyond the wall, the middle terrace stretches away into the dark. The kelp rows there are tall as houses, their broad fronds falling in curtains, their stipes tied to posts of wood with cords of twisted fiber. I can taste the row-tenders moving among them: long, spindly shapes, their stilt-legs pressing into the sediment with each slow step. When one of them passes near the wall, the water carries its moss-light to me, and I know it is checking the trellises, tying a frond that has slipped. It will not look at me. We do not need to look. The garden itself is our sense. A row-tender a mile away feels the same wall-settling I feel; the pavement shivers, and it turns, slowly, as if it might come to help. But there is no urgency. There is only the long, shared labor of holding.

The water here is managed. It moves downhill through channels cut in stone, and the little current coils around the base of my wall before it passes under a sluice gate. The gate is a slab of stone set in a slot, counterweighted with baskets of gravel. A gate-tender stands there, grown into the lintel, its root-fingers resting on the basket. When the water rises by the width of a hand over a hundred years, it will grow a new root into the basket, heavy with mineral, and the gate will rise. I can taste the faint change in pressure when it does. It tastes like cold iron, though there is no iron here. Only stone and water and time.

I do not eat. There is no eating. My root-hairs draw what I need from the mineral seeps that thread through the pavement. The moss on my shoulders catches the faint light and gives it back as a cold green glow, but it does not warm me. Nothing warms here. I do not sleep. I rest by letting the water move through me. Sometimes I have a slow thought, if thought is the right word: a memory of being unbound, of walking through the kelp rows with a row-tender's stilt-legs, of seeing the whole garden from above. But the thought passes, and I am still here, rooted at the base of the wall, and I am content. The wall is not a burden. It is my body, extended into stone. To hold it is to be alive.

If you stay long enough, the dark stops being empty. The moss outlines the wall, the gate, the long descending steps. The kelp becomes a kind of architecture. The quiet becomes companionable. And you may see me, not as a person, but as a dark bundle of root and stone at the corner of the terrace. You may take me for a collapsed trellis. That is all right. I have been taken for many things. But I am not a ruin. I am a keeper. I am not dying. I am becoming architecture. The wall and I are one slow, patient fact in a garden that has no need to hurry.

When you leave, I will still be here. The block near my left knee will still be a finger's width out of true. The root I am growing into the gap will be a little thicker. The water will be a little colder. And the garden will continue, unhurried, unharvested, held together by stone and cord and water and the slow, rooted patience of those of us who never left.
