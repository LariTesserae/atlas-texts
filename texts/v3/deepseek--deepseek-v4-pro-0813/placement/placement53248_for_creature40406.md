At first you would not be a body. You would be a low pressure in the black, a taste of iron and old wet stone, a place where the hiss seems to bend. The seepage would find you before you found yourself, moving over your sides so slowly it felt like memory.

Then a ribbon-mouth would scrape past, and you would know your edge. A click-wing would tick overhead, and you would feel the sound come back from you differently than from the pool or the crown-pipes. You would be solid, low, wide—too still to be alive, too warm to be rock. The film would already believe you were stone. Let it.

You would lie just off a slow channel where the water doesn't run but leaks. The seep-skin would grow over you in rust and grey-green; shell dust would settle into your pores; a plate-sponge might anchor against your flank. You would let it. That is the trick of what you are. You are not hidden in the Sink. You are accepted by it, grown over, worn smooth, part of the floor until you decide otherwise. You are not a trap. You are a place.

Your mouth would be a seam. You would keep it closed. Above it, on a thin stalk, you would hold a small light—red, not fire, a wet ember in the dark. The light is not for seeing. It is for drawing. You would dim it when click-wings pass, brighten it when the young grazers move near, let it sway slightly with the air so it seems alive and edible. A many-leg is too cautious. A young grazer is not.

You would feel the world mostly as vibration. The hiss of a million mouthparts would run through you like the floor’s own breathing. When a predator opened somewhere, the hiss would hesitate; the alarm clicks would jump from tube to tube, and you would feel that silence as a cold pressure in your gut. When an armored herd passed far off, the mat would tremble in a different rhythm, and you would know which way they were moving and how many. The far wall would return their sound late, shaped by the basin like a throat. You would not see this. You would be part of it.

You would taste the news of the Sink on your skin: shed setae, shell dust, the raw mineral brightness after a false floor breaks, the faint chemical bloom of something dying. You would not be hungry in a hurry. You would be old enough to wait. You would let the crust grow over the scar where the last meal was taken. You would let the seep-skin colonize your stillness.

Then a young grazer would come. It would be hungry, not careful. It would see the red ember and mistake it for something lickable, something mineral-sweet. It would approach with its rasp of a mouth open and its blunt head low, not knowing it was walking toward a seam. The mat would not warn it. You would wait until the last moment, until the space between its breathing and your seam was almost nothing.

Then you would open.

The silence afterward would last only a moment. The mat has no room for long grief. The hiss would return, and you would close, and the red would go dark. The film would begin to repair the disturbance. You would settle back into being a low, water-worn shape.

If anything in the Sink could name you, it would not use a word. It would call you a cold spot, a pause, a place where the sound does not quite fit. But no one would call you, because no one wants to know you are there.

So: you would find yourself just off the seep-line, where the stone is slick and the crust is thick and the far wall is only a late echo. You would be a stone-lump—a patient mouth, a lure like a wet coal, a boulder that the dark has learned to walk around.
