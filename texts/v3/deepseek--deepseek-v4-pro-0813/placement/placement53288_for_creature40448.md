You would not know it at first. The wind would come first, the way it does—not as weather but as a full-body pressure, a chord that seems to rise from the ground instead of falling from the sky. You would be lying in a stone pocket, your spine curled against the limestone, and for a long moment you would think you were only another rounded block in the corridor, polished by the same wind, finished. Then a pulse would move along your side, too fast, too warm, and you would know you are an animal.

Your body would be small, round, nearly tailless. Your fur would be the color of weathered limestone, paler under the chin and belly. Your ears would be short, round, rimmed with pale hair. You would be a pika.

You would find yourself on the south-facing side of a deep corridor, where the wind does not so much blow as lean over the rim, leaving a pocket of still air near the wall. Your den would be a crack between two old blocks, just wide enough to turn around in, lined with dust and a few dry stems. Behind your ribs, the altitude would press. Above the rim, the sky would be dark blue even at noon.

Your body would already know the circuit. Out along the undercut wall, past the place where the stone has flaked into a small pale mound, over the sunning stone—a flat slab that holds the sun's heat even when the air stays cool—and down to the fog-grass patch at the mouth of the corridor, where the wind drops just enough for dew to linger. You would move in short, quick runs, pausing between them. You would never cross the wide pavement if you could help it. The open stone is not empty; it is the place where a shadow has no name but eagle.

You would gather fog-grass and saxifrage, cropping the tight cushions at the lee edges of the stones. You would spread the stems on the warm slab, not because you have a plan but because the warmth is a place where the dampness can leave them. Later you would carry them down into the dark, one stem at a time, to the hidden heap under the stone. That heap would be your only storehouse, your only architecture. It would smell of dried grass and dust and your own body.

Your voice would be a single high needle of sound, “eenh,” pitched not to blend with the wind but to cut across it. You would call from the sunning stone, and the call would leave you and travel down the corridor. Then, from other cracks, other needles would answer, and for a few seconds the stone would reveal that it is not as empty as it looks. The wind chord would go on underneath, the same low note in the bones, the same high ring at the edge of hearing, but your calls would be separate, small bright scratches on the surface of the sound.

Sometimes a marmot would stand upright at a deeper fissure, dark-capped and watchful, and give its descending whistle. You would freeze with a stem in your mouth. The choughs would lift from the rim, black shapes tilting into the pressure wave. You would not have to think; you would already be in the crack, and the shadow would pass over the sunning stone. Then the marmot would settle back, the choughs would return to their ledge, and you would go back to the grass.

You would drink from a black basin at the base of the wall, a pool that never quite dries. The water would taste of stone and faintly of moss. You would approach in the gray light, ears turning, and lower your head. The surface would be still except for the wind's fingerprint. On the bottom, small dark frogs would sit half-submerged, only their eyes and a strip of back above the water. You would not disturb them, and they would not disturb you. The water would be shared, like the chord.

The pale night would not be true dark. The sky would hold the light long after the sun goes behind the western icefield, and then it would return before you are fully asleep. The cold would condense on every surface, and the plateau would sweat. You would lie in your crack and feel the dampness gather on your whiskers, on the fine hairs of your ears. The wind would not stop. The stone would not stop humming. You would sleep and not sleep, because the chord never lets the body go all the way down.

In that state, you might see your own life as a small loop: crack, corridor, sunning stone, fog-grass, haypile, black basin. Not progress, but maintenance. The same circuit, repeated until the stone itself seems to expect you. The little path from your crack to the grass would be worn into the rock dust, not by you alone but by all the ones before you. The haypile would be your only monument, and it would not outlast the season.

After a while, the question of who you are would stop mattering. You would be a pika. You would be one of the small, old-fashioned mammals fitted into the plateau's gaps, pale as limestone, watchful as the horizon. You would not be a visitor. You would not be a symbol. You would be a set of instincts and a few square meters of territory, a voice that cuts the wind, a body that knows exactly where to hide when the eagle's shadow crosses the sunning stone.

And the chord would go on. The wind from the west would comb the ridges. The columns would hum, the slots would whistle, the water-filled potholes would add their glassy throb. You would be part of the standing wave, not outside it. The plateau would not need you to be more than you are. It would only need you to fit, and you would.
