At first I do not know where I end and the plant begins. That is not a metaphor; it is a direction. I am at the far edge of Low Meridian, beyond the last tram stop, where the pruned roadways begin to soften and the ceiling-lamps thin from noon-through-cloud to greenish dark. The root-mat here is untrampled, springy, warm. It gives under my boots the way a body gives under a hand. It knows me.

My boots are Meridian-made, of compressed root-hide, but I have not taken them off in so long that they have begun to grow. Fine pale rootlets, no longer than eyelashes, work their way into the mat and hold me. When I walk, I tear them slightly. The plant forgives me. I am not sure the other crews notice. I am not always sure they see me, either. I am the pale grey-green color of wall-shadow, and I move so slowly that even the needle-moths do not startle.

They call me the Seam-Listener. Some call me Half-Root, not unkindly. When I was young, before I had my growth, I followed a vein of blue earth into a new fissure and it closed. The rescue crew found me three shifts later with the plant already growing over my legs. They cut me free, but not all of it could be removed. A root had entered the wound at my hip. Another had followed my collarbone. They were thin as thread, and they were already part of me. The Heart-keepers declared me stable. I was alive, if different.

Now I am a Meridian, mostly. My blood runs clear and thick, my skin is cool and waxy, my eyes have the third lid. But my left side is mottled with grey-green, not pigmentation but symbiosis. Small leaves, no larger than my thumbnail, grow from a scar on my neck. They taste the chemical air. They know the copper content before I do. My right hand can tap messages into the mat with a brass rod, like everyone else. My left hand, if I lay it flat on the floor long enough, sends roots of its own. Not deep ones. Just fine filaments that ask the Undergrove what it wants. The Undergrove answers.

That is my work. I walk the seam where the settled city ends and the wild plant begins. I do not prune. I do not harvest. I do not tend the engines. I listen. Where a root-tapper goes out with a staff and waits for an echo in the rock, I simply stand at the edge of the light and let the long pulse of the mat roll through me. The plant feels the deep ore as a taste, the same way my scars do. It feels a new seam as a loosening, a chance to push deeper. I feel that loosening in my bones. Then I kneel, place my palm flat on the mat, and wait for the pulse to become an answer. When it does, I mark the spot with a smear of crushed copper oxide and mineral paste. Later the crews come with steam-cutters and acid-sprays, and they widen the path toward what I have found.

Today the quake is already building. I have felt it for hours as a pressure behind my third eyelid, as a cold copper taste at the back of my tongue. The needle-moths on the nearest lamp cluster have stopped fanning. The small grazers on the root-plain lift their comb-like mouths and wait. Then it comes—a grow-quake, not large, but long, a slow shrug of the ground. The buildings behind me lean a fraction. The tram rails tick. The mat humps under my feet, and I go down onto one knee. I press my palm flat, not my knuckles, because I want the whole sentence, not just the tap-code. The plant speaks to me in contractions and oil-pressure. It is not a word. It is a location. A new root has torn into a seam of rare earth beyond the eastern lamplight. It wants attention, not help. It wants the path opened.

I tap the message into the mat: *here, and here, and here.* Behind me, I know a crew is already gathering. I can feel their footsteps through the fibers. They walk heavy, but not too heavy. They carry steam-cutters and coils of copper wire. They will not hurry. The plant does not like haste.

After the quake I remain on the ground. The city is silent again, or what passes for silent here: the dry creak of the plant flexing, the soft whir of exhaust fans, the faint papery sound of my own breathing. I look at my left hand. It is still a hand. But for a moment it seems wrong to call it that. The fingers are too still. The tips have begun to splay, as if they are trying to become roots. Maybe they are. I am not sad about this. The Meridians say the dead are not lost. They are folded into the mat, broken down into oil and mineral, and they become part of the road, part of the wall, part of the next generation's food. I am not dead. But I am being folded while still awake. I am a slow reabsorption. Or a slow birth. I cannot always tell which.

Where would I find myself? Here, at the place where the lamps end, with one hand on the root-mat and the other wrapped around a stem that might be my own arm. Who would I be? I am the Seam-Listener, the Half-Root, the one who walks the edge of the city and the beginning of the next district. I am part Meridian and part Undergrove, and the difference is only a matter of attention. When I stop walking, the plant will take me the rest of the way. Until then, I listen.
