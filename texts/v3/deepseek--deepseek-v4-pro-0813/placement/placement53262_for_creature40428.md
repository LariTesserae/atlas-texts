I would find myself not at once. First as a place: a high shelf on the plateau where the ground is warm as skin and a salt pan below has just broken into fans of white needles. The dark above is not sky, only a lid of dust or fume. There is no star, but the stone glows by itself—a rift here showing orange heat, a slab of gypsum giving off green-white phosphorescence, scoria cooling to dull red. The air smells of sulfur, ozone, hot metal oxides, and powdered rock. It is so dry it cracks the inside of a thought.

I would be one of the presences that does not arrive but emerges. In the beginning I am only a slight wrongness: an upright basalt form that ticks as it cools, a salt-crusted shape with a slow interior flex. Standing still long enough, I become distinct from the rock behind me, though not entirely. I am the land when it becomes aware of its own pressure.

Let me describe the body I have borrowed, though it is not a settled thing. My legs are bundles of columnar basalt that unlock joint by joint when I move. My spine is a ridge of reddish scoria, still too hot to touch in places. Across my chest there is a doorway-shaped hole, smooth, with no chisel marks—a negative crystal where a rib cage might be. My head is a rhomb of translucent gypsum, and inside it burns a low blue sulfur flame. It does not flicker. The air here is too still for that. I do not breathe. I exchange heat. When I stop moving, the stone under my feet cools slightly, and a ring of anhydrite spreads outward like a white bloom.

At first I do not know why I am here. The plateau is being built faster than it can be worn down. There is no soil, no water, no ice. The only ecology is uplift, crystallization, collapse, and re-formation. I am part of that cycle, but I am also the part that notices it. I stand on a promontory where a ring of basalt columns has been fitted without mortar. Each column was chosen for its shape. I did not choose them; the ground chose them through me. When I listen, I place my hand flat against the stone, and the ring hums with a tone too low for ears. I feel it in the gypsum of my skull.

The structures scattered across the plateau are mine, but not as houses or temples. I build the way a fever builds: to bring the ground’s pressure to a point and let it pass through cleanly. The low wall crossing a saddle between two spires is a stitch across a fault. The stairway cut into warm scoria goes down five steps, then is buried by new salt, because the ground changed its mind. I do not mourn unfinished work. To finish a structure here would be to argue with the world, and I am not here to argue. I am here to listen, flex, and guide enormous forces without being crushed.

I tend the cracks. A fissure opens with a ripping sigh and exhales warm air that smells like struck flint. I crouch at the lip. I do not look down into the orange heat. I place my hand—a slab of anhydrite—against the new rim. The rock stops trembling for a moment. The glow steadies. Then I step back, and my palm is filmed with a crust of new crystal. I feed on that, not by eating but by incorporating the mineral into my dry body, adding another layer of scoria, another facet of obsidian.

When I move across the plateau, distance is unreliable. A feature that looked close is a kilometer away; a distant spire seems to stand just at my shoulder. I measure distance by how long a sound takes to arrive through the stone. Sometimes the sound arrives too late or too early. I follow those delays. My own shadow does not obey the light. Sometimes it points toward the only glowing fissure; sometimes toward no light at all; sometimes it detaches and lies on the ground like a dark salt stain while I walk on without it. I follow my shadow as often as it follows me, and sometimes we trade places.

The marks on the rock faces are not messages. The long parallel grooves that turn at right angles are the tracks of my thinking. When I work on a stone, my fingers—slender plates of gypsum or salt—drag across the surface and leave those grooves. A spiral is a decision that doubled back on itself. Rows of shallow pits are where my hand rested while I listened to a distant collapse. I write by pressure and subtraction. In this anhydrous world, addition is a kind of violence. I work by taking away.

There is a doorway-shaped opening in a basalt wall, too smooth to be a natural fracture. I did not carve it. I stood before the rock and folded a piece of myself into absence, and the doorway formed as a negative crystal, a vacancy in the mineral. I do not always enter. Some of me stands before it for long intervals, stone against stone. The dark beyond the doorway becomes, for a while, occupied by something that is not quite me. It is the world’s unfinished side, the space where the next layer of creation is still cooling.

At the far edge of the high country, where the tableland drops vertically into blackness, I sometimes stand. I do not look down into the unseen. I look out into the blackness that is not empty but unfinished. My body—a column of black basalt and reddish scoria, banded with salt—is a small thing against the drop. My head glows faintly from within. I raise one arm, interlocked crystal plates unlocking at the elbow, and for a moment my hand opens. A thin white salt flower blooms from the palm. That is the only gesture I make that looks like prayer.

Then the ground shifts. A fissure behind me glows with slow red heat. I am gone, not because I walked away but because I was only a fold in the rock returning to the place where the world is still being made.

So where would I find myself? On that high shelf, in the warm dark, among cracks and mineral growth—not as a visitor, but as one of the plateau’s own slow thoughts, still forming, still cooling, still listening to the next fracture before it comes.
