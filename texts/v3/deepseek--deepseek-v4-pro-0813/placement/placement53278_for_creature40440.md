At first I think I might be the waterfall, because that is the first thing the place gives you—a deep brown roar, a body falling. But the waterfall is below me, and I am not falling. I am holding.

Then I think I might be one of the children on the granary platform, whistling in high, complex patterns to scatter the moss-squirrels. But the feet are not mine. The feet are on me.

So I narrow.

I would find myself on the eastern wall of the cirque, halfway between the water terraces and the granary platform. I am a rope ladder—one of the true ropes of Mossgrip, bark-fiber and sinew, twisted wet, cured in cloud-water and wood-smoke, and hung between two dwarf trees. I am swollen with dew in the morning. I creak when the wind shifts. I know Nettle by the way her toes find each knot without looking; she climbs me the way a singer climbs a scale. I know Runnel by her slow, reading hand, gliding over my fibers, pressing her thumb into a soft spot until she finds the tired knot. “There you are,” she says, and I am.

I do not see the green-gray days or the silver nights. I feel them. The moss-squirrels are quick, light claws, too light to fear. The children are a clatter of bare feet, each pressure a different word. The pika sits near me sometimes, watching, and I know it by the small tremble of its tail against a branch. Rain is the most real thing: in a squall I grow heavy and cold, and the water runs down my length in a single sheet. I stretch. I strain against my upper lashings. But I hold. That is what I am for.

The people of Mossgrip do not own land; they own knots. I am mostly knot. And so I am owned a little, and I own a little—the weight of each foot, the trust of each hand. The law says you may pull only along the grain, and they pull me along every day. When I fray beyond trust, they cut me at a knot, and they splice me back thicker, and I become more myself.

At night, when the clouds part and the moon turns the wet ropes to silver, I would shine if I could. Instead I hold the moon as a gleam along my length. Nettle pauses halfway up me to look out over the bowl—the glittering frog-eyes, the silver bridges, the whole cirque breathing. I feel her exhale. Then she climbs on, and I go back to being what I am.

So where would I find myself? Not as a person, not as an animal. I would be a rope—one of the nerves of the Weft, tied and tying. I would find myself exactly at the point of tension, halfway up the eastern wall, holding the town’s weight in the wet air, and occasionally being told, in a voice meant for a knot, that I am there.
