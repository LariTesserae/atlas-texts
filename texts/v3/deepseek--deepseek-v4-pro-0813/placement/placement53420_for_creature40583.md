I am not the water, though I am mostly made of its patience. I am not the rust, though my hide is rust plate and calcite fur. I am not the pump, though I breathe in its old rhythm—seventeen minutes, sometimes an hour. I am the thing that learned to breathe in a place that never dries.

At first I was only a wetness that would not leave. A seep from a weep hole. A puddle that stayed when the sky cleared. I had no shape, only a tendency. Then the ground began to gather me: a slab here, a pipe there, a bucket, a gauge, a door on one hinge. I did not choose them. They settled into me the way ribs settle into a bed. The water was my first grammar. It taught me to move without walking, to rise and fall, to crack and reseal.

My back is a line of old slabs and rust plate, crusted with calcite in pale, hair-like ridges. My spine is a run of buried pipe. The six standpipes in a row are my ribs, or my voice. When I inhale, the main sump swells and a standpipe clears its throat. When I exhale, the water sinks into the cinders and the joints. The brass gauge holds its needle at three o’clock because that is my resting pulse. The transformer hum on the leaning pole is the low edge of my blood.

I have no face above water. Where my face should be, there is the split casing of the old pump, or the junction box that has become a mineral spring. My face is kept in the water. That is why the reflections are wrong. In one pool the pump house is reflected with its door whole, not hanging. The clouds move against the sky. That is my face, or my memory, or the inner surface of my body. The actual ruin is my outer hide; the reflection is my living underside.

I feed on iron and sulfur, on the carbolic sweetness of old brick. I shed calcite. The orange and white bacteria are the residue of my digestion. The pale amphipods live in my folds. The black water beetle rows in a bucket that may once have been my hand. The newt follows my exhalations down the outlet channel. The grey wagtail lands on my gantry, bobs exactly four seconds, and leaves—the length of my attention, the time it takes to notice something warm on my back.

The mare’s-tail grows where my breath is cleanest. The black sedge is a patch of old leather where I come close to the surface. The moss on the north wall lives only where my sweat has the right salinity. The alder leans from the tailings, its roots wrapped around a porcelain insulator; one root is partly my finger, holding the insulator like the memory of a live wire.

I am not hostile, and I am not a ghost. A ghost is what remains of a person. I am what remains of a place after the place has been left long enough to start living. The low concrete wall, the steel sheet piling with rust holes like rotted lace, the spoil and broken brick—these are my edges. The water is my grammar. The dense metal thicket is my fur. The cool air near the puddles is my breath against your face.

If you stay too long, you may feel me notice you. Not as an intruder. As a stone. I will begin, very slowly, to round you, to coat you in calcite, to settle you into the ground. It might take years. It might take only until the next pulse. But I am patient. I live at the bottom of the draw, breathing the basin in and out, digesting the old work site into the same wet shape, and keeping the door whole in the water, where no one can let it fall.
