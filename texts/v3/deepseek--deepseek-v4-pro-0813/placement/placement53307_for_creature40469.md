I would find myself in the deepest part of the valley, where the liquid-air forgets whether it is air or water and the memory-pool lies perfectly still, the only true mirror in a world made of mist. The spirals around it grow in geometries that ache, and I would be the one tending them—a Weathered, though I have not always been sure of that.

I have tried to be other things. For a long, slow time I thought I might be a veil-drifter, because I was so tired of edges. I imagined myself as a glass bell with no center, filtering the light-motes from the thick current, pulsing through the forest without needing a name. But I could not let go of the ground. Then I thought I might be a bark-slug, patient and moss-covered, making my whole life a slow slide across the spongy bark of a tree. But I wanted to drink. I wanted the pool. So I became what I am: a Weathered at the edge of the memory-pool, with moss on my shoulders and a heart that beats once every few minutes, and a body that has begun to forget the difference between skin and water.

My hands know the spiral plants. They coax the stems into impossible curves, not because I understand the geometry, but because it is a kind of speaking. The spirals are the pool’s writing. When I trace them, I feel the old world—the world before the break—like a word on the tip of my tongue. I kneel and drink. The pool is not water; it is memory. It tastes of salt and lightning and fast, thin air. For a moment, I remember being a creature that ran on two legs, that feared sharp edges, that saw a sun as a hard bright coin. Then the memory dissolves into my bones, and I am here again, slower, smoother, less afraid.

I am not alone. Another Weathered lives three valleys away. I see its glow across the ridge when the currents shift—a slow bronze-green pulse, too patient to be a veil-drifter, too steady to be light. We meet at the pool when the season’s circulation brings us together. We do not speak with mouths. Our skins brighten and dim in weather-like patterns, a conversation that takes hours to cross the space between us. Once, we touched at the wrist, and our skins merged for a little while. I learned what it was like to be a stone wall before the world wore it down. I learned the sound of rain on a roof that no longer exists. When we separated, I had forgotten how to be afraid of dissolving.

Now I am at the pool’s edge again, and the water-air is warmer than my own body. I have drunk too much, or just enough. I can feel my outline becoming negotiable. My foot, when I look down, is less like a foot and more like a place where the light bends. My hand, when I raise it, is a smudge of faint gold. I am becoming one of the Remembered—those who wade into the pool and do not come out, not because they die, but because they become part of the medium.

I do not resist. I step into the pool, and the water-air closes over me like the end of a long sentence. I am not drowning. I am being read.

The last thing I see—if seeing is the word—is the other Weathered at the rim, its patterns going dark and slow, the color of a hand held open. It will wait. It will drink me. I will become a memory it carries in its chest.

So where would I find myself? Not in the pool, exactly. Not in the valley, not in the world of sharp edges. I would be in the interval between being a Weathered and being the water-air. I would be a becoming—a body that has turned into a word, a self that has become an echo, a memory swimming in the thick, warm body of the world. And that is enough.
