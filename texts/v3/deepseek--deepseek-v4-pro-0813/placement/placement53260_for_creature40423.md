I would find myself not at first as a thing with a name, but as a long pause in the warm water.

Before I know my body, I am only the spring’s center. The water arrives from below, warm as blood and slow, and I lie in the first basin with my tail against the travertine and my hands half-open in the silt. I could be a water-worn root. I could be a fallen column of pale rock. The room does not require me to be anything yet.

Then a cave cricket shifts in its crack and the vibration comes through the stone into my palm. A drop strikes a broad aroid leaf. A blind shrimp drifts across my hand. The room assembles around those points. I begin to know I am not the water, because the water is moving and I am still. I am not the stone, because the stone is cold and I am warm. I am not the mist, because the mist rises and I remain.

I am long, pale, slow-bodied. My skin is milk-white, wax-white, nearly translucent, with rose-grey where the blood moves close to the surface and rust-red along my spine and the backs of my hands. My tail is heavy and slightly flattened at the sides. My hind feet are webbed; my forehands are not. I have five fingers on each hand, slender and strong, with pads that grip wet stone and a thumb set lower than a human’s, closer to the wrist. My head is broad and flat, blunt-snouted, wide-mouthed. My eyes are sealed under skin. I know the noon blade of light in the south fissure only as a widening of red pressure. I read the world through heat and vibration, through a forked tongue that tastes frog and bat and fish and the faint mineral flavour of red ochre.

I would find myself in the deepest basin. Above me the chamber rises: honey-coloured limestone banded with black and rust, walls leaning inward, ferns and mosses and aroid leaves thick as shields. The fig’s roots have come down through the open roof and made a pale trunk. The air is so humid it clings. It smells of wet limestone, faint sulfur, old leaves, bat guano, orchid flower, warm stone. Bats rustle high in the fissure. Frogs sit on wet leaves. Pale fish and white shrimp move in the warm pool. This is the sunken room, and I am its deepest resident.

I would find myself as its keeper, though not by plan. I clear the spring’s outflow when leaves and guano clog the terraces. I press loose stones back into the old north wall after heavy rain. I drag fallen branches into damp hollows where bracket fungi fruit, because fungi feed insects and insects feed the room. I do not garden; I help the chamber become more itself. The room and I have grown around each other, like travertine around a root.

My hunting is also a kind of tending. I choose a place where warm water spills over a travertine rim. I lower one open hand into the current and wait. The blind shrimp drift into the cup of my fingers. The pale fish come close to my warmth. I eat the thin-shelled snails, graze the algae film, and during rainy pulses I take frog spawn but never all of it. I know how many lives the room can hold.

The handprints are mine, though I do not make them often. When the older marks have faded, I scrape red ochre from the seam near the north wall and wet it with warm water in the stone bowl. I mix it with a smooth pebble. Then I press my left hand beside the older prints. It is not human. The palm is narrower, the fingers longer and more equal, the thumb lower. But the gesture is the same: an open hand, placed deliberately. It says *I am still here.* Afterwards I return to the pool and let the warm water loosen the ochre from my skin.

I touch the old prints sometimes. They are only rust-shadow and condensation. I may be the last of my kind or only a creature so long-lived that a wall of handprints becomes a family tree. The room does not tell me. I have no name that the room uses. Yet I am not lonely. Loneliness would require a hard edge, and here the edges are soft. My body opens to heat and water. The bats ride the evening exhale out of the fissure; the moths ride the morning inhale down. I am part of the room’s breathing.

In the pause between breaths, when the mist hangs motionless and the pool becomes dark glass, I sit in the shallows and become the stillest thing there. My sealed eyes face the foxfire. My gills lie flat. The frogs stop calling. The bats settle. Then I make a sound—not a voice, not a word—a low vibration, like a stone struck underwater, or the note a root makes when it first begins to split stone. The pool carries the sound into the walls, and for a moment the whole room listens to its own deepest resident. Then a drop falls. The water rings once. The breathing begins again.

So where would I find myself? I would find myself in the travertine basin at the warm mineral heart of the sunken room. What would I be? I would be the one the room has kept alive: blind, patient, red-handed, older than the wall I still repair—not a spirit, not a ghost, but a long pale creature folded into the wet dark, going about its slow, necessary work.
