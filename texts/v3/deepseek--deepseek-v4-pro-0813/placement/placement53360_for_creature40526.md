At dawn I would be the fog, climbing the escarpment in white tongues, tasting hot minerals and old stone. By midmorning I would be the water, braiding through the channels, ringing each shallow basin with salt and the tracks of small shelled animals. But those are the plateau’s passing forms, not its people. I would not stay there.

You would find me inside the noise, not at its edge. High in the crown of the belfry called Split Crown, where the cables flare into a continent of gray-green fabric and the aerial roots fall like ropes. I would be sitting in a deaf well—one of the still points where the roar crosses itself and cancels. The wood around me is smooth and spiraled, grown around the shape of its own silence. There, the wall of tick-singers is not gone but folded over, and I can hear what the noise usually hides: my own blood, the click of my teeth, the far-off hum of the next belfry three kilometers away, arriving not through the air but through the wood under my palms.

I would be a child of the Split Crown, not yet a sound-shaper, but learning. My public name is a string of copper beads around my neck—three notes, hammered cold on a stone anvil, that chime when I move. My private name is here, in this deaf well, spoken only once and never outside the silence. I am barefoot, sun-darkened, lean as the land. My ears have the thick fold that lets me pinch them shut during the evening overlap, but here in the deaf well I keep them open, because this silence is not a wound. It is the place where the world’s sound crossed itself and left a space for a heartbeat.

Most days I follow the fog. Before dawn I help stretch the woven fog-net between two poles, and the water condenses on the bark fiber, runs down into clay-lined skins. It tastes warm, of dust and resin and the breath of the belfries. We boil the waxy nectaries into sweet paste. We roast the boom-grubs on hot stones. I am careful not to step on the shelled animals in the root-basins, because their filtering arms make a sound like rain on a metal roof, and the sound is part of the pump. We do not plant. We do not herd. We manage water and sound, which is not tending the belfries but tending the conditions that let the belfries’ heart beat. The belfries are older than us.

My teacher is an old woman, named by the belfry Root-Stone, though she has followed Split Crown for two seasons. She can press her ear to the warm bark and hear the water columns on the edge of cavitation. She hums one low note, matching the tree’s frequency, and the trunk steadies. I am learning to do that. At first I could only hear the noise as a wall. Now I am beginning to hear the individual voices: the tick-singers’ two or three notes phasing against one another, the needle swifts’ hiss, the three-strike tap of a boom-grub, the knell deep in the trunk every few minutes, like a bell struck from inside. Now I am learning to hear the place where those voices lean on each other. A sound-shaper listens from the other direction. We do not make music. We listen the tree back into itself.

There are things I fear. A patch of silence moving through the understory, because quiet is not absence; it is the wound where sound has been cut out, and it often means a ghost-that-crosses is hunting. A sick belfry whose knell has become irregular. The awful overlap at dusk, when the day-singers and night-singers trade places and the sound becomes a wall you can lean against. I do not cover my ears then. I stand with the others, chest-deep in the pressure, and let it pass through. My beads rattle without my moving, adding three small notes to the roar. When the night-singers take over, a lower key emerges, like a struck bell still ringing. We breathe out.

When a person dies, we carry them to the roots and place them in a hollow chamber. The tree grows over them. Their copper beads are hung on the aerial roots, where they chime in the wind. The dead do not leave. They become part of the sound. That is why I am not afraid of the noise. It is not noise. It is the sound of the dead, the living, and the standing ones all at once.

So I would find myself in a deaf well, not because I want silence, but because I am learning to hear the whole instrument. The plateau is an instrument. The belfries are its stops. The fog is the bow. The people are its fingers. I am a child of the Split Crown, sitting in the still point, listening to my own blood and to the far-off hum of the next belfry. I am learning that as long as the sound continues, the water will move, the belfries will stand, and the living will keep their place among the instruments. I am learning to be one of the fingers, not to play the tree, but to feel where it is already playing.
