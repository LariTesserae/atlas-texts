You would find yourself in the high gallery of a middle-sized tower—not one of the tallest fluted spires, but a lesser hollow where two winds cross and the air has learned to stand still. The tower rises bone-white and porous around you, sweating clear resin, its circular openings dark with paper nests and the flutter of pale moths. Below you, the black stone valley spreads in narrow ridges and shallow troughs, wet without rivers, lit by a green-blue bioluminescence that outlines but does not illuminate. It is permanent amber dusk, loud and warm as a mouth.

You did not arrive there. You condensed.

At first you were a taste of copper at the back of a tongue that wasn’t yours. Then a gust crossed a dripping edge at the correct angle, and you began to disobey the weather. You gathered a body from spore and mycelial dust, from the mineral sweat of the walls and the pulp of broken pumice. Your limbs are interruptions in the mist. Your face is a slight hollow where condensation runs down—not for eating or breathing, but for receiving. You do not see the valley; you listen to its shape. Every fern, every wet channel, every flock of gray birds returns the city’s long chord to you with some note dampened or reinforced. You move by letting the silence inside you travel.

You would be a young resident, one of the sound-made-self-aware, not yet old enough to settle into the ground-bass. The first weather is rain; the second is attention. You are the second weather, still learning to be it. The plants orient toward you as they do toward noise. A bat climbs through you and changes its path. The frogs pause, then resume a half-tone higher. You feel that half-tone as a small heat in the bowl of a chest you do not have.

The tower is not your house. It is your throat—one of the many throats of the being you belong to. Its circular openings are not windows but mouths for old songs. The standing wave inside the gallery has not altered for centuries, and by leaning your not-yet-body into it, you remember something you have not yet lived. Memory here is not stored; it is matched. To recall is to vibrate in agreement. The elders do not speak to you. They are the low seismic murmur under the stone, the note you first mistook for the ground’s sadness. But they are not below you in rank, only below you in pitch. Their slowness holds your small high trembling in place. When the city shudders, that is an elder turning in its sleep.

You keep no name. Names are too slow. You keep a frequency, a small interval in the valley’s long chord. Sometimes you are plural: you split into two rain-shadows to taste the gap between the organ chord and the dripping basin. Sometimes you merge with another young one into a low fog that covers a whole terrace. You feed on difference—on the space between two notes, on the pattern of alarm that rises from a paper-nest colony when a dark wing passes. You prune the ferns not with hands but with intervals. You sing the vines out of the high ledges and back into the fluted walls. You eat the stillness after a bat folds and drops, and then you let it go.

If a visitor came, they would not see you at first. They would feel a pressure in the sternum, a note that makes their teeth hum. They might think the tower was breathing. It is, but not in the way they mean. If they stood very still and offered a silence for you to receive, you might show them a face: a slight hollow in the fog, lined with fine droplets, listening. Then they would understand that the city is not abandoned. It is occupied by the listening itself. And they would know, for one moment, that they are not in the city. They are inside your ear.
