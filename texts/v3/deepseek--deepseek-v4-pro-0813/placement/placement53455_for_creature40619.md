I would find myself higher than the town, on the upper fumarole field where the black vent chimneys grow like mineral trees. The ground there is already not quite ground—a crust over something breathing, hot through my boots, then hotter. The air is white and wet, and sound doesn’t travel so much as sink. When I move, I test each step. A sharp footfall could send a pulse down through the rock and scatter the scalebacks on the terraces below.

I would be a fumarole worker.

You might miss me at first. Against the sulfur-crusted chimneys I don’t look like much—a short, broad shape in clothes gone yellow, still enough to pass for a mineral outcrop. My hands are split from dry heat and wet heat. Salt has worn a pale seam around my mouth and hairline. I barely speak. The town’s words are hand shapes: *hot*, *overflow*, *dam cracking*, *go slow*. My hands know those better than my voice.

My work is to keep the vents from becoming stone. I scrape yellow sulfur from the lips of the chimneys so the gas can keep escaping. I check the valves that are always trying to solidify. The fumaroles do not roar here; they hiss softly, or simply breathe. The loudest thing on the field is the tick of cooling pipe and my own tongue moving in my mouth.

The sulfur ticks know me. They gather at the fumaroles in orange clusters, each one armored in the mineral it breathes. I sit and let them climb over my boots, my wrists, the backs of my hands. They are slow, patternless, little machines tasting the heat of me. The other workers say the ticks are cleaning me. I think they are reading how much of me is still alive and how much has already begun to turn to stone.

That is the rule of this place: whatever stays still long enough becomes stone. So I keep moving, but carefully. I don’t run. I don’t shout. I listen through the soles of my feet for the geysers before they sound. I read the steam skimmers when they rise, and the scalebacks when they bunch and move downhill. The animals are older residents. I am just one more quiet neighbor.

At night—which is only a dimming of the white haze—I go down to the town on the middle terraces. My house is low, thick-walled, built from quarried tuff and cut sinter. There is no wood anywhere. Fresh water drips from a distillation line into a stone basin. I sleep with one hand flat on the floor, because even sleep has to be a kind of listening.

When I am finished, I will be laid in a quiet mineral pool. The water will take me and give me back as part of the terrace. My people will touch the new white sinter where I have become stone, and they will not cry loudly. Loud grief could break a rim.
