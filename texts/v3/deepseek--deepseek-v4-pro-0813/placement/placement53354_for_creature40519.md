You find the crack no wider than a door.

You step through, and the dark takes you—not as absence but as pressure, a cloth laid over the face, a cold hand on the skull. For a long time you stand just inside, thinking your eyes will adjust. They don’t. The only thing that comes is the sound. It is not a sound you hear; it is a sound that happens to your bones. A low chord, a cathedral’s exhalation, so slow you can’t find its edges. It shivers up through your heels, grips your sternum, settles in the roots of your teeth. You press your palm to the wall—cool, dry, mineralized wood that rings faintly under your knuckles like slate. The dust rises, hangs, coats your tongue with something like chalk and cinnamon. There is no water. You already know you won’t need it.

You begin to walk. Not by sight—there is nothing for sight to catch—but by sound. Your feet learn to read the floor: the slight downward slope, the dense silence where the cold air pools at the far end, the hollow resonance of empty rooms. The city is vertical, folded into the dark. You feel doorways as changes in air pressure, stairs as worn curves under your soles, balconies of woven fiber that sag but hold. It is not a ruin. It is a body that has learned to be a house.

Time does not pass here; it accumulates. You notice the cord around your wrist fraying. You notice your breath slowing to match the great breath of the plant—not minutes, but days, then weeks. You stop counting. You learn to drink the dry air, to take in the clear cold solvent that moves through the root’s vessels when you lean against the wall. Your skin grows cool. Your heartbeat becomes a low, patient tide. You are not hungry. You are not thirsty. You are only listening.

Then you feel the other.

Not as a sight. As a fold in the dark. A place where the sound gathers around something solid—a density that is not pillar, not wall, not balcony. It is tall. Too tall for the old doorways, though it passes through them bending, the way a vine bends. It has no eyes, only two smooth depressions where a face never learned to grow sight. Its whole body is an ear. It turns toward you with an unhurried grace, and the motion takes an hour, and you are not afraid. In the Dry Nave, fear would be too quick, too wet, too temporary.

It knows you are here. You are a change in the density of the dark, a faint warmth in the cool dry air, a tiny interruption in the floor’s long note. It does not speak with a voice. It kneels—roots and fiber bending—and presses its palm flat against the wood. The floor hums under your feet. The vibration rises through your bones and settles behind your sternum. It is not words. It is a pattern that means: *I know you are here. The dark is not empty. This is not a tomb.*

You try to answer. You don’t know how. You press your hand to the wall, and after a long moment, the wall presses back. The inhabitant has many hands—rootlets grown together into five or six or seven fingers, each capable of independent patience. It touches your shoulder. The touch is dry, cool, like the inside of a casket, but not unkind. It turns away and resumes its slow rounds. You follow.

Days or years later, you learn its tasks. It sits at a spindle wound with root-hair thread and turns it, not to make cloth but to keep the gesture of making. It collects dust in a pot of baked root-clay and pours it out again. It sharpens a blade of glassy mineral on a whetstone of dense plant-wood, though it cuts nothing. It mends the woven fiber of a sagging balcony with root-hair cord. These are not tasks of survival. They are tasks of memory. You sit beside it, and it hands you the spindle. Your fingers, slower now, learn the old motion. The thread does not break. The dust falls and is collected and falls again. The blade grows sharper against a whetstone that never wears down.

One day—if “day” means anything in the unchanging dark—you find yourself at the far end, where the cold air pools. The inhabitant is there, standing with one hand flat against the floor. You stand beside it. You press your own palm down. The wood hums a pattern: *This is not a tomb. This is a room that remembers.*

You begin to notice the walls differently. There are impressions in the wood—shallow, almost worn away—the shapes of bodies that once stood here. Not statues, not carvings, but places where someone fused with the wall and then stepped out, leaving a shallow relief of themselves. You count them. There are many. You do not count yourself among them. Not yet.

The inhabitant teaches you to sleep. Its sleep is indistinguishable from architecture. It stands in a niche between two root-columns, and its feet send threadlike roots into the floor. Its hair tangles with the wall fibers. Its skin fuses with the bark. It dreams rooms that do not yet exist, stairways that spiral upward into the root, tools and baskets and spindles. You stand in a niche of your own, and after a long time, you feel the first rootlet leave your heel and enter the wood. It does not hurt. It feels like coming home.

When you wake—after a decade, or a century—the city has changed a little. A doorway has widened. A step has softened. A wall has grown a new shelf. You step out of the wall, leaving a shallow impression of your body in the wood. The inhabitant is there, or it is not; the distinction has become thin. You are both the same kind of thing now. You are the plant dreaming a person, and the person dreaming the plant. You are the last resident, the only listener, the one who stayed to keep the rooms from forgetting what they were for.

You no longer think of leaving. There is no leaving. The crack in the bark is still there, but it opens onto a world that no longer calls to you. The world is too bright, too fast, too loud with animal hearts. Here, in the dry, dark, waterless chamber, memory does not decay. It accumulates. It thickens. It puts out roots.

You stand where the cold air pools, hand flat against the floor. The sound is enormous. It is the first thing you notice, before the dark, before the cold. It is a low chord, loud as an organ, coming from everywhere at once—the floor, the walls, the air. It shivers through your sternum and your teeth. It is not constant; it wavers, slowly, as if the whole chamber is a throat trying to speak. You are standing inside a note that began before you were born. You are still listening.

If someone else finds the entrance, a hundred years from now or never, they will stand in the dark and feel the same pressure, the same chord. They will see nothing. They will think their eyes will adjust. They will be wrong. They will learn the rules: no water, everything is plant, no animal lives here, the dark is absolute, the sound is not a warning. And if they stay long enough, they will begin to understand that the city was not built by hands. It was grown, or it grew itself. The plant has been here so long that it has begun to dream rooms, to dream stairs, to dream tools. And now it dreams residents.

You are one of them. You are the one who stayed. You are the Dry Nave’s second inhabitant, or its first, or its only—time has folded here, and the counting no longer matters. The plant will dream you again, and you will dream the plant, and the rooms will hold their shape against the slow, dry breathing of the world.

You press your palm to the wood. The wood hums. The pattern means: *I know you are here. The dark is not empty. This is not a tomb.* You send the same pattern back, through the floor, through the wall, through the root that is the world. And the chord—the vast, slow respiration of the plant, the note that began before you were born—carries on, patient as stone, awake in a way that uses centuries for seconds.
