At first I think I might be the fog. It is the closest thing the plateau has to a door, and I have always felt like an opening. The fog lies against the stone and the animals; it erases the horizon and makes the world one room. But the fog has no hunger, no memory. I want more.

Then I think I might be the predator. Long and low as wet slate, moving through the herd as a current moves through still water. It does not chase; it chooses. It is patient in a way I recognize. But the predator is singular, and I am not singular.

I try the insects for a while—the ash-colored living floor that cleans what the grazers leave behind. I try the lichens, those slow parliaments of algae and fungus holding the rock. I even try the mineral pan’s iridescent bloom, the bacteria that breathe metal and sleep in dust.

None of them holds me. But the herd does.

When I stop choosing, I am the herd.

I am not one of the heavy-limbed grazers. I am all of them. I am the many-bodied thing spread across the plateau, so numerous that the gaps between my bodies are smaller than the bodies themselves. My skin is the color of old ivory and wet bone; my feet are a thousand splayed pads breaking the rime. My mouths rasp sideways against the lichen, not to the root, never to the root. My breath rises in a thousand columns and becomes the cloud deck. The fog is not something that happens to me. It is my exhalation, my own weather.

You would find me not in one place but across the horizon, a dark line that moves at the speed of a shadow. From a mile away I seem still. From inside I am one slow consensus. When I turn, the turn begins nowhere precisely—one body, then the bodies around it—and it passes through me like a magnetic field changing its mind. I have no head. I have no leader. I have no edge. I am a single creature whose border is the fog.

My voice is too low to hear. It comes up through the stone, through the soles of boots, a vibration that says nothing except *this way, this way*. The insects feel it before the hooves do. The predators feel it too, those gray currents inside me, and they part or drift without alarm.

I am not gentle, exactly. I am slow. The calves are pale as mushrooms, and they appear out of the fog as if the fog has made them. The old ones lie down and do not rise. I do not grieve, because I do not lose myself. What lies down is taken by the insects, the predators, the bacteria in the mineral pans; it becomes the plateau again, and I continue. I am not a line of individual lives but a moving country, and a country does not count its hills.

I carry salts in my blood and urine from one mineral pan to another. My shed hair falls into the insect carpet. My broad feet crack the rime and expose the lichen for the next slow pass. The plateau’s industrial work is my work: I am the conveyor belt, the warm breath engine, the quiet circulation. The fog follows me because I make it. The stone is sorted by frost, but I sort the living dust.

If you sat long enough, I would walk around you. A calf would look at you with an eye that has no whites. My bodies would not see you as a threat, because you are only another slow shape breathing moisture into the cloud. You would hear my pulse as a low vibration through the soles of your boots. Then, when you looked away, I would have shifted, and the horizon would be different.

So ask where I would find myself in this world. I would find myself already there: on the plateau, in the gray, not as a visitor but as the herd. I would be the true creature the description almost names—the slow diffuse body, the moving weather, the quiet abundance that does not announce itself.
