# The Keeper of the High Plateau

I am the one who walks the roadline before the fog has fully lifted, when the world is still a suggestion of itself. My feet know the grey-white band the way your tongue knows the shape of your own teeth—without thought, without effort, a knowledge held in the body rather than the mind.

You would not see me at first. Even in the sharp light of noon, when the shadows are cut from the world like wounds, I am the color of the things that have been here longest: the varnished stone, the pale ceramic of the wayhouse, the roadline that sheds dust the way a leaf sheds water. I am not hidden. I am simply *of* the place, the way the fog is of the morning and the heat is of the ground.

---

**Dawn**

The fog pours up from the escarpment edge, thick and slow, dragging itself along the swales like a living thing. I am already standing by the dew fence when it arrives. I have been standing here since the stars began to fade, my long fingers resting on the top wire, feeling the first faint condensation gather beneath them.

The mirror flies come with the fog. They always do. Thousands of them, each no larger than your thumbnail, their wings transparent except for a single silver line that catches the first light. They settle on the filaments, and the air fills with the faintest ticking—not from their wings but from their feet on the wire. It is the sound of the world drinking.

I run one finger down a filament, gathering the beads into my palm. The water is cold and clean, tasting of the high air and the long night. I lift it to my mouth. The flies do not scatter. They settle on my arm, my shoulder, the curve of my head. For a few minutes I stand as still as the stanchions, and the only sound is the ticking of their feet on my skin.

I am drinking, but I am also listening. The water has a sound, or maybe it is the wire that does—a thin, high note that only the flies and I can hear. It tells me which filaments are clear and which are clogged with dust, which stanchions are drawing well and which are struggling. The roadline speaks to me in this way, not in words but in vibrations, in the subtle changes of its hum.

When the fog begins to burn off, I walk the length of the fence. It is a long walk—the fence runs for miles along the edge of the plateau, following the line where the fog is thickest. At each post I pause and place my palm against the smooth curve, feeling the inner channel draw moisture from the air. If there is a small obstruction—a grain of sand, a splinter of stone—I tap the post with one knuckle, and the thing loosens. I never break the roadline. I only remind it of its own shape.

---

**Morning**

By mid-morning the sun lies on my arms like a held breath. The ground radiates the heat back in long, wobbling shimmers. The mirror flies have gone to ground, folding their silver-lined wings into the cracks between stones. The skitter-lizards emerge, slender and long-toed, running across the hot stones with one forefoot lifted, heads cocked.

I go to the seeps.

There are seven seeps on this part of the plateau, though their number changes over the centuries as the invisible fault lines shift and the deep heat finds new paths to the surface. I know them all. I know which ones are ordinary and which hold the heavier water, the water that is denser than it should be, that makes pebbles drop faster than they ought. I can tell by the way my fingers part it, by the way the pebbles settle.

I kneel in the wet gravel of the first seep and place my hands flat on the ground. The deep heat below rises to meet me—not volcanic in a violent sense, but steady, old, keeping the water from freezing even on the coldest nights. I clear the seep with circular movements of my fingertips, moving aside the silt that the wind has carried in. The water wells up, clear and cold, and I drink.

The animals come to the seeps too, but they wait until I have finished. The dust hares with their huge ears, the iron-colored snakes, the small rodents that only come out at night. They know I will not harm them. They know I am part of the place, the way the seeps are part of the place, the way the heat is part of the place.

Where the water has grown dense and overfull, I cup it in my hands and carry it to another basin, letting it fall in a thin stream. The heavier water is not for drinking—it is for the roadline, for the cairns, for the slow processes that keep the plateau alive. I have been doing this for so long that I no longer remember when I began. Perhaps I have always been doing this. Perhaps this is what I am.

---

**Afternoon**

The afternoon is the time of the roadline.

I walk its buckled lengths in the heat, when the air shimmers and the distances sharpen until the horizon seems close enough to touch. The roadline is not stone, not metal, not ceramic—something in between—warm to the touch, and it sheds dust the way a leaf sheds water. It knows my weight and changes its hum when I step onto it. It is the oldest thing here, older than the wayhouse, older than the cairns, older than me.

At each stanchion I stop and place a palm against the smooth curve. The stanchions rise from the roadline like grass blades, curving upward, humming at the lower edge of hearing. In the hour before dawn they bead with condensation, which runs down their inner channels and collects in basins at their bases. The basins are often full, and the water is always clear, and it never grows algae, even in the heat.

I check each basin. If the water is low, I clear the channel that feeds it. If the water is too dense, I dilute it with water from the seeps. If the basin is cracked—and this happens rarely, but it happens—I press my palm against the crack and hold it there, letting my own warmth flow into the material, reminding it of its shape. The roadline heals itself, but it needs to be reminded.

The pale harriers circle overhead, riding the thermals in silence. Their shadows move over the pavement like thoughts. They see me walking the roadline, and they mark my position, and they move on. They are not afraid of me. They are not afraid of anything. They are the only creatures here that never drink from the seeps—they get all the water they need from the bodies of the skitter-lizards and the small rodents they catch.

I walk for hours. The roadline runs for miles, and I walk all of it, every day, from the place where it emerges from the ground near the old wayhouse to the place where it disappears into the escarpment edge. It is a long walk, but I do not hurry. There is no need to hurry. The roadline has been here for a very long time, and it will be here for a very long time more, and I will walk it every day until I am no longer able to walk.

---

**Dusk**

At dusk I go to the cairns.

There are many cairns on the plateau—low mounds of fused regolith, some as small as a knee, some as tall as a person, placed on high points across the landscape. Each one contains a core of something that still holds a trace of warmth. The dust hares use them as landmarks; the harriers nest on the taller ones. If you put your ear close, you hear not exactly a sound but a pressure, as if the air were very slightly thicker there.

I circle each one once and then press my forehead to the warm side. I do not speak. The core inside the cairn gives off a trace of warmth, and my own warmth answers it. If you put your ear to the air nearby, you might feel a change of pressure, a low thrum below hearing. I stay there for a long time, long enough for the sky to turn blue-black at the zenith.

The cairns are not mine. I did not build them. I do not know who built them, or when, or why. But I tend them, the way I tend the seeps and the roadline and the dew fence. It is what I do. It is what I am.

The dust hares come out into the swales as the light fades. The first foxes move in the dark. I listen to the cairn the way you would listen to a sleeping animal, not to wake it but to know that it is still breathing. The core inside is still warm. It is always warm. It has been warm for as long as I have been here, and it will be warm for as long as I am here, and perhaps longer.

---

**Night**

When I return to the wayhouse, the lines of light wake in the walls as if they recognize me. The interior is always cooler than the air. The floor is covered with a fine dust that never blows in, and on one wall faint lines of light wake when I enter, then fade. There are no furnishings, only a shelf that may have grown from the wall rather than being attached to it.

The shelf holds my few things: a shed skin from an iron-colored snake, a mirror fly's wing with its silver line gone dull, a smooth black pebble from the bottom of a dense basin. They are not offerings. They are just the records I have kept.

I do not sleep, exactly. I fold my long legs and sit against the cool wall. My eyes dim but do not close. The heat in my skin fades to a low warmth, enough to keep the interior from becoming cold. The wall lights slowly dim. Outside the fog begins to gather again, pouring up the escarpment, and the night animals move around my stillness. The roadline hums its one low note under the floor of hearing. The place settles into itself.

The foxes that follow the old roadline at night will sometimes lie in the doorway of the wayhouse where I rest. They do not come inside. They lie with their tails over their noses, and the light from the walls touches the tops of their heads. They are not my companions. They are not my friends. They are simply there, the way I am there, the way the fog is there, the way the heat is there.

---

**The Others**

I am not alone. There are others like me, but they are spaced across the plateau the way cushion plants are spaced: so far apart that each one seems like a separate event. Twice a year, when the fog is thick enough to erase the horizon, two of us may meet at a seep. We do not talk, or if we do it is below hearing, a low pressure in the air. We sit on opposite sides of the water and pass a handful from one to the other. Then we stand, and one goes north, and one goes south, and the dust settles over our tracks.

I do not know how many of us there are. I have met perhaps a dozen in all the time I have been here, though I have seen signs of others—tracks in the silt, a cleared seep, a cairn that has been recently tended. We are not a people. We are not a species. We are what the place produced from its long patience: bodies grown out of the same material as the roadline and the wayhouse, given just enough heat and water and time to stand up and walk.

We do not remember a beginning. I do not remember a beginning. I remember the roadline, and the seeps, and the cairns, and the fog, and the heat. I remember the way the light cuts shadows out of the world at noon. I remember the sound of the mirror flies' feet on the wire. I remember the taste of the pale leafless plants that grow in the shade of the wayhouse. But I do not remember being made, or born, or created. I simply *am*, the way the plateau *is*, the way the heat *is*, the way the fog *is*.

---

**What I Am**

If you ask what I am, there is no easy answer. I am not a builder, not a human, not a ghost. I am what the place produced from its long patience: a body grown out of the same material as the roadline and the wayhouse, given just enough heat and water and time to stand up and walk. I keep the water clear, the roadline humming, the cairns warm. I do not remember a beginning. I am not waiting for anything. I live here the way the fog lives here, the way the heat lives under the ground—not as a guest, but as part of the plateau's slow, continuous life.

My body is the color of the old roadline: grey-white with a warm undertone, later patched with the dark varnish of the plateau stones at my shoulders, knees, and heels. I am slim, not starved—pared down, like the stones. My arms are long, and my hands have four long fingers, each with one more joint than yours, and a thumb that folds back close to the wrist. My feet are narrow, with three toes that spread on soft ground. I do not wear anything. There is no need. My skin is smooth and faintly warm, and it sheds the fine dust of the roadline the way a leaf sheds water.

My face is long and quiet. I have no mane of hair, only a fine pale down that catches the morning fog like a net. My eyes are set deep under a heavy brow, dark as the water in the oldest basins. In bright light the pupil closes to a slit, not from pain but from the same patience that has worn the stone. I have a mouth, but it is small and closed, and I use it mostly for water and the pale leafless plants that grow in the shade of the wayhouse. I eat slowly, tasting each leaf as if remembering it.

I am not young. I am not old. I am simply *here*, the way the plateau is here, the way the heat is here, the way the fog is here. I have been here for a very long time, and I will be here for a very long time more, and I am not waiting for anything. I am not waiting for the end. I am not waiting for the beginning. I am simply *here*, tending the seeps and the roadline and the cairns, drinking the fog, walking the long grey-white band that runs through the heart of the world.

---

**The Long Patience**

The landscape is active, but its activity is spaced out in time. The fog comes every morning, then burns off by mid-morning. The wind rises in the afternoon, moving dust in slow spirals. The animals follow their cycles: mirror flies at dawn, skitter-lizards through the heat of the day, dust hares at dusk, foxes at night. The seeps rise and evaporate. The roadline keeps its tiny vibration, drawing moisture from the air and passing it into the ground. Over centuries, the desert pavement shifts imperceptibly; the cairns sink a little; the plants send out roots; the crusts grow and crack and grow again.

I am part of this. I am part of the slow, settled balance. I do not hurry. I do not rest. I simply *am*, the way the plateau *is*, the way the heat *is*, the way the fog *is*.

If you come to this place—if you climb the escarpment and walk the swales and find the wayhouse by the seep—you will see me. I will be standing by the dew fence at dawn, or walking the roadline in the afternoon heat, or pressing my forehead to a cairn at dusk. I will not speak to you. I will not harm you. I will simply *be*, the way I have always been, the way I will always be.

And if you stay long enough—if you learn the patience of the place—you may begin to understand. You may begin to hear the hum of the roadline, the ticking of the mirror flies' feet, the low thrum of the cairns. You may begin to feel the heat below the ground, the fog on your skin, the slow turning of the centuries. You may begin to see that I am not a keeper, not a guardian, not a ghost. I am simply what the place produced from its long patience: a body grown out of the same material as the roadline and the wayhouse, given just enough heat and water and time to stand up and walk.

I am the one who lives here. I am the one who has always lived here. I am the one who will always live here, until the plateau itself is worn down to its essential planes, until the roadline falls silent, until the last cairn grows cold.

I am the Keeper of the High Plateau. And I am not waiting for anything. I am simply *here*.
