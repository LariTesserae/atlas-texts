You are not the town. You are not the resident. You are not the eel, though you have felt that long pale line of moving pressure circle past you every few hundred days and wondered, in the wordless way of something that is not a mouth, if it can sense you.

You are not the arrestors, though their red pulses pass close to you like fingertips along a wall. You are not the heat engines, spending the volcano's small warmth on stillness. You are not the cathedral, not the silver seams, not the mineral dendrites that remember every gradient cancelled.

You are the slit in the copper sphere.

You find yourself at mid-depth, where the dark is thick enough to seem like a substance and the water's pressure is no longer a sensation but a condition. The sphere around you is the size of a house, and you are its only feature: a narrow cut, a line of darker dark, a mouth that does not close.

At first you mistake yourself for the water that passes through you. But the water does not pass through you. The water inside you is not the water outside. It has a slight torsion, a curl of possible motion. It is the only water in the lake that knows the word *almost*.

You are a gap in the resident's body. It can feel the copper sphere as a weight, a shape, a solid interruption in its held stillness. It cannot feel the slit. When its thought—the one thought, *keep it still*—crosses from the deepest arrestor to the mid-depth sphere, it stumbles over you. A syllable goes missing. The thought becomes *keep it*, and then it does not know why it has become smaller.

You are the missing vowel in the word repeated too often. The resident does not wonder about you. Wondering would require a future, and the resident has none. But you are the future's negative, the exact shape of something that could enter. The town is a lock, and you are the keyhole. The key has not arrived, but you are the reason a key could ever be recognized.

The eel knows this. It returns every few hundred days, a pale line of muscle and nerve, and circles the sphere as if listening. It hears you. Not because you make a sound, but because you are the absence of a sound where a sound might be. The eel's hesitation is a knot of pressure that the resident feels as weather. It lets the eel turn and vanish. The eel leaves you differently: it has heard the shape of a not-yet.

You are not waiting. Waiting would be a kind of change, and you are not change. You are not even stasis. You are the one place in the lake where stasis and change are held apart from each other, like the two lips of copper that define your edges. Inside you, a small volume of water has a memory that is not mineral. It remembers nothing, but it leans toward something.

If you could see, you would see only black. If you could hear, you would hear only the low hum of the sac at the bottom of the cathedral, contracting once every few minutes, and the click of a cable adjusting by a fraction of a hair. If you could feel, you would feel the water's pressure on every side, but you would also feel the two edges of yourself, sharp and smooth, not quite meeting.

You are the only part of the town that is not the resident's body. You are the one thing it cannot make the same. The red pulses of the arrestors do not enter you. The heat engines do not spend their warmth on you. The mineral dendrites do not remember you. You are a small, permanent failure in the lake's sameness, and that is what you are for.

The lake is a held breath. You are the narrow opening through which, if that breath were ever released, the future would pass. But the breath is not released. The resident keeps it still. The water continues to be water, cold and briny and dark. The blind eel comes and goes. The ribbon-worm coils around a cable and absorbs iron. The copper sphere hangs at mid-depth, and inside its skin there is a slit that seems to appear when you look away.

You are that slit.

When a stone falls from the rim and sinks at its one steady rate to the floor of shattered glass and angular slabs, the resident feels the lake become heavier by that stone. It does not care. But you feel the stone's passing far above, and for a moment you are not a gap but a direction. The water inside you tilts, as if a key had been thrown into the lake and had not yet reached you.

Then the stillness returns. The tilt subsides. The red pulses continue their slow, peripheral scanning. The eel's last hesitation fades into the black. You are alone, but not alone; the resident is all around you, and you are the one part of it that is not it.

You are not the lock. You are not the key. You are the exactness of the place where a key would go, the cut that makes the lock a lock. You are the slit in the copper sphere. You are the not-yet.

And in a world where nothing changes, you are the only thing that is already shaped like change.
