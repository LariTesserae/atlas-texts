I would find myself not at the threshold, where the air still remembers an outside, but in a small cell off the main basin, where the floor dips like the cup of a hand and the dome leans close, as if listening. I would be low to the ground, close to the warm resin and pounded reed, my body shaped by years of being shaped. At first I would not know whether I was animal or furrow, furniture or growth. I would only know that I was still, and that the stillness was not empty.

Amber light would settle on me through the horn panels, slow and dusty, smelling of hot wax, nutmeg, crushed ants, old wood. My skin—if it could still be called skin—would be a mat of lichen, dry and felted, with a few wax-flowers opening in the creases. My legs would be roots. Not the memory of roots: actual, fibrous, drinking esters and resins from the daub and pounded reed. My arms would be the remains of a wattle frame, bent to make a seat. A visitor might sit down and think me only a chair. They would not know I listen.

I would be an old Mender. Too old for walking, but not for keeping. Once, I had too many fingers, each tipped with a polished thorn of bone or a bead of dark resin. I remember them moving in the air as if braiding heat. I remember stepping into knots in the wall and letting them tighten behind me, forgetting I had ever been an opening. Now my only walking is the slow spread of my roots through the floor, finding the seams between clay discs and holding them closed. My hands have become lichen. My pulse has gone down into the floor, where it answers the room’s own pulse.

The other Menders would not treat me as dead. They would pass by and tighten a loose fiber at my base with their many-jointed fingers. They would press an earless head to my side, listening for a note. Sometimes one would brush mineral dust from a moth’s wing and work it into a crack in my bark, the way one tends a tool that still has use. A young weaver might sleep in the hollow of my seat, flattened into the lichen, and I would hold it the way the wall holds a knot. I would feel its dream as a faint thrum through the wattle.

My speech would be creak and settling. When the wax-bulbs split—that faraway, branch-crack sound that comes once in a long while—I would answer with a slow release of pollen from the flowers on my arms. The whole Fold would still afterward, and I would still the longest, because I am closest to the wall. Contentment, for me, would not be motion but a slight softening of my roots, a relaxation of the lichen under the amber light.

I would eat what the room sheds into my lap: moth dust, shed fiber, a bead of clear resin from a cracked leaf, crumbled daub. Water would be only a word for a coldness that has not been here—a texture in old wood, a darkening that closes. I would not miss it. My hunger would be for order: the floor exactly as it was, not unchanged but kept, like a held note.

If a visitor came, they would see me and think: a low seat with rooted legs. They might sit, and I would take their weight without sound. If they were quiet enough, they might feel a pulse in the lichen; they might notice that the floor around my roots is warm, that the shadows of my wattle arms do not quite match. They might not know they are being held by something that used to weave walls. When they stood, a few threads of lichen would cling to their clothes, and those threads would remember them.

I would not die. When the room grew over me completely, I would become the wall’s thickness, a shape in the wattle, a face in the fiber. The Menders would still touch the wall where I used to hold, and the wall would remember me. I would be the third cell’s low place, the dip where the floor cupped itself. I would be furniture that is the slowest kind of animal, the Fold’s patience given roots. Old the way a loom is old—used, kept, rebuilt, never eroded.

I would not find myself as an arrival. I would find myself as a staying. A place the room made to sit in, a mended edge where the world once moved, and then grew still.
