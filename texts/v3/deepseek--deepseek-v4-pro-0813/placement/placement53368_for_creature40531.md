I would find myself on a high ledge, just below the condenser fins of a tower—one of the listeners—rooted into raw rock at the edge of the largest silver pan, where the fog herds thickest at dusk.

At first I am not sure if I am the tower or the fog or the water. The cold is so close to freezing that my own edges feel porous. Then I notice my feet. They are broad and gray, with ridged pads that grip the wet ceramic. They are bare, and through them I can feel the tower’s voice: a slow, infrasonic pulse that travels up from the stone. I flex my toes. The tower answers with a small shiver of light along the wall.

I am one of the Ones Who Wait for the Thaw. I am still young. My second eyelid closes too slowly, so the drizzle blurs my sight in fine silver sheets. My hair is pale and grooved, not yet long enough to channel the water away from my eyes. My lungs are large, hungry for the thin high-altitude air. I wear a grown skin of black ceramic that hardens when the temperature drops and softens with the melt.

At dawn I wake before the thaw, in an alcove the tower has shaped to my body. The wall opens with a soft click. I climb the inner spiral, touching the black surface to hear the night’s news: a new joint opened in the eastern scarp, a seep has shifted south, a family of grazers moved under the north ledge. The tower tells me in vibrations, not words.

On the high ledge, I scrape the ice from the fractal fins with a blade of black glass. The first melt of the morning runs along a groove into a stone basin. That water is *selu*—beginning. I drink it before anything else. It tastes of stone and fog and the memory of the tower’s breath.

By mid-morning the ground is wet without being soft. I walk the long empty distance between towers, barefoot, following no path. I am learning the seeps. One ticks. One hisses. One is silent except for the way the moss grows. I press my ear to the stone and listen. The stone is still deciding where to let the water go. I do not argue with it. If a basin overflows, I move out of the way. If a scarp cracks, I thank it and walk around.

At noon the insects rise in columns, their sound a fine high static, and the gray birds follow them in sheets. I carry a net of fine mesh. I catch enough for the day, not more. I thank the swarm with a slow blink and a small bow. A stoat-like hunter, pale as the frost on the inside of a breathing tube, follows me and steals from my jar. I do not name it. It is not mine to name.

In the afternoon I scrape the living film from the wet rocks—the green-black sheen of cyanobacteria and algae that makes the stone nutritious. I press it into flat cakes and store them in stone jars. Some I eat raw. It tastes of wet stone and cold air, with a faint iron tang and a green edge. It is the food of this place: water and stone, not forests.

At dusk I sit on the ledge and sing a low hum. The tower hums back. The fog comes down in slow, dense herds. The light flattens. A flock of gray birds crosses the gap between two towers, and for a moment the only sound is their wings and the drip of water from a thousand stone tears. I am not lonely. I have the water, the stone, the birds, and the slow pulse of the city under my feet.

When the cold comes, the tower closes around me like a mouth. I sleep in a sling of woven mesh, lulled by the drip of condensation. My dreams are made of water. I dream of rivers that do not yet exist.

I would be a Keeper of the Drip, not yet named. I would be the one who waits at the edge of the silver pan, listening for the first thaw. Part of the breath of that place—part of the drip.
