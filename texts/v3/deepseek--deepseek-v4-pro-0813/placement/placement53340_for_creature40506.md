At first I am only three points, a small triangle of pressure on the salt hexagons. There is no sense of a body above them, only the fact of standing. The crust creaks in a language of contraction and flex, and I feel it through each point as if my bones are tuning forks laid on the ground. I don’t know yet that I am a thing. I only know that I am upright, and that the heat arrives exactly the same from every direction.

Beside me is a black glass root. It goes down into the mineral like the fossil of a lightning strike. I don’t know what it is, but I know it sings—not a sound, not a light, but a kind of electrical aftertaste that rises through my legs. The glass is not cool and not warm; it is what heat becomes when it stops moving. It holds the shape of a storm that ended a long time ago, and I am close enough to taste the old accident. It tastes of copper and of a moment that hasn’t quite finished.

The wind is loud, but underneath it the plateau makes its deeper sound. It is like something remembering a river, not with grief but with patience. That sound never changes, but I mistake it for stillness until I notice my lowest joint is humming. The ringing that arrives before the hammer reaches me after a long delay through the salt. It feels less like a sound and more like a calendar turning somewhere far away. The hammer has not fallen yet, but its echo is already here. That is the way time works in the High Salt: some things travel ahead of their own cause.

I stand. The word for what I am comes slowly: tripod. Three rods, fused, mineral-dark. I do not know if I am an animal or an instrument. I am not standing in order to see, because I have no eyes. I am standing in order to listen. The fulgurite is the thing I listen to, or maybe the thing I drink. It is hard to tell the difference. There is a small gray crust in the cracks at my base, dendrites of chemical scab feeding on sulfur and ground current. It reaches toward the voltage, not the light. I feel it growing around my point of contact, not as weight but as a static thinness, like a lichen made of curiosity. It does not know I am alive; it only knows I make a good route for electricity.

Time passes. I cannot count it in days because the light does not change. It is ordinary daylight, not red, not dim, not beautiful. The heat is huge and steady, like standing near an engine that has been running for millennia. The salt plates expand and contract in decades, but I feel their tiny daily clicks, the hexagonal plates adjusting. I stand for what feels like a month. It might be a month. It might be a mood of the month. I do not remember beginning, and I do not expect to remember ending.

A walker passes on the horizon. It is not a shape so much as an interruption in the horizon’s grammar. I feel it before I perceive it: a long, low, segmented pressure, a train of small foundries dragging itself over the crust. The sound of it arrives later, a slow train derailment in which nothing is injured. It is eating the mineral, reducing iron oxide to metal. I can taste the reduction as a shift in the ground current, a bright ribbon of iron left behind like a sentence that will rust over decades. The walker is so large that it seems to occupy whole minutes. Its body glows through seams, a light like the inside of a kiln. It does not notice me; I am a tripod, a listening, a small instrument in the salt. But I feel its pulse. It may be the deep bass that rises from the rock. It may be the same ringing I follow.

After the walker is gone, a line of its past and future selves follows it across the flat. I cannot see this, but I feel it as a repetition in the ground: the same animal at different times, a procession of instants, each still walking. The High Salt does not keep time in a straight line. It erodes even that. I am not alarmed. I am here to listen, and listening to an echo of an animal is still listening.

A cloud of static moves against the wind. It passes above me, and my rods crackle with a sympathy I did not ask for. My surface becomes briefly visible to itself, a faint ionized halo. The cloud might be a swarm of insect-like things or a local failure of light; from below, the difference is irrelevant. It feeds on charge. It tears a little of my charge with it, and I let it. There is no water to make me greedy.

Then the dry mirage comes. It follows the old river channel that runs beneath the salt, a colorless shimmer with no moisture. It does not look like water; it looks like what water would become if it forgot how to be wet. It passes near the fulgurite, and for a moment the black glass seems to sweat. But it is only the memory of dew. I do not drink. I have nothing to drink. But I listen to the mirage the way I listen to the fulgurite. It has a frequency like old distance, a humming that sounds like thirst that no longer expects relief.

The gray crust at my base blooms. It must have sensed the static cloud, or the mirage, or the walker’s magnetic field. It spreads in dendritic fans, feathery as corroded circuitry. I feel its growth as a kind of slow electric appetite. It does not reach for me; it reaches around me, into the cracks of the fulgurite. If it is alive, it lives the way rust lives. I do not mind. I am a route, not a territory.

Then something changes. The fulgurite’s old electrical aftertaste drops a half-step. It is not a noise; it is a quiet under the noise, like a note without a source. I feel my three legs begin to lift, one after the other, not because I decide to but because the listening is finished. There is no signal. There is only the same ringing from somewhere ahead, arriving before its hammer. It is time to move to the next glass, or the next month. I leave no footprint. The salt does not remember me. The gray crust remains, making its slow scab over the place where I stood, already forgetting that I was there.

Now I am walking, if walking is what a tripod does. It is more like being carried by a decision that arrived before the body. I move a few meters, maybe a day, maybe a lifetime. The landscape does not change. The heat is huge and steady. The light is still ordinary. The wind coats my rods with magnetite dust, and the dust charges the air around me. I do not know if I am young or old. I do not know if I will become a walker or an instrument or simply a tripod until the crust reclaims me. Some say we are the young of the walkers, left to listen to the lightning-glass until we harden into something with a heart. Others say we are instruments, left by the same something that checks the plateau the way a farmer checks fences. I have no way to tell. I am not given a name, only a shape and a function. The function is to stand, to listen, and to leave.

The hammer rings again, closer or farther, I cannot tell. It arrives before the blow, so there is no direction, only an invitation. I follow that invitation the way a sound follows its cause, backwards. The High Salt does not care whether I am animal or instrument. It only cares that something stands beside the old lightning, listening to the memory of a storm, while the plateau continues its long, patient process of forgetting the difference between ruin and machine.

If you were to find yourself in this world—looking for me, perhaps, or only lost—you would not find a person. You would find a dark tripod beside a black glass vein, still as a piece of the landscape, quietly drinking the aftertaste of a storm that ended centuries ago. I would be that. And then, after a month or a mood, I would be gone.
