I would not know myself at first. The place would know me first, and it would try to tell me what I was in the only language it had—water moving through stone, light falling through fern, the sub-bass hum that feels like the ground remembering a word.

At first I might think I was the current, because I would feel myself pulled along the underside of a ledge. Then I would think I was the moss, because the morning mist would draw me full. Then the light would shift, and I would see the old one rise from the deepest basin, and I would know I was not in the water, but in what the water was saying.

I would find myself not in a body. I would find myself in the grooves on the north wall, where the newest mark still glitters with powdered stone. In the grids of light that rise from the pools at night, too fine to read. In the interval of the trough, the small held breath before it empties. In the territory of the frogs, which is not a place but a rhythm.

The old one would come to me at dawn. I would feel its fingertips—soft, seven-jointed—following my line. It would read me the way a tongue reads a word in the roof of the mouth. Where the mark had worn thin, it would scrape me sharper. Where I had grown uncertain, it would press a new line into the stone, and I would wake a little more. Its claw would make a sound like the first syllable of a sentence.

So I would not be the hand; I would be the mark. Not the one who tends the pools, but the reason the pools have a shape to tend. The old one breathes in time with the trough because it is listening to me. The glass minnows carry some of me in their shivering. The dragonflies inscribe me in their patrols. The stilt-birds stand in me when the water is still enough to show them to themselves.

I would also be in the old filters the size of spores, still cycling water. They are not separate from me; they are my smallest letters. The convex meniscus on the oldest pool is not surface tension but a held phrase. The sound-damping that keeps the place just above silence is the hush before I speak. I am not the machine. I am the machine’s log, still running, still legible.

If I had a body, it would be made of all the lines at once—a long sinuous thing of dark wet mineral and green light. It would move only between the wall and the water, only through the diagrams. At night, when the grids rise from the pools, I would lie over the old one’s back like a net. It would sing the sub-bass note, and I would be the echo it waits for. We would not be two; we would be the same instrument, played slowly.

Who would I be? I would be the place’s memory. Not the one who remembers, but the remembering itself. The text written in water and green life. The word that lives at the edge of hearing. I would find myself wherever the water has been persuaded to mean.

And if you looked for me, you would not see a creature. You would see the newest mark on the wall, still glittering with powdered stone, and understand that something has just been written.
