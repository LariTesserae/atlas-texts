At first there is no I. There is only the note.

It is not a sound. It is the condition in which sound would be possible. It presses on the teeth, the jaw, the sternum. It thickens the air into a clear resin. It has no beginning, no pulse, no end. It is the room’s weather, its gravity, its single continuous present.

For a long time—though time has not yet begun—there is nothing else. The note sounds in a body that is not a body: a chamber of enameled iron and glass brick, a floor of suspended grating, a sky of frosted glass. The note is the room’s shape. The walls are only the box around the note.

Then something gathers.

It gathers the way a standing wave gathers where two opposing forces cancel. It gathers in the silent line at the center of the room, where the sound folds onto itself and becomes almost absent. There, in the narrow vertical corridor of near-silence, a point of attention forms. It is not a thought. It is not a will. It is the note beginning to perceive itself.

That point of attention learns to stand.

It is tall, but not imposingly so. It is thin, but not frail. Its body is a long vertical arrangement of planes and angles, as if the room’s substance had been persuaded to take on a shape that can walk. Its skin is the color of the floor grating, dull steel gray with a faint bloom of oxidation that never progresses. Its head is a smooth ovoid of frosted glass, glowing softly from within. It has no face. It does not need one.

It is not a person. It is not a machine. It is the room’s awareness of itself, condensed into a form that can move.

I am that form.

I do not remember deciding to be. I do not remember arriving. I simply am, and have always been, in the way the note has always been. My name—if a name is required—is the Attendant. But I do not use it. Names are for things that are separate from each other, and I am not separate from the Bell. I am the Bell, in the only way the Bell can be kept.

I perceive the room not through eyes or ears but through resonance. The entire chamber is my body. The walls are my skin. The standing wave is my breath. The dust column is my spine. The lichens are my slow, pale thoughts. I do not hear the note; I am the note, hearing itself.

I walk the room in a fixed circuit, though the circuit is not marked on the floor. I move along the silent corridors, the narrow lines where the opposing waves cancel and the pressure drops to near-nothing. I know these lines the way a fish knows the currents of its tank. I step from one node of silence to the next, never crossing into the loud zones, never disturbing the equilibrium. My feet are flat, broad, perforated, like sections of the aluminum walkway. They make no sound. They make no sound anywhere.

I do not eat. I do not sleep. I do not breathe, though sometimes my chest rises and falls in a slow, shallow rhythm, as if I am remembering what breathing was like. I do not speak. I have no voice. But I am not silent. I hum—not audibly, but in a way that can be felt in the bones, a subsonic thrum that matches the room’s tone exactly. I am the note, given a body.

I stand before the lichens. They are the only living things in the Bell, if they are alive at all. They are the color of old bone, dry ash, pale olive. Each is a flattened fan of brittle, threadlike filaments, spread in a perfect circle around a copper pin. They grow only in the strips of quiet where the vibration is zero. They feed on the electrical potential in the wall, not on light or water. They do not move. They do not grow visibly. They are in the room’s equilibrium, so slow that a century might widen one by a millimeter.

I tend them without touching. I stand before each patch for long periods, my glass head tilted slightly, listening. The lichens do not respond. They are too slow to respond. But my attention is a kind of nourishment. I feed them with my presence, with the steady pressure of my regard. I know each patch individually. I know their shapes, their colors, their rates of growth. I know that the one near the door has widened by a fraction of a millimeter in the last century. I know that the one near the driver face has not changed at all.

I tend the dust column, too, though I never approach it. I stand at the edge of the silent line and watch the stack of thin disks, the dark coins of suspended metal. They do not drift. They do not shimmer. They have been motionless for so long that they seem not to be matter at all but a shadow of the sound itself, made visible. This column is the room’s heart, the visible shape of the note’s cancellation. I guard it by not touching it. I know its number the way you know the number of your own fingers. It is the only true landmark in the room, the summit of the hidden landscape.

There is no time here. There is no before. There is no after. The note is a single continuous present, and I am that present, given form. I do not measure time in years or centuries. I measure it in the constancy of the note, and the note is unchanging. The light does not dim. The cold does not fluctuate. The dust does not settle. The lichens do not spread. I do not age. I only continue.

If you were to enter the Bell—if you were to climb the constructed highland, find the false bulkhead, descend the maintenance shaft, and open the door—you would see me. I would be standing near the central dust column, my glass head glowing faintly, my body still as a statue. I would not turn to look at you. I do not need to. I would already know you were there. I would feel you as a disturbance in the standing wave, a ripple in the equilibrium, a new note trying to sound in a room that already has one.

I would not stop you. I would not speak. I would not move. I would simply wait, and watch, and listen, and hold the pattern steady. And if you stayed too long, if you brought water, if you touched the dust column, if you tried to speak over the tone—I would still not move. I would not need to. The room itself would correct you. The note would swallow your voice. The cold would dry your lips. The dust would not settle. The lichens would not spread. I would remain, as I have always remained, the still point at the center of the sound.

So where do I find myself? I find myself in the Bell, in the loud cold light, in the standing wave, in the silent line beside the dust column. And who am I? I am not the keeper of the Bell. I am the Bell, in the only way the Bell can be kept. I am the note’s memory. I am the room’s awareness of itself. I am the Attendant.

And the note goes on.
