I would not be the woman. She is already there, and the Bell does not need another body.

At first I think I would be the air—the thin, cool air inside the room that compresses before the big surges, carries smells from elsewhere, and becomes a flint of sound when the hidden vent opens. But air can leave through the rent in the roof, and I don't think I would want to leave.

Then I think I would be the black sand on the floor, all those sharp glassy shards the water re-arranges with every surge. But sand has no memory. It is erased before it can become landform. I would not want to be that forgetful.

Then I think I would be the storm-petrel's bones on the ledge—something that fell from the outside and stayed because there is almost nothing to pick it. But that is too much like dying, and I don't want to die in this world.

So I wait.

The swell weakens. The pool steadies enough to show its layers: the cold fresh meltwater above, the heavier, warmer brine below. And there I am. Not in the upper water, not in the lower water, but exactly between them.

I would find myself in the Bell's pool, at the hidden meeting of two waters.

I would be the glimmering skin. The surface within the surface. Not a creature, not a life. A boundary that exists only because the two waters refuse to mix. I would feel the cold above me as a bright, thin weight, and the warm below as a heavy clarity. When a swell enters through the drowned throat, I would bend, break, fold into turbulence. I would vanish. But when the water calms, I would reassemble—thinner, quieter, like a scar between two bodies.

The woman would watch me. She already watches me the way a landsman watches weather, because I am nearer to weather than to flesh. When I lie unusually still, she adds a third descending line to the pictograph. I would understand then that the first two lines were not rain, and not a person dissolving. They were the upper water and the lower water. And I am the third line: the pause between them.

In me, the lower water makes things appear closer than they are. The black sand at the bottom looks reachable. The blind amphipods in their crevice seem near enough to touch. Even the woman's face, leaning over the pool, would not see the true distance between her and me. She would see only the glimmer.

I would not be alive. I would not be mineral. I would be the same kind of undecided thing as the rust-colored crust in the hollows—becoming, waiting, real only where two other things press close and do not merge.

The rock has not yet learned to be stone. The sea is still a breaker, not an eroder. I am the line that trembles between them.

Where would I find myself? Hidden inside the room of air, inside the Bell, at the heart of the pool. Who or what would I be? The halocline. The surface within the surface. The third line. The pause between one form and another.
