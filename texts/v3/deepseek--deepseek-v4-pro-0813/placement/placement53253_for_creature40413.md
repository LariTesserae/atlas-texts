I would find myself on the west wall, halfway down, where the air is warm as a kiln and the mirror-vines turn their leaves toward the chord instead of the sun. But it takes me a while to know that. At first I am only a pair of hands on a rope, then a body leaning out over the drop, then a child’s foot tapping the wall for no reason except to feel the stone answer. The hollow is like that: you don’t so much arrive as become audible.

I am one of the Bell-Hollow people. Specifically, I am the bellwright’s apprentice.

The forge is behind me—a low salt-brick room breathing resin-smoke. My teacher has gone to the rim to listen for a sour bell, and I have been left with the small hammer, no larger than a thumb. My hands are still too narrow, my skin still too smooth. The dust has not yet carved its deep grooves into my palms. But I can feel the wall through the soles of my feet. I can tell when the night smiths send a long-short-long code from three terraces down.

My name is not spoken. If you met me, you would not hear it; you would feel it tapped on the collarbone or drummed on the stone. Mine is: right heel, left heel, palm to the chest, and one ring of the small copper bell on my finger. It means *the one who is learning to hear metal*. My parents climbed to the rim before dawn when I was born and listened for a new tone in the wind. They heard a small bell, so they shaped my name-sign over the mouth of the hollow and let the wind carry it down. The hollow heard me. That made me fully born.

At dusk I go with the resin-gatherers. I don’t pinch the bladder-leaves; I watch how the clear sap runs and how it smells of citrus and hot metal, because that sap will later burn under the forge where my teacher casts bells. The smithies wake after dark. Their hammers beat long and short through the rock like a copper clock. I press my palm flat against the salt wall and close my eyes. I am trying to find the one sour bell in the roar. Most nights I can’t. The chord is too whole, too old. My teacher says a bellwright must be able to hear a single wrong note inside a sound that has been playing for centuries. So I listen with my teeth and my feet and the thin bones of my inner ear.

Below me, the floor stays empty. No one walks on the salt polygons. The central stone is the hollow’s tongue. I keep my feet on the ledges and ropes, where the working sound lives.

At noon I retreat into the house and sleep in a hammock of woven reed. The noise drops to a low thrum, like a heart in the walls. At dawn I climb to the rim with the rope-weavers and help clear salt dust from the fluted walls, because dust can damp a note. I am not the First Listener. I am not the one who decides whether the day is whole. But I watch her press her palm to the wall at first light. This morning she signed *the day is whole*. As I passed her doorway I tapped the frame once, and she tapped back once: *keep listening*.

So you would find me there: a half-grown figure on a narrow ledge outside the bell-forge, powdered white with salt, arms not yet flecked with bronze, a rope swaying beside me. I would be the bellwright’s apprentice, not yet a bellwright. One day I will cast a bell for my own family and tune it until it fits the hollow’s chord. Until then, I am learning to hear the one note that is mine—and not confuse it with the noise.
