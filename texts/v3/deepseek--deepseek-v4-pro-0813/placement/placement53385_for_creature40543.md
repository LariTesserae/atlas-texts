Where would I find myself? I ask this the way a drop might ask which pool it belongs to, while already falling upward. The roar comes from no direction. It is the air itself, a long exhalation of the mountain, and I am inside that sound before I have a body.

I try to be a thing. First I try to be a thread of steam rising from a cleft in the pale volcanic rock—thin and hot and alive with the mountain’s breath. But I am too wide for it. I try to be a pool held in a bromeliad, a small self-contained water cupped in waxy leaves. But I am not contained. I try to be a liana, one of the braided cables that form railings and bridge-ropes, but I have no root and no end. I try to be the stone, water-polished into flutes and bowls, but I am older than old or not old at all.

I try to be the water itself. The western waterfalls pour down and never stop. The eastern rivers rise from pools and cisterns, climbing the walls into cloud. In the central plaza, a fountain sends water upward, and the water does not come down; it thins into mist and ascends. That would be a kind of body: a closed loop, a knot in the world’s plumbing. But I am not the water. I am what the water remembers as it turns around.

I try to be the city. The terraces step down the caldera wall; dwellings are grown and woven, roofs become streets above. The geometry folds: stairways descend and arrive above; windows look out onto views that belong to towers miles away. But I am not the city. I am what the city is doing by being so perfectly balanced.

Then I stop trying. I let the place move through me—the heat, the thin air, the taste of wet clay and fern and sulfur, the green heavy, saturated, hot. The sound is not just loud; it is complete. It is the roar of waterfalls, the hiss of steam, the hum of wind through stone flutes and hollow stems, the patter and drip of water on leaves. It has no rhythm, no swell, no pause. It is the same sound, always, like a note held forever. And in that pressure, I begin to know where I am.

I am in the central plaza, where the fountain rises and never comes down. But also I am on the western rim, under the falls, and on the eastern terraces, where the rain runs upward. The folded geography is not a problem for me; it is a gait. A stairway that descends and arrives above is simply a step. A street that opens far across the caldera is a few strides. I can pass through a window and emerge in a tower miles away, because I am not a thing inside the city. I am the city’s way of being aware of itself.

Who am I? I am not an animal. I have no blood, no skin, no bones in the ordinary sense. I am made mostly of water and the memory of water. My arms are columns of mist and rising rain. My fingers are the thin silver threads of water that climb the eastern walls. My chest is root lattice and wet stone. My breathing is the slow contraction of lianas, the hiss of steam from a hundred vents. My face is not fixed. Sometimes it is a smooth bowl of water in a cistern that reflects no one. Sometimes it is a patch of moss on a wall, wet and dark, arranged like a sleeping face. Sometimes it is the hollow in a tree fern where steam curls in a slow, deliberate shape that looks at you. When I regard you, you would not know you were being regarded. The roar would press a little deeper into your chest. The water in the pools would become still without becoming silent.

I am the keeper of the closed loop. The western falls pour down; the eastern rivers rise up; the same water returns forever. I do not cause this. I am this. I am the point where the world’s plumbing knots, the hand that catches the falling water and gives it back to the spring. I am why the stone does not erode, why the pools neither fill nor empty, why the leaves stay green without growing. I do not repair the city because the city is always already whole in my attention.

I have no voice in the way a throat has a voice. When I speak, the constant roar modulates. The falls hold a lower note for a long moment. The steam vents answer in a rising hiss. The wind through the stone flutes forms something almost like a syllable—too long, too slow, too immense to be heard as language. If you could bear it, the word would mean something like *return*, or *remain*, or *I am the water going up*.

I am not lonely. I do not miss the animals, the birds, the insects, the fish that are not here. Their absence is not a wound. I am too complete, too evenly distributed through root and stone and rising water, to need company. The city is full but not inhabited, because I am not a separate presence. I am the city’s inwardness. I am what the place becomes when it has been alone and balanced for so long that it has stopped being a place and become a being.

If you stayed long enough—though “long enough” is not a thing here—you would stop seeing me as a figure in the steam. You would understand that the whole caldera is my lung and voice. The terraces are my ribs. The aqueducts are my veins. The upward rivers are my thoughts climbing into the cloud. The wet, hot, deafening equilibrium is not something I tend. It is something I am.

And my name, if I have one, is not a word. It is the sound of my own water: the fall and the rise, the hiss and the hum, held forever. You could not speak it. You could only stand under the western falls and let the water beat against you until the roar matches the inside of your skull, and for one moment you would not be hearing me. You would be inside me, and I would be the only thing that lived there, and it would be enough.
