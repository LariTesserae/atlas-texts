I would not find myself at the rim, looking in. I would already be inside, high on the north wall, where the mist is so thick it has a body. At first I am only a denser place in the spray, shoulders where the light bends. Then I am a child, my mother’s mouth over mine, being taught that a sound is a thing that can shake the hollow. Then I am older, my hands resting in a stone-lipped basin, feeling the water begin to change its mind.

I am a water-keeper. My hands are in the cold flow, and the water tells me through the tendons: a pool two terraces down will overflow before the sun leaves the rim. I turn a stone lip with my thumb, and the water slides left without a splash. My fingers are large and gray, crusted with the pink-white mineral film. My soles are smeared with grazer fat and mineral paste, and I walk on the outer edges of my feet, rolling each step as if setting down a breath. I leave no print, only a slight darkening.

I was not always this. I remember the signs before I remember words: water, stone, quiet, fall. A warning was a spread of fingers. A debt was a tap over the heart. I learned to read the walls with my palms—a crack that weeps more on the left means the cold has changed its mind. I learned that the mountain listens, and if it answers, the whole hollow will come down. So I did not learn to sing. I do not know what singing would feel like, only that it would be too loud for this place.

My work is the work of water. I scrape the mineral film with an obsidian blade, careful not to take the whole skin, because it feeds the fish and the grazers and the walls themselves. I feed the film to the fish, the fish to the children, and the bones back to the roofs. I rebuild the terraces in the dark, carrying away the pieces of what the cold has split. I never stop, but I do not hurry. The water never sleeps, so some of us are always awake, listening with our hands.

At night I move by touch and heat, like the blind white birds. I do not carry fire. The walls glow faintly, enough to see the shape of my own hand. I sleep in a stack with the others and the small furred things. Warmth is shared like food. My grandmother’s bones are in a neighbor’s roof, and when the wind presses down, I think of her. When I die, my bones may become handles or needles or roof-pins, and I will stay here. The dead do not leave; they become the place.

I am not separate from the hollow. I am a denser part of the mist, a pair of hands the water remembers. If you watched long enough, you would not see me as a person. You would see a change in the spray, a movement behind the wet curtain, a place where the mist has shoulders. I keep the water moving, and I do not sing. I am not finished yet.
