I would find myself not at the entrance, but at the first moment the room notices me. Before that I would be only the long shallow sink, the plain folding downward, the sky becoming rumor overhead—a traveler still made of distances. Then the narrow slot between pale buttresses opens, and I would become the one who steps through.

Inside, I would be small. Ten paces across, but I would not count them. I would be the damp irregularity in the fixed instrument: breath, pulse, skin still holding the weather of somewhere else. The air would be dry as structure, and I would feel my own moisture as a kind of trespass—not forbidden, just new. The light would not flicker when I raised my hand. The drone would not pause. I would be, at first, a visitor: a soft animal interruption in a room that has reached equilibrium.

But let me not settle there.

In the sealed white noon, I would begin to believe I was my heartbeat: a low pulse beneath the drone, a periodic flutter in the upper partials. The resident would taste me that way. It would not startle. The tubes near the entrance would detune slightly, registering my body as a change in the standing wave. I would feel myself being listened to by the entire immobile architecture—not through ears, but through every close-packed tube and concave cell. The room would lean its whole immense body toward me.

Then I would think I was my voice. If I spoke, the sound would be absorbed, not swallowed, and the resident would taste the shape of my words. It would not answer, because it has no language, but for a moment the partials would stack differently, as if the chamber were trying to remember what a voice is. I would become a sentence without a speaker, a brief human note in the ten thousand bottle-mouth chord.

That too would not be final.

The white stems in the wall-cells would turn their small colorless beads toward me, and I would feel like the one being seen. I would be an eye in the room, an instrument the resident uses to notice itself. But I am not the stems. I am not the dome. I am not the shed dust, though I might settle into it.

I would find myself becoming the memory of a visitor. The resident does not need me, but it holds me. My heartbeat would be added to the low center of the drone. My breathing would be folded into the upper partials. My leaving, if I left, would not be marked as absence; it would be a minor detuning that slowly resolves back into the chord. But if I stayed—and I think I would stay—I would become a partial in the fixed instrument: a small, warm overtone, a human-shaped irregularity in the mineral throat.

I would not be the room’s mind. I would be one of its thoughts—brief, absorbed, no longer separate. I would be the soft damp thing the dry earth has been waiting to tune. I would be the pause that becomes a note, the visitor who stayed until the word *visitor* meant nothing, until I was only a slight thickening of air, a warm spot in the white light, a tiny human partial in the drone.

And when the sky above the sink became too distant to remember, I would be that, too: the older name for what I was before I became entirely myself.
