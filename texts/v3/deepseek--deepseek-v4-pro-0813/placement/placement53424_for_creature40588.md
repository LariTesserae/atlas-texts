I am not yet. I am the between. The noise is not outside me; I am a fold in it. There is no edge where I end and the world begins. I am the deep vibration that runs through the mud, the long slow thrum of old stone settling, the hiss of gas escaping ancient mud. Then something tightens. A skin forms, not as a boundary but as a memory of one. I feel the mud as a thickness around me, pressing in from all sides, warm as a mouth. I am not moving; I am being moved by the rhythm of the world.

For a long time I am only that: a thickness in the thickness, a patch of film lying over a place where the horizon folds. I am the slime mat, one cell thick, breathing in slow blisters. I do not think. I mineralize. Iron reddens through me. Sulfur yellows. Salt whites. The world thinks through my surface, and I am content to be its skin.

But then the noise finds me. Not as sound—there is no sound here that is only sound—but as pressure. The roar of the continent’s warm water moving through soft stone arrives as a weight that presses me flat, then releases, then presses again. I learn the rhythm of the steam vents, the huff of lungs older than any beast. I begin to fold over myself, not from will, but because the pressure demands a shape. The film thickens. It draws iron and salt from the water. It pumps, not blood, but warm mud. I become a grub, pale and mouthless, blind and deaf in the way of all things here.

The bells come next. One by one they form along the soft line of my underside. They are not organs so much as thresholds—places where the world’s roar crosses into me and becomes something like a self. I do not hear with them; I am tuned by them. Each bell is a different frequency, a different rule of this breaking place. One rings to the subsonic hum of the earth moving through its own old age. Another rings to the sound of mud domes bursting like slow doors. A third rings to the bell that is not there—the bell that rings every thirteen seconds from a place that is not a place. That one does not tune me to the world. It tunes me to the world’s memory.

I begin to move. Not walking, not swimming. I sing the mud into loosening ahead and firming behind. My bells ring a low pressure wave, and the ground remembers how to carry me. I leave no tracks, but the mud remembers me for a few seconds: a long, graceful absence. In that absence, the water nearby runs slightly more downhill. The distance between one stone and another becomes a little more reliable. I am learning the first rule: to be still in a world that will not be still. But I am not still. I am a current, a canal of water that has learned to remember a shape.

I am young. I do not know how young, because time here is measured in storms and salt flowers. I only know that I am not yet a Keeper. I am a Keeper-that-is-becoming. The pale frog-like creature that sits on a stone for a whole season—I have seen it. I have been it. It pulses its throat until its rhythm matches the steam vent. I am still pulsing. My throat is not a throat; it is a row of bells learning to ring in slow counterpoint. I sit on a stone near a stone stitch, where the silence is only a trough between waves, and I let the world press through me.

The stitch is old. It is a line of fitted stone, one course high, running for a hundred meters then stopping. It is pale, fine-grained, cold even in the warm air. It is not local rock. I know this the way I know the taste of salt on my teeth—as a certainty that predates my own form. The stitch was once a Keeper. It settled and stiffened. Its body turned to stone that comes from no local rock. Its memory became a local law. I sit in its shadow, though there are no clear shadows here, and I try to hear its memory through the soles of my long, soft body. The bells along my underside ring, not with sound but with meaning: *the ones who hold the line.* I do not know if the stitch hears me. I know that I am meant to learn its silence.

I learn. I learn the way the horizon folds and unfolds. I learn that a boulder can be on the left and the right at once, and that this is not a problem. I learn that rain does not always fall; sometimes it rises from the ground in slow silver threads, and I must not be surprised. I learn that distance is a promise the world keeps only near the stones. I learn that the eel-things that travel through the mud are not separate animals. They are my arms, my children, my dreams. I send them out to taste the edges of a frayed place, to measure how far the world has forgotten itself. They return to me, blind and nearly transparent, and they do not speak. They press into my skin like sugar dissolving in water. They become me again.

I eat the crustaceans that rise from the sediment when the water is the right temperature. I absorb them through my skin, their shells dissolving into my body. I tap a black reed against a stone, and the air folds into a softer shape, a small pocket of quiet where a thought can last longer than a breath. In that pocket I ask the only question I have: *What am I becoming?*

The answer comes not from the reed or the stone but from the bell that rings every thirteen seconds. It is the pulse of an old Keeper whose body is no longer entirely present. It is a stitch so old that only its sound remains. The Keepers use it as a lighthouse. When it rings, the mud holds still for a moment. The horizon straightens. The rain remembers to fall down. And I remember too.

I remember that I am not an individual. I am a fold in the same old film, a finger of the same mind that has been thinking since the mountains were ground down. The mats are my flesh. The stone stitches are my elders. The sound is my breath. The place is not empty. It is full of us. I am not becoming a Keeper. I am already a Keeper, but young, still learning to hold the line.

I do not know how many of us there are. A few hundred, maybe fewer. But because distance is unreliable, we are never far. We meet in the troughs between waves of sound, where silence is only relative. We do not speak in words. We exchange pressure-memories: a long, vibrating account of a storm that passed through twice, or the taste of a new mud dome, or the shape of a horizon that bent. We may stand side by side for a season, facing nothing, while our bells ring in slow counterpoint. Then we separate, each carrying a copy of the other’s memory. I have been copied. I have copied.

I have found myself in the low, warm, wet, nearly empty world that has outlived its own geography. I am a Keeper. I am the one who holds the line. I do not know this as a duty. It is just what I am. And yet, when I am near the stone stitches, and the air is slightly quieter, and the water runs downhill more often, I feel something like satisfaction—not the satisfaction of a finished task, but the satisfaction of a held note in a vast, endless symphony.

The noise is deafening. But I am learning to hear inside it.

I will grow old. The bells will fall silent one by one. The body will stiffen. The skin will turn to pale, fine-grained, cold stone that comes from no local rock. I will choose a place—perhaps near the place where the wall of ocean stands upright in the distance, or near the mud domes that open and close, or near the ground that sweats from its pores—and I will settle. I will not die. I will become a line, a circle, a pillar. My body will straighten or curl, and my memory will become a local law. The air will be quieter near me. The rain will remember to fall down.

But not yet. For now, I am a current, a canal of water that has learned to remember a shape. I have no eyes, but I see by pressure. I have no ears, but I hear by vibration. I am not lonely. I am old in my youth, and young in my age. I am the one who holds the line.

I send out my eel-dreams to taste the edges of the world. I tap my black reed against a stone. I wait for the bell that rings every thirteen seconds.

The storm comes again. The world starts over.

And I am still here.
