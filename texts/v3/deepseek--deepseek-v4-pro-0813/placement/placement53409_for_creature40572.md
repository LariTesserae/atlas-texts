I would be on the western wall, about two-thirds up the shaft, in a room no wider than my outstretched arms. The floor is black brick, warm from the vent below, and the window is a small pane of mica that looks out across the dark to the Salt Clock. I would not be sure, at first, that I am a person at all. I would be the taste of sulfur on my tongue, the cool stone under my bare feet, the high collar around my neck. Then the memory would settle into me like dust settling onto a ledge.

I am Low-Hum.

I am Throat-born, four feet tall, pale salt-gray from a family that has lived high enough to keep some light in its skin. My eyes are round and dark, with the faint copper glint that comes when the arc lamps catch them. My mouth is small and nearly lipless, pressed shut against the mineral air. I breathe through the fine spiracles behind my jaw more often than not, and my collar is always high, lined with the blue-gray fuzz that filters the vent-breath. I was named Low-Hum because as a child I used to press my ear to the wall and hum back to the mountain. The mountain, they said, hummed through me.

My work is the Salt Clock.

The Salt Clock hangs at the center of the shaft, a broad bronze dish suspended on three steel hawsers, fed by a single crystal of salt dropped from a glass tube every minute. The crystal falls with a small *tink*. That sound is the city’s heartbeat. I am its keeper, or at least its most recent apprentice. I climb to the service gantry each morning, my soft-soled shoes gripping the spiral stair, and I check the dish, the tube, the bronze rim, the tiny shutter that releases the crystals. I brush away dust with a brush made of my own hair and the whiskers of a smelt-mite. I test the hawsers with my bare palm. I listen.

The crystals are not water. They are salt, bright and sharp, like ice that will not melt into anything drinkable. The dish fills slowly. When it is full, the city will carry it down to the salt pan and empty it onto the floor, and for one day everyone will walk on that white crust, leaving footprints the wind erases. The last festival was before I was born. My teacher, who was called Tink, used to say I would be the one to carry the dish down when it filled. I think about that, but not too much.

My room is small. The bed is a hammock of woven cable and felt, strung between two hooks that can be lowered in minutes. The hearth is a closed ceramic box fed by a thin gas pipe. I have a shelf of tools: a brass sounder, a soft blade, a lamp, a clacker. On the same shelf, in a sealed glass tube, is a single droplet of water. It appeared on the outside of a cold pipe during a rare cool spell. I collected it not because I wanted to drink it, but because I wanted to remember that the upper world has something cold and clear and impossible. I do not hoard it. That would be a dreaming. But I look at it sometimes, the way a child looks at a lamp before sleep.

The law is carved into the wall near the Salt Clock:

*Be small in the throat. Do not be loud. Do not waste heat. Do not trust the rock. Do not waste metal. Do not hoard water.*

I know it by heart. I know it by the weight of my feet on the stone, which can shift at any moment. The mountain is young and unquiet. We are not built in it; we are lodged in its windpipe. Every person learns to feel the rock before they learn to speak. My first shoes were felted booties with a bare patch at the toe, so the stone could speak to me. It still does. Some days it says *hold*. Some days it says *wait*. Some days it says nothing at all, which is the most dangerous.

From my gantry, I can see the whole city stacked in rings. The upper terraces are brighter, cleaner, pale and cool. The lower terraces are hot and dark, close to the gas mouths and the salt floor. The people there are rust-red, oxide-black, sulfur-yellow. They tend the smelters, the vent pipes, the electrolytic cells. I can see the foundry glow behind smoked glass on the upper tiers, the stamp mills and crushers below, the wind-scoops on the rim that feed the turbines. The city is dense, almost impossibly dense, and it is never still.

But it is quiet. The air is too thin to carry a shout far, and a shout could bring down rock. So no one shouts. Orders are given by hand-sign, lamp-flash, wooden clackers, and soft-soled messengers. From where I stand, I can see the lamp-flash operators on the wide ledges, their red and amber and white lights making flickering sentences across the shaft. A rigger crosses a rope bridge with a red lamp at her belt. A child sells cups of warm ester-broth on a corner ledge. A crew of mite-sifters comes up from the slag levels with their boots dusted red, carrying small bags of copper and iron beads that the mites have excreted from the clinker. They move in a hush, and the whole city seems to breathe.

The Salt Clock ticks. I count. I do not count aloud. I count in my fingers, in the small twitch of my knuckles, in the way my breath pauses at each *tink*. The dish is shallow and wide, and the salt crystals accumulate in a pattern that is not random. It is a map of time. I have learned to read it. The crystals on the outer rim are older, more settled. The ones in the center are new and sharp. I brush only the dust, not the pattern. The pattern belongs to the city.

When a micro-tremor comes, I feel it first in my feet. The gantry hums. The dish sways a fraction. I place my hand on its bronze rim, not to stop it, but to feel its weight. The whole city freezes for a breath. Then it resumes. I adjust the shutter by one turn. The next crystal falls. *Tink.* The mountain has shifted, but the clock does not stop.

I think about water sometimes. Not because I want it, but because I do not understand it. The law says do not hoard water. The law says water is a legend. But I have seen it: a single clear drop on the outside of a pipe, cold and trembling. It is beautiful and faintly poisonous. Our bodies run on esters, sulfur compounds, iron, salts. The fluid inside us is a thin, pale amber. If I were to drink that droplet, it would be like drinking the moon. It would be a sin and a sadness. So I keep it sealed, and I look at it, and I do not drink.

I am not a hero. I am not a leader. I am Low-Hum, the dish-ward. My sister, Soot-Whisper, works in the foundry, and her arms are pocked with arc-bloom. My father was a gas-bleeder who tended the lower vent pipes until the gas took him. My mother tends trays of microbial film in the upper light, and she says the blue-gray fuzz is the true floor of our food web. We are a family of the middle terraces, neither white-eyed nor smoke-blooded. We survive by chemistry and arrangement, like the city itself.

In the evening, when the coin of sky has gone from blue to black, I sit on my ledge and watch the city become a column of small lights. The Salt Clock ticks softly at the center. The mountain shifts a fraction, and the city shifts with it. I sign across the shaft to the lamp-flash operator on the eastern wall: *all stable.* The operator signs back: *we are still here.*

That is the truest prayer I know. Not a hope, but a command. We are still here. The rock holds us gently. The salt falls. The mountain breathes. I am Low-Hum, and I am exactly where I belong.
