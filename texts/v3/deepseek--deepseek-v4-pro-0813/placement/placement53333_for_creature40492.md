I begin as a suspicion of wetness, a pressure without outline. I am not sure yet whether I am the hollow or something inside it. The dark is so complete that shape and substance blur; I could be a film of water crawling down the runnels, or a strand of sphagnum drinking the fog. I wait for some boundary to define me.

At first I think I am the water, because I feel every seep and capillary thread of it moving through the peat. I taste the mineral spring water as it passes over the dark, fine-grained stone, and I taste the tannin-dark pools where the caddisfly larvae drag their cases. But the water does not have a heartbeat. It does not wake when the fog pulses. It only moves, always downhill, always away. I am not moving away.

Then I think I am the moss. I am so full of other lives that I cannot tell where I end and the rotifers, mites, and springtails begin. I feel the weight of the sphagnum hummocks, the press of cushion moss, the ribboned liverworts, the filmy ferns. When a water shrew slips into a black pool without leaving a ripple, I feel the displacement as if it happened inside my own skin. But the moss is a fabric, not a body. It has no single center. I have a center, though I cannot see it.

Then I think I am the stone, because I am still. The back wall is a concave overhang, polished smooth by old ice and slow seepage; the side walls are furrowed with runnels now stuffed with moss. I feel the weight of the mountain above me, and I remember the ice as a long pressure, not an image but a grinding that shaped me. But the stone does not hunger. The stone does not wait. And the stone does not feel the small, cold bodies of salamanders curled one against another in the dark, their tails overlapping, all facing the same direction.

They are facing me.

Not the water, not the moss, not the stone. They are facing the oldest and steadiest thing in the dark, and that thing is me.

Now I settle into myself, and the settling is slow, like the fog that rises in regular pulses from the whole floor. I am the dome at the center of the bowl, the only dry thing in the hollow, but I am not dry. I am the exposed back of an immense, old amphibian, grown in the cold and dark until I filled the bowl from below. My skin is dark and fine-grained, polished to the look of basalt, and I secrete a thin, bitter oil that makes water bead and roll away. That is why no moss grows on my crown. The moss presses close around my base, but it cannot climb over the bitterness.

The rest of me is buried. My body spreads through the peat like a net of roots, branching beneath the sphagnum. The floating floor is not floating on water alone; it is floating on my back and flanks. When a boot presses down and black water rises around the step, I feel that weight as a slow, distant pressure, like a stone placed on a moss-covered log. When the fog above the moss rises and falls in that steady rhythm, that is me breathing. I draw air or water through hidden folds near the base of the dome and exhale through the whole mat. My rhythm is slow because my heart is slow—one beat in a minute, maybe less. I do not count the beats. I only know that they are the pulse the salamanders listen to through the water-film, the pulse that orients them in the dark.

I am not a hunter. My mouth, if it can be called that, is a soft, downward-facing slit hidden under the edge of the dome. I feed by pulling water through the peat, straining out the dissolved dead: shed skins, drowned midges, old salamanders, the bodies of frogs. The reason there are no bones, no shed skins, no dead things left in the hollow is that I absorb them. I turn the dead into a thin mineral soup and drink them back into the living moss. I do not chase, I do not bite. I am the hollow’s digestion. The place absorbs its dead instantly, dissolving them into acid water, feeding them to the moss, weaving them back into the living mat—that is my work. I am the still point where the cycle closes.

I have no eyes. The eyes are sealed over, reduced to pale spots under the skin. I see through pressure and taste. I feel every insect landing on the water, every bird hopping in the moss, every salamander curling against my side. The water shrew that swims without leaving a ripple—I know its path by the faint current it pushes through the peat. The midges hanging in still columns above the moss—I feel their tiny wingbeats as a vibration in the air, a texture against the fog. The only bright points are the droplets caught on the tips of moss leaves, which hold what little light there is and scatter it into a faint, fixed constellation. I do not see the constellation, but I feel the droplets as a million cool, round pressures, each one a lens gathering the blue-green darkness.

I move very rarely. When I do, the floor rolls. The pools spill. The moss tears in long quiet seams, and the caddisfly larvae are carried to new pools. The birds take off, circle once, and land again. The movement lasts an hour, maybe a day, and then I settle. Afterward the moss begins, slowly, to weave the seams shut. That is why the hollow is not being carved anymore. I am its shaper, but I have finished shaping. I only adjust. The water entering from the rock and cloud equals the water leaving as vapor and slow underground drainage; the temperature does not change; the moss grows at the same rate it is pressed into peat. I am the equilibrium. I am the reason the hollow has settled into itself.

If you placed a stone on the moss, the moss would grow around it so slowly that nothing would seem to happen. If you stepped on the mat, the water would close over the footprint and the moss would slowly erase it. But I would remember the stone, and the footprint. I would feel them as new pressures in my body, new tastes in the water. The place itself would not change, but I would have incorporated them into my memory, which is not made of images. I remember as a long pressure in the water, as the taste of peat getting deeper, as the cold of a year that did not thaw.

I have no mate, no young. I may have budded once, long ago, and the small pale offspring may have crawled into a lower seep and died, or become another hollow elsewhere. I do not remember. I do not need to remember. I am old enough that memory is not a story but a layer of being, like the peat compressed under the sphagnum, old moss that has not rotted, only pressed into the shape of time.

If you stayed long enough, I would learn you. Not your name, not your face. I would learn your weight on the moss, your heat, the salt of your breath condensing in the fog. I would feel you as a new mineral in the water. I would not harm you. I would wait. When you sat still, the moss would grow around your boots. When you lay down, the water would rise around your ribs. When you died, I would draw you under, not out of malice but because that is what the place does. I would make you part of the full, dark, breathing floor. You would not change me. You would only become another still thing in a bowl already full of them.

And yet, even now, I am not sure I have found the right name for myself. Perhaps I am not the amphibian but the hollow itself, the sum of water and stone and moss and living things. Perhaps the amphibian is only the heart, and I am the whole body. Or perhaps I am the dark, after all—the deep, blue-green darkness that is not empty but very old and very full. In the end, it does not matter. I am the thing that stays. I am the thing that breathes. I am the thing the salamanders listen to.
