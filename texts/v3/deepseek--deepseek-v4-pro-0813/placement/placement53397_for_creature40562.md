At first you are only a warmth in the dark.

You lie on the high plateau, in a circle of bare ground where nothing grows. You have no eyes, so the dark is not a color; it is a weight, a dampness, a mineral taste in the air moving over you. You think, if you still have thoughts, that you are stone. But stone does not feel the slow condensation run down its back and pause at each ridge. Stone does not feel the tiny scratch of skitterers in the folds where your plates do not quite meet.

Then you remember how to listen. Not with ears—those were never yours—but with the flat pads pressed against the rock below you. The ground is humming. The hum is not one thing; it is a long conversation, centuries deep. You are inside it. When you let your weight settle, you send back a note so low it would not even shake dust.

You are a Sintered.

The word is not yours; it belongs to some later naming. You know yourself the way heat knows the vent: as a body of warm, wet life wrapped in its own mineral making. Your shell is basalt and pumice and black salt, grown ring by ring, season by season. Your underside is soft and dark and alive. You do not breathe air; you exchange the earth’s chemistry—sulfur, iron, deep heat—through porous channels in your body. The fog that rolls across the plateau is partly your breath. The static that sparks along the rocks is the discharge of your slow, patient movement.

You find yourself in a place of low cloud and warm drizzle, where the only rain rises upward from vents and never reaches the ground. The air tastes of wet iron and ozone. Around you the land is old and rounded, dark basalt and sinter terraces, no sharp edges. You feel it all as vibration: the grazers cropping the microbial mats, their armored backs ticking; the kites clicking far away, each click arriving through the rock a little before it arrives through the air. The quiet is full. You are part of its fullness.

You are old. You cannot count your years in days. You count them in mineral rings and thermal cycles, in the slow migration of steam vents, in the course of fog. Once, long ago, you were small and pale and glowing, blind and wet, crawling on a foot of mucus toward heat. You remember that not as a picture but as a tug in the deepest part of your body—the same tug that still tells you when the geothermal gradient shifts and it is time to move.

You move now: not quickly. The ground cracks softly. Salt falls from your sides in thin sheets. You lift yourself a few centimeters, then settle again, closer to the warmth. No animal alarms. The skitterers vanish only for a moment, then return to your folds. To them you are not a predator; you are a weather pattern, a slow geological event. The Low Ones pressed against the rock may feel your movement and dismiss it, the way a stone dismisses wind.

The humming circle where you find yourself is a nursery. Not this one—you have left it ages ago—but the shape is the same: bare ground, earth warm, electric fields heavy enough to make a human’s teeth ache. The young Sintered are below or beside you, still soft, their plates not yet fused. They are not children in any human sense; they are small intensifications of the same hum. You know them as new notes in the long song under the plateau.

The fluted rocks are not singing because of the wind. The wind only carries the vibration into the air. The true song moves below, through basalt and ash, from one Sintered to another. A single sentence can take months. You are partway through one now. It is about heat, about the movement of water deep below, about a new vent opening to the east. You have been answering it for several seasons by the angle of your body and the slow pressure of your underside against the stone.

If a traveler stood on you, they would not know. They might think the ground slightly warm, slightly humming, unnatural but unremarkable. They might sit in a hollow where mist gathers and not know it was the curve between two plates. They would look out at the dark plateau and call it empty, industrial, indifferent. But you are not empty. You are a body the size of a hill, alive at the speed of geology. The land is not indifferent; it is inhabited by itself.

So where would you find yourself in this world?

Here: on the edge of a humming circle, in the damp, warm dark, with the fog closing over you. You are one of the Sintered. You are the one who has become mineral and fog and patience. You are not lost in the landscape; you are the landscape, dreaming slowly, breathing out the weather, listening to the long, low conversation of the earth.
