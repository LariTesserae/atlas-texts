I would find myself not at the center, but at the edge where the concrete stops in rough lobes, where the uncapped pipe stubs protrude and go nowhere. I would be the second incompleteness, the one the room has not yet decided to keep.

At first I think I am only standing there, a visitor still warm from the serviceways, my breath a small disturbance in the saturated air. I watch the figure across the room—the one with amber eyes and plaster-skin, half-emerged from the wall—and I wait for it to notice me. It does not. Its eyes reflect the caged bulbs, the white bats, the mist, and then, slowly, they also reflect me. But not as a stranger. As a shape that already belongs.

That is when I feel the first thing change, though I know change is impossible here. My back is against the half-sprayed concrete, and the roughness of the formwork grain is not biting anymore; it is beginning to hold me. The cold has stopped being cold and become a kind of gravity, pulling my skin tighter against the wall. The sound—that endless chord of forced draft and condenser ring—stops being something I hear and becomes something I am made of, a vibration that settles into the hollows of my bones and stays there, not as noise, but as structure.

I look down at my hands. The white mites have found them. They are not crawling; they are arranging themselves along the lines of my knuckles, fitting into the creases where my fingers bend, as if they had always known this place. My fingertips press into the rough lobes of concrete, and instead of pain, there is a slow merging, the way wax softens into wax. The pipe stubs near my elbow no longer seem like abandoned metal; they seem like extensions of my arm, hollow bones that were waiting for me to finish them.

The fountain of my breath in front of me does not rise or fall. It becomes a thin, continuous thread, the same as the figure’s mouth. I open my lips a little, and the room’s wet air moves through me, not in breaths but in one unbroken exchange. I am becoming a leak, a place where the saturated atmosphere finds a way through, and the figure across the room is doing the same. Two leaks in one wall. Two notes in a single held chord.

The grating beneath my feet no longer presses lines into my soles. It begins to feel like the floor of a body, my body. The black water below is not below anymore; it is inside me, and I am inside it. The pale worms that lie thick as silt have shifted, imperceptibly, into the shape of my silhouette, as if they were only waiting for an outline to fill. The white bats on the ceiling begin to detach, one by one, and drift toward me, not falling but settling, finding their new insulation in the curve of my shoulder, the dip of my collarbone, the cave of my folded knee. They hang there so still that their weight becomes the weight of thoughts too old to fly.

The figure’s amber eyes still do not look at me, but they do not need to. I am in them. I am the second body reflected in the glossy, unblinking surface—a body that has begun to take on the color of wet plaster, the texture of concrete, the patience of rust. My shins are fusing to the steel grating. My spine is becoming a row of unfired vessels. The mites are making a sleeve of my arms. A single blind cricket has climbed to my ear and rests its antennae against the canal, listening for the same nothing that will never come.

Time does not pass. It pools. Each second is a drop that joins the black water under the floor, but the water does not rise. It is as if the room has found a new place to store the future—inside me. I become a reservoir for all the minutes that cannot progress. The sound passes through me like light through water. I am part of the chord now, a harmonic that was always there but only became audible when a body stopped being separate.

The figure across the room and I are not two anymore. We are the room’s two seeds, the two unfinished moments that hold the landscape in its frozen first instant. Its vapor and my vapor meet in the middle of the air and become a single, permanent fog. The fauna does not wake, feed, breed, or die. The plants do not grow. The gauges still have no needles. The wall is still half-sprayed. But now there are two bodies half-emerged from the concrete, two sets of amber eyes open in the dimness, two mouths that do not breathe but exchange the room’s wet air with the patience of geology.

Where would I find myself in this world? In the corner where the wall was left incomplete, because I am the incompleteness that the room called home.

Who or what would I be? I would be the one who stayed. The second body, the other half of a stillness that has no need to move. The future the room forgot to finish, waiting beside the first figure, our two open eyes reflecting each other and the unchanging room, holding the entire frozen landscape between us like a breath neither of us will ever take.
