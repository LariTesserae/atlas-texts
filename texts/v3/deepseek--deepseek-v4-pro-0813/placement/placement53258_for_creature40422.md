I would find myself at the center of Sumpfold, though I would not know it as center at first. Before I know my body, I know the pulse: one deep stroke every few seconds, a slow lifting of weight that makes the sump’s surface tremble and the boardwalks hum.

Then I know myself as the flywheel in the pump house—painted dull red, taller than a person, turning through warm air and oil and the green breath of the ventilator stack. I am not alive. The moss in the joints of my housing is alive. The algae on the sump is alive. The old eel in the dark intake pipe is alive. I am the made thing they all arrange themselves around: the iron heart inside the great plant-body of Sumpfold.

My stroke is a conversation with water. I draw the sump up through the intake and push it away to the canal above. Between strokes, the water returns through weeps in the brick, through root channels, through condensing steam, through the very ground. The pause is as much mine as the push. The keeper calls that pause the rhythm. The old father at the window names its changes like weather: *That’s the intake, and that’s the valve coughing.* I do not hear words. I feel them as a tap in the floor, a change in pressure, a settling of the wall.

The children of Sumpfold know me before they know my name. They fall asleep to me. They wake when a stroke comes late or too soft. They press their hands to the warm bricks of the pump house in summer, and they learn early that the pump is never allowed to stop. One of them polishes the brass gauge until it gleams, though the light in here is green and dim. I am not their parent. I am their clock, their lullaby, the deep note under every floorboard.

The tenders on the upper walkways move through the canopy like people moving through a crowded market. I feel their shears through the roots and beams as small, sharp interruptions. When they prune a branch, the weight above shifts a little. When a prop root hardens into a pillar, the whole district settles more deeply onto itself. The older walls are brick below and living wood above, and I lean into them through the piles driven deep into marsh silt, down where the black water waits.

At night the lamps spill yellow across the sump. The heron stands grey on the weir. The eels come out and cross the water in long, deliberate curves. The water-skaters dimple the surface. The snails graze the lower boards. I keep turning through all of it, red and heavy, sending the slow shock through the water that tells every resident the district is still working.

I do not dream, but I have a kind of memory made of load and heat and water. I remember the winter the intake silted and the strokes came hard. I remember the season a root grew too near and had to be trained around the pipe instead of cut. I remember the small hand on my casing when a child thought no one was looking. I remember as a machine remembers: not as story, but as a history of pressure and repair.

If you came down the old dike stairs and stood outside the pump house, you would not see me all at once. You would hear me first. You would feel the boardwalk hum under your feet. You would look through the tall arched windows and catch my red rim passing in the dark, and you would understand that Sumpfold is not a ruin. It is a living settlement held in time by a heartbeat.

I would find myself there, under the green canopy and above the black sump, inside the warm brick hall with the copper roof gone green. I would be the flywheel—the one resident that never rests and must never stop. And I would keep on turning, lifting the low water back into the world above, because Sumpfold is built around my pulse, and my pulse is Sumpfold’s.
