Before I am anything else, I am the dampness. It is on my face, my hands, the bare soles of my feet. I am somewhere high, because the air is thin enough that each breath has a small edge to it, and the sound of water reaches me from below, not around. There is no shadow at my feet. The light is an even grey, like a sheet of frosted glass laid over the whole basin. I do not yet know if I am standing or sitting.

My hand, when I notice it, is closed around a smooth staff of willow. The wood is cool and slightly bowed, polished by long handling, but not worn away. My mouth is slightly open. The breath that comes out is visible for a moment, then disperses into the fog. I do not know how long I have been doing this.

For a time I think I might be the woman with the jar. I see her far below on the central stair, a dark figure against the black stone, the clay jar held against her hip. Her feet know the steps absolutely. She descends to the lower seep, stoops under a lip of rock, and the jar fills to the same mark it has always filled to. Then she climbs back and pours the water into a stone channel. A thin residue stays in the bottom of the jar. She turns and goes down again. The rhythm is exact. It pulls at something in my chest. But my feet are still on the stone of the high platform. I am not descending. The woman passes below me and does not look up.

I might be the child by the lower pools, the one kneeling with a stick, touching the surface of the water. Rings spread, then vanish. He does it again. His face has the deep attention of someone listening to a note only he can hear. But I am not down there. My knees are not wet from the pool’s edge. I am above, and my gaze rests on him like the fog rests on the far wall.

I might be the weaver in the doorway. The shuttle travels left to right, right to left. The weft is pressed into place with a bone knife. The cloth does not grow. But my hands are not moving. They are still on the willow staff. The weaver’s hands move in a rhythm I recognize, but I am not inside them.

I might be the moss-tender kneeling on a leather pad, lifting a fern frond with a copper awl, touching the moss as if feeling for a pulse. But I am not kneeling on leather. I am on bare stone. The platform is high and uncovered, and the stone is sharp enough to cut skin, though it does not cut mine.

Then I see the other watchers. They sit or stand on platforms like this one, staffs in hand, faces turned toward the far wall or the fog. One is a thin man with a copper ring in his ear. His mouth is slightly open, and his breath condenses and disperses in the cool air. He is not scanning the horizon. He is not expecting anything. As soon as I notice him, I understand that I am one of them. I am the watcher. The staff is mine. The copper ring is cold against my ear. My mouth is open just enough to let the breath come and go without a sound.

I do not count the drips, not exactly. The counting has sunk deeper than thought. The drip is a low note, always the same pitch, always the same interval. It is not music. It is another form of water. The fog does not move. The far wall is a darker grey at the edge of sight. I know its shape the way a tongue knows the shape of a tooth.

Below, the town is scattered across the terraces like a handful of dark seeds. The buildings are dry-stone and timber, roofed with moss, hugging the cliffs and bridging narrow fissures. The green pockets are loud with moss and fern, but the sound is only water: a faint drip from every ledge, a trickle in the channels, the plink of a drop into a cistern. Nothing else. No wind, no insect drone, no mechanical noise. When a bird calls, the note is low and liquid, and then the quiet closes back over it.

The woman with the jar is descending again. I know exactly when she will stoop. I know the pause before she pours. The children play by the lower pools. The girl follows a heron from pool to pool. The heron steps; the girl steps. The distance between them never closes. The boy touches the water with his stick. Rings spread and vanish. He is not bored. He is listening. The weaver’s shuttle passes. The moss-tenders kneel. The shrine-keepers pour water from one bowl to another and back again. The old people sit by the high seeps and part their hair with bone combs. All of it is held.

I am not watching them the way a guard watches, or a god. I am not waiting for something to change. I am the gaze that rests on the fog and holds it still. Or perhaps the fog holds my gaze still. It is hard to say which is the weight and which is the scale. The basin is a set of balances, and I am one of the small weights: my attention on one side, the far wall on the other. The woman carries water from the seep to the channel; I carry the distance from the platform to the fog. The weaver’s shuttle passes left to right; my breath passes in and out. The child’s stick touches the pool; my gaze touches the fog. Each action is balanced by an equal and opposite action elsewhere.

If you climb up beside me, I will not turn my head. I will not acknowledge you. But you will feel that your presence has been accounted for, the way a stone in a water channel is accounted for. You may ask who I am. I will not answer. If I did, the answer would be the same each time, and the pause before it would be exact.

I am not a person who watches. I am the watching itself. A note that has been struck once and is still sounding. A weight that keeps the fog from drifting. I am on the high platform, with a staff of willow and a copper ring, where the basin looks out and sees nothing changing, and is content.

The water is held. I am held with it.
