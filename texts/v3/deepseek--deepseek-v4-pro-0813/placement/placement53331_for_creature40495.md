I dreamt I was a terrace, and when I woke the mineral water had already begun to climb my fingers. No—it was only the warm spill from a new vent, running between them. My hand is still flesh. My hand is still mine.

For a long moment I am not sure what I am. I could be a film on the wet rock, a mat of sulfur-yellow eating the water. I could be a grazer, a bird, a fox. The Kettle is loud with all of them. Then the body returns: the wide flat feet, the calluses like a living sandal, the salt crust around my eyes.

The air tells me before my eyes open: warm, wet, pushing back against my face. I am down in the Kettle. The light is ordinary white daylight, but it comes through so much steam and mineral dust that it feels like something I could press my palm against. I flex my toes. The stone beneath me is not dry and not flooded. It is damp, always damp, as if the whole place has just exhaled.

I came down by the old switchback path once, but the Kettle has been laying new stone over that memory. Now I am not an outsider. My skin is the color of wet travertine, darker where mineral dust has settled into creases. There is salt painted around my eyes—white, crusted, slightly tight when I squint. I remember putting it there, or maybe I remember someone teaching me. It keeps the steam from blinding me. It also says: listener, not hunter. Not a rope-maker. Not a climber of rookeries.

I am one of the small people. We tend no fields and herd nothing. I carry my tools on my belt: obsidian flakes wrapped in sinew, a bone needle, coiled cord. The cord is my map. Every knot is a turn, a warning, a source of food. Some of the knots are old enough that I no longer remember what they warned about. That is all right. The Kettle will teach me again when I reach that place.

An elder taught me to read the terraces. A faint crack means a new vent will open. Rust mats turning pale blue means the water has shifted and the path is no longer safe. A pool that holds light rather than images can be trusted for salt, but not for drinking. I learned these things the way other people might learn seasons or stars. The stone is my weather.

Now I walk—not straight, but slightly sideways to the steam, the way the long-legged birds do. The ground is new under me. Travertine terraces spread like steps of pale, wet bone. Sinter cones grow at the mouths of vents, some no taller than my hand, others taller than a person. In the distance a dust devil mixes with a steam column, and rain comes in a warm burst, then stops, leaving every surface slick.

I pass bands of grazers, small and shelled like buttons, their feathery legs sweeping the film from the rock. They leave a glowing blue-green trail in the microbial mat. It will fade in hours. I step around it, not because it is sacred, but because it is a record. The Kettle is full of records, and most of them are still being written.

At the mud pot I sit. It is a wide mouth of gray clay, breathing. Its rhythm is not steady. I tap the ground with two fingers: one short, one long, a pause. The pot answers. Plop, hiss, plop. It might be language or coincidence. It sounds, for a moment, like my name—or the only name I need. I laugh, or maybe I only exhale through the steam. The sound joins the hiss and the clatter.

This is the place I would find myself. It is not a camp, not a landmark, not a place I can keep. It is where the ground answers. I am the woman with white salt around her eyes. I am a reader of stone. I am not the Kettle’s master. I am another kind of mineral, slow to settle, quick to read.

If I stop moving, the others will lay me on a warm terrace. My tools will be arranged around me, and the mineral water will climb. In a season my bones will be armored; in a year I will be a low mound; in a lifetime I will be part of a terrace. They will say I have become stone. That is not an ending. It is the one way to stay.

For now, I tap again. The mud pot pops back. I do not need to know if it is language. The Kettle goes on making itself. I go on being made.
