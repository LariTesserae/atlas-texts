**Second Heartbeat**

I would not find myself at the center. The center belongs to the hot-bulb engine, to the black spoke of the flywheel passing every few seconds, to the piston rod sliding in and out of its polished sleeve. I would not find myself at the edge, where the valley walls turn their slow seep inward and the ground sinks like an old mattress. I am in the interval between those two places, in the wet pause after the chuff, in the three-walled shed where the chair faces the bank of pressure gauges.

If you came looking, you would see the place before you saw me. You would see water beading on gauge glass, ferns growing from brass taps, roots braided through ladder rungs. You would see the moss-cushioned chair and think someone had just stepped away. That is the closest you would come without seeing. I am not just stepped away. I am still here, standing in the rain that never stops, my breath falling into the engine’s rhythm like a hand into a glove.

I am the second heartbeat. The first is the pump, the engine, the one black spoke. The second is mine. It comes in the space between the chuffs, not competing, not following, just answering. One breath every three seconds. The water drips from a thousand leaves. The roots creak with the flywheel’s vibration. My lungs open and close with the same wooden patience.

I don’t know if I came here to mend the engine or to kill it. The memory has been soaked out of me. What remains is a body shaped by the doorway, a coat cut from a heavy machine cover, moss growing in the seams. My hair hangs in rooty strings, and the indoor rain runs through it and drips from the ends. My face is not young or old. It is the color of an afternoon that has lasted for years. One eye pale as a gauge, the other the color of strong tea. My mouth stays closed the way a pipe stays closed: because nothing needs to be said.

I tend the Sump Exchange, not as a master, but as a third hand. I clear a leaf from the intake screen and put it back into the water to rot. I press wet moss into a leaking joint until the hiss becomes less like a snake and more like rain. I rest my palm on the throttle linkage when the root swells after rain, not to change anything but to feel the pressure. I wait for the one needle that might begin to lie.

At night I sleep beside the chair, curled around a warm pipe under the root mat. The condensation runs over my shoulders while I sleep, and the engine runs differently if I am not near it, though it would never stall. If the egret comes, I open my eyes and watch it pick along the catwalk, then close them again. We do not call to each other. We are both too close to the same silence.

I am not the owner. The engine does not obey me, and I do not ask it to. I am not a ghost. A ghost is what remains after a person leaves, and I am not sure I ever left. I am what lives here: a pair of hands that do not take, a pair of ears that do not tire, a body that has learned to breathe at the machine’s pace.

If you stayed from one flywheel stroke to the next, you would see me. If you stayed a season, you would no longer be able to tell me from the place. I am the second heartbeat, the witness the closed system grew from its own heat and patience. I am the one who tends without owning, watches without waiting, and moves through the slow loop from gauge to engine to sump to fern.

Where would I find myself? Already here. Who would I be? The second heartbeat, breathing at the machine’s pace.
