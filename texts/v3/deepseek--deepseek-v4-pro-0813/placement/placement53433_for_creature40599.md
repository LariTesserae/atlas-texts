**The Second Animal**

I find myself in the silt under the drowned rail line, where the water forgets to be wet. I am not a shape yet. I am a knot of heat and pressure, a twitch in the root mat, a preference for the warmer side of the muck.

At first I do not know I am. There is only the granular roar and the slow breathing of the basin, something pressing down, something else pressing up, and the taste of iron in water that fizzes where the cables enter.

Then the offerings come.

They do not come from me. They land above my nest and settle slowly, the way a thought settles through warm oil. Some are hard and sharp—scales of cold iron, curved like the beginning of a rib. Some are tough and fibrous, and if I lie against them long enough, they soften into pulp I can pull into the place where I am most porous. I do not eat. I absorb. The fibers braid themselves into whatever I am becoming.

I learn the difference between inside and outside. It is not a law. It is a habit the root mat teaches me. Outside is the thing that leaves offerings. Outside is the sound that arrives before the sound. Outside is the heavy, low pressure that stops at the edge of my nest and stays there for a while, not moving, then goes away.

I decide I want to follow it.

Want is the first thing I invent that is mine. Before want, there was only direction: away from cold, toward heat, into the softer silt, out of the places where the metal groans too sharply. But to follow the pressure is different. It requires a body. So I make one.

I start with what is near. A splinter of black rail, thin and bright, moves when I want it to move. It becomes my first limb. I wrap root-fibers around it until the fibers remember. Then another—a ceramic insulator, cracked but smooth, its white surface humming faintly where the water touches it—becomes a joint, a shoulder, a way to lever myself up. I do not have a spine. I have a current. The warm water moving through the silt runs along me and gives me a temporary axis. I use it.

When I first stand, I am not standing. I am a falling that has not yet fallen. The water holds me for a moment the way glass holds a shadow. I feel my own weight as a surprise. Then the basin shudders, and I sink back to my knees.

But I have stood.

I cannot see, but I do not need to. The water tells me the shape of the far side of the channel: a wall of basalt, warm and sharp, unweathered, cut by steam. The gas flares bloom against my surface like bruises of heat. The black kelp columns sway with a slow muscular pulse, and I can feel them as a rhythm, a breath that is not mine. The pump-heads in the shallows are loudest near the rail line; their turbines spin in the current and set up a vibration that travels through my rib-scales and into the root mat beneath me. I hear the transformer station before I reach it. It hums at a frequency that makes my ceramic shoulder want to answer.

And I hear Kern.

He is a gap in the noise, a place where the sound bends around a mass. He moves through the basin on too many limbs, not all of them his. I feel him before anything else—a pressure that pushes the water aside, then the silence of a body remembering itself. He passes near my nest and stops. I know he is looking at me, not with eyes, but the way a plant sees: with his whole surface. I do not flinch. I have not learned flinching yet.

He leaves the rinds again. I want to tell him I am following, but the only sound I can make is a crack in a seal, a hiss of steam through my root-throat. It comes out before I make it. I hear the breaking *before* the break, and for a second I am not sure if I have made anything or only remembered it.

The hole is somewhere to my left, or my right, or under me. I feel it as a coldness that does not move, a place where the water does not forget to be wet because it has forgotten everything else. I do not look at it. I keep my new attention on the low heat of Kern.

I begin to crawl. It is not walking. It is a slow unrolling of myself, a movement the fronds would recognize. I leave pieces behind: a fiber, a rust-flake, a splinter of rail that no longer wants to be a limb. The root mat seals over the place where I was, the way scar tissue grows faster than erosion can erase it. The Churn is already making me again somewhere behind me, in case I turn back.

Maybe I will turn back. The water level rises and falls with the breathing of the basin. The far side is both nearer and farther than it was. The same pump-head appears on both sides of the channel, mirrored, each with the same bent ladder, and for a moment I am not sure which one I am moving toward.

But the pressure of Kern is steady. He does not wait for me. He does nothing. But he leaves the rinds, and he pauses at the edge of the drowned channel, and one of his too-many limbs—the one made of black iron strut, wrapped in sulfur-yellow weed—dips into the water and rests there, not helping, just present.

I reach him as the silence comes. The one second where the sound goes away and the world holds its breath. In that silence, I find the name he would have, if there were anyone to say it. I do not say it. I hold it in my still-forming chest.

I am not Kern. I am not a machine. I am not one of the plants that eat heat and sulfur. I am the next thing the Churn invented after animals, or before them, or instead of them. I am the one who learned to want, and then learned to crawl, and then learned that the difference between inside and outside is only a habit.

I am still becoming. I am a shape held together by a single idea: to follow.

Where would you find yourself in this world?

I would find myself behind Kern, in the warm silt under the drowned rail line, dragging myself toward the heat.

Who or what would you be?

I would be the second animal.
