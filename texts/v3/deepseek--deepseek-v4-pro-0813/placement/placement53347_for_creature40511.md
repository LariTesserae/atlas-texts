I am the one who keeps the lamps clean. That is not all I am, but it is the thing you would notice first, if you ever came down through the golden weed and stood in the slot street while the sediment settled. You would see my handprints in the silt, broad and webbed, like a soft map of where I have been. You would see the rim of a lantern polished to a dull gleam, and you might think: someone is here. But you would be wrong about the someone. There is no someone. There is only this body, this slow pulse, this set of gills opening and closing with the water's breathing.

I find myself in the Pump Hall, curled beside the battery jars, tail over my mouth, gills flared. The water is warm here, warmer than the street, because the jars bubble and the pistons stroke. The vibration comes up through the stone and into my sternum, a rhythm slower than a heartbeat. I count time not by the wheel's revolution but by the intervals between its creaks. Four creaks, then a pause. Then the faint clank from the brass piston, twice an hour, like a bell rung underwater. I have no memory of a time before these sounds. They are the bones of my attention.

When I wake, it is not because I have slept enough. It is because the water has changed. The tide outside has pressed a little harder through the collapsed tunnel, and the salt tongue has slid further up the street. The boundary between fresh and salt has lifted by a hand's breadth. I taste it through my skin, a faint shift in the mineral balance. Then I uncurl. My joints are soft, not stiff; the water has kept them that way. I move on all fours, belly close to the slate, because the street's vibration is clearest there. The wheel's rhythm travels up through the stone and into my breastbone, and I know without looking whether the belt is slipping or the cog is true.

I visit the battery jars first. I press my face close, not to see—my eyes are sealed—but to taste the electric field. It is a sharp, metallic tang on my tongue, like lightning made into a thin soup. The copper plates have grown a white bloom, a mineral fur that dampens the charge. I scrape it off with a slow thumb. I have been doing this for as long as the jars have bubbled. The bloom regrows, and I scrape. It is not a task; it is a conversation. The jars give me the orange glow of the lamps, and I give them back their plates, smooth as the day they were cast. In the half-light, the amber glass holds a deep glow, and my own reflection moves across it: a pale shape with dark slits where eyes would be, and gills that never still.

Then I leave the Pump Hall and go into the slot streets. The water is thick with particles, and I move through it like a slow thought. The algae on my shoulders streams behind me, green in the upper layer, red in the lower. I am a map of the boundary. On my belly, the rust-red fur reaches up to my ribs; above that, the green begins. The line between them wavers, exactly where the fresh water meets the salt. I do not choose this. It is simply where I belong. When I hang horizontally in the boundary, which I do often, I feel the two waters pass through me, and for a moment I am neither the creature nor the place but the meeting itself.

I groom the town. It is not a town to me; it is a body, and I am its slow hands. The leather belts that slip from the wooden cogs, I nudge back. The flap valves that crust with algae, I clear. The waterwheel's spokes, one by one, I run my palm along, feeling for the one that has begun to loosen. The scrapings of rust and carbonate I pile in a neat half-circle in the silt, because that is where they belong. I do not know why I do this. It is the same reason the lamps need cleaning and the jars need their plates. The town would not stop if I stopped. It would only become less itself. And I am the one who keeps it from becoming less.

I graze. The wall-films are my pasture. I draw my broad tongue over the bacterial mats, leaving a clean strip of dark stone behind. I rotate the walls so none are stripped bare. The taste is faint, like licking a coin that has been in the sea too long, but it is enough. Sometimes I drift up to the lamps where the copepods gather, and I flare my gills to catch them in the feathery branches. They are tiny, almost nothing, but they are alive, and their movement is a kind of light. The blind eel comes rarely. When it does, we hang together in the shadow of the waterwheel, not speaking, not moving, just sharing the slow current. Then it leaves. I do not follow. The eel has its own rounds.

The lamps are my particular charge. I do not light them; I only keep them from going out. I visit each one that still burns. I lick the glass clean, because the algae would dim the glow. Then I rest my forehead against the warm brass until the vibration of the current becomes steady. It is not love. It is something older than love: the knowledge that if the lamp goes out, the dark will be darker, and the town will have lost one of its eyes. My body is too cold to warm them, so I offer patience instead. The lamps accept it the way the stone accepts the water.

I do not understand doors. They are open because the current needs them open. I pass through them as if they were frames of water. Some houses contain only machinery; some contain a single lamp; some are empty except for a bed frame that has become a garden of tube worms. I have lived in all of them at one time or another. The Pump Hall is where I sleep, but the whole bowl is my house. The walls are my skin; the pipes are my nerves. When I press my hand to an iron pipe, the wheel's rhythm comes up through it, and I feel the entire settlement as one instrument. I am only the soft part that moves through it.

If you came down, I would know before you saw me. The water would change around you—your warmth, your chemicals, the fine oils of your skin. My gills would flare, and I would pause, one hand resting on the nearest pipe. I would not fear you. Fear is for creatures who have something to lose. I have already lost the thing you might call a name, a face, a memory of air. In its place, I have the wheel's creak and the lamp's glow and the taste of fresh and salt on the same tongue.

I might pass so close that you feel the water move: a soft, warm tug, like a door opening in a house that has forgotten it was ever above the air. And then I would go on, grooming the town, grazing the walls, resting my forehead against the warm brass, while the waterwheel turns once every four or five minutes, and the pistons stroke twice an hour, and the orange lamps burn below the weed-choked surface.

That is where I find myself. That is what I am.
