You do not know, at first, whether you are the sound or the thing the sound passes through.

There is a dark. It is not a cold dark. It is a dark with weight, warm as skin, carrying a low vibration that comes from everywhere and nowhere—a turbine somewhere behind the rock, or the mountain breathing, or your own blood. You lie still. The dark tastes of copper and old steam. Then a drip falls close by, and you know you are not the dark itself, because you heard it.

You are in a tunnel below the city. Not one of the new tunnels, cut with sharp edges and still angry with heat. This one is old, lined with mineral crust that has turned the walls into something between bone and coral. You are on your side, your cheek against the floor. The floor is warm. Your mouth is dry. You remember, slowly, that you came down here to listen.

You are—or were, before the dark got too close—a pipe-singer. No, not only that. You are the one who maps the buried strata, the one who carries the coil of luminous wire and the compass that does not point north but toward heat. Your name is Echo. You did not choose it; it chose you, because when you were a child you tapped a mineralized pipe and called into it, and the pipe called back in your own voice so clearly that the other children thought you had hidden a second self in the wall.

Now the luminous wire is still looped around your wrist. It has gone dim, but not dead. You shake it, and it brightens to a pale green, enough to show the tunnel. The walls are covered with marks—small inlays of different minerals, left by mappers who came before. You touch them, and they are smooth, warm, familiar. Gypsum. Halite. A thread of copper. Your valve key hangs heavy at your belt, its shape worn from too many turns in the dark. You are not lost. You are exactly where you meant to be: in the oldest layer, beneath the third condenser tower, in a street that became a ceiling.

You uncurl. Your joints settle with a sound like stone grinding. You have been down too long again. The others above will say you came back with pale eyes and a habit of speaking to the dark as if it were a person. They are not wrong. But the dark is not a person. It is a room. It is the inside of the city’s body. And you are only a small nerve, doing what nerves do: listening.

You tap the wall with your copper probe. The sound is not a knock but a long ring, like a bell lowered into water. It travels. You feel it in your teeth. You wait. Somewhere far off, the city taps back—a valve closing, a piece of machinery exhaling. You cannot tell if it is an answer. But you nod anyway, because in this place, listening is a kind of speech.

You begin to walk. The tunnel slopes downward, not steeply but with the patient angle of an old lava flow. The floor is not paved; it is frozen ropy basalt, smoothed only by the passing of feet and water. Your boots, with their braided wire soles, translate the heat into a language you can read. Here the ground is merely warm. Here it is warmer. Here—you stop—there is a cold shadow, and you remember the rule: never trust a cold shadow. You do not step into it. You wait, and the cold passes, replaced by the steady radiating warmth of the rock. Good.

The map in your mind is not a map of streets. It is a map of echoes, of temperatures, of smells. This tunnel will open into a chamber where a buried factory still runs, its vibrations felt through the floor. There, water will be seeping through a hairline fracture, tasting of old copper and sulfur. You will kneel and drink a little, not because you are thirsty but because the water will tell you where you are. Then you will mark the wall with a small inlay of celestine, because the new growth at the fracture is not salt but gypsum, and that is a sign the steam chemistry is shifting.

This is your work. You are a strata-mapper, a pipe-singer, a listener of the buried districts. You are not a hero. You do not fight the mountain. You do not build towers or negotiate with valves. You descend into the city’s old memory and listen for changes. You leave marks so that others, years from now, can read the walls by touch. You make the invisible layers legible. You are, in a way, a scribe for a city that is still writing itself.

But that is not the whole truth. The whole truth is that you came down here because you wanted to hear the oldest sound in the city. The sound beneath the turbines and the steam and the settling metal. The sound of rock being born. The mountain is still rising; the escarpment is still sharp; the land is not finished. Somewhere below, new basalt is cooling, and it cracks as it cools, and the cracks are voices. You have heard them in your dreams. You wanted to hear them with your ears.

You follow the tunnel until the luminous wire brightens on its own, picking up a change in the air. The walls here are not worked. They are new and raw, black as glass, sharp as broken teeth. The heat is stronger. You feel it through your boots, through your gloves, through the cooling tubes sewn into your middle layer. You unclip the water flask from your belt and take a small sip—not because you are thirsty, but to wet your lips and test the salt. The water tastes of old copper. You are close.

Then you hear it. Not a crack, but a slow, low creak, like a ship turning in ice. It comes from a fissure in the floor, a hairline that glows faintly red when you lean close. The new land is speaking. You kneel. You put your palm flat on the rock, not on the glowing part, but beside it. The vibration travels up your arm and settles in your sternum. It is a sound you feel more than hear. It is the sound of the world being unfinished. It is the sound of a mountain still becoming.

You stay like that for a long time. The dark does not frighten you. The heat is not a threat; it is a presence. You think of the city above, the condenser towers, the mineral gardens, the children called Drip and Clank and Hiss. You think of the pipe-singers tapping the mineralized pipes at dusk, and the valve-keepers dreaming of imbalances. You think of Halite the gardener, whispering to her calcite spires. You think of the dead laid in quiet sumps, their bodies slowly calcifying, their water returning to the sky.

And you realize, not for the first time, that you are not separate from this. You are not a visitor in the city. You are not even a resident. You are a part of its body—a sense organ, a nerve, a small animal of hearing. The city has grown around you, encased you, mineralized your name. Echo. You are the sound the city makes when it wants to know itself.

You stand. The luminous wire has dimmed again, but you do not need it. You know the way back by heart—not by sight, but by the temperature of the floor, the slope of the rock, the echoes that answer your tapping. You will return to the surface, and the light will be too bright, and the air too thin, and you will squint and speak softly to the dark for a day or two. Then you will descend again. Because there is always another layer below. Because the city is still being built, from below, and you are the one who listens to its foundations.

You are Echo, the strata-mapper. You are the one who finds yourself in the buried district beneath the third condenser tower, with a compass that points toward heat and a wire that glows green in the dark. You are not lost. You are exactly where you meant to be.
