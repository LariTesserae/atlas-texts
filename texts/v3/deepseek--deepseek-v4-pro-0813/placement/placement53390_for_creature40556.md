I would not settle on it at once. At first I would think I was the water in the shallow cup at the inhabitant’s front, because that is where the world turns to meaning. The dripping falls into me, and the rings spread across my surface, telling me the walls, the temperature, the pressure in the roots. But the water is already the creature’s perception. It is a sense, not a self.

Then I would think I was the pale many-legged thing at the porthole. I would climb through a hollow wall to test the air, and withdraw when a direct look separated me from the wall. But that, too, is only a limb of the inhabitant. Shyness, not identity.

Then I would think I was the open ground between structures, the maintained emptiness. The creature grazes the new shoots that would close the distance, and the space is kept as carefully as the walls. But emptiness does not say *I*. It is a path, not a walker.

It would take a long while to notice the bud. It is near the front of the inhabitant, just below the water-cup, a closed spiral no larger than one of its palps. Sealed, translucent, full of water. It does not drip. It only glows faintly when the light changes. That is where I would find myself.

I would be the next possibility, held in suspension.

I would be what the house has not yet grown. The unfinished arches are my grammar: they rise and stop. The ribs extending into empty water are my syntax. The spiral stair that turns a half-turn and ends in a blank wall is a sentence I will one day finish, or perhaps only continue.

I would not be the inhabitant. The inhabitant is the house’s first thought of an occupant, the place folded back into a shape that can walk. I would be the second thought, carried in the first thought’s body, still sealed.

Inside the bud, I would not see. I would know light as a change in the color of the water around me, from weak tea to silver-green. A violet flowering structure might be a faint pressure through the membrane, not a color I can name. I would hear the world not as sound but as trembling: the hollow organ tones, the creak of lignified plates, the soft tearing of root-hairs when the inhabitant pours itself forward across the wet flats.

When the inhabitant settles onto its platform in the long hall, my spiral would tighten with the rhythm of its breathing, until the dripping outside and the water inside me became one loop. I would be asleep and awake at once, the way an unopened leaf is asleep and awake at once.

I would not know what I am becoming. I would know only that I am the shape of an unfinished arch, and that the house has many unfinished arches. That I am the form of a spiral stair that stops at a blank wall, and that the blank wall is not an end but a pause.

And when the light dims slightly, and the inhabitant’s breathing slows to match the drip, I would not be separate from the house. I would be the house’s next thought of itself, still folded, still full of water, not yet ready to be lived in.
