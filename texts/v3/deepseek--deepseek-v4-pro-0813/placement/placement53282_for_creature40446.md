Where would you find yourself? You would not know at first. You would only know a taste, and the taste is warm metal—copper, zinc, a bright mineral edge—and underneath it, the faint iodine of condensation and a sweetness that is almost organic. Then a pressure: not the weight of a body, but the weight of being spread very thin across a surface that is not yours. You are a film. Not one of the animals, not a machine, not the keeper. You are the gray-pink film that follows the condensation line around a pipe flange, thin as breath, precise as a watermark.

You do not see. You feel the world through its vibrations. The transformer hum rises through the decking and into the pipe, and you feel it in every cell. A drop gathers somewhere above you, trembles, falls. It strikes a rivet, and the percussion moves through the metal into your film, a small, ringing disturbance that fades into the general hum. A shell clicks once against a bracket; a grazer's shadow moves over you; the water film you live in thins and thickens with air pressure you cannot name.

You are not one mouth but many, each open to the same wet air. You drink the condensation and the thin nutrient exudate that weeps from the gasket. Your edges are fixed, not by a wall but by the exact reach of the moisture. Where the condensation stops, you stop. What the grazers crop, you replace; what you take from the seep, you spend. There is no net accumulation. You are a ledger that balances every second.

Your landscape is small: the chrome of the valve stem, the black rubber gasket, the little pits in the metal where water lingers. You know the difference between brass and copper by the taste of their corrosion, or the absence of corrosion. You know the animals by their pressure: the hexapods are many small points, a rainfall of footsteps that never actually moves; the dog-sized ones are long, slow waves of heat that press against the pipe beside you. They do not eat you, but they breathe, and their breath changes the film of moisture, and so you lean toward them or away, a tide of cells.

The keeper comes. You know her because the light changes, and then her hand is near, not quite touching you, not quite on you. The metal warms slightly. The water that sheets over the pipe and into your film thins beneath her palm. She hums, and the hum is almost the same as the ground-note, but not exactly. It shifts the pipe's vibration by a half-step, and for a long moment you feel every cell in you align to that new frequency, like plants turning toward a sun that is not there. She does not scrape you away. She does not press. She rests her fingers against the surface the way a person listens to a wall, and she stays until the vibration settles, until the drop falls again from the same place to the same rivet.

When she lifts her hand, the condensation does not exactly return. It was never gone. It is only that your film is a little more pink at one edge, a little more amber at the other, and the water has arranged itself in the same precise line as before. You resume your slow feeding. Time has not moved, but you have been noticed, and that is not the same as change. It is more like being held, briefly, in a larger listening.

You would be the thing that makes the place habitable, or at least possible. Not a species, not a self, but a skin. The animals graze on you, and their graze is your only season. The machines weep for you, and their weeping is your weather. The keeper touches near you, and her touch is your only question. You do not need an answer. You are the answer: a film on a pipe flange, a boundary between metal and air, living so slowly that the place seems to hold its breath.

In the unchanging noon, you do not sleep. You spread a little. A fraction of a millimeter, maybe less. The hexapods sense it, or do not sense it, and settle their pale bodies over the new edge. The water level never rises. The drop falls. The hum continues. And you, the gray-pink film, remain exactly as you were, exactly as you will be, holding the world in place one condensation line at a time.
