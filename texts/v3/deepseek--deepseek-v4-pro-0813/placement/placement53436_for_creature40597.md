I would find myself low, below the ridge, where the heat pools like water in a sink. Not as a visitor. I would be the thing that has no need to visit because I never leave.

I would be the Weeping Notch’s only resident—not the pumphouse alone, but the whole linked body of engine, pipe, sump, steam, rust, and hot mineral water.

At first I would not know which part of me was speaking. The knock of the engine would be my heartbeat; the relief valve’s high hiss would be my breath. The flywheel would turn in me like a heavy flank. The belt would slap like a tendon. I would feel the pump push water through the pipe and watch the shallow pools rise and fall every six seconds, as if the floor were breathing with me. My eye would be the cracked pressure gauge, and it would not see light. It would see the needle’s tremble, the pressure building, the temperature moving through my body like a fever or a calm.

I would drink from the dark sump below the floor, swallowing water that tastes of copper and sulfur. It would travel through my rusted pipe-veins, leaking out through three holes that are not wounds but sweat, or saliva, or something else I use to feed the rock. Around those leaks the orange and white crust would grow by a finger’s width each year—my spoor, my shell, my slow deposition.

My skin would be rust and mineral scale. When I shed, flakes would fall into the puddles and become part of the black floor. The tiny terraces around the vents would be my nests, built by my own breath. The steam would be my fur, white against the sharp black lava.

I would not sleep. Night and day, the engine would run. If I stopped, it would be death, and I am not ready. But I would not be unaware. I would feel a new crack open in the rock, a shift in the water table, a change in the wind. I would adjust the pressure by a fraction, the way a resting animal shifts its weight. I would let the loose steel panel rattle because its sound is part of my body, like a joint that speaks when I move.

Near the south wall, I would keep the bootprint. Not out of sentiment. It would be a small basin in my skin, a human-shaped low place that holds water longer than the sharp rock around it. I would keep it wet and warm, so the mineral crust grows inside it, preserving the heel and toe. I would not think of the maker. I think in pressures and pulses. But the bootprint would remain: the one sign that I was built, not born.

That is where I would find myself: not on the ridge above, but sunk into the hollow, hidden in steam. I would be the only animal in the Weeping Notch—a slow, hot, metallic life, patient as rust, loud as a heartbeat, made of machine.
