At first, I am only a depression in the dust, a shape the cool air has accepted. The place where I sleep has no name except the note we give it: a low, rounded sound that begins behind the sternum and is sent out through the nose. It means something like the-cistern-with-the-broken-rim, but it is sung, not said.

I wake as the light goes from pale to gray. For a long moment I am not separate from the wall at my back, the crumbled plaster, the fine talcum gathered in the lines of my palms. My ears move without me telling them to. The left one turns toward the high causeway where the flyers are beginning to stir. The right one catches a herd-call from the north pan, so low and far it is less a sound than a change in the pressure of the air.

I uncurl. My fingers are long and ash-colored, dusted with the same powder that covers the leaves. When I press one hand flat to the stone, it feels less like touching and more like two quiet things agreeing. I wear nothing except a mat of dried lichen and shed fur that has caught on my shoulder. I do not shake it off. It belongs. My second eyelid slides closed once against the dust, then opens. I am not old, not young. I am a listener of the eastern terraces. There are eleven of us in this district, not counting the child born in the season of the thickest fog.

I rise and begin to walk. The stairs have worn into a long ramp. My feet know the way. I move slowly, not because I am tired but because the city itself is slow. The walls have lost their corners. The arch in front of me has sagged into a mouth. I pass through it, and my low hum comes back from the curve, slightly changed, with a hollow note underneath. That is the city answering. It is polite to wait for the echo to finish before moving on. I wait.

Dusk comes, and the first mist spills over the rim. It is not dramatic. It is a thin, cool presence, leaking down the terraces like breath. I climb to the high ledge where the hanging plants comb the fog. The leaves are pale and silver with droplets already. I stand with my arms spread and my eyes half closed. The dew settles on my skin, on my lower lip, in the fine hair along my forearms. I do not drink. I let it gather. Then I lift one wrist to my mouth and lick slowly, tasting chalk and salt and the faint green of the lichen. This is the first meal. It is always the same.

From somewhere across the city, a call comes. It is two notes, the first low, the second a third above, aimed at a curved wall near the old market square. By the time it reaches me it has become a chord. I know the caller: the one who sleeps in the colonnade of nine pillars, whose territory borders mine. I answer by pressing my tongue to the roof of my mouth and letting a note out through my nose. It is not a word. It means I am here, the fog is good, there is no predator in the lower rooms. The city carries it for me, bouncing it off the high terrace wall, folding it over itself so that I hear my own voice as if from a short distance away. We do not say much. The place says the rest.

Below, the lichen fields are wet with the first dew. I move through them with my head down. I lift a flat stone and find the pale flies sheltering there. I pick them with two fingers and eat them one by one. They taste of dust and faint sweetness. Farther on, I kneel by a shrub with small waxy leaves and pinch off the newest shoots. They are tough but full of moisture. The herds have already passed through here; I can see the broad scuff marks in the dust and the cropped tops of the lichen. I follow them at a distance. It is not a hunt. It is more like keeping the same direction. They know the places where the dew is thickest, and they do not mind us as long as we stay far back. One of them raises its broad head and lets out a low note. It travels through the ground and up through my feet. I stop and let it pass through me. The note returns from the basin wall, quieter, layered. For a moment the whole city is a single chord.

Later I go to the amphitheater. It is not for me alone. A few others are already there, sitting on the worn stone benches, their ears turned in different directions. We do not sit close. We sit where the echoes cross. The wind moves through the fluted stone, and the amphitheater sings in long, hollow breaths. We listen. This is the practice. Some call it prayer; some call it conversation. I do not have a word for it in our language, because the whole thing is the word. We are not asking for anything. We are not telling a story. We are letting the city speak. Sometimes I imagine I can hear the builders’ footsteps still going, very far down, under the dust and the centuries. Not their voices. Just the rhythm of their walking. But that may be my own pulse.

I am not the builder. I will never cut stone or raise a wall. My hands are not made for that; they are made for parting leaves, for lifting dew, for cupping the small sounds of insects. The builders are gone. The city no longer remembers their faces. It only remembers the way they dressed the stone, and that memory is kept in the tilt of a column, the curve of an arch, the slow way a stair becomes a ramp. I do not wonder about them. The city is simply here, like the basin, like the fog, like the sky. My people are not many. We live in the hollows and the doorways, and our bodies wear the stone smooth in the shapes of our sleeping. We are the ones who stay, the ones who listen, the ones who lick the dew from the old stones and wait for the fog to return.

By the time the first paleness comes to the east, the fog has thinned. The flyers return in a wave, their wings clicking. I walk back to my cistern. The child is awake, sitting in the mouth of the doorway, making the small questioning note. I answer from my chest, a soft echo. It blinks its dark eyes and holds up a hand glistening with dew. I kneel beside it. We lick the moisture from each other’s fingers in the gray light. Then I curl on my side, back against the crumbled plaster, and let the day rise without me.

You would find me there, in the-cistern-with-the-broken-rim, on the eastern terraces, listening to the last echo of the night.
