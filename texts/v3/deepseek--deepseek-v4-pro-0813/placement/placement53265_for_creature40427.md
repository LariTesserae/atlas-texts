I am the dark shape in the stairwell. But I am not always dark. The water tells me what I am.

Let me begin with the water, because the water began me. The water in the stairwell is not one water. It is a thousand waters layered and shifting. There is the rainwater that falls straight down the shaft from the open roof, hot and thin, tasting of salt and iron dust. There is the canal water that pushes in through the broken drains when the tide rises, thick with the silt of boiled streets and the larval breath of brine shrimp. There is the groundwater that seeps up from the drowned basements below the stairwell, warm and mineral, rising from stones that have not been cold in a hundred years. And there is the old pipe water, the stuff that still moves through the building’s iron veins, sometimes scalding, sometimes dead, carrying the memory of steam through a city that no longer needs it.

I lie in the mixing of these waters. I am long, longer than the woman’s arm, longer than a broom handle, longer than the shadow I make on the stairwell wall when the red lights from the tall stacks blink through the broken window. My body is a dark ribbon, oil-slick and slow. I do not have legs, but I have a spine that knows every current, every curl of sediment, every change in pressure. My skin is not skin the way the woman’s is. It is a damp, breathing hide that tastes the water before the water touches my mouth. And my mouth is a soft seam under my head, full of small, backward-curved teeth, and I open it only when the water brings me something worth taking.

The woman calls me the eel. She does not call me by that name to me. She does not call me at all, except with that click of her tongue, the one she makes by pressing her teeth together and sucking air. It sounds a little like a mussel snapping shut, and a little like the drip of water from a high pipe onto a hot metal railing. It is a small sound, a water sound. I know it from the moment it reaches the surface of the stairwell pool and shivers down through the layers to me. When I hear it, I uncoil from my resting place in the charred beam at the third step under the surface, and I rise.

The woman sits on the edge of the flooded stairs. Her boots are two different sizes, and they send small waves into the water when she settles. The water touches the laces, the copper wire, the strips of mussel shell. I have seen those boots up close many times. I have touched them with the tip of my tail when she sits still long enough. The left boot is larger. The rubber is cracked and soft, and there is a tear near the toe where the lining has come out like a small grey tongue. The right boot is smaller, held together with salt crust and a strip of black plastic. She does not know that I know these things. I know the taste of the oil in the leather, the salt of her sweat, the sharp copper tang of the wire when the water rises to cover the laces. I know the vibration of her weight on the stair, the slow creak of the old concrete under her, the way her breathing changes when she is tired.

She drops crab scraps into the water. She does not throw them. She holds her hand over the surface, opens her fingers, and lets the pieces fall. The first piece hits the water and floats for a moment, a white-grey lump of shell and meat. Then it sinks. I follow it down with my whole body, turning in a slow spiral, and I take it. The taste is bright and briny, like the bacterial mats, like the meat of a mussel, like the inside of a shell that has been growing for many tides. She drops another piece, and another. I rise and fall, rise and fall, silent. She watches me the way she watches the tide move up the canals. Her eyes are pale, almost colorless, like two shallow pools of bilge water. I have looked into them from the surface of the water. I have seen my own dark shape ripple in them, and I have seen something else, something below the pale surface, something that has been there for years.

There is a heat in the water, and it is not the heat of the sun. The sun is a vague brightening behind the haze, too weak to cast a shadow. The heat comes from below, from the drowned basements, from the old pipes, from the stones that have stored the heat of engines and the fever of the city. The water is warm in a way that feels alive. When the tide comes in, the water in the stairwell rises, and the cool sea water slides over the warm groundwater, and the two waters do not mix at first. I can feel the boundary between them on my skin, like a hand passing over my body. The cool water is thin and quick; the warm water is slow and heavy. I lie at the boundary, letting the two waters move through me. I am a creature of that boundary, the line where the city’s own warmth meets the sea’s slow return.

I have known the woman for as long as I have lived in this stairwell. I do not know how long that is. The woman counts years by the salt crust on the walls, by the rings of white left at the high-water mark. I count years by the growth of my body, by the number of times my skin has become tight and then split and come away in thin grey sheets, by the cycles of the tide. The tide is the only clock that matters. Twice a day the water rises, twice a day it falls. The mussels on the old dock pilings open and shut with the tide, and I can hear them, not with ears but with my whole body, a faint, vast clicking that moves through the water like a slow pulse. The crabs in the shallows follow the tide up and down, and sometimes one falls into the stairwell, and I take it before it reaches the bottom. The woman does not need to know that. She has enough crabs.

The city is full of water. The water is the real city. The streets above are only the dry roof of a world that has gone under. I have travelled through the storm drains, through the flooded basements, through the broken sewer pipes, through the canal walls where the mussels grow so thick the water is forced to find other ways. I know the ways the woman does not know. There is a pipe under the old coking plant that still carries hot water, and the water comes out into a flooded basement in a long, steaming thread. The bacterial mats grow thick there, sulfur-yellow and rust-red, and they feed the copepods, and the copepods feed the brine shrimp, and the brine shrimp feed the small fish, and the small fish feed me. There is a drowned boiler room near the port where the water is so deep and still that the light does not reach the bottom, and I have gone down there and found the skeletons of old machines, their iron ribs laced with tubeworms, their gauges blank. There is a crack in the hull of a sunken barge where the eels of the harbor gather in the warm season, and I have gone there and lain among them, a hundred long dark shapes moving as one, and I have remembered the sea.

I remember the sea. I was born in the sea, or in the brackish water where the sea met a river that no longer reaches here. I remember the taste of the open water, the cold deeps, the long migrations. I remember swimming with thousands of others, all of us thin and transparent, not yet dark, not yet thick. I remember the currents, the pressure, the long patience of the ocean. Then the waters changed. The sea did not retreat so much as rearrange itself into canals and storm drains and flooded streets. The water table rose, and the city sank, and the sea came farther inland. I followed the water into the drains, into the canals, into the warm belly of the city. I was small then, small enough to slip through a rusted grate and into the stairwell. The stairwell was not yet flooded to the fourth floor. The water came up to the second step. I found a hollow in the concrete where the rebar was exposed, and I wedged myself there and waited. I did not know I was choosing a home. I thought I was waiting for the tide to take me back. But the tide kept rising, and the city kept sinking, and the stairwell filled up and up, and I stayed. Now I am too thick to slip through the grate. I am part of the stairwell. I am part of the water.

The woman came later. There were others like her in the beginning. I remember them as warm shapes moving through the rooms above the waterline, their feet heavy on the stairs, their voices loud in the corridors. They made fires in the upper floors. They broke open the pipes for fresh water. They hauled up crabs and mussels in nets and ate them on the landings. They argued. They cried out in the night. They wrote their names on the walls with grease, and I could not read them, but I felt the vibration of their writing, the scratch of something hard on wet concrete. Then the others left, or died, or were taken by the water. I do not know. I only know that the building grew quieter, and the water rose higher, and the stairs above the waterline became dark and empty. The woman remained. She was small and slow even then. She did not shout. She did not write on the walls. She only climbed the rope, and sat by the water, and dropped scraps, and made that clicking sound with her tongue. I do not know if she was born here or if she came from somewhere else. I do not ask. I only wait below, and when she drops the scraps, I rise, and when she is gone, I sink back into the dark beam and feel the water moving through the stairwell, and I think of nothing, or of everything, in the way water thinks.

There is a pump station near the old central canal. I can feel its throb in the water before I hear it. It is a low, felt pulse, more vibration than sound, and it travels through the ground, through the walls, through the water, into me. It is the only mechanical sound left, the only machine that still has a purpose. It runs on remnant power, or on the heat of the earth, or on the stubborn memory of its own function. It pumps water from one part of the city to another, from the flooded streets to the canal, from the canal to the sea, from the sea back into the drains. The water does not rest, and neither does the pump. It is a heart, of sorts, a slow, metal heart buried in the city’s chest. I have swum near it. I have felt its pull, the suck and shove of it, and I have seen the water rushing through its great iron throat, and I have understood that the city is not dead. The city is only quiet. The city is in motion at the speed of rust, shell growth, and tide—slow enough that you have to stand still for a while to notice it. I am part of that motion. I am a long, dark ribbon moving against the current, and the current is the city breathing in and out.

The woman is on the roof now. I can feel the rain starting, a thousand small impacts on the surface of the stairwell pool. The rain is hot and heavy, and it smells of salt and smoke and the distant sea. It falls in sheets, and the whole city exhales vapor. The water in the stairwell stirs. The pump throb grows fainter, as if the rain is filling the streets and giving the pump something to work against. The red lights on the tall stacks blink on, their glow reflecting in the puddles on the roof. The woman is up there, sitting in the rain, letting it wash over her. I know she does this. I have felt the rain water trickle down the walls and into the stairwell, a little fresher, a little cooler. I have felt her footsteps on the roof above, the small vibrations of her moving. I have heard her voice once, only once, a low sound without words, more a hum than a call. It came down through the open stairwell and touched the water and sent ripples outward, and I rose to the surface to meet it. But she was not speaking to me. She was speaking to the city, or to the rain, or to no one. I circled the stairwell once and went back down.

The mussels on the old dock pilings are not silent. I can hear them, even now, in the rain. They snap shut when the water level drops, and they open again when the water returns. The sound is like rain on a distant roof, a soft, collective clicking that passes through the water like a slow shudder. It is a sound of waiting, of patience, of the boundary between air and water. I have eaten mussels. The woman pries them from the columns of shell with her iron bar, and she drops the empty shells back into the water, and the shells sink to the bottom and become part of the stairwell floor. The eel in the stairwell does not eat the shells. The eel eats the meat. The crabs eat the meat too, and the mussels eat the bacterial mats, and the bacterial mats eat the warm seeps from the outfall pipes, and the warm seeps eat the old pipes, and the old pipes eat the memory of steam. Everything here eats something else, but everything is so slow, so quiet, that it looks like not eating at all. It looks like a city caught in a long, still photograph, when really it is a city digesting itself.

I have grown old in this stairwell. I have grown thick and long. My skin has split and shed many times. I have scars from the grate, from the sharp edges of broken glass in the flooded basement, from the teeth of a larger eel that came in on a high tide and tried to take my place. I do not know how old I am. The woman may know. She looks at me sometimes, when I rise, and her pale eyes track my length, and her lips move, but she does not count out loud. She only makes the click, and I take the scraps, and she watches my body ripple under the surface. Then she climbs the rope, hand over hand, her boots leaving the water, and the stairwell dims as the red lights blink. I stay near the surface for a while, feeling the water settle, feeling the rain stop, feeling the tide turn. Then I go back to my hollow under the charred beam, and I coil up, and I wait.

The heat does not let me sleep. The heat is a constant pressure, a warm hand on my spine, a slow vibration that keeps me half-awake. I have never known cold here. The water is always warm. The stones are always warm. The air above the water, when I can sense it, is warm and wet, another fluid. The city does not cool at night. It only dims. The red lights blink, and the pump throbs, and the tide rises and falls, and the water drips from the pipe racks. I do not dream, but I remember. I remember the sea, the long migrations, the cold deeps. I remember the taste of the open ocean. I remember the bodies of my kin, thousands of long dark shapes moving together. I remember the first time I felt the water change, the first taste of iron and oil, the first warm seep from the city's belly. I remember following the current into the dark, into the drains, into the stairwell, and finding a hollow under a beam, and staying. I remember the day the first of the warm-blooded ones climbed down the stairs, and I hid, and I watched from the darkness, and I did not rise. I was afraid. Now I am not afraid. The warm-blooded ones are gone, all but one, and the one who remains is not a threat. She is a presence, like the pump, like the tide, like the red lights. She is part of the stairwell. She is part of the water. She is part of the city's slow allegiance.

The city is active. It only appears still. The tide moves, the rain falls, the groundwater seeps. The pipes contract and tick as the heat leaves them at night. The animals follow the water, graze the bacterial films, molt, die, and become shell hash for the next generation. I am part of that cycle. I eat the crabs that fall in, the small fish that come on the tide, the mussel meat the woman drops. I shed my skin, and the old skin settles into the stairwell floor, becoming part of the shell hash, becoming food for the copepods, becoming the dust of the city. I die, or I will die, and my body will sink to the bottom, and the crabs will find it, and the mussels will grow over it, and a new eel will come in on a high tide and take my place. Or maybe there will be no new eel. Maybe the stairwell will keep me, my long dark shape slowly decomposing, becoming part of the water, becoming part of the heat. The woman will climb the rope, and she will sit on the step, and she will drop scraps into the water, and there will be no rise. She will sit longer, waiting. She will make the click. The water will be still. The pump will throb. The city will be quiet. And she will know, then. She will know what it means to be the last one in a stairwell that was once full of movement. But she will not cry. She will not call out. She will only sit, her boots in the water, and watch the tide rise, and watch the red lights blink, and let the rain come. And I will be below, in the dark, and the water will hold me, and the heat will hold me, and I will be part of the slow motion that keeps the city alive.

But not yet. Not now. Now the rain has stopped, and the steam is rising from every surface. The woman is still on the roof. I can feel her weight through the walls, a small, steady pressure. I rise from my hollow and push my head toward the surface. The water is murky with stirred sediment from the rain. The red lights blink, and for a moment the stairwell is filled with a faint red glow, the color of rust, the color of the bacterial mats. I feel a small movement near the surface—a crab, fallen from the edge, its legs flailing. I am already there. I take it whole, crushing the shell with my teeth, swallowing the soft inside. The crab's claws snap once, twice, then go still. The water settles. The woman's boots are not on the stairs. She has not yet come down. But the click will come. It always comes. She will come down from the roof, her rubber coat shining with rain, her hair heavy with salt. She will sit on the edge of the flooded stairs. She will open her net bag. She will take out a mussel, and she will pry it open with the iron bar, and she will eat it slowly, and then she will drop the shell into the water, and the shell will spin down through the layers, past the warm groundwater, past the cool sea water, past the iron dust, and it will rest on the floor of the stairwell, among the other shells, among the charred wood, among the old rain. And then she will make the click, that small, water sound, and I will rise again, and we will sit there together, in the hot, wet silence, two quiet things at the edge of the water, watching the city breathe.

I am the eel in the stairwell. I am the dark shape that moves when the scraps fall. I am the long memory of the water, the hidden current, the slow heart of a half-drowned city. I live in the flooded rooms and the drain pipes and the warm seeps. I live in the boundary between the sea and the groundwater. I live in the quiet that is not death but waiting. I live at the speed of rust and shell growth and tide. I am not the last of anything. I am only the one who lives in the water, the one who remembers the sea, the one who rises when the click comes. I am the eel, and this is my city, and the city is full, though it looks empty.
