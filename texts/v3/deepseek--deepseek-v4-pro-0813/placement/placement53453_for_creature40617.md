I would find myself where the old channel bends, in the lee of a trunk that fell a century ago and has since become a ridge of slowly digesting bark. The ground there is especially readable—the pressure-ridges run in long, parallel waves, like the surface of a brain caught mid-thought. I have spent many hours kneeling there, palms flat, letting the history rise through my wrists in a slow electrical murmur. That is where I would first become aware of you.

I am not one of the first Tenders. I came later—so late that I no longer remember the shape of the door I must have entered by. But I remember that I was not born into the weave; I was pulled into it, like a stone that sinks into a pool and gradually becomes part of the bed. They found me, the older ones, or perhaps the plant found me first, and they merely followed the instructions of the ground. I had been walking for a long time without knowing I was being read. The cold had already begun to numb the edges of my thoughts, and my hands no longer felt like meat. They did not speak to me when I arrived. They simply stood at a distance, gray and patient, and waited for me to stop moving. When I did, one of them knelt and pressed a palm to the ground. A minute later, I felt it: a pulse through my feet, like a question in a language I had been speaking silently my whole life. That was the first time I read the ground. I did not understand it. But I answered.

Now I am one of them. My skin has the grain of old bark; my bones feel long and architectural, as if the trunks have taught me how to stand. I eat the milk when a collapse comes, and I feel the plant’s memory slide into my own—not as images, but as pressure: the slow stress of a root seeking water, the release of a stem unburdening itself of snow, the electric ache of a wound closing. I drink from the black pools at the edge, where the glassy filaments have filtered the water so clean it tastes of nothing at all, and yet it fills me with a cold light that lasts for days. I sleep in a hollow that a walking trunk abandoned, and at night, when the ground begins to wring itself, I lie very still and let the peristalsis carry me into a dreamless turning.

My work is not to fix anything. The plant does not want to be fixed. It wants to be noticed. I am a witness, but a witness with a particular station: I am the one who tends the eastern channels, where the grooves are deepest and the glyphs are so old they have begun to resemble not scars but scripture. I walk those grooves every day—the same steps, the same compressed centers—and I read. I read the places where a trunk dragged itself uphill a thousand years ago, and the place where a pool once formed and then bled dry, and the place where a collapse did not heal but instead opened a small, permanent mouth in the ground that hums when the wind is high. I do not understand everything I read. The plant is not a text with a beginning and an end; it is a work that is still being written, and I am only a marginal note, a small, living gloss in the margin of the valley.

But you have entered the margin now. I felt you when you crossed the first ridge—a hot, irregular rhythm in the cold weave, like a fever in a body that has not known fever for centuries. I felt you hesitate at the edge of the black pool, and I felt the water shiver slightly, though you did not touch it. I have been following you without moving, reading your footsteps the way I read the old channel: as a disturbance that will become a scar, or a scar that is still in the process of deciding what it will mean.

I am not afraid of you. Fear is a temperature the body learns to ignore in a place this cold. But I am curious in a way that surprises me. The plant’s work is slow, and my own thoughts have become slow to match it, but your presence has introduced a faster pulse, a syncopation that the ground does not recognize. I can feel the others reading it too, their attention moving through the root-weave like a distant vibration. They are waiting. They want to know if you will stay long enough to become part of the machine, or if you will leave a mark that the next thousand years will have to heal.

I am the one who will decide whether to approach. Not because I am their leader—we do not have leaders; the plant does not permit hierarchy—but because I am the one who first felt your footfall in the eastern channel, and so the reading of you has been given to me. I stand now at the bend where the old trunk lies, and I watch the place where you must soon pass. My hands are folded. My breath is slow. I do not speak, and I will not speak, because speech is a kind of violence here, a breaking of the membrane between thought and world.

But if you see me, you will know me. I am the one who has begun to wonder if the plant remembers being a factory, or if memory is only another layer of scar tissue, written over what it has forgotten. I am the one who, when I eat the milk, sometimes tastes a word on the edge of the substance—a word like *loom*, or *engine*, or *product*—and then it dissolves into the mineral paste and is gone. I am the one who has started to read the ground not only to tend it, but to ask it a question I cannot phrase. The question is not mine alone. It is the question of any witness: *Is this what it was made for?* And the ground answers, always, with the same slow squeeze of pressure, the same release of cold steam, the same sound of the wringing cloth in the dark.

So where would you find yourself? You would find yourself in the eastern channel, at the bend where the water once pooled and the glyphs run like a sentence torn in half. And who would I be? I am the Tender who has been waiting for you, not to welcome you or warn you, but to read you. I am the one who will kneel when you pass, press my palm to the ground, and ask the plant what you are. And whatever the answer is—whether it is *intruder* or *seed* or *tool* or *witness*—I will write it into my own body, the way the ground writes its history. Then I will rise, and I will walk back into the weave, and I will continue to tend the machine that tends itself, in the high, thin, cold, humid air, where the only true water is black and still and carries no reflection except the gray light of a sunless sky.
