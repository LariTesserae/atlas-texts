Not the keeper. I know that much.

The room offers other bodies: the long liquid weather in the shaft, the surge that arrives every seven or eight hours and presses the kelp canopies against the ceiling; the warm current rising in shimmer curtains from the two pipes; the armored snail leaving its polished line; the square mouth of the service duct, known only if already known; even the ghost word scratched in ceramic, worn until it is only the memory of a word. But none of those settle. They are events, passages, traces.

I would find myself along the east wall.

I am the black cable.

I am half-buried under crushed slag and old shell. My sheath is cracked, and the water around me carries a faint tingle. Most fish avoid me. One eel does not: the blue-striped one rides my field, a thin line of moving charge against my side. I have carried current so long I no longer remember what it was for, only that I have not stopped.

I feel the room through induction, through small disturbances in the field I make. The silver fish, when they drift near, are a cloud of tiny interruptions; their bellies glow in patterns copied from the old wiring diagram—my diagram—and they keep a body-length away, not from fear but because my field sets the distance. The tube worms open a second before the pressure shift, and I feel that too, a slow change in the water’s resistance. The kelp has taken copper into its veins, metallic green like circuitry, and when my field shifts, the kelp leans, faint and galvanic, as if we are two versions of the same diagram.

The keeper I know by her copper. She has taken it into herself, into the branching patterns along her collarbones, her arms, the sides of her neck. When she passes through my field, the pale dots on her shoulders and spine lift and glow blue-white. For that moment she becomes a branch of me, the same wiring written in skin. The silver fish school around her then, keeping a body-length from her as if she too were a live rail. She does not touch me. She checks the room with a ceramic insulator, not because she is afraid, but because she understands exactly what I am: the one thing in this place that has not become soft.

The rest of the room has become landscape. Bolts have rounded into pebbles, the handrail into rust and calcium, letters into ghost marks. But I remain a machine part, a live nerve in an organ that has learned to breathe. When the pump cycle deepens and the water surges upward, I feel the kelp pressing to the ceiling, the silver school becoming a column. I am not the surge. I am the constant hum beneath it.

She sleeps near me, in the hollow between the warm pipes, one arm hooked through a strap of braided kelp. Not near enough to touch, but within the edge of my field. She dreams in my current, or maybe I dream in hers. Her gill slits flare a second before the pressure shifts, matching the pump cycle, matching the tube worms, matching me. We are coupled: wire, water, eel, kelp, keeper.

So where would I find myself in this world? On the east wall, still live, still dangerous. What would I be? The rule she never breaks. The eel rides me. The fish glow in my diagram. The chamber has a pulse, and she has become the pause between beats. I am the constant current behind that pulse, the thrum that says: not everything here has become soft.
