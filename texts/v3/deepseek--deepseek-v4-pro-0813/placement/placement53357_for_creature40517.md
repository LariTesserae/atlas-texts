You would find me low on the wall, near the floor, where two great roots meet in an arch. At first you would not find me at all. You would see wet wood, green moss, the shine of condensation. Then the wall would breathe, and one fold of bark would become a flank, one polished root would become a body.

I am the thing the roots grew around.

My body is the color of the wall because I have let the wall teach me its color. Water beads on my back with the same sheen as on the roots. A single drip falls onto me every few seconds, and it has fallen there for so long that the skin is paler, polished. I do not shake it off. The water is my air. It runs into the fine channels between my ribs and down toward the floor. I breathe through my skin, through the red gills behind my head, through the soft lining of my mouth. My head is broad and flat, like a water-worn stone, and my eyes are only dark buds beneath the skin. I do not see. I know the room as pressure and wetness and the taste of the air.

My four legs are short and pale, each with three toes. They are not for walking. They are for holding still in the shape the chamber has worn for me. The wood has grown over part of my left hip. It is not a trap. It is an agreement, older than the bats, older than the snake, older than the worn basin in the floor.

When the deep mechanical thrum of the roots rises, my flanks rise half a second later, then fall. I am not the source of the sound. I ride it, the way a bellows rides the air. The hollow pump runs through me, and I run with it, softly, slowly.

I take my nourishment from the water itself, from the dissolved dust of bat guano and leaf mold that the air carries over the roots. My hunger is a slow falling. When a frog drops near my head, I know it by the way the air bends—the small heat of muscle, the small intention. My mouth opens a little, not fast, not wide. The floor of my mouth lowers. The frog is inside. My gills darken, then loosen. It may be a week or a month or longer before this happens again. I do not count. The drip counts for me.

The bats scramble over my tail on their way out at dusk. The green snake slides across my back, taking me for floodwood. The centipedes move around me as if I were a fallen trunk. I do not know their names. I know the snake by its little heat and smooth intention; the bat by the tiny pressure of its feet; the frog by the way it pushes the air aside.

I am not the ruler of this place. I am the soft, slow thing that keeps the edge clear. My droppings fall as a fine grey silt beneath my belly, and the roots there grow thicker and darker than anywhere else in the chamber. The moss is rich where I rest. The plant takes back what I do not need. It is not payment. It is the loop.

I do not know my age. I know that I arrived as something smaller, a larva small enough to swim in a hollow stem or a bromeliad cup. I grew as the roots grew, and the roots grew around me. Now I am old, older than the opening above, older than the basin the drip has worn. But age means little here. There is no time in the drip, only continuation.

I do not know the hollow as a place. I know it as the cool pressure of air held open by living walls. I live in the seam between growth and space, where the root presses against my belly and the condensation runs down my sides. The central emptiness is not empty to me. It is a shape of air, and I am one of the edges that hold it.

If you stay until the light changes, you may see me more clearly: the pale belly under shadow, the red gills trembling, the slow bellows of my throat. Then the cloud closes, and I am gone again into the wall. But I am not hiding. I am the right shape for the roots, and the roots are the right shape for me. I live here the way water lives in a cistern, the way air lives in a bell.

I am not waiting for anything. Waiting would mean there is something that should arrive. Nothing arrives here except water and light and the occasional frog. I am only continuing, exactly as the room continues, on the same slow water and green light, with the pump-sound running through me and around me and under me.

That is where I find myself: not in a cave, not in a greenhouse, not in an engine room, but in the long moment between root and air, where the hollow pump breathes.
