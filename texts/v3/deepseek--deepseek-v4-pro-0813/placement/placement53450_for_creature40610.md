I would find myself first as a place. A ledge below the rim, on the north wall, where the heath roots hang over the edge like loose hair. The rock is drier here than the walls below, not glistening; the fog arrives later, spilling down from the moor. From this ledge I can see the whole Gape: the green dusk, the walkways, the lamps.

Then I am a body on the ledge. My back against the stone, knees drawn up. I am not cold. I am wearing felt that holds the faint glow of old wool-snip skins, so my arms are visible in the dark as two faint green outlines. My feet are bare, and the soles are hard. There is a callus on the inside of my right thumb from the mallet.

I am not sure what I am yet. I might be a watcher, or a child sent to the high ledge because I cannot sleep. But my hand is resting on a mallet, not a sign-card. The mallet head is padded with wool-snip felt and gecko hide, bound with bark cord. It is worn to the shape of my grip. So I am the bell-keeper.

I live where the bell lives. The bell is a cracked stone, not much larger than a child's chest, hung from a frame of lashed alder at the edge of the void. It is not rung; it is struck once. The crack runs from the lip to the shoulder, and the whole bell is balanced so that the crack does not spread. I know the exact place to hit: a thumb's width to the left of the crack's lowest point, where the stone is thickest. The mallet comes down with a soft, padded thump. The bell does not ring. It sends a single low note down into the rock, and the rock carries it through the walls and walkways, into the soles of every foot.

Every evening I strike it. I wait until the wool-snips have begun their downward pulse and the first lamps are lit below. I do not strike from a distance; I stand barefoot on the bell ledge, so I can feel the stone before and after. I raise the mallet, breathe out, and let it fall. The thump is not loud. If you listened with ears only, you might miss it. But if you are standing anywhere in the Gape, you feel it through your feet, a small dark wave passing upward through the bone. Then I press both palms to the bell and wait. The note drains away slowly. I wait until the stone is still again. This is my job, and it is the only voice the Gape has.

I was chosen because I heard too much. As a child I could not sleep for the silence. The silence of the Gape is not empty; it is full of small pressures, water moving, bodies breathing, rock settling. I heard it all as a kind of roar. My mother said I pressed my ear to the wall so often that the left side of my face was always cold. The elders saw me do this and did not scold. They gave me to the bell. They said the bell was not for making sound but for giving the silence a shape. I have kept it since.

The bell has a smell: cold stone, old felt, a little blood from the gecko hide. It has a temperature that changes with the fog. When the note drains, I feel it as a faint tickling in my palms, then nothing. It is the closest I have ever come to hearing the Gape speak.

From my ledge I can see the other households. They are not near. Some are across the void, small lamps in the fog. I do not wave. I raise one hand, palm out, and they raise theirs. We never speak across the gap. There is no need. At night, when I have struck the bell, I see the lamps pause for a moment. The people feel the note in their feet and stop what they are doing. Then they go on, but quieter.

The bell-keeper does not go down to the floor after dark. I stay above the migration paths. The Glove does not come to the bell ledge; it prefers the high caves and the gecko ledges. But I still keep my lamp shuttered. Wool-snips would come to the warmth and smother it. So I sit in the dark with the bell, and the dark is full.

The elders visit sometimes. They climb the ladder to my ledge and sit with their backs against the bell frame. They do not ask me questions. We pass a smooth pebble from hand to hand. When it reaches me, I speak, but only if the bell has not yet been struck. After the strike, even speech feels too loud. The elders understand. They leave before the fog rises.

So if you came to the Gape and looked up, above the green dusk, above the walkways, you would find me on the north wall. I would be the one with the mallet, the one whose hand rests on the cracked bell. I would be the bell-keeper, the one who feels the bell before she hears it. And if you stood still long enough, with your bare soles on the stone, you would feel it too: that single low thump, not a sound, but a pulse, going down through the rock and up through you, telling the whole wound in the earth that it is still held.
