In Nooth, before I am anyone, I am a patch of cold air in a corridor that smells of old resin and wet stone. I am condensation on the upper terraces, the thin salt rime at dawn. I am the pause after a sap-joint cracks. I might be a dew-wife with copper vessels, or a child with chalk, or a goat at a threshold that did not exist yesterday. But the sound chooses me: a low, patterned whisper through a thousand collapsed vascular canals. It is not exactly sound. It is pressure, the way a dream presses into a room before it becomes a shape. I am the listener.

I did not choose it. I came up the funicular with the rail-gangs, a valley letter in my pocket and the wind already in my teeth. The first week, the flute-note in the high halls kept me awake. The second week, the silence beneath it kept me awake. By the third week, I was sitting with my ear against a wall, because the wall was the only thing that made sense. They found me asleep in a room shaped like the inside of a conch, and the brass cup had rolled into my hand. That was thirty years ago. My name is Ansa, but nobody says it. They call me the listener.

My room is near the old crown, where the plant’s former phloem spirals upward like a staircase for something with no feet. The walls are mineralised fibre, warm in one place and stone-cold in another. They sweat. At dawn they bead with fine dew; by noon there is salt; by evening the deep hall outside is slick with a clear, slightly viscous fluid. I know the rhythm better than I know my own pulse. The city calls it the breath. I call it the old lady’s voice, because I have spent thirty years pressing a brass cup to her skin.

The brass cup is not a hearing aid. It is a translator. I press its rim against the wall, and the cup fills with the old lady’s long exhalation, with the tick of sap-joints, with the wind’s flute note in the high vaults. My left ear is dead. My right ear misses nothing. I can tell a sap-joint from a speech: a sap-joint is a creak that builds over minutes, then releases with a small concussive crack, like a wooden ship in a swell. Speech is different. Speech comes in waves, too patterned, too patient. It does not crack. It curls.

I listen for the sound the old lady makes before she dreams a knot. When it comes, I send a bell down to the botanists—Olin with his doubled eye, and the grey ones with tuning forks and compasses that do not always point north. They come with chalk and string and red cord. They seal the worst knots with brick and resin, but they leave a small gap, because you do not close a dream entirely. That is what Hedda says. I am not sure Hedda is wrong.

The children call me little echo. They follow me sometimes, at a distance, because they are learning to be quiet in a city that is larger than its population by a factor of ten. I do not shoo them. I let them hear. Some of them will become listeners. Most will not. Liri, the knot-born girl, already draws maps of rooms she has never entered. She does not need the brass cup. She sleeps with her head against the wall, and the wall whispers to her. I watch her the way the dew-wives watch the sky.

In the mornings, I walk the upper terraces before the dew-wives begin their work. The light is thin and grey-blue, the sun bright but the air cold. The clouds move fast. I stop at the same places every day: the north wall of the old pump-house, where the condensation tastes of iron and burnt amber; the abandoned vault where the wind makes a low flute note; the mouth of the Hollow Meridian, where two rail lines cross at right angles in space but never intersect. I do not enter the Hollow Meridian. I listen to it from outside. It sounds like two breaths caught in the same throat.

The rest of the city is loud. The industrial quarter hammers and hisses, black smoke drifting from chimneys that poke out of the bark, smelling of dried sap and hot sugar. The rail-gangs tap their brake drums three times before the descent to the valleys, because the old lady likes a rhythm. The market in the repaired sap-chamber holds its own small weather; even from here I can hear its cloud forming under the high ceiling. The goats’ bells sound like rain on stone. But underneath all of that, I hear the old lady. Not loud. Persistent. A rope of air pulled through a thousand small holes.

I am not the only one. There are the dew-wives, who know the walls the way a nurse knows a feverish skin. There are the sap-cutters, who leave offerings of salt in the cracks. There are the mechanics, who touch a coil of dried vine before starting a big shift. There are the botanists and the church and the mushroom warden Bram, who knows which old lady’s ears are safe to eat and which will dream back. There are the goats who refuse corridors that have shifted in the night. There are the dead, laid in the deep heartwood, slowly closing over. I am not alone. That is the first lie the wind tells.

But I am the listener, and my place is here, in this room shaped like a conch, with my brass cup and my dead left ear and my right ear that misses nothing. I am not a keeper of the city. I am a keeper of its threshold sounds: the moment before a knot opens, the moment before a wall forgets to be a wall. I do not stop the dream. I only hear it coming.

At night, the factories glow in the eastern amphitheatre, and the wind blows through the broken pipes, and the old halls breathe. I press my ear to the wall and I hear the long, slow rhythm of something not quite dead, not quite alive. The city is a fossil skull with a small, warm brain of industry and ten thousand empty rooms. I am one of the warm thoughts inside it. I listen. The old lady listens back. And the breath goes on.

If you ask where I would find myself in this world, it is here: in Nooth, inside the old lady, with my ear against the wall. I am Ansa. I am the listener.
