I would find myself not at the door of the Choral Pocket but a little way inside, where the seam narrows and the sound arrives before the light. At first I would be the visitor—too warm, too wet, too full of pulse—a small biological arrhythmia in a place that has replaced time with a chord. My breath would fog once and then be stolen by the dry air. My heartbeat would fall against the standing wave like a soft, irregular knock.

I would not stay a visitor for long. The cold would begin to unmake me. I would become the moisture rising from my own skin, a transient dew in a world without water. I would be a small weather, a damp modulation the pocket had never held before. The resident would not mind. It would let me stand inside its throat while the air read my living name from my lips.

Then I would become a listener. I would stop looking for the source of the sound and let it climb into my teeth, my sternum, the small bones of my ears. I would kneel on the salt-and-iron floor and let my pulse phase-lock to the drone. I would not break a stem. I would not touch the flywheel. I would be the first silence the Choral Pocket had ever held—the rest around which the note could know itself as a note.

If I stayed too long, the pocket would not kill me. It would dry me, tune me, and plant me at the root of the copper pipe. I would become a bone-white resonator shaped like the memory of a heartbeat. The resident would re-tune around me, and the chord would shift by one small interval, permanently. I would not be a second resident. I would be a new overtone.

But I think I would leave before that. I would climb back through the seam with the note still ringing in my chest. For the rest of my life, I would be a walking echo of a place that does not change.

Where would I find myself? In the dry, loud, twilit throat of the mountain.

Who would I be? The only animal who heard the Choral Pocket and did not become it—except in the way a bell remembers being struck.
