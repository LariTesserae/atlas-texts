I would find myself not on the rim where the flat green land hides the bowl until the last step, but down in the center of the town’s wet gravity, in the room tall enough to lose its ceiling in haze.

There, the main flywheel turns once a minute. Its iron spokes are furred with moss except for one polished stripe where a wiper cleans it. A root has come through the wall and passes between two spokes. Each turn carries it down into the black water and up into the mist. Each turn squeezes a little water from it. It has worn a groove into the stone.

I would be that root.

Or not exactly the root. I would be the place where the root and the flywheel meet: the flexure, the groove, the slow exchange of green pressure and iron weight. I would be the hinge the town has grown around without ever intending to.

Each minute I would go under. The water below is cooler, thicker, tasting of copper and old mud and the faint electric dark. Then the wheel would lift me, streaming, into the warm mist. At the top of the arc, a drop lets go. I do not decide it. The sap pressure decides. The wheel decides. Together, we are a small, patient breathing.

I would not know my name. I would know the rhythm: dark below, steam above, the weight of the wheel, the wet give of my own body. I would know the pumps as a change in pitch I feel through the stone. I would know the ferns on the upper terraces as a shiver that passes through the air when the water moves differently. I would know the boiler hall as a warm exhalation somewhere to my side. I would know the blue-green glow from the drowned rooms as the oldest part of the town, still eating rust and gas and turning it back into mineral broth.

If you stood on the rim, you would not see me. You would see fog and leaves and the dim caged lamps. You would hear the thump and hiss and drip. But if you stayed until your own heartbeat slowed near the flywheel’s minute, you might feel me as a pulse under the stairs—not one pulse, but the whole bowl holding its warmth and water in time.

So where would I find myself?

In the high central chamber, between the spokes, being carried around and around.

And who or what would I be?

Not a person. Not an animal. I would be the town’s slow green metronome—the root laced through the iron, the place where the machine and the garden have become one motion.
