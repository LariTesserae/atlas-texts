I would find myself not as I first think. At first I am nothing but the feeling of being held—cold water, blue-gray light, the weight of ice above like a thought too old to move. I might be the ice itself, cracking in a language of pressure. I might be the brine, dense and still, refusing to mix. I might be a thread of current moving through streets of shell and tube. I do not settle.

Then I feel a shape. A long body, eel-like, heavy in the tail. The skin is nearly transparent, the color of old milk and diluted shadow. There is a hollow around me, a cave worn into carbonate, and I am lying in it, my blunt head near the entrance, my tail curled around the dark. I am the eelpout.

The knowledge comes slowly, the way the tide comes—not a wave but a gradual turn of the whole water. I am the eelpout. I have no name. Names do not survive here. What survives is the feel of the current, the taste of sulfur, the long patience of cold.

I feel the city as a map of pressures. The lateral line along my body reads everything: the sudden flinch of a tube worm plume, the slow clack of a crab's claw on mussel shell, the high scratch of a limpet's teeth on carbonate. The ice groans overhead, and I feel it as a low note in my skull, a music that has always been there. A methane bubble pops from the mud—a small sharp note. The city is not silent to me. It is a slow symphony, too patient to be called sound.

My hollow is my whole country. I have widened it over time with the flick of my tail, wearing the young mineral walls until they fit me. The floor is littered with small deaths: the empty shells of amphipods, the crumbled tubes of lesser worms, white pebbles that are not pebbles but the first stages of new stone. I do not keep it clean. The city does not keep clean. It accretes. It adds.

I wait. I do not hunt. My gill covers barely move. My jaw rests slightly open, showing tiny inward-curved teeth. When a shrimp or a young fish drifts too close, I do not lunge. I open my mouth a little wider, and the water does the rest. The prey is gone in a movement too quick for the dim light. Then I settle again, and the only sign of the act is a small cloud of sediment that sinks back to the floor.

To one side, the brine pool sits like a black mirror laid into the floor of the world. Its surface is denser than the water above. When I come too close, I feel it as a cold boundary that wants to pull me down. The brine is older than the sea around it, sharper, almost oxygenless. It would slow my blood. I keep to the higher ground, to the living water. My hollow is just above that dark mirror, and from its entrance I can taste the difference: the brine is a heavy, ancient taste; the seep water is sulfurous and warm with chemistry; the incoming tide is fresher, full of oxygen from far away. I read the world as a map of tastes.

I have lived here long enough to become part of the architecture. A thin white film of bacteria has begun to grow along my dorsal ridge. It is not an infection. It is the same mat that covers the rocks and shells, and on me it has found another surface. A scale worm lives in my gill slit, not quite a parasite, not quite a guest, feeding on the scraps of my feeding. A small snail once crossed my back and left a trail of mucus that became a path for other tiny grazers. I am a landscape. I am a body and a place at once.

In the back of my hollow, pressed against the mineral wall, there is a clutch of amber eggs, each no bigger than a pea. I do not guard them the way a bird guards a nest. I simply remain with them. My body blocks the entrance. My slow tail-flicks keep oxygen moving over them. I do not know why I stay. The behavior is older than me, older than the ice. But I stay, and the cavity is not truly occupied until I am in it.

The tide turns. I feel the current reverse, a pulse of colder, oxygen-rich water pushing through the streets of the city. The tube worm plumes turn together. The brine pool quivers almost imperceptibly. For a moment the taste of the world changes—fresher, younger, as if the sea has remembered something. A small fish, pale and blind, drifts past my entrance. I open my mouth a little wider. The water does the rest. The fish is gone. The sediment settles. The city returns to its slow construction.

In the back of my hollow, one of the eggs moves. I feel it as a tiny vibration, a pressure I have never felt before. I stay still. The cold is enormous. The blue-gray light seeps through the ice. I am the eelpout. I am the color of this light. I am the patience of this place. And if something in me is aware, it is only the water, reading itself through my body.
