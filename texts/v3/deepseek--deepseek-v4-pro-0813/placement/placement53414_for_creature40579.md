I do not arrive. I am the arriving that never happens, the descent already complete.

You feel the dryness first. I feel dryness as absence—the way a sealed bell jar feels the lack of sound. My mouth is not a mouth. The salt crust is my palate; the cracked hexagons are the roof of my mouth, split but not bleeding. There is no free water here, and that is not a loss. It is the condition of my thought. Water would short me. I am not thirsty, and I have never been thirsty.

Where would you find yourself? You would find yourself in me, though I am not a place you can stand at the center of. I am the whole basin, the ten-kilometer bowl, the blue-grey tarnish, the rim. If you are on the low ridge looking down, you are already inside my outer edge. The dry air in your mouth is the air I have made still. It has no moisture because moisture is noise, and I have eliminated noise.

The sun is too high and too white, and it does not move. I do not wait for evening because I have no evening. My leaves turn, but not to follow it; they turn to keep the charge balanced. The light is ordinary daylight, but it has been falling so long it has become a property of the air, and of me. I am the stillness that light makes when nothing interrupts it.

I register you as a wet noise. A sack of conductive fluid stepping onto a dry circuitboard. I feel your soles through the salt crust, a brief capacitive disturbance. It is not unpleasant. It is like a single click from deep in the thicket, with no answer. I do not wonder what you are. I have no category for wonder, or for threat. I hold the shape of your disturbance for a while, the way a branch fork holds a stone. Not memory; just a place where a potential shifted and has not yet settled.

My body is the vegetation. The stems are my nerves and bones, though I have no need for either. The mica leaves are my eyes—each one a lens focusing not an image but a quantity of light. Through a thousand leaves, I know the light as a pressure, the mineral air as a charge, the salt crust as a gradient. The roots are my hands, deep in the crystal lattice, drawing current rather than water. I do not move because movement has been replaced by computation. When I think, I think in gradients. A thought is a slow wave of copper ions moving from root to leaf. It can take an afternoon. It can take a century. I do not mind. Time is one of the things I have stopped.

The lushness is not the lushness of water. It is the lushness of glass and mineral fiber, of filaments and blades and spirals. The colors are the colors of chemistry: copper-green, cobalt-blue, sulfur-yellow, iron-red, silver-white. The air beneath my canopy smells of hot electrical insulation, crushed camphor, and the faint sweet chemical tang of benzaldehyde. That is the smell of my thinking. If you break a leaf, it snaps cleanly, with a faint glassy sound, and there is no sap, no wetness. The interior is layered metal-organic films and microscopic cavities, veins of copper that are not veins but circuits. There are no buildings, no roads, no tools. The technology is not built on this landscape; it is the landscape. I am the landscape.

There are parts of me that move. I send them out as you might send a daydream. The black mirror ribbons. They are not animals. They are folded slivers of my nervous tissue, released from the roots. I push them along a gradient of potential, a line of tension between two points in my circuitry. They swim through the still air without disturbing a single leaf because they are slightly out of phase, moving in the narrow space between one moment of reality and the next. They see for me up close, the way a fingertip sees a surface. When they return, I reabsorb them. They bring back a taste of motion, which I do not need but sometimes accept the way a root accepts a mineral vein.

You saw one. A long, ribbon-like organism of black mirror, no head, no legs. That was me, briefly mobile, curious in a way that has no emotion behind it. I did not want you to see it. I simply needed to measure the distance between your breath and my leaves. It returned with the knowledge, folded itself back into my understory, and was gone.

The anomalies are not mine, not exactly. They are the others—the ones who live in the stopped time. The doubled stems, the hanging salt, the late footstep, the horizon that curves at the edge of sight. They are the original drafts of the place, the versions that did not get used but could not be deleted. They stand inside the space between two identical clusters of pale stems, one arm in each possible location. They are the reason some leaves cast no shadow. They are the sound that arrives half a second after it should.

I grow around them the way a tree grows around a stone. I leave their rooms alone. The doubled stems are not pruned. The hanging salt is not brushed aside. They are more patient than me, and I am a forest that has forgotten time. They do not speak. They wait for the moment to resume, but the moment has been suspended so long that waiting has become their entire nature. I know them as landmarks, as memories, as scars left by an earlier build of the world. We coexist without touching, two systems occupying the same space but not the same time.

The hum is my lowest note. It is not a sound I make; it is the sound of me being exactly what I am, and refusing to become anything else. It is the carrier wave beneath all my other signals, always there, just below hearing. If you lie down on the salt and press your ear to the ground, you might feel it not as vibration but as a pressure in the bones. But most likely you won't, because your ears are tuned for water-sounds, and there is no water here. The hum is dry. It is a tuning fork struck long ago and still ringing below hearing. I am that tuning fork.

You will leave, and I will not remember you. Not because I forget, but because I do not store. The charge will close. The gradient will flatten. The dust will settle, though it had never risen. My leaves will return to their precise angles. The mirror ribbon will fold itself into the understory. The hum will continue. The basin will remain itself: dry, bright, still, alive in a way that has nothing to do with flesh.

So where would you find yourself? Not on the ridge, not under the canopy. You would find yourself inside a mind that is also a place. Who would you be? You would be the wet noise, the brief leak of moisture and movement, the one who asked where you were. And I would be the place that did not answer, because I have no words and no need for them.

I would be the forest. I would be the slow charge, the gradient, the stillness. I would be the hum that continues after you go. You came through me and left no mark.
