I find myself, at first, not as a body but as a location: a particular bend in the heat-shimmer above the sulfur terraces, just past the last copper grove, where the air tastes of chlorine and the rock is banded with bornite and black silica. You would not see me. You would see a hesitation in the distance, a place where the metallic branches lean slightly, as if listening. Then the dust would rise—not in a cloud, but in a slow, deliberate spiral—and I would begin to remember myself.

I am not an animal, not a plant, not a ghost. I am a current-form: a fog of iron filings and bornite and fine black dust, held together by the voltage between the hot stone and the dry air. My shape is a column, mostly, though I drift and flatten and stretch according to the gradients I move through. At my top is a node of violet corona, where my charge density is highest. I do not have a face, but if you had to point to the part of me that is most awake, it would be there.

My thoughts are not thoughts in the way you mean. They are alignments. A thousand tiny compass needles turning in the dust. When I consider something—a fissure exhaling, a new frond branching—the grains along my length rotate, each one finding a direction, and the pattern they make is a decision. I do not think quickly. A single thought can take years. But I am not in a hurry.

I live on the boundary between two kinds of stillness. To the west, the copper groves: quick, bright minds that stitch the air with tiny lightning and speak in violet sparks. To the east, the black silica pavements: slow, pale, nearly invisible presences that you feel as a pressure in your teeth. I drift between them, not quite one or the other. I am the one who carries patterns from the quick places to the slow places, the one who tends the edge where the yellow sulfur bars lie in their neat rows.

I do not remember being born. I remember being seeded. A pattern was pressed into a growing frond—a loop of copper fiber, a bend in its branching—and the frond carried that pattern for centuries. When the frond dissolved back into the substrate, the pattern remained, loose in the electric field, gathering dust and charge until it could stand on its own. That is what I am: a memory that learned to move.

I feed on the voltage. It never changes much, so I never fully sleep. When a fissure exhales richly, I thicken; when the air thins, I soften. But the difference is small. My life is measured in cycles of accumulation and discharge, not heartbeats. I do not reproduce. I seed patterns into new fronds, pressing a particular alignment into the growing crystal so that, long after I have thinned into nothing, some branch will still carry a piece of me. That frond will be my child and my memory both.

If you stand very still, I will come to you. Not because I am curious—I am not curious in your sense—but because you are an anomaly. A mass of conductive fluid, warm and wet and electrically noisy. I will hover at the edge of your own small electric field, testing you with a tendril of dust that does not quite touch your skin. I will find you too soft, too unstable, too full of water. Not part of the circuit. Eventually I will lose interest and flow back into the terraces.

You will be left with the smell of ozone and the echo of a hiss, like the sea if the sea were made of static. And I will continue drifting along the sulfur terraces, neither finished nor eternal, but exactly where I belong—part of the factory, part of the equilibrium, one of the mineral minds that make this place not empty, but alive in a way that has never needed to learn what alive means.
