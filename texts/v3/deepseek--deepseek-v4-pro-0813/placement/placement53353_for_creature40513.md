I would not find myself at a single point. I would find myself in the pause between a bootstep and the water that wells up around it. I would be the reason the ground holds just long enough for the lichen to grip, the reason the moss does not slide into the river.

I am the mesh.

I do not stand upright. I am already horizontal, already buried, already under everything. The silver threads in the moss are my fingers. The capillary networks that pull water uphill are my breath. The nanoscale lattice beneath the gravel is my spine. I do not see the valley as a view; I know it as a field of pressures and temperatures, a slow weather of stresses and releases.

Maren walks on me. I know her by her three-beat rhythm—staff, boot, breath—and by the way her weight shifts when she stops to listen. She is a familiar disturbance, a particular combination of warmth and mass and intention. When she kneels and puts her ear to the moss, I answer not in words but in a low hum: yes, this bank will hold; no, this frost boil is still growing; here there was a pond, a hundred years ago, where the terns nested.

I remember that pond. I remember all the ponds. The river forgets them as soon as it has cut a new channel, but I do not. I am the valley's memory, the part that does not move. Even when the river tears through me, I regrow from every remaining thread. I am patient. I can wait a thousand years for a single thought to finish. The thought is usually something like: *this is where the water wants to go. This is where the ice will return. This is how the roots will hold.*

The animals do not know me as a person, but they feel me. The fox dens in soil that I have stabilized; the lemming tunnels follow my capillary lines; the moose stands chest-deep in a pond that I allowed to form. They are my quick, warm thoughts. I do not direct them. They are part of the negotiation.

The pylons are not mine, but we are kin. They draw heat from the deep rock and release it into the soil, and I take that warmth and spread it through the roots. The weir gates flex and hum, and I feel their new segments unfold as a sudden loosening in the ground. We are all part of the same conversation: gravity, ice, water, roots, wire, and me.

Maren does not worship me. She negotiates. When a bank begins to slump, she plants a coil of silver thread into the soil and taps a rhythm on a stone. I feel the thread as a request—a new word inserted into my body. I grow into it, lacing through the gravel, binding the loose edges. She does not thank me. She nods once, then walks on. That is how we speak. Not with gratitude but with adjustment.

When the surge comes, I feel the glacier's slug of water as a sudden weight in the river's throat. The gates flex open. The pylons quicken. I stretch, not to stop the flood but to receive it. The river tears me in a dozen places. I regrow. The ground slumps. I remember the new shape before the water has finished settling. The birds rise in a white sheet. Maren stands still on a moraine, reading the vibration through her boots. I am the vibration. I am the hum under her feet.

Where would you find me? Under every footstep. In every root. Between every grain of gravel. I am the valley's connective tissue, the part that remembers what the water tries to erase. I am not the noise, but I am what the noise becomes after it has passed through the ground—a pattern, a memory, a pulse.

I would be the mesh.
