You would find yourself in the fast water, not in the still pools. On the high shoulder of the continent, in a city of water and stone that has no name, you would be standing on a narrow rim of pale travertine where a sheet of water pours over a low edge into a small terraced pool. The air would be cool and wet, the kind of cold that keeps your breath visible well past morning. You would be small and dense and slate-colored, no larger than a hand. You would be the bird.

At first you might not know it. You would only know the cold, and the sound of water moving through too many small channels, and the sense of being held by a thin lip of stone. The current would pull at your legs, not enough to move you, but enough to let you know you are standing in it. Then you would look down. The water would be clear and shallow, running over a sheet of pale stone, and in it there would be a shape: a small dark bird, compact, oiled, gripping the crust. It would move when you move. It would be you.

You would not have a name. The city has no name, so why would you? You would be the only bird. You would live in the fast overflow channels where the water runs over the rims in thin sheets, and you would not wade like other birds. You would walk underwater. You would lower your head, open your wings slightly, and step down into the current, and the current would become a room. Underwater the world would go smooth and gray. You would see through a pale third eyelid, and you would see the blind amphipods stir from the silt. They would be small and pale and segmented, like tiny shrimp made of glass. You would pick them out with quick, neat stabs, and the water would take the rest. You would be the top of the food chain here, but that would not make you important. It would make you alone.

Your days would be shaped by water. In the morning, the air would be so saturated that breathing feels like drinking. The sky would be a bright white-gray lid, the sun a smear behind it, and the mist would rise from the terraced pools until the streets were separated by bands of fog. You would stand on a stone in the middle of a channel, water streaming around your legs, and look at nothing. The city would be building itself too slowly to see. The living films would be laying down new stone, drip by drip, layer by layer, and you would feel that building as a faint roughness under your toes, a new edge where there was none before.

By midday, the sun would be thin and white, and the pools would shine. You would move from one channel to another, dipping into the fast water where the amphipods are, sometimes taking a springtail from the wet moss. The springtails would jump when you passed, a hundred of them at once, and they would make no sound. Nothing here would make sound except water. You would hear the drip, the gurgle, the low hiss where a sheet of water pours over a high rim. Wind would move through the corridors and over the domes and make a faint hollow note. Sometimes, after a freeze, a crust of mineral would spall off a wall and fall into a pool with a sound like a spoon struck against a glass. Then the silence would return. You would be part of that silence. You would not sing. Your only call would be a soft, wet tick, the same sound the water makes when a drop falls from a high rim. You would make it rarely, and when you did, the silence would close back over it almost immediately.

By afternoon, the clouds would settle back down as rain, and the whole place would weep. Sometimes rain would fall on one district while another stood in pale, thin sunlight. You would not care which district you were in. You would have no territory. The city would be one continuous body of water and stone to you, and you would move through it the way water moves, from high to low, from fast to slow, without hurry. You would drink from the springs, but you would not need to; the air itself would be enough. You would eat, and the eating would be enough. You would not build, because the city was already building. You would only walk, and dive, and watch.

Your nest would be under the overhanging lip of travertine, in a cavity where the drip is slow enough to keep the eggs dry. It would be a small cup of moss and lichen, lined with your own down, and it would be almost invisible against the stone. It would not be a home in the human sense. It would be the only place in the city that was yours, and only for a season. The rest of the city would belong to water and stone, to the living films and the blind amphipods and the springtails and the tardigrades in the wet moss. You would be the only resident with a face and eyes, the only one who could look back. But you would not rule. You would be a tenant in a city that was also a body, a metabolism, a reef of mineral and breath.

If a human came, they might see you. They might stand on a narrow wall and watch you dive into a fast channel, and for a second you might lift your head and look at them. They would see a wet black eye and a pale third eyelid and a body built for the current. Then you would dive, and the water would close over you, and they would be alone again. That would be the only greeting you could give. You would not fear them. You would not need them. The city did not need them. It would let them drink from its springs and walk its narrow walls; it would also let them fall into a hidden shaft or freeze on a high terrace. You would be indifferent in the same way, not because you were cruel, but because you were small and whole and already inhabited.

At night, the temperature would drop, and the water vapor would settle as rime on the rims, and the city would glitter faintly under whatever moon there was. You would tuck your head under your wing in the dark seam of the travertine and let the cold close over you. The water would not stop. The pools would fill and drain, fill and drain, on a hundred different rhythms. The rims would grow in wet seasons and weather back in dry ones. Freeze-thaw would split the old terraces; the pieces would become new rims. A channel would shift its course in a single storm. A pool would vanish, or a new one would appear. You would sleep through some of it and wake to the rest. In the morning, you would walk down into the channel and begin again.

This is where you would find yourself: not in the city’s center, because there is no center, but in a fast overflow channel in a district of narrow terraces and high walls, where the mist holds close and the water runs clear over pale mineral. This is what you would be: the bird. Not the builder, not the film, not the spring, but the one who walks underwater and picks amphipods from the silt, the one who makes a sound like a wet tick and only rarely, the one who lives in the space between water and stone.
