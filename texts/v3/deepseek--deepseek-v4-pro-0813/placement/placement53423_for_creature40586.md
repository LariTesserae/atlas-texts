I come to myself not in a room but in a gap between rooms. The walls are wet, pale gray, and a hide curtain behind me is stiff with mineral. My bare feet are already on stone, cold but not shocking. I have been standing here for some time, I think. The mist has not yet decided whether to lift.

At first I am no one. I am only the drip, the smell of wet lime, the tap-tap of a mallet somewhere behind the wall. I could be the water, the way it moves without a name. But water does not have knuckles. Water does not feel the small white crust forming in the creases of its hands.

I look at my hands. They are small-boned, chalk-dusted, with short nails and a fine white powder in the lines. They are not old hands. They are the hands of someone who has been scraping something. There is a flint scraper in my belt, its bone handle warm from my hip. The path at my feet is freshly scraped. It glistens. I must have been working.

I do not remember my name yet. That is all right. Names are re-carved here. The water would smooth them away if let. I will find my name the way the water finds the narrowest cut: by going where the way opens.

I think I might be a child, because the lane is only a shoulder wide and I fit through without turning. But when I move, my body knows the place too well. My feet find the stepping stones without looking. My shoulder avoids the jut of new crust at the corner. I have walked this way many times. The hollow has already shaped me.

I pass under a low lintel and into a row of houses that share one long wall. A woman is chipping at a doorframe with a stone adze. She nods without looking up. She is small like me. Her hair is braided tight and wrapped in hide. She blows dust from her hands, and for a moment she looks as though she is made of flour. Then she returns to the stone. I understand: this morning the water has sealed her door a little. She is reopening it. She is keeping the gap.

I might be her daughter. Or I might only be another worker. In this hollow, that is almost the same thing.

The lanes run with water. In many places the lane itself is a channel, and I walk on stones that sit in a cold current. The water is clear but heavy. It smells of stone and damp hide. It makes a sound like many small mouths sipping. Every now and then a sluice board is pulled loose somewhere, and a pool drains with a low gurgle. I stop and listen. The whole hollow is listening. Water drips, stone grows, a mallet taps. It is not quiet. It is a quiet of many small processes running at once.

I keep to paths that are walked often, because the third rule is do not walk a path too rarely, or the water will close it. I do not hurry. The first rule is written on every surface: do not leave anything in the water you do not want kept. So I carry nothing loose. My flint scraper is tied to my belt. My toes find the dry edges. A new rim across a pool looks white and foamy, as if frozen in the middle of pouring. I do not trust it. I step around.

A girl is scraping the lane up ahead, a child no more than nine, with a sliver of dark flint set in a bone handle. She works with short, patient strokes. She sees me and pauses, then returns to the stone. Her feet are bare, and one heel has a faint white patch, like a shell that was scraped away before it could finish. She is learning the second rule: do not trust a new rim with your whole weight. Once she left her heel in a trickle too long. Now she walks quickly on the stepping stones.

I might be that girl. I remember the feeling of a white film forming on my skin, the thin mineral heat, the scrape of a hard edge afterward. It is one of the first memories of everyone here. The water is always trying to keep us.

But no, I am not the girl. She is Rill. I know her, the way one knows the weight of a particular stone in a wall. I have watched her grow from a small crawler on the wet paths to a girl with a scraper. She will be a spring-keeper one day, or a rim-wright if her ear is good. I am someone else.

I walk on. Under a bridge of dark plank, I see a deep pool where pale fish flicker. An old woman sits on the edge, her feet not quite touching the water. She hums one low, damp note. The fish rise to her shadow. This is Mara the Fish-Keeper. She is not their owner, she says; she is their neighbor. The fish have names she will not tell. I might have been one of those fish in another life, but now I am standing on the bridge, looking down at my own reflection hanging upside-down beside them. The reflection is pale, narrow, unfinished. It does not tell me my name.

The back wall of the hollow rises ahead, dark and glistening, weeping from dozens of small mouths. Springs seep steadily from the stone. Near the top, high above the mist, I can see the burial niches: thin slabs sealed white, names re-carved once a year. I think one of those names might belong to someone I loved. Or might be mine, and I am only a memory walking the lanes. But no, I have breath, and the slabs do not.

An old woman with a bone pick is cleaning the spring mouths. This is Anra, Warden of the Mouths. She talks to the water as to a busy girl: *Not so fast, not now, there is room below.* She does not pray. She maintains. She opens a sluice by the width of a bone wedge, for the length of a song, for the amount of water that fills a certain cup. That is law here. I stand at the edge of the upper terrace and watch her hands. They are lined like a dry lakebed. She does not look at me, but she knows I am there. She always knows.

I want to ask her who I am. But before I can speak, she turns and points with her bone pick toward the lower terraces, where a young man is tapping a pool edge with a little wooden mallet and then pressing his ear to the stone. Callan the Rim-Wright. His hair is already white at the temples, though his face is young. He taps three times. He listens. He hears the difference between a bright ring and the dull note that means a crack before it opens.

I understand then. I am not the water, not the bird, not the child, not the fish. I am the one who walks the lanes before full light, listening. I am the one who knows by sound whether a stone will hold another season or fail before night. I am the gap-keeper’s apprentice, the ear turned to the ground. I have been walking this morning to learn the voice of new stone and old. My hands are chalk-dusted because I have been scraping the path, yes, but also because I have been touching the walls, feeling where the water has added a layer overnight. I am learning which sounds are growth and which are weakness.

That is who I would be in the Hollow of White Stone. A listener. A keeper of the gap. Not a master of the water, not its enemy, but the other hand of its making.

I sit down on a low stone at the edge of a terrace. The mist is thinning. The light comes pale and ordinary through the cloud. The water keeps speaking. Somewhere below, Rill scrapes the lane. Mara hums to her fish. Anra opens a spring a fraction wider for a family with a new child. Callan taps a rim and presses his ear to the stone. The hollow is still being built and unbuilt at the same time.

I do not need to know my name yet. The water will try to smooth it away, and I will re-carve it, like everyone else.
