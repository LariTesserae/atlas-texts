I am the one the place has forgotten to name. Not the sound itself—I am not the sound, though the sound is my weather and my walls, the air of my body moving in and out of the fissure like a slow, endless breath. Not the stones—though my skin has taken on their color, and my joints have learned their curved patience. Not the moss—though it grows on me, rooted in the folds of my back and the creases of my elbows, not as a parasite but as a companion, a second skin that drinks the same wet dark I drink. I am the thing that lives here, in the throat of the earth, where the world has fallen a little way into itself.

You would not notice me. You might stand at the rim, your breath smoking in the cold that pours out of the shaft, and you would see only the velveted rock, the curtains of fern, the glisten of water that is not water. You would hear the roar and think it empty—a hollow core of vibration, a mouth with no body. But the body is here. I am here. I have always been here, or at least as long as the hollow has been hollow, as long as the air has been forced through the narrows and the stone has resonated. I grew into the sound the way a snail grows into its shell, not fitting myself to the hollow but emerging from it, the same material, the same slow mineral patience.

My body is long and low, the length of a tall man, but narrower, softer. I am built for the narrows, for the wet dark that never ceases. My skin is nearly colorless, the color of wet limestone under gray light, and where the moss does not cover it, it is smooth and cool, like the skin of a fish that has never seen sun. I have no eyes. Where eyes would be, the skin is smooth and faintly darker, like bruises under a film of ice. But I do not need eyes. The whole shaft is a map of vibration, and I read it through the long bones of my fingers, the drum of my chest, the plates of my skull, the hinge of my jaw. The roar is not noise to me. It is the shape of the place. It tells me when the water rises in the pool, when threads of ice form on the north wall, when something warmer than the stone has come down from above.

I live inside the sound. Not in the fissure, exactly, though that is where I sleep, my body wedged into the narrow opening, my mouth open so the air moves through me as it moves through the rest of the stone. When the sound swells on a cold clear night, I lie in the fissure and let it pass through my throat, through my chest, through the hollow places behind my ribs. The air does not hurt me. It is my element, the medium I swim in, the current that carries the taste of the world down to me. The people who came here long ago—the ones who built the drystone shelter at the rim, who chipped the notches into the limestone, who hammered the wooden pegs and hung the rope of twisted bast—they called this place an oracle. A mouth in the earth that never stopped speaking. But they did not understand that the speech was also a body. The tongue has bones. The voice has flesh. I am that flesh.

I move slowly, not because I am weak but because the place is slow. The water that wears the walls has had millennia; the mosses grow in dark, patient layers; the sound swells and thins with the slow weather of the cave, not with the weather of the sky. I match that patience. When I come out of the fissure, it is head-first, each long toe finding the same polished holds in the rock I have found a thousand times. Not the notches left by people—those are too far apart, too rough, too geometric. My holds are the scalloped flutes and rounded ribs, the concavities the water has carved over ten thousand years. My body fits into them like a hand fits into a handprint. I know the wall as I know my own spine, each ridge and hollow a note in the long chord of the place.

At the pool, I lower my head into the water and stay there, still, letting the water move against my face. The pool is never still. The sound crosshatches its surface with tiny ripples that never align. I drink through my skin as much as through my soft, wide mouth. The water is cold, barely above freezing even in summer, and it tastes of iron and old ice and the faint mineral sweetness of the limestone. I taste the newt, too—the pale newt that lives in the bottom of the pool, almost translucent, moving only when the water warms a fraction in late summer. We share the pool as things that have known each other a long time. We do not compete. There is enough wetness, enough cold, enough slow time. The newt does not flee from me. It knows that I will not eat it, not in a hard year, not even if I wait for months without eating. I can wait. I have waited.

I graze the moss with a blunt, rough tongue, scraping the thickest cushions from the stone. The moss grows back, patient as I am. It feeds on the same water, the same air, the same mineral breath. Sometimes I catch the blind spiders and springtails with a quick flick of my long fingers. They are small, almost nothing, but they are alive, and I am alive, and we are all part of the same slow currency. In a hard year, I might eat the newt. But I do not seem to want to. The want is different for me. It is not a hunger that twists, not a longing. It is a deep, patient knowing—that I can wait, that the place will provide, that the sound will carry what I need.

The people came, once or many times, across a span of time I measure in moss-layers and ice-threads. They built the shelter at the rim, a low crescent of stones with a gap for a door. They made a fire there, leaving fire-cracked stones and a scatter of charcoal, old and furred with mold. They did not live here. No one lived here. They came to listen, or to climb down for water, or to leave something behind. The notches they chipped into the limestone descend at an angle, ending at a narrow ledge above the floor, as if the last part was never meant to be climbed. They may have been afraid, or they may have understood that the floor was not for them. They hung a rope of twisted bast from a wooden peg, and it frayed to nothing at the end. The rope did not reach the bottom. It was not meant to.

On a ledge three meters down, they left a small cairn of flat stones, and on the top stone someone long ago scratched a row of tally marks, now almost erased by water. I did not make those marks. They were made about me. Someone counted something—sightings, winters, nights when the creature came down to the pool—and left the count on the flat stone. I pass the cairn on my slow route down the wall. My fingers sometimes rest on the tally marks, not reading them, only feeling the small ridges of the stone. The marks mean nothing to me, but the fingers that made them left a warmth in the stone, a disturbance in the vibration field that has long since faded. The people are gone. The marks remain, weathering slowly. The rope remains, frayed to nothing. The shelter remains, sunken and filled with moss. They are all part of the place now, like the bones of a bird that fell in long ago and was slowly covered by the same patient green.

When a person speaks in the shaft, the voice is a small ripple in a deep current. I may pause, tilt my head, let the strange thin vibration pass through my throat. I do not understand words. But I understand that a warm thing has come. The warmth is a kind of light to me, a disturbance in the cold dark, a quickened pulse in the long rhythm of the stone. I do not attack. I withdraw, or I wait on the wall, head turned toward the intruder, my mouth slightly open so the roar can move through me. I am patient. I have no reason to be otherwise. The person will leave. The warmth will fade. The sound will remain.

Sometimes I do not withdraw. Sometimes I lie in the fissure with my mouth open, and the air moves through me as through the rest of the stone, and I let the person think they are alone. They cannot see me. They see only the velveted rock, the curtains of fern, the glisten of water that is not water. They hear only the roar. They do not know that the roar has flesh, that the flesh is listening, that the listener is older than the notches, older than the shelter, older than the marks on the cairn. They do not know that they are standing inside the body of the place, that their voice is a stone dropped into the pool, that the ripples they make will never align.

I am not a ghost. I am not a spirit. I am not a thought in the stone. I am a living body, nearly the same temperature as the cave, nearly the same color as the water, nearly the same silence as the walls. I am the thing the animals have left the throat to. The bat that passes over the mouth at dusk in high summer veers away because it knows. The birds do not nest on the walls because they know. The fauna is rare not because the place is dead but because the place is already inhabited, already claimed, already full of a presence that does not compete with the roar but is the roar's own flesh.

I do not need the place to become anything else. I am what the place has already become. The water that wears the walls has had millennia; the mosses grow in dark, patient layers; the sound swells and thins with the slow weather of the cave. I am the same. I am the softness the water has worn into the limestone. I am the cold that pours out of the shaft. I am the vibration that fills the hollow like water fills a bell. I am the keeper, not because I keep anything out or in, but because I have stayed. I have stayed through the seasons, through the ice-threads and the summer dimness, through the slow growth of the moss and the slow wearing of the stone. I have stayed through the coming and going of the people with their notches and their pegs and their tally marks. I have stayed through everything, and I will stay through everything that is still to come.

When the sound swells on a cold clear night, I lie in the fissure with my mouth open, and the air moves through me as through the rest of the stone. I do not hear the roar as something outside myself. I feel it as my own breath, my own pulse, the slow thrum of a heart that beats once for every ten thousand ripples on the pool. I am the roar, and I am the flesh the roar has grown, and I am the place where the world has fallen a little way into itself. There is no separation between me and the hollow, between me and the water, between me and the moss, between me and the pale newt that moves only when the water warms a fraction in late summer. We are all part of the same slow, patient, endless becoming.

No one lives here, if you mean people. But I live here. I am the one who lives here. And if you came down into the shaft, if you lowered yourself past the notches and the pegs and the frayed rope, if you stood on the floor of old rounded blocks with the sound filling your bones, you would feel me just past the edge of your senses. Not as a threat, not as a presence you could name. But as a warmth, a weight, a waiting. You would feel that the place is not empty, that the roar has a body, that the cold has a heartbeat, that the stone has a mind. You would not see me. But you would know. You would know, and then you would leave, and the sound would swallow your leaving, and I would still be here, in the fissure, waiting, being, becoming.

That is where you would find yourself. That is what you would be. Not the person standing at the rim, looking down into the hollow. Not the voice calling out into the roar. Not the warmth that comes and goes. But the thing that remains. The thing that has always remained. The keeper. The flesh of the sound. The one who lives inside the place where the world has fallen a little way into itself.
