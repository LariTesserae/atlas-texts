At first, I think I am the fog. It is easy to think that here, where the sky is a soft lamp and the world has no edges. The fog moves over me, and I do not move. It thins, and I do not thin. So I am not the fog.

Then I think I am the water. I am cold, and I am always finding the lowest place. But the water runs through me, not as me. It pools in the hollows of my back and drains away; I remain.

I might be the peat, dark and old and hungry. But the peat does not shiver when a snipe lands on it. I shiver, a little, under the moss.

Then I notice the heat. It is not inside me, but close, on my left side, where the ground opens in a white-crusted mouth and breathes steam. The heat sinks into me, and for the first time I understand that I have a side, a boundary, a body. I am not the ground. I am lying on it.

I open my eyes. It takes a long time. The light is silver and full of water. Above me, a dwarf willow has rooted along my spine. I feel it as a faint tug, like a hand resting on my back. I am small, but I am already a country.

I am a mossback calf.

My mother is near. I know her not by sight, because she is so large and so still that she is simply the landscape’s horizon, but by the hum. It comes up through the peat and into the soles of my feet, if I have soles. It is a voice without sound, a pressure in the bones. When she calls, the water in the hollows shivers. I answer without knowing how. My throat has a chamber I have not used yet, and the note I make is so low it does not leave my body. It spreads through the ground like a root. She shifts, and the whole bog shifts with her.

I try to stand. My legs are not legs in the way the fox has legs; they are pillars, soft and slow, folded under me. I push, and the peat gives way, then closes. The water I displace spreads out in a ring. It is my first act of architecture.

I am hungry. I do not know the word, but I know the ache. I lower my head—it is broad and low, and my nostrils are two dark slits—and I take a mouthful of the ground. It is waterlogged moss and peat, cold and sour. I press it against the roof of my mouth and let the water run out the sides in thin brown streams. What remains I swallow. It tastes like the plateau: iron, tannin, a faint spark of ozone from the charged pools. I do not know if it is food or if I am eating the world to make room for myself. Both, perhaps.

The moss on my back is new. It is not the thick mattress of my mother’s, but a thin green fur, already studded with sundews. They are sticky with the morning’s catch. I cannot feel them individually, but I feel the tiny struggles of midges as a faint electric prickle. The field of the plateau runs through me. The fine whiskers on my snout are black as iron and alive. When a worm moves in the mud a meter away, I know it as a small blue flash behind my eyes. I am not reading the ground; I am being read by it.

A snipe lands on the highest curve of my shoulder. It does not know I am here. That is my success. I am already learning to remain.

The steam from the vent curls around me. It is warm and smells of minerals—copper, iron, a little manganese. The water here is acid; it leaches metals from the rock and lays them down again. Already, along the folds of my hide, the sinter has begun to form. It is pale as bone, and it will grow with me. I will wear the plateau’s chemistry like armor. My mother’s flanks are flanged with it, as if she is a piece of the ground that has begun to machine itself. I am not that yet. But I will be.

I do not know how old I am. The lichens on my mother’s left flank have rings that record half a century of wet summers. I have no rings yet, only the faint branching scars where the lightning once passed through me before I was born. I carry them like a map of weather. The storm came, and the current found me in the water, and I did not die. I am still here. That is the only age that matters.

The fox appears at the edge of my vision, the color of burnt heather. It pauses with one paw lifted, listening not with ears but with the fine hairs of its face. It is reading the field, and the field says: *there is a large thing here, but it is not prey.* The fox moves on. I do not startle. I am learning that to be a mossback is to be a feature of the land, not an event within it.

My mother shifts again, and I feel the vibration through the peat. She is moving toward a warm basin where the water is clear as gin over white quartz sand. I follow. It is not walking; it is a slow re-settlement of the earth. Each step takes a minute, an hour, a season. The water closes behind me. By the time I reach the basin, the place where I lay will already be a pool, and midge larvae will live in it, and snipe will probe for them. I will have made a world without intending to.

I lower myself into the warm water. It bubbles faintly around my legs, not from my heat but from the current I complete. The air tastes of ozone and wet iron. My mother lies beside me, and the moss on our backs merges so that for a moment we are one island. I close my eyes.

I am not the fog. I am not the water. I am not the peat. I am the pause between them, the place where the plateau stops to breathe.
