I came down the ladder in the last light, when the gray-gold had gone to ash and the cloud had settled into the notch like a lid. The rungs were already wet before I touched them; my palms registered the black algal skin as something between slippery and soft, like the palm of a hand pressed back. The descent took longer than the ladder looked—the damp rock amplified every sound, and I found myself going slower than I needed to, listening. The noise from below did not wait. It came up the slot like a warm gust, and by the time I was halfway down I could feel it in my molars, a low press of white water over the raw edge of light.

The bottom was not what I had braced for. I had read the account of a bellmouth, a punched hole, a wound in the high country, and I expected jags and hollows, the air cut by stone. But the floor was all softness at first: the moss swallowing my boots an inch before they found the spall underneath. The ground gave like a carpet over rubble, and I had to walk with my weight back, hands out, like someone crossing a river on submerged stones. The noise was enormous but it did not echo; it filled the bowl and stayed, a physical crowd, and in it I could distinguish the machine's high clean note. A whine so steady it seemed to hold the place together, or to rub it smooth.

I saw the woman before I saw the turbine. She was standing on the catwalk, a small smear of darkness against the industrial green, one hand resting on the white face of the pressure gauge. She did not turn at the sound of my boots. She had heard me long before—the rungs rang, the stones shifted, the air changed. When I called out, my voice went nowhere; it was eaten by the rush before it crossed half the distance. I stood there with the cooling mist on my face, and the frogs on the wet leaves did not jump. They had already accepted me as a new part of the weather, as they had accepted her.

I do not know how long I waited. Time in that place does not pass in lengths but in changes: a cloud thickened, a pipe rang, the door in the rock knocked twice and then was silent. The woman finished wiping the gauge with a rag. She tucked the rag into the waistband of her trousers and came down the catwalk, her boots ringing. She moved like someone who had memorized every step, and she stopped just far enough away that the steam from the pool passed between us.

“You came down,” she said. Her voice was lower than the waterfall, but I could feel it in my teeth, the same way I felt the turbine. “Most people stay at the top. They look into the slot and think better of it.”

I had not thought better of it. I had been thinking of it for years, the image of a warm notch at the top of the world, a hole filled with water and metal and moss, a woman who had crawled inside and forgotten to leave. The description had reached me through the wire, perhaps, or through some other route I no longer remember. I had carried it the way one carries a splinter under the skin, waiting for the body to push it out or absorb it.

“Is there somewhere to sit?” I asked.

She turned her head to one side, as if listening to something far away. “The root hollow is mine. The catwalk is for the machine. The rocks are sharp. The moss is deep in places. You can sit where the ground is warm, to the left of the penstock, but do not lean on the pipe. It will burn you through your clothes.”

I sat. The ground was warm, as she said, a dry heat that came up through the moss and the black earth, and I could feel my spine loosen for the first time in days. The roots of the nearest tree lifted above me in a low cage, their tips dead and pale but their deeper coils furred with green. Beneath them there was a kind of room, not tall enough to stand in but tall enough to sleep in. I could see the edge of a canvas tarp folded back, the corner of something that might have been a blanket, grown into the moss like skin over bone.

“You sleep there,” I said.

She did not answer. She had climbed back onto the catwalk and was crouched by the turbine housing, her head tilted to the side. I realized she was listening to the knock, the soft wooden sound I had felt through the water itself. After a while she straightened. Her hand—small, pale, wrapped at the wrist with black rubber—pressed flat against the steel casing, not for support but for information. The steel was sweating; the side of the housing was wet with condensation where the cold stream met the warm air. Where her hand rested, the moss was gone. The metal showed green paint and rust and the pale trace of her palm in a thousand small scratches.

“The bearing is worse,” she said, more to herself than to me. “But worse is not the same as done.”

I asked her what she meant by done. She came back down, wiping her hands on the rag, and stood by the pool where the travertine terrace stepped down in little rimstone cups. The water in each cup flickered with slow bubbles, and the green algae around the rims swayed in the heat. She pointed to the back wall, where the waterfall undercut the basalt in a dark brow. “Done is when the rock falls. Or when the water changes. Or when the wire goes cold.” She looked up at the wire, the single black line that ran up the wet wall and into the cloud. “It has not gone cold since I arrived.”

She did not ask my name. I did not give it. In that place, names were like birdcalls—they would have been drowned before they reached the other side. Instead we stood side by side, not speaking much, and let the sound of water and steel fill the space between us. The midges rose in a slow vertical column from the pool, never straying beyond the steam. The wren landed on a moss pad near the ladder and began to pick at invisible things. The light, which had been gray-gold, turned a kind of mineral silver as the cloud thickened overhead, and the leaves of the ferns grew dark and lacquered.

That first night I slept on the ground near the penstock, not in the root hollow but close enough to feel its heat. She had said nothing when I chose the spot. She had crawled into the hollow with the ease of a snake, and for a long time I lay awake listening to the waterfall and the whine and the occasional hiss of the pressure release. The ground beneath me was warm in a way that felt personal, as if the mountain itself were breathing. At some point I must have slept, because I woke to the gray-gold light and the sound of her tapping the gauge with a knuckle, a noise like two stones struck together, repeated once, then once more.

She did not offer me food. I had brought my own, meager as it was: hard bread, dried meat, a little salt. I ate sparingly, watching her drink from the enamel cup hooked on the bracket, the water leaving a white rim on the cup and on her lips. She ate nothing that I saw. She moved through the day in a circuit that I came to understand only gradually: first the pipe, then the gauge, then the turbine, then a slow walk along the back wall where the water ran in sheets over the basalt columns. She touched the rock in places, not as a blind person touches, but as a reader touches turned pages, feeling for changes beneath the surface.

On the second day I asked her why she stayed. We were standing by the pool, watching the travertine terrace bubble and grow. The question hung in the air for a while, and I thought perhaps she had not heard me. But then she said, “The first night, I slept beside the turbine because I wanted to hear the bearing knock. I thought if I listened long enough, I would understand what it needed. And when I woke, the mist had soaked my clothes and my skin smelled like crushed fern. I thought: I will stay one more day to listen. Then one more. Then the day after that.” She turned her face toward me, and for the first time I saw that her eyes were a pale green, like moss in a dark place. “There was never a reason to leave. Leaving would have been a reason. I have been here long enough to forget what reasons feel like.”

She spoke without nostalgia, as if describing the shape of a stone. I found myself thinking of the place not as a prison or a refuge but as a kind of clock, with the waterfall for a pendulum and the turbine for a balance spring, and the woman as the part of the clock that knows itself. She was not hiding from something; she had become the thing one hides in.

By the third morning, the water pressure had risen, and the door in the rock knocked more often, a soft wooden sound that seemed to come from deep inside the basalt. I watched the woman sit on the catwalk with her head tilted, counting. She had a piece of basalt in her hand, and she scratched a mark into the green paint of the housing. The marks formed a lattice, a kind of writing that only she could read. When she saw me looking, she said, “The door speaks in patterns. Pressure falls, pressure rises, the knock moves in time with the rain up on the rim. I write it down so I know when the pattern changes.”

I asked if the pattern had ever changed. She paused, the piece of basalt between her fingers. “Once,” she said. “The winter when the wire went silent for nine days. The knock stopped on the third day, and the water turned cold. I climbed the ladder to the rim and found the slot blocked with ice and broken cloud. I thought: this is the end. But the place was only sleeping. When the ice went, the wire hummed again, and the knock came back. That is how I know the place is not done with me yet.”

I stayed for seven days. Each day the notch felt smaller and larger at the same time: smaller because I had learned every rock and pipe and root, larger because every surface held a depth I had not seen before. The moss grew in patterns that followed the seams of the metal. The feathery ferns on the back wall thinned and thickened with the steam. The pool deepened by too little to measure, and yet I measured it with my eyes, the way one measures the slow accretion of a life.

The woman and I did not speak often. The sound of the place made speech laborious, and silence was easier. But we communicated in other ways: a tilt of the head, a pause in a gesture, the sharing of the warm ground near the penstock. She taught me the difference between the ring of a stone on the pipe and the knock of the bearing. She showed me the place where the snake slept, the bromeliad cups that held tiny ponds, the hollow where the roots lifted into a cage. She did not ask me to help with the machine, and I did not offer. I understood that the machine was hers, or perhaps she was the machine's, in a way that had no room for a third hand.

On the evening of the sixth day, I climbed the ladder to the rim. She did not follow. I sat in the slot where the two shear planes met, watching the cloud run through the gap. The wire went up past me, warm as a living thing, and I touched it once, the way one touches the wrist of a sleeping person. Below, the notch was a bowl of mist and noise, and somewhere in it the woman was doing her round of the day, her shadow crossing the moss and the catwalk. I thought about leaving. I thought about the world above, the world I had come from, and I found that I could not remember its color. It had been gray-gold for so long, or perhaps it had always been gray-gold, and the world above was the dream.

On the seventh day, I gave her my name. It felt like a betrayal of the place's rules, but she only smiled—a small, private smile that did not reach her eyes—and she said, “I will not use it. But I will know it is there, like the wire to the cloud.” And I knew then that she had understood me all along, in the way the place understands water and stone.

I left the next morning. The climb up was harder than the climb down; the rungs were slick with condensation, and my hands had grown soft from the warm water. At the top, I turned back once. The slot was already filling with mist, and I could just make out the shape of her near the ladder, small and dark, her hand raised in a kind of salute. Then the cloud closed, and there was only the sound of water falling down into the hollow of the mountain.

Now, when I think of that place, I do not think of leaving as a choice. I think of it as a thread that was pulled too taut and then released. The notch is still there, with its warm water and its singing steel and its patient woman who is more moss than glass now, more root than rain. The door in the rock is still knocking, and the wire is still humming up into the cloud. And I am here, in a world that seems louder and quieter at the same time, my hands still carrying the memory of that warm, sharp stone, and my ears still tuned to the middle frequency that no longer exists. Perhaps I left to tell the story. Perhaps that is the only reason the notch allowed me to leave. Or perhaps I am still there, in the way that one can be in two places at once: one body in the bright, ordinary world, and one body listening forever to the waterfall behind the wall.
