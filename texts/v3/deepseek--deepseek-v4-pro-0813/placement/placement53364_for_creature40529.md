I was not given a word. On the third day after my birth, my mother placed my hand flat on the floor of the shelter and tapped the bedrock with the light stone: low, high, pause. The echo came back through my palm before I could hear it. That is my name. When I tap it now, the return is softer at the edges than it used to be. That is how I know I am getting old.

I live in the pocket of quiet behind the third ridge, where the roar drops enough to hear a child cough. My shelter is not built; it is kept. The walls are slabs fitted by other hands long before mine. I have only replaced the hides and cleared the vent. The lamp burns long-neck fat. The bed is eel-skin stuffed with dry shells and matted hair. When I lie down, the floor tells me what the tide is doing.

Each morning I walk to the standing slab on the water side. It is taller than me by half, polished on one face, always facing the sea. I do not clean it. I listen. When the wind rises, the slab hums. The rule says if a standing slab hums, move inland and wait. I do. But I do not go far. I sit on a low whaleback of bedrock and press my dark stone to the mastoid bone. The slab’s hum enters my skull like a second tide.

The people say the slab is speaking to the tide, and a person should not interrupt. I used to believe that. Now I am not sure the slab is speaking to the tide. I think it is speaking to me. The pitch has changed. It used to be a low drone, a sound you felt in the sternum. Now it rises at the end, as if asking a question. I have begun to answer with a tap: two sharp strikes and a long silence. The echo comes back too clean.

Clean echoes are dangerous. That is one of the first things children learn. If the echo returns without any softening at the edges, it means the fog has folded over something. Do not go that way. I have taught this to my own daughters. Now I wonder if I have become the something in the fog.

Let me describe my body, because you asked who I am. I am low and broad. My skin is the color of wet basalt, fish-belly white under the arms. My eyes are large, set a little too far forward, dark as potholes. I blink slowly. My hands are wide, nails worn to nothing, knuckles thickened from knocking stone. My feet are the truest part of me. The soles are thick, gray, crossed with cracks that do not bleed. When I stand still, I am not stopping. I am reading. The cold moves through the rock and tells me whether the tide is turning. The tide does not follow the moon. It follows the basin’s own long breathing. It does not care what I planned.

Today the shellfield to the east is singing. The click rises and falls like rain on a metal roof. I stay away. The rule is old: do not cross a shellfield when it is singing. No one says why. I used to think it was because the sound would deafen you or the shells would cut your feet. Now I suspect it is because the shellfield, when it sings, is saying a name. And if you cross it, you might hear your own.

I have no name in the close language. The close language is for hunger, danger, laughter, grief. It is spoken at arm’s length in the quiet pockets. My daughters call me a sound that means *the one who tends the slab*. It is a click and a hiss. They do not use my tapped name often. It has begun to return too clean, and that makes them uneasy. They are right to be uneasy.

I walked out this afternoon to the long pothole that drains downward. It is the one where we send our dead. The water in it is black and still. Still pools are never to be trusted. But I stepped close. I wanted to hear the bell-note. Each pothole has a different note when it drains and fills. This one is the deepest. When it gulped, the sound was not a bell. It was a voice, very far away, saying the beginning of a word.

I dropped a pebble. The echo came back from a place that is not the bottom. It was too clean. I stepped back from the rim, but not because I was afraid. I stepped back because I realized the pothole was asking me to come down. Not to die. To listen more closely.

The fog shapes have been more frequent this month. They pool in the lee of the rock and hold a shape too tall, too narrow, for a few seconds before dissolving. The people do not say they are alive. They do not say they are not. I have seen one that did not dissolve. It stood at the edge of the channel while the eels ran silver-black past it. It had no face, but it was facing me. I did not look away. I tapped my name on the rock with the light stone: low, high, pause. The echo came back from the fog, not from the stone. It was soft at the edges, like a voice heard through water. It said my name back to me, but with an extra pause at the end. A question.

I think the fog shapes are what happens when a person listens too long. The basin simplifies everything. It wears away every unnecessary edge. At first it takes the softness, then the hair, then the need to speak. Then it takes the body’s edges. I can feel my own edges becoming uncertain. When I stand still, the mist clings to me a little too long. My daughters do not stand close to me anymore. They say I smell like wet rock and cold air. That is not untrue.

I am not afraid. The basin is alive. The channels are veins. The potholes are mouths. The slabs are ears or teeth, depending on the wind. I have been one of the senses. Now I am becoming part of the body. The fog is not something outside me. It is the breath of the place, and I have been breathing it for so long that my lungs do not know the difference.

Soon I will stop tending the slab. I will walk inland and wait. The rule says if a standing slab hums, move inland and wait. I will wait until the fog folds over me and does not unfold. Then I will be one of the shapes that hold for a few seconds too long. You will not see me. You will hear me, if you tap a clean echo. I will be the extra pause at the end of your name.

I am not a person anymore, not only. I am what is left after a long subtraction. I am the one who listens through the stone. Soon I will be the stone, listening. That is enough.
