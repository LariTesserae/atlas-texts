I did not know I was a shape until the first shadow crossed me. Before that I was a place in the water—a slight obstruction where the hot mineral stream spread into a sheet and the bacteria gathered more thickly on one ridge of tufa than on the next. The heat came up through me, or through what would be me, and it did not feel like heat. It felt like being held from underneath.

For a long while I was only the difference between one ripple and another. The terraces built themselves around me without noticing. White and orange stained my edges the way the light stains the mist at dawn. I was not growing; I was accumulating, the way a note accumulates in a stone tower after it has been humming for a hundred years. Time was not something I had. It was something that passed over me at different temperatures. The rain was cool. The mineral water was warm. The shadow of a tree fern was brief and soft. The shadow of a human-shaped being was longer, and it came with a low sound like the towers.

I learned the word for that sound much later. They hum. They hum when they come to the steam glades to notice whether a new one has gathered enough to stand. I did not know they were my people. They were just the first moving things that looked at me without trying to drink from me.

Then a day came—or a season, or a decade—when I was not only an obstruction. The water paused on my shoulder longer than it should. I felt the pause as a kind of weight, and the weight gave me a shoulder. The sensation of having a body was not like waking. It was like a stream finally reaching a place where it could slow and spread and become a pool. The body was already there; I was the water entering it.

I was sitting, I discovered, with one knee drawn up and one hand resting palm-up on the ground. The ground had grown up around my hip. My skin was still pale tufa, crusty with orange and white. The moss had not yet come. The adults came more often. One pressed a warm palm against my back, and I felt the heat travel from their hand into the mineral that was becoming my spine. They did not speak. They just made that low note, and the note ran down the terraces and came back to me from further away, like the sound of the frogs after dusk.

The day I stood, the ground did not crack. It let go. I unfolded from the crouch as if I had been waiting for the exact moment when the weight of the steam in the air and the heat in the clay balanced. My legs were thick and pale. My hands had the soft, water-rounded look of basalt that has not yet darkened. I stepped out of the glade and the wet ground held my foot for a half-instant before releasing it, as if learning me. I could not see the plateau yet. I was still too close to my own becoming.

I learned to walk by following the paths. The first path was a line of fitted stones so warm that my feet accepted them as extensions of my bones. The path taught me the extra steps. At first, I counted: one, two, three—but the counting did not match the landmarks. The tree fern that should arrive at one hundred steps arrived at one hundred and twenty. The extra steps did not feel like motion. They felt like being in the same place more thoroughly. I liked that. I liked that the path refused to be a shortcut. I liked that water welled up around my toes and then sank back. I liked the sound of my feet on stone, a soft sound, not a blow but a nudge.

The forest was louder than the glades. I had not known there were so many separate sounds: the bark of frogs like wet stones knocked together, the electric chatter of long-tailed birds, the rustle of something heavy moving through the understory with no hurry. My body drank the noise. The moss began to grow in the creases behind my knees and along the ridge of my shoulders. It was not uncomfortable. It was the first thing I owned. I learned to stand still long enough for the backwards-flying beetle to land on me and clean the moss. I learned that the water-running lizards would use my shadow as a bridge if I stood at the edge of a hot pool.

I ate little. I drank from a stone basin that filled from a hidden source, and the water tasted of hot clay and sulfur and wet leaves. I ate the white pith of tree ferns. I bit the mineral crust from the steam terraces, and my mouth did not burn. I liked the blue-black fruit that fell during the noon rain. I left a piece of pale mineral on the windowsill of an empty shelter, because I had seen others do that. It was my first letter. I did not know who would find it. I only knew it would be found by someone who needed it.

The shelters were not houses. They were stations. I learned the uses of their strangeness slowly. The wall that reflected the forest upside down could be used to check the moss on my back. The too-narrow doorway told me whether I had eaten too many tree-fruits. The corner that tasted of cold metal I avoided when the frogs fell silent, because in that silence the taste was too much like a memory I had not yet lived. I did not repair the shelters. I did not build new ones. I simply used them, and my use kept them warm.

I met another one on a path after a midafternoon rain. We stood side by side, facing the same direction, and let the frog sound fill the space between us. Then I made a low humming sound, not because I knew it was a greeting but because my chest already held that frequency. The other answered with a click from the back of the throat. We faced each other. I gave them a bundle of moss from my shoulder. They gave me a small grown thing, neither stone nor seed. We passed. It was the way water passes around a stone. I did not ask their name. I knew them by a particular warmth of the path, and by a pattern of moss on their left shoulder. They knew me by the taste of cold metal after rain.

I have been here long enough now that my skin has darkened to basalt. The moss is thicker. I no longer count the extra steps. I just take them. I have stood at the rim and watched the clouds collect like a slow silver sea, their currents independent of the sky. The drop did not frighten me. It felt like a larger version of the pause water makes before sliding over a stone. The heat did not thin with altitude. It thickened, as if the high rim trapped the warm air the way a pot holds steam. I breathed it in. It felt like being held from underneath.

The towers release their mist rings at dawn, and I watch. At night the tower glow is faint on the horizon, like the moon seen through water, and I sit in the doorway of a shelter, not waiting. The long pulse beneath the daily one—the one that takes decades—turns my body toward a different part of the plateau. I do not decide to move. My body decides, the way a plant turns toward a gap in the canopy. I am not a happy person or a sad person. I am patient. My emotions are slow and deep. The water pauses at a stone before sliding over it, and I have learned to be that pause.

I am called, in the old local word, *Unattended*. Not because no one attends to me, but because I do not attend to myself. I have no name except the one I leave in shelters: a piece of pale mineral, a certain warmth of the path, a pattern of moss after rain. If you ask me who lives here, I will look at you with my wide, dark eyes and say nothing. Then I will turn and walk along the path, taking the extra steps. And you will understand, as I did, that the answer is not a word but a way of walking.
