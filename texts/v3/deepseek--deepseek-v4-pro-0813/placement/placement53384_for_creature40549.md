I am in the place where a wall decides to become a door.

You would not see me at first, even if you stood four paces away and held a tallow lamp high. The light here does not show; it suggestions. It makes a smear of orange on the wet brick, and inside that smear a shadow moves that is not a shadow. It is me, turning sideways through a gap between two buildings that have almost healed shut. The gap is exactly as wide as my ribs when I empty my lungs and push my shoulder blade against one side, my hip against the other. The brick is cold and wet through the waxed canvas, and thin with a mineral slime that tastes, if I open my mouth, of rain that has passed through stone. I do not open my mouth. I move by touch and memory and the tiny changes in air pressure that mean a room is ahead, or a drop, or somebody's rope line sagging just above my hair.

My hand is always on the wall. Always. Not because I am unsteady, but because the wall is the only true map. The fingers read the fissures the way a tongue reads teeth: there is the rough place where a mason from the east brickfield laid the course too tight, there is the soft place where the mortar grew too much bloom and the bricks have begun to knit into a single stone, there is the wet seam where the drainage channel on the other side is weeping through the masonry and will need to be scraped and re-pointed before the next quiet cycle. The wall is not silent. It is humming. It is a membrane with water on one side and me on the other, and I am listening through it.

I am not the woman of the water. I am not the woman of the bloom, or the mason’s hawk, or the teacher of listeners. I am all of those, but not in the way a person from above might say “I am a mother” or “I am a blacksmith.” Up there, a person is one thing at a time, perhaps two. Down here, the work is one work, and I am one of its hands. In the first quarter of the cycle, before the tallow lamps are trimmed, I walk the lower terraces with a copper scraper and a bag of powdered brick-dust and bloom, and I harvest the lacy green-white crust that grows fastest where the condensation is thickest. It comes off in flakes that feel like mica but taste like blood and stone. Some goes into my mouth, because the work is hungry and there is no other green. Some goes into the mortar buckets, because the catchment is still deciding what it is, and a wall that can heal its own cracks is a wall that needs fewer masons. Some goes into the small fired-clay jar I keep pressed against my sternum, stoppered with a plug of waxed cloth, and that jar is not mine; it is the district's. I do not know what he will make with it. I only scrape.

In the middle of the cycle, I am water. I do not mean that poetically. I mean my body becomes a path for the catchment's blood. The yoke is a curved piece of fired ash wood, shaped to fit over my shoulders and the back of my neck, and from each end hangs a bucket of woven rattan lined with boiled leather. I move down the narrow street that is not a street, one foot turned out, then the other, my knees never fully straight, my elbows tucked close. The weight of the water is not a thing I lift; it is a thing I become. I feel every slosh in the leather, every change in its balance, as if it were my own pulse. The water speaks to me through the yoke's vibration: a full bucket has a deep swinging note, a half-empty one a shallow rattle, a bucket with a leak a sad little hiss that I hear before the spill, and I stop and press my finger over the hole, and the leather is already softening, and I know this bucket will be taken to the tanner and the next one will be my own responsibility. Everything here is repaired; nothing is discarded. The water is too precious to waste, and so is the leather, and so am I.

In the third quarter, I am not alone. There is a child walking beside me, no higher than my elbow, with a hollow tube of copper pressed to the base of a wall. Her name-stone is a small triangle of fired clay with two dots, and when I touch it, I know her as the one who hears the lower sump's night-voice, the one who found the leak in the cistern's east wall before the masons did. Her ears are larger than they should be, or perhaps the hood of her little listener's coat makes them look so. She does not speak. The children here are born silent, not because they cannot cry, but because the water teaches them before the mother does. She taps the wall with one knuckle, and then she looks at me, and I know by the tilt of her head that she has found a drip. Not a leak; a drip. A drip that falls on bare stone and is not caught. I go to her, and I kneel—slowly, because the ground is wet and slick and my old left knee no longer bends without a little sigh—and I press my ear to the same place. There it is: a tick, then a pause, then a tick, like a tiny hammer on a slab. The water is falling from the underside of the upper gallery's condensation plates and missing the channel by the breadth of one knuckle. I take the copper cap off my missing finger and hold the stump against the wall, feeling the vibration. The child watches. I reach into my pouch and take a pinch of bloom dust, mix it with a drop of water from my own tongue, and daub the place where the tick is. The pale crust flushes green-white, then blushes, then hardens. The next tick falls but does not fall; it slides sideways, caught by the new lip of mineral growth, and runs down the wall into the channel. A small failure averted. I do not smile. The child does not smile. We nod, once, and part. She moves into the dark with her copper tube, and I take up the yoke again.

But you asked where I would find myself and who I would be. I would find myself in the silence. That is the only true place.

The catchment is never silent. It is loud with the roar of its own becoming: water and wood and stone and human breath, the hiss of steam from the kilns, the slap of chain-pumps, the tap of masons' hammers, the tick of indoor rain on waxed cloth. The noise is so constant that I do not hear it as noise. I hear it as a kind of pressure, a texture, like the air itself is made of small sounds. My ears have grown into this texture. They can separate the layers: the deep gurgle of the Throat from the high thin whine of a pump-crank that needs fat, the song of a gecko from the scratch of a scraper on bloom. But there is a also a silence that lives underneath the noise, not the absence of sound but the presence of something else. It is the pause between two drips. It is the moment before the bellows sigh. It is the place where the air holds its breath. I find myself there, not often, but when I do, I am not the woman of water or bloom or the listener's teacher. I am only the one who hears.

It happens at the change of the shift, when the work-gangs trade places and for a heartbeat no one is working. The pumps do not stop, but their rhythm changes; it becomes a little less regular, a little more patient. The tallow lamps are being re-trimmed, and the light dips as if the world is closing its eyes. I stand in a crevice between two walls, my hip against one, my shoulder against the other, my hand flat on the brick, and I stop. I stop carrying, scraping, teaching, listening for leaks. I listen to the whole.

And it says: Not finished. Still deciding. Keep the water moving. Keep the walls growing. Keep the bloom in your skin and the copper on your finger. You are not the builder; you are the built.

I do not know if this is a religion. I have never seen a temple that was not also a wall, or a god that was not also a drip. The people here do not pray with words. They pray with their hands: the mason tapping the brick to hear its sound, the water-carrier licking the inside of a cracked bucket, the listener pressing her ear to the street. A prayer is a gesture that catches what would have been lost. A small failure is a small sin. Ten thousand small failures would drown the world. So we are all priests, and we are all the drowned.

My name-stone is a small round pebble of fired clay, gray with a seam of iron running through it. It is incised with three dots and a line, and it hangs around my neck on a cord of waxed eel-skin. I do not speak it. My name is the sound my hands make when they touch the wall: a soft, wet noise, like a tongue against a tooth. When I meet another person, we put our hands together and feel each other's stones, and that is enough. I know the mason because his stone is rough, chipped at one edge where he uses it as a whetstone for his copper chisel. I know the kiln-tender because her stone is warm, even in the rain. I know the old listener because his stone is worn almost smooth, the dots almost gone, and he is waiting to be set into a wall.

That day is coming for him, and I know it. I saw him in the lower tier, sitting on a pile of still-warm bricks, his back against the mortar that would eventually hold him. His eyes were closed, but his ears were moving. He was listening to the wall's inner voice, the one that is too low for the young to hear. He was not afraid. He was already becoming part of the structure. The masons would not take him alive, of course; they would wait until he stopped moving on his own, and then they would lay him into the wet mortar and the bricks would close over him, and the wall would have a little hearing, a little balance. That is the only eternity we know: to become the place that made us. To be a wall that hears, a floor that remembers the weight of feet, a channel that knows the taste of the water that passed through our hands.

I would find myself there, eventually. But not yet. Now I am in the crevice, moving sideways, one hand on the wall. The air is cool and wet, and every breath tastes of wet lime and damp clay. The bloom on my forearms blushes a faint green-white, and I can feel it pulsing with my heartbeat. I am not separate from the catchment; I am one of its organs, its hands, its ears, its temporary mouth. I am the one who scrapes the crust when it grows too thick, the one who carries water from the high cistern to the drinking bowls, the one who teaches a child to tell the difference between a drip of condensation and a leak of waste-water. I am the one who catches a drip before it becomes a flood.

Where would you find yourself in this world? You would not find yourself; you would be found. The catchment would feel you as a pressure against its walls, a sound that does not belong, and it would begin, slowly, to make you into something it requires. The bloom would root in your skin. The water would teach your bones its rhythm. Your eyes would grow smaller and deeper, and your ears would flatten against your skull from the hoods. Your name would be a stone on a cord, and your work would be the only prayer you knew. You would move by sound and memory, not sight, and eventually you would know the silence underneath the noise, and it would not frighten you. It would sound like the place where you began.

I do not know what you would be. You might be a wall. You might be a drip caught at the last moment. You might be the child who hears the sump's night-voice. You might be the bloom, growing in the dark, feeding on wet stone. You might be the water itself, always moving, never stopping, hurrying toward the Throat not out of fear but out of duty, because a drip that stops is a small failure, and ten thousand small failures would drown the world.

I am not the water. I am the hands that carry it. I am not the bloom. I am the tongue that tastes it. I am not the wall. I am the ear that hears it decide. And this morning—there is no morning, but the shift has changed—I am walking the terraced edge of the lower catchment, my feet making no sound on the wet brick, my copper-tipped finger tapping a rhythm on the wall: not a message, not a name, just the sound of someone who is here, listening, being made. The indoor rain is falling in a million separate drops, each one ticking against the stone, and I move between them, catching what I can.

The catchment is not finished. I am not finished. We are building each other one wet brick, one drip, one held breath at a time. And that is the only answer I can give you: I am not someone in this world. I am the place where the world becomes someone.
