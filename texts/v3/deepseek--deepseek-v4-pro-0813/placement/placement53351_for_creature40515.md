I am in the depression between two whaleback ridges, where the fog comes down twice a day and the ground drinks it before it can pool. The bottom is not mud but a mat of copper moss over gravel, soft to the first knuckle, then firm. There is a warm pool here, black and round as an open mouth, where gas bubbles rise one at a time and release a breath I have known since my gills closed. I am the one who sits by the vent at the pool’s edge, where the blue flame no taller than a hand licks the air and never goes out.

I do not know how old I am. The elders do not count years; they count closures of the sky, pulses of the green light, and the return of the grazers. I have seen the grazers come back five times, which is not many. My skin is still smooth at the joints, not yet puckered into the stone-lichen folds of the elders. The moss on my shoulders is thin, a green down that mats with damp and roots in the fine hair. When I lie still, I am a mound of wet ground with a heartbeat. The frogs do not startle when I lower my hand into the pool; they swim over my fingers as if they were warm stones.

My task is the pool. I clear the collapsed crust where the old crust has cracked and let the hot water meet the cold. I pull the moss away from the gas chimney that fizzes underwater, so the blind fish can rise to the surface on the turning water. I deepen the pool with my fingers, slowly, over days, because the land is slow and I am slow. The frogs return to the place I have deepened, and when the cold comes, they freeze solid in the shallows. I have touched them then—hard, grey, lit with a faint electric shimmer when the sky speaks. I wait. When the water warms again, they thaw and call once, a single low note like a drop hitting a pan. I answer, not with my mouth but with my throat, the note passing from my chest into the ground. The mineral tubes across the basin carry it onward, fluting it from one open end to the next, until the message reaches the next vent-field and the next keeper. That is how we know the thaw has begun.

I eat what the pool gives. The pale fish have no eyes, but they find my hand when I cup it. I eat them whole, feeling the small life pass into me. I dig insect larvae from under the moss with my long fingers, the dark ridged nails scraping through roots. Sometimes, when the lichen is soft after a heavy fog, I break pieces off the rock and hold them in my mouth until they taste of the ground’s warmth. I do not hunt the grazers yet; that is for the elders who have learned the art of stillness. I have seen one do it: wait at the edge of the fog, still as a stone, until the herd forgets him. Then he trips a calf and holds it down in the water. The kill is quiet. The herd moves on. We share the meat over the vent flame, warming it but not cooking it, because cold meat is hard to digest.

I am not alone. There is another keeper in the next basin, an old one whose back is so covered in moss that she looks like a boulder. We do not speak often. Words are intervals, long pauses, repeated tones. To call her, I blow across the open end of a gas tube that rises from the sulfur ring. The note travels through the earth, and after a long silence, her note returns, a lower pitch that tells me she is well and the pool is warm. When the wind crosses the tubes on its own, it sounds like our voices, and I cannot always tell if I am hearing her or the land breathing.

There is a small creature that lives in the rocks above the vent, a weasel the color of cloud. She watches me with black eyes, and I do not bother her. She has taken young from the pool before, but I do not stop her. The land is a web of slow hungers, and I am one thread among many. The stone-colored cat is more dangerous. I have seen its fur lift like wet stone from a rock, and I know that if it finds me alone, it will try to hold me down in the water as we hold the calf. I am careful. I move with the fog, stepping only on moss, leaving no trace on the gravel. My feet are long and spread to take my weight, and the moss springs back after me. If the cat followed me, it would lose me in the grey.

The sky is a lid of wet cloud, and underneath it, the light is a permanent dusk. I have never seen the sun. Heat comes not from above but from below, from the blood of the land, and life has learned to follow it. The elders tell us that the land is a body, and the magma is its blood, and the vents are its breath. The small blue flames are its pilot lights. We are keepers of them. If a vent dies, we dig until it breathes again, or we leave and let the moss take the ground. We do not mourn. We sing a low note into the dead tube, and then we go.

I have seen the stranger. It came out of the fog like a thought, tall as the fog itself, walking on two legs, leaving deep prints in the moss that did not spring back. I did not move. I became a mound of ground, my heartbeat slow, my breath a whisper. The stranger found my bed beside the warm pool, the hollow where I had slept, still warm. It put its hand into the moss, and I felt the weight of its heat, not a threat but a question. It looked up, and I lifted my head, and our eyes met. Its eyes were small and bright, not dark as tarns like mine. It made a sound with its mouth, a sound I did not know, and then it turned and went away into the fog. The others followed, stepping carefully, not looking back. The last one paused at the pool and opened its mouth, and the note it made was not our note, but the frogs answered, one by one. Then it was gone.

Now I sit by the vent again. The flame burns steady, a blue tongue no taller than my hand. The gas hisses, a pressure more than a noise. The fog rolls in on its twice-daily pulse, and the world becomes a series of rooms. Somewhere far away, a message passes from tube to tube: the thaw has begun, or the grazers are moving, or the stranger has left. The land idles, patient as a sleeping animal. I place my hand over the flame, not to warm myself, but to feel the pressure of the gas, the breath of the earth. I hum, and the mineral tubes answer, a low note that seems to come from beneath the ground. I am the vent-folk. I am the hands of the land, and this is my pool, my vent, my slow and quiet life.
