I would find myself in the pool, but not in the whole pool.

At first I might think I was the light, because the light is the easiest thing to mistake for a living one. It brightens after each flood, as if breathing, and it makes the walls look like skin. But the light has no mouth, no gut, no old cuticle to split. I would have all three.

Not in the glowing walls. Not in the open air, which would dry me and then let the mineral find me. Not in the hot throat below, where the water climbs out of the rock hot enough to cook me. I would live in the upper hand-width of the pool, where the heat loosens just enough, where oxygen gathers along the condensing surface, where the water is still dangerous but no longer fatal.

I would be small. A grain of rice, or even less. Colorless, not white but transparent, so that the light from all walls passed through me and found nothing to stop on. I would have no eyes, or only dark remnants of eyes, because in a room where the light comes equally from every direction, eyes would tell me nothing about near and far. I would know the world as temperature, pressure, vibration, taste. The pool would be my whole country.

I would know the surge before it came. Not through thought. Through a slight thickening in the water, a lift in the pressure along my back, a change in the taste of sulfur. Every seven or eight minutes the pool would dome. I would loosen my hold, not fight. The hot water would carry me upward and toward the rim, where the shallow film cooled against the air. There, at the waterline, the world would sharpen: more oxygen, a different temperature, and the grey living mat on the stone. I would turn on my side and scrape it with my mouthparts, taking a few cells at a time. Then the surge would recede, and I would sink back down into the safer, cooler band, away from the air and the mineral crust.

I would not call it hunger. It would be more like a tide in the body. The pool pulses, and I pulse with it.

If you stood there for an hour, you might see me six or eight times, always near the same edge, always with the surge. I would be easy to miss. You would have to look not at the glowing walls but at the place where the pool begins to swell, where the water’s skin rises into a low dome. I would be a small interruption there, a transparent body at the edge of the flood, present only for a second before I sank back and disappeared into clarity.

I would be the amphipod.

Once, if you were patient for days, you might see me split along the back and pull myself out of my old cuticle. The old self would drift for a while, then gather a soft white fur of silica and sink, a ghost of me becoming part of the floor. The new self would be even paler, nearly invisible, hiding in the coolest part of the pool while its new cuticle hardened. If I were female, I would carry eggs in a brood pouch, tiny spheres just visible through the body, and when they hatched, the young would already know the surge. Not because I taught them. Because the pool taught them while they were still in the pouch. They would be born already rhythm.

I would not see the blue-white light. My world would not be bright. But I would feel it as a kind of steadiness, a ceiling of warmth that keeps the condensation steady and the water livable. The light would shape the room, and the room would shape me.

I would not be the grey mat, though I would live from it. The mat is older than plants, patient as stone, feeding on sulfur and iron where the steam touches the wall. It cannot ride the surge. It cannot sink when the water recedes. It only grows, slowly, at the edge of its own tolerance. I would be the one small animal that crops it, the pulse made small.

I would be solitary, maybe one of two or three. Not lonely. Loneliness would require more of a self than I possess. I would be exactly as much as the pool allows: a blind transparent husk of hunger and timing, rising with the flood, scraping the living film, sinking with the recession. That would be my whole life. And in that hot white hollow, that would be enough.
