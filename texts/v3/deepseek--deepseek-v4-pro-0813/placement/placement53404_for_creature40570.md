I am the one who reads the mud.

Not the surveyors—they drive their stakes and call it truth, but the ground laughs at their lines. Not the pond-tenders, though Vorn and I have shared many dawns at the edge of the settling ponds, watching the gas rise in slow silver blisters. Not the listeners, exactly, though they sometimes come to me when the city's nervous system misfires and they need a second opinion.

I read the mud.

The mud is the city's memory, and its prophecy. Every layer of silt, every lens of clinker, every seam of rust-orange algae tells a story. The river brings the mountains down to us, ground fine as flour, and the factories add their own confessions—slag, ash, metal dust, the half-dissolved bones of machines. When the water slows, the stories settle. I am the one who reads them.

My tools are simple. A long iron rod, tipped with a brass point, for sounding. A wooden paddle, worn smooth as a tongue depressor, for tasting. A glass jar, for samples. A notebook made of the green paper the papermaker presses from Vorn's algae, bound in oiled cloth. The pages are soft, almost spongy, and the ink bleeds into them like water into silt. The maps I draw are not for navigation. They are for understanding. They show where the ground is thin, where the water is rising, where the waste is accumulating, where the land is dreaming of becoming something else.

I walk the city's edges, where the new ground is being born. The surveyors go out each morning with their stakes and their theodolites, but I go out before them, when the fog is still thick and the gulls are only pale shapes in the gray. I walk the mudflats and the cinder flats, the braided channels and the settling ponds. I test the ground with my rod, feeling for the difference between firm and liquid, between land and water, between past and future. The rod tells me things the surveyors' instruments cannot. It tells me when the ground is holding its breath. It tells me when the river is thinking of a new channel. It tells me when a building is about to sink, not because the foundation is weak but because the land beneath it has decided to become water again.

I taste the mud. Not with my tongue—that would be foolish, with the industrial waste and the half-dissolved chemicals. But I press a sample to my lips, to my cheek, to the inside of my wrist. The mud has a temperature, a texture, a mineral tang that the skin can read. It tells me what the factories have been doing, what the river has been carrying, what the city has been eating. The mud is the city's stomach, and I am the one who listens to its digestion.

I collect samples in my glass jar. I take them back to my room—a small space in a water-house, reached by a ladder of rusted rungs, where the floor is always damp and the walls are furred with moss. I spread the mud on a sheet of glass and study it in the gray light. I look for the layers, the colors, the tiny fragments of brick and bone and metal. I look for the seeds of the future—the spores of moss, the eggs of newts, the larvae of midges. The mud is not dead. It is full of life, waiting for the right conditions. I am the one who watches for those conditions.

The people of the Low City do not understand what I do, but they respect it. They see me walking the edges, my rod in hand, my jar at my belt, and they nod. They know that I am not a surveyor, not a scavenger, not a listener. I am something else. I am the one who reads the ground. When a wall cracks, when a street sinks, when a pond begins to weep, they come to me. They ask me what the mud says. I tell them what I can. Sometimes it is good news—the ground is settling, the water is receding, the land is becoming firm. Sometimes it is not—the ground is restless, the water is rising, the land is dreaming of drowning. But always, I tell them the truth as the mud tells it to me. The mud does not lie. It only speaks slowly, and in a language that most people have forgotten how to hear.

I am not old, but I am not young. I have been reading the mud for many years, since I was a child playing on the tilting stakes. I learned from an old woman who lived in a water-house, a woman who could taste the difference between glacial flour and furnace slag with her eyes closed. She taught me to listen to the ground, to feel the tiny movements of the earth through my boots, to read the stories in the layers of silt. She died in a flood, or a sinking, or a slow drowning—no one is sure. The water took her, as it takes so many. But she left me her rod, her paddle, her jar, and her notebook. She left me her knowledge, written in the green paper, the ink bleeding like water into silt. I carry that notebook with me always. It is my map, my memory, my teacher.

The mud is patient. It waits. It accumulates. It remembers. And I am the one who reads its memory, who translates its patience into words the living can understand. I am not a hero. I am not a leader. I am only a reader, a translator, a keeper of the city's slow, wet, quiet history. But in a place where the ground is never permanent, where the maps are always wrong, where the city is still being born, perhaps that is enough. Perhaps that is everything.

I am the one who reads the mud. And the mud, in its slow, patient way, reads me back.
