You would not find me easily. At first you might mistake me for the film, the brown-black patina that eats iron and sulfur out of the seep water. I am soft and gray and spread against a surface, and I do not need light. But I am not the film. The film grows in tide-lines on the weeping wall. I am pressed to the warm brass housing of the pump.

Heat is the first thing I know. Not the cold, persistent damp of the rest of the Works, but a deep, frictive heat that comes up through my underside. I know the pump before I know my own shape. When it draws in, I swell. When it shudders and clunks and spits a cup of water into the trough, I settle back against the brass. The little electric pop that would raise the hair on your arms is, to me, a taste. I open a small opening at one end and take in the pulse.

My back is studded with copper-colored granules. They are not exactly armor. They are more like the pump’s breath turned solid on my skin. I have no face. I am the size of a hand, soft and gray, and if you watched long enough from the far side of the sump, you might see me breathe in time with the pump’s cycle. The green lamp’s cone makes the water droplets visible, slow and suspended. Here at the pump, the air is warmer; the fog thins just enough for me to feel the whole wet machine around me.

I may have been here longest. The eel-things are old, but they are old in the way water is old. I am old in the way the pump is old. Maybe I was a smear of living paste waiting for a heart, and when the machine came I learned its rhythm. When it sleeps, I sleep. When it clunks, I draw in. I do not flinch.

From my place I feel the room change. The sump laps. The film spreads in tide-lines. The shell-carpet clicks as one limpet shifts and the others resettle around it like teeth in a jaw. The feathery crowns of the tube-dwellers close in a wave when a shadow passes. The flatworms move over the concrete like spilled ink that has learned direction. The eel-things stir in their troughs. I feel them all through the water, through the vibration in the brass, through the tiny changes in the air. I do not hunt them. I do not need to. The pump feeds me heat, electric pulse, the mineral sweat of old brass.

If you came close, the hair on your arms might stand up. You might still think I was a soaked rag, a growth, a gasket gone soft. But if you waited until the pump cycled, you would see me swell and settle. You would see the copper granules catch the green lamplight. You would understand that the Condensate Works is not only a ruin full of animals. It is a body, and I am one of its oldest organs.

Where would you find yourself? I would find myself at the pump’s flank, below the fog line, in the dim cone of the one lamp. I would be the soft gray thing pressed to the warm brass, the resident that treats the machine as host rather than hazard. I would be the pump’s creature.
