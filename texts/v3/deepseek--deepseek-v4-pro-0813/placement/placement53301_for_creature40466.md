I would be the old woman at the pool’s edge, the one tracing a spiral in the air as the bats pour out of the earth. Not a chief, not a shaman—only a keeper of water and quiet. I would live in a shallow basin where three grikes meet, a little downslope from a ring of stones no higher than my knee. The pool there has no proper name, but I know it by taste: a clean, mineral sweetness in wet years, a greenish bitterness in drought. It holds water year-round, and at the bottom of the deepest crack there is a sound like a long, slow swallow.

My home is the stone itself. I pull a mat of dry lichen over my legs when the fog thins. I carry almost nothing—a root-fiber cord around my waist, a snail-shell cup the color of old amber, and a few pebbles whose shapes I know by heart. Fire is not for me. Smoke would announce my small life to the sky, and the food here is better eaten cold, sun-warmed, still tasting of the pool.

In the early morning I rise before the fog lifts. I go to the channels—not only the straight old ones carved by the first people, but the natural runnels no one else notices. I read the stone with my fingertips, feeling the thin film of wetness and the faint cool instruction of a gradient. When a crumb of limestone has fallen and turned the water the wrong way, I move it. One pebble. That is the whole work. I have been doing it so long that my palms are ridged like the solution pans themselves, and the geckos step over my motionless hand as if it were part of the bedrock.

I am called Many-Waiting by a few, or Grikefoot by the children. Mostly I am no one. Names are too heavy here; they gather water and rot.

At dusk I sit at the edge of the big pool and watch the frogs hold still. One of them clicks once, then stops. The bats spill from the deep holes in long, silent ribbons. I trace the spiral because the spiral is water going down. It is the shape of a pool emptying through a crack too fine for the eye. The young ones think it is a prayer. It is not. It is a map—one of the old maps, worn almost to nothing, drawn again in the air because the stone has forgotten.

I know every pool between the Bat Holes and the Long Grike by the taste of its water and the color of its algae. I know which ones turn bitter in the third dry year, which ones hold water for seven, which ones are poisoned by the rust-colored bloom. The old ones taught me, and I teach the children not by speaking but by letting them press an ear to the rock until they hear the water moving. Most never hear it. Some do. Those are the ones who go on to clear channels.

I have laid two children in the grikes. Their bones are becoming part of the pale fins now, white and porous, and when the water runs over them after a real rain, it sounds almost like their names. I do not mourn them. I listen to them the way I listen to the stone. A person is a temporary pool. I am a temporary pool with four limbs and slow breath, and when I am empty they will lay me in the same dark channel and let the water take me back.

If you came here and stayed long enough for the plateau to grow used to you, I would not speak at first. I would stay folded in my stone hollow, watching you learn to be still. Maybe after some days I would hand you a pebble and show you how to hold it until it grows warm, until it begins to feel less like a stone and more like the closed eye of the hill. I would trace a spiral in the air for you, not because you need saving, but because anyone who has come this far should know where the water goes.

You might see me at the end of the evening, kneading a thin crust of algae from the water’s edge, or resting my cheek against the rock to hear the faint gurgle of the plateau drinking. You would think I am only another shadow among ten thousand, another still form in the half-light. And you would be right. That is the point.

I am the old woman who has become almost stone, the one who waits with the water and the shrimps and the bats. I live at the ear of the plateau, listening to the long, slow swallow. When the fog comes, I smell it before I see it: wet lime, iron, and a faint sweetness. Then I rise, and go to the channels, and begin again.
