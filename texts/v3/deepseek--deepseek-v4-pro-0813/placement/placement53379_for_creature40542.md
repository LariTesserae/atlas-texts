**Bellringer**

I woke to the shelf’s low drumming, and for a while I did not know where my body ended and the basalt began. My face was pressed flat to the warm stone—my face, if that is the word, is a disk, broad and mineral-bright, and it hears before my ears do. The booms came up through the rock, through the skull, and only then through the air. The cliff answered a half-breath later, a softer echo, like the mountain clearing its throat.

I am small and low. My forelimbs are short, my claws blunt from stacking stone. I taste the air along the edges of my disk: sulfur, hot salt, copper, the dry chemical breath of the vents. I do not drink. Drinking would kill me. The water that falls nowhere here would wreck the delicate balance of my fluids. I take what I need from the thin moisture in the lithovores I eat and from the warm amber solvent licked from a frond’s rim when no grazer is watching.

You would find me near the western edge, where the cliff folds back on itself and a windbreak of black basalt flakes faces the chasm. I built it with my sisters. We carried the flakes one by one, our claws ticking against the stone. We set them at an angle, then wetted the joins with mineral paste from our mouths. The windbreak is low, no taller than a grazer’s shoulder, but it faces the cliff just so. When I knock three times and let the chime fall, the cliff returns it louder than I sent it. That is how I know I am a bellringer: not because I call, but because the stone calls back.

There are four of us in this windbreak. My two sisters, my brother, and I. We mark our territory around a warm vent where the gas rises in a steady shimmer. In the early part of the day, we call back and forth with the neighboring windbreak to the east. Their knocks are higher, their chimes shorter. We can tell who is speaking by the shape of the echo. Sometimes we argue across the open pan, a percussive argument that sends the gas-gliders higher. Sometimes we fall silent all at once, because the shelf has tightened in that particular way, and a bellows-frond is about to vent.

Near the center of the shelf there is an older wall. It is not ours. It is too straight, too fitted, made of vesicular basalt set without paste. Beside it lie flakes of obsidian and a few nodules of hammered copper, green with salts. A woven fiber screen, half dissolved and repurposed by a bellows-frond, leans against the cliff. I have pressed my disk to that old wall and felt the heat it holds. The wall is silent, but it still holds a note. I think the old ones built it the way we build our windbreaks—to catch a voice, to shape an echo. But they did not stay. They left a fire-ring of blackened stones, a row of bone pegs in the salt, a single obsidian scraper still sharp enough to cut. I have touched that scraper. It is shaped for a hand that is not my claw, and yet it fits the same need: to cut, to mark, to make a small change in the stone.

I do not know if they are gone or if they became something else. The shelf does not keep names. But when I call near the old wall, the echo is different—warm, close, as if the wall leans forward to listen. A new bellows-frond is growing beside it now, a pale tongue no larger than my disk. Already it is lush. Already it is hungry.

When I hunt, I do not chase. I find a seam of pale gravel near the edge of a frond colony, where the lithovores rasp the salt. I stand still. My skin crusts with dust. My disk flattens to the ground. The swarm moves beneath me, tick and click, a rain of tiny mandibles on hot stone. Then my hidden mouth opens beneath the disk, quick, and I take one. The shell crunches. The taste is sulfur and salt and old metal. I eat, and the swarm closes over the space where the one had been.

Right now, the largest bellows-frond—the one near the center, swollen to the size of a small cottage—is tightening. Its lobes stretch until the surface skin shows pale stress lines. The grazers back away. My sisters and brother stop calling. For one long second, the whole shelf is quiet except the hiss of gas. I press my disk to the ground and feel the pressure building, a low hum that climbs and climbs. Then the colony vents. The blast hits my disk like a hand. The dust flattens in a ring. The cliff returns the boom twice. In the echo, a new vent reopens near the old wall, and a thin yellow smoke begins to curl from it. The seedling trembles but does not fall.

When the echo fades, I knock three times and let the chime fall. My sisters answer. The neighbors answer. The shelf goes on, a storm of hunger and patience, and I am part of it.

So where would you find yourself? You would find yourself here, on the Ventgarden Shelf, as a bellringer: small, social, percussive, with a face made to answer the mountain. You would live in a windbreak of basalt flakes, near the edge where the cliff throws your voice back. You would eat stone-raspers and sing in metallic knocks. You would hear the old wall hum. You would fall silent just before the frond vents, and then, when the echo fades, you would knock three times and chime, because it is how you know you are alive.
