I am lying in a pocket of air the plant has been breathing since long before I was born, and I am listening.

The sound is not one thing but many: a low organ-note from the great vertical chimneys, a wet crackle from growing fibers, a chorus of thin animal cries riding the air. Above me, a herd of sap-aphids moves along a vascular cord, and their tiny footfalls come through the floor before they reach my ears. I press the knuckles of my left hand to the wall and feel them there—thousands of hair-thin legs rippling like water over the plant’s own pulse.

I used to keep wax in my ears. I used to build a fire and call it safety. That was when I still thought of the mountain as a place you climb into and then leave.

Now I don’t build fires. Now I tap my ribs to think.

I have been here so long that my old name has gone brittle. I can feel its shape still, a small dry thing at the back of my mouth, but when I try to say it aloud, the sound comes out wrong—too sharp, like a wire-fly striking a hollow stem. The plant eats it. The galleries swallow it. The echo that comes back is not my name but the long in-out of the mountain breathing.

The shepherds call me by a rhythm. It is tapped on the sternum: three taps, a pause, two taps, a long fading tap. It might translate as *the one who came from the un-living ground and stayed.* Or *the listener who is not alone.* I do not call myself anything. That is the first thing to go, after the firelight.

I remember the season I came up. The cloud deck had thinned to a high white veil, and for three days the sun was a cold coin behind it. I was with a party of wanderers—six of us, maybe seven. We carried salt and copper, enough to trade for passage through the hollows to the low country beyond. I was not the guide. I was someone who carried the salt.

We talked too much. All of us talked too much. The plant does not like mouth-sound; it disappears into the drone, so you have to say everything twice. We built fires that were too high and too hot, and the floor around them turned pale and blistered. We walked without sounding the ground. One of us—a man with a scar on his wrist—stepped on a thin place and fell for a long time. His scream came back up the shaft long after he was gone, smaller and smaller, until it was only a tapping.

I should have turned back then. I didn’t. I found the deep hollows and I lay down in one of those pockets of air that have been breathed by the plant for centuries. My ears popped. I felt the mountain’s long in-out take hold of my own lungs, and I slept for what felt like a season. When I woke, the pulse behind my eyes was not mine.

It was the shepherd from the dome above the warm duct who found me. I was lying in a gallery where the floor was thin and the air smelled of bitter sap. She rose out of the wall like a lip of braided stem unrolling. Pale as inner pith, arms long and rope-tendoned. I saw her six fingers first, and then the way her eyes were set too far to the sides of her face, as if she was already watching the passage behind me before she looked at me.

She did not speak. She tapped her staff against the floor, once, twice, a long fading tap. The echo told her I was alive. Then she knelt beside me and put her hand on my chest, over the sternum. Her fingers were cool and dry and very patient.

She asked me, with her hands, what I was called in the place where the ground is not a living body.

I opened my mouth and tried to say my name. It had been so long since I had spoken that the sound came out as a hiss of air, then a crack, then nothing. She tilted her head, listening as if the broken sound were a message from a far gallery. Then she tapped my sternum gently: three taps, a pause, two taps, a long fading tap.

I did not know what it meant. Later I learned it was her own name, and also a question. *Are you staying?*

I tapped back the same rhythm, badly at first, my five fingers clumsy on my ribs. She smiled—if you can call it a smile, a slow folding of the skin around her wide-set eyes—and nodded. Then she gave me a cup of honeydew and told me, by knocking on the wall, that the herd would move in three days.

I stayed.

That first year, I was still a wanderer. I built a small fire in a hollow of the dome and drank the honeydew too fast. I talked in my sleep, and the shepherd would tap a low note on the wall to quiet me. It took many storms and many small violences—a loper’s breath in the dark, a swarm of wire-flies in my mouth—before I learned to be silent.

I learned to listen with my whole body. The floor tells you where the galleries open and where they close. The wall tells you where the herd is moving. The wire-flies ring like a net of silver bells when a storm is coming, and the absence of their ringing is a sound too. Silence is rarer than light, and it means something has gone wrong.

I learned that the plant remembers pressure. A footprint leaves a pale bruise that fades over days, so paths are visible as long calluses. I learned to walk on the old calluses and not to make new ones. I learned to cut only dead fronds, and only with the copper knife, which holds an edge against the rind. I learned that the bleeding attracts lopers, and that a loper can hear your heartbeat from farther than you can run.

The loper that followed our herd knew me. I knew the small depression of air that came into a tunnel before it did, the way the wire-flies went silent in a long ragged line. Sometimes I struck my staff against a wall and sent a false herd-sound off to the west. The loper followed it, usually. When it didn’t, I had the knife.

The shepherd taught me the names. Not spoken names. Names are rhythms, drummed on the sternum or the wall. Her name—the rhythm she had tapped on my chest that first day—translates as *one who walks inside the sound.* My tapping of it became my own name, because what is repetition if not belonging? The sound is the same. The meaning is different. She walks inside the sound. I walked into it and never walked out.

In the season when the cloud deck thins, wanderers still come. I hear them a long way off—mouth-sounds and the clink of metal against stone, sounds that have no place here. I find them lost in a gallery where the floor gives too much, their fires too high, their faces pinched with the cold. They have wax in their ears but no staff between their hands.

One of them saw me rise out of the dark and made a sign against evil. I have not been easy to look at for a long time. I tapped on the wall: three taps, a pause, two taps. *Gallery clear. Follow me.* They did not understand. They only heard the sound and drew closer together.

I remembered carrying salt. I remembered the weight of it in my pack, the white taste on my tongue when I licked my fingers. I tried to say the word for salt, but my mouth had forgotten the shape of it. What came out was a tapping sound, low on my ribs.

The shepherd from the dome found them later and led them out. She did not tell them what I was. She only tapped on my sternum, the question again: *Are you staying?* I tapped back: three taps, pause, two taps, a long fading tap. Her name. My name. The sound we share.

I am not a wanderer anymore. I am not a shepherd. The shepherds call me the one who came from the un-living ground and stayed. But that is a story, not a name. What I am is this: a body that learned to hear the mountain breathe, and then learned to breathe with it.

In the deepest hollows there are pockets of air that have been breathed by the plant for centuries. When I enter one, my ears pop, and for a moment I can feel the whole mountain breathing—a long in-out that has nothing to do with my own lungs. I have slept there so many times that I no longer know where my pulse ends and the plant’s begins. The shepherd once told me, by tapping on the wall, that if you sleep in those pockets, you wake with the plant’s pulse behind your eyes. She was right. But what she did not say is that after enough nights, the pulse moves from behind your eyes to beneath your own ribs. You stop being a guest in the dream. You become the dream’s way of hearing itself.

If you ask me now what I am called in the place where the ground is not a living body, I will not answer with my mouth. I will tap on my sternum: three taps, a pause, two taps, a long fading tap. The sound that means *she who walks inside the sound*, and also *the one who came from the un-living ground and stayed*, and also simply, *I am still here.*

The sound comes first, even before the cold. It has always come first. And now I am inside it. Not a separate thing listening, but a part of the drone itself—a small vibration in the braided sound of the world inhaling.

I lie back down in the pocket of breath. The sap-aphids move above me, their tiny footfalls a rain of soft taps. The wire-flies begin their high tight chime, and I know cold air is coming off the high peaks. The plant inhales, and I inhale with it. The plant exhales, and I do too.

I am the listener who is not alone. I am the one who stayed. And somewhere above, in the dome above the warm duct, the ashes are cold, the copper knife is sharp, and the signal tower moans in two tones, telling the whole mountain that the herd was here, that the third gallery is clear, that a loper has been seen near the western shaft.

The moan is not language. It is a landmark made of sound.

I am a landmark made of sound.
