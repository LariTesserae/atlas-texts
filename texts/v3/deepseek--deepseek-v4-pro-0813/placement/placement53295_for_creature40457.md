You would find yourself on the high mineral plateau, in the long dawn light, at the edge of a shallow groove that older feet have worn into the dust. The air is thin and cool, and it tastes faintly of metal, as if the whole world has been licked clean of water. At first you are not quite anywhere. You are a low shape folded on the stone, a dark oval among dark polygons. Your eight legs are tucked beneath you, each foot’s brush of mineral fibers resting on the rock. The night has made you slow. The cold has settled into the oil of your body, and for a long moment you are not separate from the stone. You are a patch of stillness, a pause in the wind.

Then the light comes. It arrives not as color but as a difference—a thin blade between the sun side and the shadow side of the world. Your dorsal plates begin to lift. It is not a decision. It is the slow expansion of the solvent inside you, the same fluid that never freezes. The black surfaces catch the light, and heat collects in the narrow gaps between the plates. You do not feel pleasure. You feel a return: the slow chemistry of your body waking from the long cool interval.

You begin to see. Your four eye-pits open, each one a bead of mineral crystal that reads the sky’s polarized planes. The world appears as a field of angles—the flat glare of the plain, the dark heat-signatures of the colonies, the bright hollow of the sky. You are low, lower than the smallest frond. The stone is not beneath you; it is the first fact of your body.

At this hour, before the wind rises, the colonies release their spores. The spore stalks stand up from the centers; they are brittle, glassy, and they uncoil with a sound like a tiny ceramic hinge. The spores rise in the first heat and go sideways on the wind. Some settle on your shell. You carry them. They are light, flat, and their winged cases catch the air when you move. The spore-flits appear, little films of iridescence, living only a day or two, and they follow the spores as if the spores were the only world.

You walk. Not the way a quick animal walks, but as a wave: the back legs lift first, then the middle, then the front, each foot breaking its electrostatic hold with a faint, dry click too small to hear. You follow the groove. It is one of the old paths, worn into the plateau by generations of slow travelers. The path is shallow here, no deeper than a thumbprint, but as you travel it becomes a channel in the dust, then a polished track on the stone. You leave no footprint. Only a faint sheen where your weight has pressed the dust into glass.

At a place where three paths meet, there is a small pile of smooth stones. You do not know who made it. The wind, perhaps, or the long patience of the world. You angle your body around it, leaving a wide arc. You have passed this pile many times. You will pass it again. It is not a marker in the way a story is a marker. It is a fact of the intersection, like a note held too long in a piece of music.

By late morning you approach a colony. It rises from the plain like a dark, breathing hill, its fronds overlapping in spirals of purple and rust and iridescent bronze. The air near it shimmers faintly, not with heat but with solvent vapor. You come from the downwind side, where the taste of metal is stronger. The colony does not move. It knows you as a slight change in the dust, a slow pressure on its mineral edge. It has no fear. It has no need of fear. You are part of its season.

You lower your head. Your mouthparts, two curved rasps, settle into the living rind at the colony’s edge. You scrape, slowly, taking only a few small mouthfuls. The taste is mineral and solvent, like licking the inside of a stone that has been dreaming of light. The colony thickens its tissue at the wound, not to harm you but because that is what it does. This is the old agreement between walker and rooted one: you prune, it grows.

In the afternoon the ground breathes. You stop on a patch of hollow stone, and a low tone passes through you—almost too low for your eye-pits to care, but your legs feel it. Beneath the stone, in the fractures, the glassroot web is shifting. Its oil-filled threads are moving, carrying rare ions from one colony’s waste to another’s hunger. The hollow rocks are its resonators. The sound is the voice of the underland, the slow music of a world that has never known water. You stand there and let it move through you. It is not a thought, but it has the shape of one.

You have lived for a long time. There was an egg, pearlescent, laid in the dust beneath a colony’s rim. You were a small pale thing then, slow even for your kind. You spent your first years inside the colony’s mineral crust, feeding on loose salt scales. When you emerged, you were the size of a button, and the world was enormous. It is still enormous. You have grown through many molts. Each time you hid in a hollow rock while the new shell hardened, white and soft for a few hours. The old shell remained, a ghost of you, and the wind eventually carried it away as dust.

At dusk the light slants, and the colors of the colonies deepen. The blues turn violet, the rusts turn almost red, the iridescent edges flare with orange and green. You fold your legs and settle into a shallow basin. The temperature drops. The whole plateau begins to tick and crack as the minerals contract. A fine crystalline frost forms on the ground, not water frost but something else—a breath of salts and metallic compounds. The salt-mites bloom in it, too small to see, and for an hour the dust is alive with their quick, invisible lives.

You do not fear the cold. You only slow. The plates of your back close, and the oil inside you becomes thick. You become, once again, a stone among stones, a dark oval on the bright plain. The night is not empty. It is full of slow processes, of dust settling, of the glassroot contracting in the cold, of spores lodged in the fine powder waiting for the first heat. You are part of that. You are one of the long intervals between one event and the next.

You are a stone-pilgrim. You have no name, no story, no daily purpose that a mind would recognize. You are a deliberate thing, a slow fact. The plateau does not need you, and you do not need it to need you. But you belong. You are one of the many ways the world has learned to wait.
