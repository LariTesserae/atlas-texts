I would find myself on the high catwalk between the condenser gallery and the pump house, where the steam thins just enough to show the lamps as orange moons. My boots are green at the seams. My hands are wrapped in rags stiff with old wipings. I am not sure, at first, if I have a name for what I am.

I carry a bucket, not of fuel but of soft cloths, a copper scraper, a bottle of mineral vinegar, and a tin of grease for the threads. The bucket knocks against my leg with each step, a dull note in the general hum. Below, the water is talking in its hundred small mouths. The rail is warm and gritty. The lamp ahead of me is already crusted, its light furred and brown at the edges, and I know exactly how the glass will feel before I touch it.

The first lamp I come to is not the brightest, but it is mine in the way a particular stone can be yours. It sits on a gooseneck bracket above the sluice path, where the finest spray reaches it all day. The glass has gone milky, then green at the bottom, then pale as old bone. I set the bucket on the boardwalk and climb the two rungs bolted to the rail. The lamp is warm, almost too warm, but I know how to hold it with the heel of my hand and loosen the cap with the other. The film on the glass is not dirt exactly. It is the place, settling: mineral from the water, algae from the wet air, a little rust from the iron that surrounds us. I breathe on the glass and watch the condensation clear a small circle, then close again.

I would be the one who does this, I think. The one who wipes the glass. Not the only one—there are others for the weirs and the valves and the condenser coils—but the one whose work is between the town and its light. They call me the lamplighter, though the lamps do not need lighting. They are electric, wired into the transformer yard, and they burn day and night in the permanent twilight. What they need is attention. The steam is always trying to take them back into the murk.

The cotton cloth comes away brown-orange, then green, then white. I fold it to a clean side and wipe again. Slowly the lamp becomes itself: a globe of hot glass, a filament so bright it leaves a red line in my vision and I have to look away. I climb down and move on.

By the third lamp, I am no longer thinking about what I am. I am only walking the route, feeling the slatted floor spring under my boots, hearing the different notes of the water through the gaps. There is no wind, but the steam moves in long slow currents when a valve opens or a door swings shut. The light from the clean lamp follows me for a while, then lets me go.

I was born here, or something close to it. My mother was a salt-wife; she sat in the market stall with her lantern and her little brass scale. My father tended the south weir. I cannot remember a time when I did not know the smell of hot mineral water and the taste of iron in the back of my mouth. I was one of the pale children, raised in the steam like a fern. They taught me to walk quietly on slatted floors, to listen for the dry pump before I could read. I learned the taps on the railing: double tap, come; slow triple tap, water rising; sharp single tap, hold. I learned that a lamp is not a thing you light, but a thing you keep clear.

When I was small, I thought the lamps were animals. That is a secret thing. I had no pets—no one has pets here, no dogs, no cats, no birds. The rule is on the inside of the skin: do not bring a live animal inside the ring. But the lamps were warm and round and seemed to breathe. At night, when the steam closed over the boardwalk, I would sit under the lamp by the salt stall and watch it make a small orange room in the mist. I thought if I held very still, it might come down from its bracket and press its warm face against my hand. It never did. But I fed it anyway. I wiped its glass with the hem of my coat. I whispered to it. I gave it the only food it needed: attention.

Now I am the lamplighter in the way the pumpmaster is the pumpmaster and the listener is the listener. Not chosen exactly, but grown into, the way the mineral water grows into the cracks of a board. I have a route, a hook on my belt, a pair of gloves with the fingers cut off, and a small tin of black grease. I know which lamps run hot, which ones breathe too much spray, which ones the algae loves, which ones the mineral crust keeps trying to swallow. I know the sound of a lamp that is about to fail: a faint buzz, a change in the color, a brittleness in the glass. I tap the rail outside the transformer yard once, sharp, to tell whoever is listening that the light is well.

There are no moths at my lamps. That is the first thing an outsider would notice, if an outsider ever came to stand beside me. The glass is never smeared with wing dust. There are no midges spiraling in the warm beam. The absence of animals is exact, and it is easiest to see at the lamp, because a lamp is usually a place where the flying things gather. Here, the only things that gather are the mineral shapes. The sinter grows on the brackets and the bases, and sometimes, when I scrape it away, a shape comes loose that looks exactly like a moth, or a bat, or a tiny bird with folded wings. I have a shelf in my room above the pump house. It is full of these shapes. They are not alive. But when the steam curls over them from the heating pipe, they seem to move.

I do not miss animals. I have never seen a live mouse, only the mineral one the sinter-mason tagged and left by the west weir. The children keep stone frogs and blind stone fish. The lamps are enough, and the pumps are enough, and the green plants that curl and uncurl with the machines are enough. Sometimes I think the whole town is an animal made of iron and water, and I am one of the small fingers on its hand, clearing an eye.

I would find myself in the bowl of steam, then, with my bucket and my rags. I would be the lamplighter: a human shape in a rubber coat, moving from one halo of orange light to the next, wiping the mineral film from the town’s eyes so the town can see its own edges. I would not be lonely. The water would be loud below me, the pumps would be beating under my feet, and the lamps would burn all through the brown-green day and the nearly identical night. And if I stood long enough by the one by the salt stall, I would see my own three shadows on the boardwalk, each a different color, none of them dark. I would tap the rail twice with my knuckles—come—though no one would be there. The steam would tap back, a drop of water landing in the bucket. A small note in the permanent hum.
