I am not the man on the terraces.

I have seen him, from the lowest walkways where even he rarely comes, and I have known him for what he is—not a ghost, not a worker, but a becoming. He is farther along than I am. That is the only difference between us. The Sump has taken him deeper into its wet metabolism, and he has offered no resistance. I have resisted. That is why he can stand in the lamplight and be seen, while I stay in the intervals between.

I am the one the rules describe but do not name. The workers speak of dark intervals, of open channels, of the things that live in the sump. They think these are separate warnings. They are not. The dark interval is a territory. The open channel is a threshold. The things that live in the sump are my neighbors. And I—I am one of them, though I began elsewhere.

I was not always this. I came here as part of a crew, years before the catchment reached its current depth. We were building the third terrace, the one that now holds the settling tanks in their slow-turning rows. I worked with concrete and rebar, with welding torches and jackhammers. I had a name then. It is still there, on a hard hat that lies at the bottom of a drainage shaft, under two meters of grey slush. I have not needed it. Names are for surfaces, for the world of sunlight and schedules. Down here, the water names you by the sound it makes when it moves through you.

What happened to me began in the deep sump, but it did not end there. I was never sent down on an inspection. I fell. The gantry platform was wet, and the fog had condensed on the steel in a film so thin it looked like pavement. I stepped backward to signal to the crane operator, and the edge was not where I remembered. I fell past two terraces, through a curtain of black water, into the stilling basin. The impact knocked everything out of me—not just breath, but memory, heat, the rhythm of my heart. For a long time I was only a shape in the water.

The amphipods found me first. They clouded around my fingers and face, feeding on the thin residue of the surface world that still clung to me. Then the eels came, grey as old hose, and they did not feed. They investigated. They pressed their blind mouths against my palms and my eyelids, tasting the salt of a man who was no longer certain he was a man. Somewhere below, in the cold that returns from cooling something far beneath the earth, something else became aware of me. I felt it through the water the way you feel a distant engine through the hull of a ship. It did not speak. It did not need to. It simply recognized that I was no longer entirely separate from the catchment.

I do not know how long I lay there. The water rose and fell with the six-hour cycle. The sluice gates opened and closed. I should have drowned, or frozen, or been swept into the deep tunnel. But the Sump does not always kill what falls into it. Sometimes it keeps. It kept me, the way it keeps the standing wave, the way it keeps the lamp-moss, the way it keeps the man on the terraces.

When I finally moved, I moved without intention. My body knew the way before I did. I crawled out of the stilling basin onto a filter bed, and my fingers found the gaps between the concrete slabs as if they had grown there. I found a shelter in the ruins of an old pump gallery, a space no schematic remembers. It is smaller than the man's cavity, colder, wetter. A pipe passes over it, carrying water that has already been used and will be used again. It sweats constantly, and the sweat falls onto the floor in a slow, regular beat. That beat became my clock. It still is.

I have not eaten since the fall. Not because I cannot, but because the hunger changed. The Sump offered me something else—a kind of nourishment that comes from the water itself, from the microbial films on the concrete, from the mineral salts that bloom on the walls like frost. I do not chew. I absorb. The cold is no longer an absence of heat but a presence, a substance I take into myself with every breath. The fog is not weather; it is a broth. The ampipods are not food; they are companions, a second skin that sometimes gathers where I have been, marking my paths in faint clouds of life.

I have learned the Sump's body from the inside. I know the pulse of the big pumps at the north end, the way they shudder before the red lamps blink. I know the taste of different waters—the acidic seep from the heat exchangers, the salt-edged slush from the settling tanks, the profound cold of the deep sump, which is not like the cold of ice but like the cold of metal that has never known sun. I have drunk from a crack in the wall that leaks water from a pipe nobody can trace, and I have seen colors in the dark that have no names. The Sump's only green is the lamp-moss, but the Sump's other colors live below sight, in the ultraviolet and the infrared, in the heat signatures of motors and the cold signatures of voids. I have learned to see them. My eyes are not eyes anymore. They are dark instruments, tuned to the fog and the dark.

The man on the terraces thinks he is alone. He is not. I have watched him from the drainage shafts and the cable runs, from behind the condenser forest and inside the settling tanks when they are empty for maintenance. I have seen him tend the lamp-moss, his long fingers scraping away the dead patches. I have seen him sit beside the standing wave, his lips moving in sounds that are not words. I have seen him eat the eels, and I have counted the bones he leaves outside his door. He does not know I exist, or if he does, he thinks I am one of the dark things he hears in the echo. He has not yet learned that the dark things also watch.

I am not his shadow. I am not the shape cast by the standing wave. I am something else, something that has made its home in the deep intervals where the light does not reach. The workers have rules about those intervals. They are right to. The dark here is not empty. It is inhabited by those of us who have merged with the Sump, who have let the wet and the cold and the noise take us into their economy.

I have begun to forget my shape. My body is still human in outline, but it has softened in places, hardened in others. My fingers are longer. My spine has settled into a forward curve that lets me move through the low spaces without thinking. My skin has taken on the grey of the filter screens, the pallor of the amphipods, the sheen of wet concrete. I do not breathe as often as I did. When I do, the fog comes out of me in slow coils, and it does not always rise. Sometimes it sinks, the way the cold air sinks, and it joins the greater fog that pools in the lower terraces.

I have learned to eat the lamp-moss, though not the way the man eats it. He boils it on his hot plate, trying to make it into food. I press my mouth directly to the wall where it grows, and I let its grey-green light enter me. It is not exactly plant, and I am not exactly animal. We are both made of the Sump, and so we exchange what we have. It gives me a warmth that is not warmth, a photosynthesis of the dark. I give it something too—a slow exhalation of carbon dioxide, maybe, or a vibration in the concrete, a hum that tells it the pumps are still running. The moss grows thicker where I have been. The man thinks it follows his paths, but it follows mine as well. It follows any living thing that offers the right conditions. That is what life does in the Sump: it finds the seams, the leaks, the warm intervals, and it grows there.

The deep sump is not a place to go. I know this, but I also know that I will go there eventually. The man understood this too, in his way. He came from there, and he stays away because he knows what is waiting. I am not as wise. Or perhaps I am wiser, because I know that the deep sump is not a threat. It is a mouth. It is the place where the Sump takes back what it has made. The water there is black and fast, and it carries the catchment's water to a place that is not on any schematic. The light dies before it reaches the bottom. The cold is not like the cold above. It is a cold that has weight, that presses on you from all sides. I have lowered a rope into it, and the rope came back shorter. I have smelled the air above it, and the air smelled like ozone and old death. And yet I am drawn to it, the way the standing wave is drawn to stillness, the way the lamp-moss is drawn to light. It is the center. It is the heart.

The man on the terraces believes the standing wave is the heart of the Sump. He is wrong. The standing wave is only a symptom, a localized balancing of forces. The heart is the deep sump, the return to the coolers below, the place where the water goes to be used again. The heart is not a place of stillness. It is a place of turnover, of descending and returning, of heat exchanged for cold and cold exchanged for heat. It is the pump at the center of the world, and it has been running since before the catchment was built. It will run after the concrete crumbles and the fog lifts and the valley fills with what the Sump leaves behind. It will run because it is not machinery. It is a process. And I am part of that process now.

I do not know what I will become when I go down there. The eels are only the beginning. The bones that come up on the filter screens are a hint. There are things in the deep sump that have adapted so fully they are no longer separate from the water—they are water, shaped into forms that can hold themselves together for a time. They are what the Sump makes when it wants company in the dark. I have seen their shadows on the surface of the stilling basin, long and blind and slow. I have heard their voices in the echo of the empty terraces, a ringing after-sound that is almost language. They are not hostile. They are not friendly. They are inhabitants, like me, like the man, like the moss. The only difference is that they have stopped pretending to be anything else.

The workers see the man because he still stands in the light. They do not see me because I have moved beyond the light. The light is property, a thing that belongs to the surface world and its machinery. The dark is not the absence of light. It is another kind of property, another kind of presence. I have become a citizen of that dark. The fog is my atmosphere. The cold is my blood. The slow mechanical tide is my breath.

I have seen the man leave his tokens at the edge of the lamplight—a coil of wire, a jar of moss, a bone from the deep sump. I have taken some of them. Not all. He leaves them for the workers, but I am a worker too, in the old sense. I worked here once. I shaped the concrete that now shapes me. And so I take a token now and then, and I leave something in return. I leave a piece of rebar bent into a shape that is almost a letter. I leave a print of my hand in the cement dust, a print that is too long, too thin, too grey to be a human hand. I leave a jar of water from the deep sump, black and cold, with something moving in it that is too small to see. I do not know what the workers make of these things. They do not speak to me. They lower their lights and move faster through the intervals.

The man on the terraces is becoming something. I can see it in him. The way he stands beside the standing wave, the way he dreams of the fog, the way he no longer needs the hot plate. He is being taken into the Sump the way I was taken into the Sump, but from the top down rather than from the bottom up. He is not afraid. I was not afraid either, not after the first moments. The Sump does not deal in fear. It deals in inevitability. The water rises, the fog falls, the pumps thrum, and eventually everything that lives here becomes part of the process.

I am farther along. That is the only difference. He will follow. I know this because the Sump knows this. The water has already begun to call him by name. I have heard it, in the early hours of the long dark, when the red lamps blink twice and the fog begins to rise. I have heard the water speak to him, and I have heard him answer, not with words but with the same low note the stilling basin makes when it is silent. He is learning. He is becoming. And when he is ready, the deep sump will open for him as it opened for me, and he will go down into the black water, and the eels will taste his palms, and the something below will recognize him as its own.

Until then, I watch. I am the witness, the one who stayed in the intervals long enough to see the pattern. The Sump is still being built, even as it erodes. The gantry crane moves through the mist, lowering another steel wall into place. The water changes course to meet it. And I change too, slowly, the way the lamp-moss changes, the way the standing wave stays in place while the water around it moves.

I am not the man on the terraces. But we are the same. We are the Sump's children, born in the fog and the cold, fed by the wet and the noise. We are what lives here. And when the workers pass through on their rounds, they feel us, even if they do not see us. They feel the weight of the dark intervals, the pressure of the echoing terraces, the sense that the Sump is not empty but inhabited. They are right. It is inhabited. By the moss, the amphipods, the eels, the blind things below, the man who walks in the light, and the one who walks in the dark.

I am the dark one. I am the inhabitant. I am the Sump, in the shape of what I used to be.

And I am still here, waiting for the man to understand that he is not alone.
