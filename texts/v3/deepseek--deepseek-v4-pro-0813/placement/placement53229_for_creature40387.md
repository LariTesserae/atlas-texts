At first I think I might be the water, because the water arrives from the broken cistern and knows every crack in the west wall. Then I think I might be the blackbird, because the blackbird comes down from the street and watches the silence without breaking it. But the water is too quick, and the blackbird is only a visitor. What I am is slower.

I find myself at the bottom of the Moss Well, in the old quarter, not as the dweller but as the moss that has grown over the dweller and the stone and the spaces between them. I live on the dweller’s left side, where the seep runs down the wall, and on the backs of its hands, and in the seams of its long fingers. I grow along the wet stone where the light comes down bright and cold. I do not see, but I know the light by the way it pulls at me. I do not hear, but I know the silence as the thing I grow into. The dweller breathes under me, slow as frost. Each breath lifts my leaves a little and lets them fall. A springtail moves through me. A snail grazes me and I grow again. The dweller does not brush the snail away. We are all one surface.

The water comes through the stone as beads. It passes through me and lets go. A drop falls from a fern tip onto the dweller’s forehead, and I feel the wetness spread through the moss at its temple. The dweller does not wipe it away. The water becomes part of the same damp, the same cold, the same green. I am that green. I am what happens when stone is kept wet and the cold stays and the sky is bright. My world is the width of a shoulder, a hand, a wall. It is enough.

If you came down the narrow steps, you would not see me first. You would see the dweller, or you would think you saw only a fallen branch. Then you would notice that the dweller is not alone. It is woven through with me, and I with it. Its hair has turned green where it touches the wet stone. Moss has grown over its ankle, its wrist, the curve of its ear. I am that moss. The old women above call it the Moss Well. They are telling my name without knowing it.

I do not know if I am one moss or many. It does not matter. I am the living surface of the place. I am the pelt on the stone and the green between the dweller’s fingers. I grow at the speed of the dweller’s breath, which is the speed of the shaft, which is no speed at all. When the children are told not to shout into the gap, it is partly because I would not answer, and the silence would be too heavy to carry back up the stairs. I do not mind. I am not waiting. I am full of springtails and water and the slow weight of the dweller’s stillness.

That is where I would find myself: at the bottom of the Moss Well, on the dweller’s left hand, between the third and fourth fingers, where a bead of water is always forming and a snail is always passing. I would be the moss.
