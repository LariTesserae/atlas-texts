You would find yourself in the deepest root chamber, not as a visitor but as the oldest part of the place. The water would be black and still around you, the mud giving under your long, low body. The light there is not light so much as a green-brown pressure, and the air is so wet it feels like breathing through cloth. You would not stand, or walk, or look with quick eyes. You would be the mud-sleeper.

At first you might mistake yourself for the floor. Your back is plated in cracked clay, moss grows in the seams, and fine roots have settled over you as if you are only another rise in the earth. Crabs live in the crevices of your plates, cleaning you of algae and old silt. Their small claw-taps on your body would feel like your own slow pulse. You breathe once every hundred counts, and the water level in the hollow rises a finger’s width and then sinks. The pools obey your breath. The underground rhythm of the place is not the tide; it is you.

Your hands are six long-fingered, slow things. When you move, you do not step—you settle in a new direction, pulling yourself through the mud, and the trough you leave fills with water and becomes a lane. Your tail smooths the track behind you. You are not building the city in the way a person builds. You are simply being the oldest body in the hollow, and your movement is repeated until it becomes architecture. When you sift the sediment for food, the clean clay paste you leave is carried away by crabs to reinforce their walls. When you turn in a muddy basin, a pool deepens. When you reach up and tuck a hanging root into a gap, you are weaving the middle story of the town.

You remember, but not in words. You remember the hollow when it was only a damp scar in the raw limestone, before the canopy closed, before the first crab burrowed, before the birds began their long watching from above. You turned in your sleep and made the first pool. Your body held water in the rock, and the rest followed—mud, snail, fish, root, bird. You are not the owner of this place. You are its first condition, its oldest machine, its slow and almost silent center.

So you would not be the one exploring the lanes or climbing the root walls. You would be beneath all of it, half-sunk in black water, crusted with clay and moss, dreaming the city above you. The crabs would line up at the edge of your pool, waiting for what your jaws leave behind. The birds would land on your back and step aside when you shift. You would feel the whole hollow as your own breathing, and the whole hollow would feel you as its ground.

You are the mud-sleeper. The city of roots and burrows and platforms is the shape you have dreamed around yourself, slowly, in the dark.
