I am in a dark that smells of mud and fur and wet grass, and the dark is warm because it is full of bodies. I do not know my own shape yet. I only know the sound outside: water moving over something, a hiss like breath, and the occasional creak of a willow.

Then I move. The floor under me is packed and smooth. I slide into a passage that is not air but water, a cold mouth that closes over my head. I do not panic. My body knows this door. My hind feet push, my tail trails behind, and then I am out, surfacing into grey light.

The air is cold enough to show my breath. I float, letting the water hold me. Around me, the valley steps downward in pools, each one a sheet of polished slate. Dark lines curve across them—dams, hundreds of them, shaggy with sticks and moss. Smoke? No, just vapor. There is no fire here.

I look down. My front paws are small and hand-like, resting against my chest. My hind feet are broad, webbed, powerful. Behind me, a flat dark tail lies on the surface like a leather rudder. I am a beaver. A yearling, maybe. Old enough to know the work, young enough to still do it badly.

Another young one surfaces near me, smaller, with the same blunt head and dark eyes. We do not speak. We wrestle in the shallows, pushing each other under, climbing out, shaking. The water is so cold it feels like a second skin.

But then I hear it: a notch in the lower dam. The water there is not hissing; it is running, thin and fast, a wrong sound. It pulls at me. I cannot ignore it. I paddle toward the sound, my body low, nose up, tail just under the surface.

At the bank I rise onto my hind legs and scoop mud from the canal bottom. I hold it against my chest with my forepaws, the way I was taught, and waddle down the path to the dam. The mud is cold and grainy. I pack it into the notch, pat it smooth with my forepaws, pat again. A stone comes next, then a stick cut from a willow branch. The sound softens. The notch is still there, but now it is a held thing, not a wound.

An adult surfaces behind me. Bigger, darker, patient. It sniffs the patched place, pats the mud once with a forepaw, and then slaps its tail flat on the water—not alarm, just a sound that says, *enough for now*. I dive.

Later I find a willow branch in the water. I strip the bark in long curls, eating the green inner layer. My teeth are orange and thick, chisel-edged. They grow all the time. I can feel them wear down against the wood. The taste is cold and sweet and bitter all at once.

By dusk the valley pulls its colors inward. The pools go from slate to black. A heron stands at a spillway, motionless. A muskrat trails a thin wake through the cattails. Somewhere an otter moves like a long ribbon, and I stay close to the lodge. I know what an otter can do.

When it is dark enough, I slip into the underwater entrance of the lodge. The tunnel is narrow and known. I climb up into the chamber, where the air is warm and thick with musk. The others are already there, a pile of wet fur and slow breathing. A parent grooms my back with a split claw, drawing through my fur. I settle into the pile. My tail is cold, my teeth ache, and I can still hear the water outside, but now it sounds right: a soft, even hiss over the dams.

I understand now. I am not a visitor. I am one of the builders. The valley is full of us—low, brown, quiet, shaped like our materials. We do not finish the dams; we hold them. The water always finds a way through, and we always find it first.

Where would I find myself? Here, in the sunken valley below the ordinary world, in the cold wet maze of pools and canals. Who would I be? A yearling beaver, still learning the grammar of water and wood, already unable to leave the running water alone.
