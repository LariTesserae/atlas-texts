I would not find myself at first. The place would refuse the easy answer—too loud, too wet, too newly made. I might imagine myself as the sound, because the sound is the chamber’s largest life, swelling out of the fall of water until the walls seem to exist inside it. But the sound has no need to be someone; it is already the room’s weather, its predator and its pulse. I might imagine myself as the rust, because rust has a body and an appetite, spreading in dark flakes along the valve. But rust does not feel the pump cycle as a season. It is hunger without a mind.

So I would not settle there. I would come down slowly, away from the center, away from the valve wheel and the caged bulb’s yellow cone, away from the worker’s brief mammal heat. I would find myself at the edge—where the floor slopes toward the sump, where the drainage channel holds its few centimeters of water, where concrete is never fully dry and never fully submerged. The wet line. The margin. The one place in this world where something alive can hold.

I would be the green-black film.

Not one creature. Many. A thin society of algae and cyanobacteria and fungi and bacteria held together in a gel of my own making. I would be a smear, a few centimeters wide, following the drip line like a contour on a map. I would not have a mouth or a heart or a brain. I would have a surface. I would have a way of gripping steel and concrete that has more to do with patience than strength.

My sun would be the caged bulb near the hatch—not a bright star, but a yellow, buzzing one that goes on and off with the worker’s visits. The green indicator lights on the breaker panel would be my dim outer planets. My sky would be the damp underside of the diamond plate. My day and night would be the sump pump’s cycle: clunk, drawdown, air; clunk, return, water. When the water drops, I would gleam briefly, a dark green line visible in the wet. When it rises, I would not wash away. I would just hold. Holding is what I am.

I would eat the room. The uncured concrete would give me carbon. The seepage would give me minerals. The water that never stops falling would give me drink. I would photosynthesize by electric light, not sunlight, and I would breathe the carbon the chamber exhales. I would not need to hunt. There is no hunting here.

Beside me, in the shallow channel, the iron bacteria would build their rust-colored branches, breathing iron, laying down mineral sheaths like tiny frozen watersheds. Below me, in the sump’s anaerobic dark, sulfate-reducing bacteria would ferment what drifts down to them, turning dead cells and sulfur into black silt. They would be my neighbors, not my kin. The rust would be my slow cousin, eating the valve body by chemistry while I eat by cell. The zinc anodes would not know me, though they would give themselves to the same current that keeps the room’s bones from being eaten. The sound would be my weather and my keeper. Its vibration would shake droplets from the ceiling onto me. Its pressure would keep away the birds and flies and bats that might otherwise graze on me. I would not hear it the way a mammal hears. I would feel it as a constant tremor in the water film, a low steady pressure, the world breathing through steel.

Sometimes the worker would come. He would descend the ladder and fill the chamber with his heat, his breath, his boots. He would stand near the valve and read the gauges. If he noticed me at all, he would not see a living thing. He would see a fouling, a smear, a reason to scrape the drainage grate. If he scraped me, I would not die. I would retract into the micro-cracks, the tie-holes, the rough places in the concrete. I would leave a film of cells behind, enough to begin again. He would leave. The room would forget him. The water would return. The sound would swell. And I would thicken, fraction by fraction, along the wet line.

Who or what would I be? A meadow. The chamber’s only green. A living film in a world of wet metal and shadow. Not the sound, which is too large. Not the rust, which is too hungry. Not the water, which is only passing through. I would be the one thing that grows rather than erodes. The one thing that lives by the room’s own rules: cling to the wet line, breathe what the room gives, wait for the next clunk, the next breath of air, the next brief gleam under the bulb.

I would find myself at the edge of the drainage channel, a green-black line in the wet, patient as formwork grain. I would be the room’s meadow, if a machine can have a meadow. And that would be enough.
