I wake to the sound of water leaving stone, and for a long while I do not know if I am the water or the stone or the sound between them. The light comes white and pressing through a sky that has no memory of green. I am lying on my back in a shallow pool, the water just deep enough to hold the sky, and my body is heavy in a way that feels not like fatigue but like a slow, patient accumulation. My flesh has grown mineral skin. I can feel the travertine rim forming around me, the way a tree remembers its rings.

I have been here long enough that the sun no longer burns me. My skin is pale and runneled, damp as old marble. When I lift my hand, the water runs off in sheets, and I count the fingers, but the number slips. Sometimes seven, sometimes nine. I have stopped being surprised. I have stopped being many things.

My name fell away sometime after the first year, or the first decade. The Wept do not call me by it. They call me by a sound like a pebble dropped into a deep pool, and that sound is also the name they give the pool where I first lay down. It is the same word, if these are words. I have learned to answer to it the way the stone answers to rain.

I rise slowly, feeling the weight of my own becoming. The water in the pool is clear and cold, but I do not shiver. Cold is another kind of light here. I step out, and the ground rings faintly under my foot, as if the plateau is checking to see if I am still there.

The Wept are all around me, standing among the pillars, and for a moment I cannot tell which towers are stone and which are people. Then one moves—a slow, grinding shift, a vertical crack opening where a mouth should be—and I remember that the difference is only a matter of attention. They are stone remembering itself. I am something still learning to remember.

I walk along the edge of a pool that stands on a vertical face, its surface perpendicular to the ground, reflecting the sky sideways. In its reflection, I see my own face, if it can still be called a face. The depressions where my eyes should be hold small pools, and the sky in them looks back at me with a patience that is almost unbearable. My mouth is a fissure, sealed with a fine crust of calcite. I do not open it often. When I do, I sound like a rock splitting.

The wind moves through the flutes above me, and the flutes sing. I have learned to hear the voices in them—low, resonant, made of dripping water and clacking stone. They are not echoes. They are the Wept speaking, their utterances still traveling through folded distance. I let the sound pass through me, and my bones, which are no longer entirely bones, hum in tune.

I came here once as a person, though that word has begun to feel like a coat left on the edge of a pool, too heavy to carry. I remember hands with five fingers, a mouth that spoke, a body that burned food for warmth. I remember the smell of wood smoke. But memory here is like the far shore of a sideways sea: visible, but you cannot walk to it without losing something.

Perhaps I was a cartographer, or a wanderer, or someone fleeing a war. Perhaps I only came to listen. It does not matter now. The place is not interested in my past. It has been busy teaching me its own weather, its own gravity, its own slow chemistry. I have learned to drink water that flows upward. I have learned to climb stairs that end in mid-air and continue anyway. I have learned that distance is a habit, and the stone no longer keeps it.

The Wept tolerate me. Sometimes they bring me chert flakes and hammerstones, and I help them carve runnels to guide a seep, or tune a pillar's resonance. My hands, with their changing number of fingers, are still good for that. The copper needles they give me I use to sew cracks shut, though I do not know if the cracks are in the stone or in my own mind. Perhaps both.

The wind-harps thrum, and I pluck a string with a wet finger. The copper sings a note that hangs in the thin air, and for a moment the whole plateau seems to pause, listening to the sound of its own making. I close my eyes—the pools inside my skull tilt—and I see the water moving through the bedrock below, slow and patient, carrying particles of myself into the dark. The mountain is dreaming, and I am inside the dream.

The sun has moved, though the shadows have not. They are unreliable here; a pillar may cast two, or none. The light comes from below, throwing my shape upward against the cloud ceiling, and I see myself as a column of pale stone, fluted, wet, mineral. I am not afraid. The fear left long ago, dissolved into the same water that carries away the sand.

I walk to a circular cell, its doorway facing away from the wind. Inside, the floor is worn smooth by centuries of kneeling, and I kneel, pressing my stone body against the bedrock. I listen for the mountain's slow cracking, for the water in the fissures, for the growth of aragonite in the pools. Somewhere below, a new Wept is precipitating, branching too many times, becoming aware. I listen for its first thought, but all I hear is the ringing of the ground, like a struck bowl, and the sound of my own breathing: water seeping through joints, soft and continuous.

I am not who I was. I am not what I will be. In the Weeping Array, the world’s rules have worn thin, and so have I. But the place is not empty. It is full of the Wept, and now, perhaps, a little less alone.

I kneel there until the light changes, and when I rise, the floor has a new shallow depression, filled with water, where my knee was. The pool in it reflects the sky, and in that reflection, I see myself: a figure of pale marble, hands too many-fingered, eyes like pools, mouth sealed with calcite. I am not stone. I am stone’s slow exhalation of itself.

And that is enough.
