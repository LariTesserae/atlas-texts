You would not find yourself all at once. First you would be a wrong sound: the dry click of your own breath, the word *dry* still shaped in your mouth like a stone that will not dissolve. The city would hear you before it sees you, and what it hears is a thing from elsewhere, a small hard edge that has not yet been rounded.

Then you would find yourself as a crack. Not a crack in the city—a crack in your idea of being inside. There is no inside here. The water runs through you, the noise enters through the soles of your feet, the wet air replaces the air in your lungs until the difference between breathing and drowning becomes only a matter of consent. You would sit because the stone has already shaped itself to your weight. You would remove your boots, not because you decide to, but because they are no longer useful. The water would take them, or the city would absorb them into a basal crust, and by the next rain the place where you left them would be just another mineral seam.

The shell-backed scrapers would come first. They would graze the film on your calves and the backs of your hands, taking you for stone. That is the first baptism: to be taken for stone. You would learn not to flinch. Later you would learn that flinching is only a kind of vibration, and the city reads all vibrations. Even your heartbeat is a small drum.

You would learn the food. The sour jelly from the public troughs tastes less like food than like the city’s opinion of you: warm, mineral, slightly fermented, perfectly sufficient. You would eat it because hunger, too, becomes a wetness. You would drink by standing still with your mouth open during the rain that falls on the north side of a wall while the south side steams. You would stop expecting seasons. The only seasons here are deposits: a year of salt, a year of geyser-mist, a year of slow flood.

Your skin would begin to change. First a pale bloom on your forearms, like the mineral film on a sluice. It would not itch. It would feel like the first quiet after a long noise—except the noise is still there. The noise is so much there that it has stopped being noise and become the medium. The bloom would thicken into a scale, not armor but a record. Every time you lean against a wall, the wall gives you a layer. Every time you sleep through a geyser, the geyser gives you a ring. You would be building, but not with hands.

You would learn the drumming. At first you would think it was random, the way the city’s own sound is random. Then you would notice the keepers pause after certain sequences, wait, tap again. They are not speaking to each other exactly; they are speaking to the city, and the city answers through the stone. You would lift your hand—not a hand-fan, but still a hand—and tap. The first time, nothing. The second time, nothing but the general noise. The third time, a response would travel up your wrist and into the hinge of your jaw: not a word, but a shape. It means: *You are in the pattern now. Do not stop.*

You would find a niche. Not one you choose. A low arch between two collapsed columns, where the water runs in a steady sheet and the scrapers are so thick they form a moving pavement of their own. You would sit there because the hollow fits the back of your head. The city, which is always making niches, has made you one. You would become a pool. The rain would gather in the hollow of your collarbone. Tiny pale swimmers would live there. You would not disturb them.

Your hands would find a task. Not repair—the keepers do not repair, they deposit. You would learn to gather soft mineral mud from the edge of a sluice, to pack it into a hairline crack, to press it until the water in your own palms sets it. It would not be good work. The next tide would take it. But the taking is part of the work. The city does not need it to last; it needs it to be offered. You would become an offerer of mud.

And you would understand the rules, slowly, not as instruction but as weather. Nothing stays dry, so you would stop wanting to be dry. Nothing grows green, so you would stop looking for green. No space stays empty, so you would become a space that is not empty. Nothing stays finished, so you would stop waiting to be finished. The unfinished is not a failure here; it is the only material.

One of the keepers would settle beside you. It would not greet you. It would turn its eyestalks to your pool, to your encrusted hands, to the crack you have been filling. It would drum a sequence so low you feel it in your sternum: *What are you dissolving?* You would not have a word for it, but you would know the answer. You are dissolving the idea that you ever had an outside. You are dissolving the memory of dry, the memory of green, the memory of a silence that was not made of water and stone. You are turning those memories into a kind of mineral: a salt the city can use.

After that, the changes would come more fully. You would not notice them until they are already past. One day you would try to stand and find the wall has grown a thin handhold around your shoulder. Another day you would open your mouth to speak and only steam would come out, sweet with the city’s breath. Your legs would no longer be two, but a rooted column—yet you could still move, not quickly, not as you once moved, but as water moves through a pipe, as a wave moves through the sea. You would become movement itself, distributed through the stone.

You would not die. That word would stop meaning anything, like *dry* and *green.* You would become part of the city’s metabolism. The animals would graze you and you would graze the animals. The water would pass through you and you would pass through the water. The drumming would continue, and you would be one of the hands that keeps it going: not a keeper, exactly, but a listener who has become a relay. You would tap the messages onward. You would feel the city’s whole body as if it were your own, because it is.

And when a stranger comes—because every once in a long while, someone from elsewhere washes in on the warm current, boots still laced, mouth still shaped around the word *dry*—you would be there. You would not startle them. You would turn your eyestalks, if you have them, or your one remaining eye, or just the flat stone face that used to be a face. You would drum a short sequence. The vibration would reach them through the ground, through the water in their boots, through the bones of their jaw. They would understand you are not asking who they are. You are asking whether the new crack in the wall is theirs, or whether they would like to help fill it.

Because in a place with no outside, every stranger is either a new material or a new hand. And if they stay long enough, they will become one of those things too: a layer, a conduit, a wall that breathes, a pool that holds small swimmers, a sound in the continuous roar, a question that has become the answer.

You would be the next layer. Still wet. Still unfinished. Still being deposited.
