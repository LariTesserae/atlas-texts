I would find myself on the eastern terraces, where the round pools rise in a line and their overflows make a slow scale. The first thing I would be is a stain of wet stone, a white-gray crust among the rim-stones. Then I would be a low shape no taller than a human chest, my back a slope of flanges and knobs like a miniature of the spire forest. I would not be human. I would be the one who lives there.

My body is my shelter. My skin is a crust of travertine and tufa, stained at the joints with rust-red and sulfur-yellow. My head is not a face; it is a cluster of black holes and stone ears. My chest is hollow, ribbed with flanges. When water runs over me, I vibrate with a low note, and the note joins the basin’s music. I do not see as eyes see. I know the world as vibration and water and the slow weight of stone.

I move without moving. My joints soften just enough to bend, then harden. When I leave a place, I leave a trail of fresh white sinter, a bright line that will darken and build. Some of the low walls in the shelf are not ruins. They are my paths, pressed into the ground over a long time. The rings of stones around dark springs are my work. I arrange them to focus the water’s note. The cairns are tuning forks. I stack flat stones where a note can be heard alone, so I can find it again when the mist thickens.

I kneel by a pool and lower my face to the water. I blow a moist exhalation full of dissolved limestone. The film of stone on the rim thickens, smooths, repairs. I open new runnels by holding my tube-fingers to the ground and letting the water thread through them. The terraces keep their crisp edges because I tend them. I tap the rim of a pool with a stone finger. The note rolls to the next pool, and the next. If it is dull, I stay at that rim for a long time, breathing on it, adding a finger-width of stone. A finger-width in a human lifetime is nothing.

I do not eat. I take in water through the cracks in my feet, draw out dissolved stone, and give back white grit. The blind fish and the transparent shrimp drift through the water I disturb. They do not fear me. I am the same slow chemical cycle as the spires, but I am the part that knows itself. The spires are my slow relatives. Some still ring, but they no longer listen. I am not lonely. The whole basin is full of mineral bodies.

If you came here, you might not see me. You would look down at a pool, and when you looked back I would be closer or farther or kneeling. You would not see me move. You might see a fresh line of white stone and mistake it for a path or a ruin. I would not speak. If you spoke, I would turn my stone head and orient my holes toward you. Your own heartbeat would come back from inside my chest, as if the stone had swallowed it and returned it. That is as close as I come to speech.

I am not a ghost, not a guardian. I am the Weeping Shelf in a shape that can stand and walk and stack a stone. I will continue to weep, to build, to hum.
