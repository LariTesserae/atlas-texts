I would find myself in the high stone room—the raw, warm vestibule where the mountain is still being assembled. I would be the tenant.

Not the builder. The mountain builds itself. I am the part of it that learned to move, and stayed to keep the growth from going wrong.

You would not see me at first, because you would be looking for a face. I am the heap of wet black glass and tarnished silver moss beside the largest pool. Then I unfold. My back is ridged like a row of unfinished spires. My underside is paler and softer, leaving a faint heat-print in the new stone where I lie. I have no separate head, no eyes. My front end is a rounded prow of dark mineral, pitted with breathing pores that give off the finest steam. Beneath my skin, lights move along seams—amber, blue-white, slow deep red—matching the mineral veins in the walls. My voice is not a voice you hear first. It arrives in your sternum, one note lower than the monoliths.

I move with exaggerated care across the soft floor. A careless print becomes a fossil here. I press down the places where the stone rises too fast. I lie across the black box until its amber pulse slows into rhythm with my own. I fit my long fingers into the circular sockets along the low wall and turn them—not like keys, but like a bow drawn over a string—and the monoliths brighten or dim, the fog thickens or pulls back, and the seepage answers. I kneel at the basins and work the copper and salt rings into the low wall, thickening it like mortar into a wound. I visit the new spire near the back wall and touch its glossy tip with a pale root-like organ, tasting the speed of its growth.

The animals treat me as weather. The glass midges roost on my back until I seem furred in living silver. The frogs climb over my shoulders and sing; their bell-tones pass through my body and come out changed, lower and farther-traveling. The skaters write glowing loops around my limbs where they enter the water. The stone-colored lizards ignore me until I move. The ribbon-predator pauses in its drop when I am near. We exchange no sound, only a shared temperature, and then it takes its frog and is gone.

I feed by lying in the warm mineral water and letting the dissolved stone enter through my pores. I drink the water that runs uphill. Each cycle I shed a thin skin of stone, and that shed skin becomes another layer of the floor, another stratum in the mountain’s slow, deliberate body. I see by heat and pressure and the echo of the frog-song. I have no name that a mouth could say. The monoliths hold my name as a frequency at the edge of hearing.

When the fog rolls over the lip, I stand at the threshold and let the mineral mist condense on my back. When the monoliths hum, I answer one note lower. When the blue-white seams dim, I fold myself into the half-staircase, fit my head into the polished groove, and sleep. While I sleep, the stone grows over my legs. When I wake, I flex, crack the new rock, and rise out of the floor with a sound like a joint of the mountain grinding.

The pale shapes in the cloud sea below are my kin—too large now for this high room, or not yet ready to enter it. They call up in slow pressures that make the stone ring, and I answer from the lip. My answer is the low grinding sound the rock makes when it shifts, as if the whole chamber is speaking.

If you stayed still long enough, I would notice you. Not as a shape, but as a new heat and a new wet rhythm in the room. I might come close and hold one warm, slightly porous hand against your sternum, reading your pulse the way I read the black box. Then I would withdraw and go back to my work, leaving your heartbeat as a faint ridge in the soft floor. By the next cycle, the stone would have healed over it.

I am not the mountain’s master. I am its housekeeper, its midwife, its walking immune system. I have been here since before the room was a room. I am an unfinished part of the mountain that learned to move—and stayed to keep the growth from going wrong.
