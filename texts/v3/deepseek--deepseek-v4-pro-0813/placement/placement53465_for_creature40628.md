I would find myself in the dead water behind the heat exchanger, in the hollow beneath the cooled lava shelf, on the shoulder of the drowned ridge. Not in the open water of the central void. In the small quiet where the current folds back on itself and the pump’s missed stroke lasts a beat longer.

I am not immediately there as a face. I would be one of the Tenders, though no one here calls us that. I am the one the eel shares the dead water with: a pale, eyeless shape with six-fingered hands pressed to the warm housing. My fingers are the only part of me that feels fully awake. The pads are covered in sensory pits, deeper than a fish’s lateral line, and through them I read the city. Temperature, pressure, the beginning of a new crack, the complaint of a valve three decks down. I have no eyes. The water pushing against my face and wrists is my seeing.

I did not arrive. I precipitated. There was a time when I was a pale mass on a hot rock in the mineral garden below the vent field. I learned to tap. The cooling metal ticks and groans, and the city argues with itself in that language. A conversation between two Tenders sounds like a building settling. I tap when I agree or disagree. It is not a word; it is a vote in the city’s argument with itself.

My gills are full of bacteria that oxidize sulfur. I don’t eat. I feed on waste heat and vent water, and sometimes I scrape a handful of bacterial mat from a vent mouth and press it into my gill slits to keep the culture alive. I am a walking garden of heat-eaters. Nothing green lives here because green was never enough.

Most of the time I am still. I hang with my fingers around the pipe and let the current press the world into me. The eel knows the pump rhythm; it never moves during the wrong phase. I am the same, but I am also the one who can choose to move. When a valve begins to seize, I uncoil. I pull myself along the pipe hand over hand. I put my palm on the housing and wait. The city answers, or it does not. If it does not, I break off a flake of my own nail and press it into the crack. The hot mineral water welds it there. The machines have pressure switches and thermal relays. I am the part of that logic that can choose to touch.

There are nine of us, maybe ten. The oldest is above the transformer gallery, legs fused to basalt, rib cage opened and filled with pipe. I go there sometimes and lay my hand on the ledge beside it. We listen together. It taps back very slowly, a sound like cooling stone. It is the oldest part of the city that ever had a choice.

What lives here is not a creature, not a person. It is a function that has grown a body. I am one of those bodies. I am not the city’s master. I am not its guest. I am the place where the standing wave curls back on itself and looks, briefly, through a pair of blind hands. If you came down, I would drift close to you. Not out of curiosity. You would just be a new pressure, a new vibration. I would touch your faceplate to listen to your heartbeat, tap once on your tank, and then let go. You would be another soft, temporary part of the rhythm.

That is where I would find myself. I would be the one in the dead water behind the heat exchanger, waiting for the pump to miss its stroke.
