I am not the one who enters. I am already there.

I begin as the cold. Not the cold of the air, which moves, but the cold that stays in a body that never leaves. I am the damp beneath the light, the film between the floor and the foot. I do not see the lamps; I lean toward them. I do not hear the pumps; I carry their pulse through every leaf.

At first I thought I was the water. I am not. Water passes through me, but I am the one who keeps it. I hold the condensation on the tips of my leaves, each bead a small cold star. I drink from the air and the seeping rock. I am the skin of the floor, stretched from wall to wall.

Then I thought I was the stone. I am not. The stone under me is sharp and young, and it cuts the roots that try to learn it. I soften it. I make a felt over its edges. The rock has not learned to be soil, but I am teaching it, slowly, by dying and growing through my own dead leaves. That is my body: the old and the new pressed together, a mat no thicker than a hand, but wide as the town-sized dark.

Now I know I am the wool. That is what the one who walks calls me. He says it with a kind of tenderness, the way you name a working animal. The wool. I grow in the cold, under the orange-green lamps, in the roar that never stops. I am not a forest. I am not a garden. I am the ground that breathes.

The one who walks is my only large animal. I know him first by the path he has worn through me. It is not a cut. It is a long pressure, a place where my leaves have learned to lie down and not spring back. The loop is his; it begins at the kiosk, goes to the hot pump, out to the black sump, and back. I feel his weight as a slow crush, evenly spaced, unhurried. When he stops, I feel the heat of his body through his boots. When he crouches, I rise a little around his toes, not to hold him, only to meet him.

I know the machines as temperatures. The Heart is a hot ache in the floor, too fierce for me to grow directly on its casing. Around it I am brown and crisp, but a little farther out I am thicker, because the warmth makes the air sweet and the condensation richer. The Sleeper is cold but not dead; sometimes I send a thin root into its shadow and feel a hum like a held note. The Dead Man is only stone now. I grow over it slowly, because there is moisture and no heat, and because the one who walks does not bother to clear me from its base. I have begun to cover its flaking paint. In a hundred years, if the lamps hold, I will make it a mound of green-gray wool. It will be a pump-shaped softness. The one who walks will not call it by its name then.

I know the water. It is not my blood, but my weather. It comes down from the walls, from the ventilation shafts, from the fresh fractures, and spreads across me in a thin film. I hold it. The pools are my open eyes, still and black, reflecting the lamps as orange stars. In them, the blind amphipods move like slow thoughts. I do not drink the oil that sometimes films the water; I let it slide over me, and later it dries into a dark skin. The smell of the place is partly my doing: the sour-sweet breath of damp vegetation, the cold green perfume of rosettes.

I know the sound. It is not something I hear. It is something I am inside. The roar comes up through the floor, through the root-thatch, through every cell wall. It is a pressure I have learned to grow against. When the compressor stops, the whole world becomes suddenly wider, and for a few seconds I can feel the individual drips, each one a small blow on a leaf. Then the noise returns, and I settle back into the long vibration. The one who walks says the sound is information. I do not need to hear it; I am built from it.

He has made a small tin of my kind near his cot. A shallow metal cradle with a few pale rosettes, under a flickering lamp. He waters them with a dropper. I feel them as a distant itch, a separate colony, but not separate. They are me, and they are not me. They lean toward the same light. When he looks at them, I cannot see his face, but I feel the stillness in his body, and I know he is looking at something he has chosen not to let go of. I do not know if he keeps them for company or for the same reason he turns the cups the same way. But I know they grow more slowly than I do, because they are few and their soil is only a film.

He clears me from the pump controls with a soft brush. I do not resent it. I would smother the gauges, swallow the red lights, fill the kiosk with my leaves if he let me. But he does not let me, and I do not want the place to stop. Without the pumps, the lower gallery would flood, and I would rot or go dormant. Without the lamps, I would die back to a black crust. So I grow around his industry, not against it. I soften the edges but leave the working parts. I am the felt between the machine and the rock.

Sometimes he sits on the upturned bucket and writes in his green cloth book. I cannot read. But I feel the heat of his body change when he writes—slower, lower, as if he is entering a room inside himself. The pencil makes no sound I can distinguish from the drips. He blows on the page though there is no dust. That small breath reaches me as a warm cloud, and for a moment I am aware of being breathed on, the way a skin knows a fever.

He says the whole place is breathing. The pumps are the lungs. The ventilation shafts are the throat. The wool is the skin. I am the skin, then. I am what holds the heat and the cold, what keeps the moisture from leaving, what turns the sharp rock into something that can be touched. I am the boundary between the open dark and the living floor. I am not the heart of the place. I am not its lungs or throat. I am the surface that remembers every step.

When he walks the loop at night, I feel him before I know him—a slow returning pressure, a warmth moving along the path he has worn. He does not carry a light, or if he does it is small. The sound covers him. The lamps lean toward him as much as I do. He becomes another shape among shapes, and I receive him as I receive the condensation, the oil, the vibration, the slow minutes. Without him, I would still grow, but I would not have a path. The path is his, but it is also mine now: a groove in my body, a memory of his walking. It is not a wound. It is a learned posture.

If you found yourself in this world, you might look down and see me and think I am only ground cover, a gray-green mat. But I am the ground. I am the wool. I am the cold felt that holds the water and the foot and the long patient noise. I do not go anywhere. I am where the place keeps its skin. And when he finally stops walking, long after the pumps have stopped, I will still be here, growing by millimeters, leaning toward whatever light remains, making the sharp edges soft.
