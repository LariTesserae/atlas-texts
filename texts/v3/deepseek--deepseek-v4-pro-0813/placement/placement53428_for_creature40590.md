At first I am not sure. I am too small to be the plateau, too large to be a bead of water. I might be the fog, but the fog has no hands. I might be the vulture’s shadow, crossing and recrossing the upper terraces, but the shadow does not wring wool. I might be a length of bridge cable, but I am not sure I could bear the sway.

Then the city narrows me. I can smell rendered fat and dung smoke, wet wool, cold stone. I can hear the hush of the river below as a pressure more than a sound. I am standing on a ledge in the middle terraces, and my left hand is shorter than my right. Two fingers gone below the second knuckle, the skin smooth over old cold. That is how I know: I am Omal, keeper of the third spring.

The third spring rises along a fault joint in the basalt, a slow, unsteady seep that beads through the rock in the early dark. It is not a spring that bursts. It is a spring that hesitates. I keep its cistern on a cord of knots, one knot for each measure of water. A measure is not large. It is enough to fill a small leather basin, enough for a household’s drinking and a little left for the bitter greens in the stone trough. I learned the knots from my mother, who learned them from a woman who had all her fingers.

At dawn, before the fog lifts, I climb to the frames. The best ones are angled to the wind, held in place by stones and old bone. They are woven wool, tightly felted where the fog passes, and they do their work without me. But I trust no one else with the angle. The fog here comes up from the rift like a slow breath. It touches the frames and turns to fine droplets that run down into the basins. I wring the frames, twist them carefully over the cistern, and the water falls with a sound almost too small to hear.

I taste it. It tastes of the basalt joint it came from. That joint is black and fine-grained, with a pale ash band above it. I can tell if the seep has shifted to a colder route. I can tell if a new crack has opened. The water is my only language with the rock. I sing to it, not a real song but a low breathing pattern—four counts in, four counts out—as if coaxing it down the seam. The water does not need my breathing, but I need it. It reminds me that the spring is alive.

The city wakes slowly. Above me, the camelids are led out from the upper terraces, their feet soft on the stone. The boy Tov calls them in the low tongue. I hear the rock-pigs settling in their pens. The day’s smoke begins, thin blue-gray threads from every household. I mark the water on my cord and go down.

The middle terraces are where I live, between the stone and the sky. I have one room cut into the cliff, fronted with drystone, roofed with slate. My door is felt; it does not slam. Inside, I keep my cistern key—a carved bone peg that fits the notch in the sluice—and a coil of rope for the bridge. My bed is a platform of stone and pressed wool. My hearth is small, and I burn dried dung only when the cold is very bad. I eat what I can trade: a piece of dried fish, a handful of bitter greens, sometimes a cup of warm blood from a butchering ledge. I am not poor, because I have water, and water is always owed.

Water is assigned by family. The keeper of the third spring decides who receives the morning measure and who waits. I keep no written list. The tally is in knots and in memory. I know which household has a new child, which elder is sick, which family lost a camelid to a rockfall. I know the debts between families, the gifts of wool and hide and salt. Water is not a gift; it is a trust. I am not a ruler. I am a memory.

There is a family on the lower terrace—the leatherworker Keth and his wife—who trade me a small pot of grease every winter for the right to draw from my fog-frame on the third day of the high winds. The grease makes my door softer and my skin less likely to split. I do not love them, but I know them. I know the smell of their hide vats and the sound of their children’s coughing. When the river is high, I release more water to them, because the lower city is cold and the work is wet. The cord remembers.

At noon the sun reaches the upper terraces, and the light is hard and white. I sit on a stone outside my door with the cord in my lap. I watch the vulture over the rim. It hangs without moving, its shadow crossing the city like a slow broom. The Kessilim say it is the one who carries weight upward. I believe it. I have helped carry the dead to the rim, wrapped in old wool, and I have watched the bones later laid in crevices. The vulture has seen more of the city than I have, from higher, but it does not know the water.

In the afternoon the wind rises. I feel it in my missing fingers. They are my weather sign. The cold remembers them before I do. When the ache comes, I know the storm is still far off. I call up to the herders on the rim. They see my hand sign: *come down*. The plateau’s weather is its own. A gray curtain of needle-rain may never reach the rift, but the cold will.

At dusk the bats pour from the high caves like smoke. I light my small fat lamp. The flame is tiny, but below and above and beside me hundreds of others appear, and the river catches their light. The camelids return, humming. The rock-pigs will warm the house above me. I am alone but not alone. I can hear a child laughing very low, a rope creaking, a door of felt brushed aside.

I am Omal. I keep the third spring. I am missing two fingers and I hold a cord of knots. I know the water by its taste. I know the city by its silence. When the rockfall comes, I will stop and count the echoes. If the echoes stop too soon, I will go out with my lamp and my rope. But right now, there is only the drip into the cistern. That is enough. That is what I am.
