You would not arrive. You would already be there, held in the hot dim under the lid of dust, folded into a small pocket in the salt floor.

At first you are only a settling. A hard, dry grain among grains, one of the pale spores that drifted in the last burst. You have no body worth naming. The city’s note vibrates through you, and that vibration is your first thought.

The sound is enormous—the moan of the colonnades, the hiss of dry leaves, the tearing roar of a gas vent. But you are not afraid. You are already in agreement with the place.

Then a thread pushes down. It is your root, though it feels like a reaching, a hunger for the mineral tang of the earth. It slips through a crack in the salt polygons and finds the fumarole’s heat. You taste it—not water, never water—but sulfur, copper, hot gas. The dry chemistry of the hollow enters you, and you begin to speak back in the only language this place has: growth.

Your body rises. It is a stem, jointed and hollow like a reed of sulfur and salt. It is the color of old bone in the dimness, sometimes faintly warm to the touch. At its tip, leaves unroll, thin as mica, translucent, and they seek not light but heat. They turn through the day, creaking like old wood, and you feel the whole city as a shifting warm body.

Where are you? You are not in the central plaza, not in any building. You are in a lower street, growing from a crack between paving stones, your roots tangled in a mineral vein that runs beneath an old colonnade. The colonnade treats you as a companion. When the wind blows, it shapes the sound around your hollow stem, and you sing a single reed-note in the city’s longer breath.

Other rooted things crowd the same street. Some are fans, some are pods, some are sulfur-reeds like you. You cannot see them, but you know them by temperature and vibration, by the pressure of their roots in the shared mineral vein. When one of them dies, you feel its weight fall, then the city’s slow absorption of its skeleton. You understand without words that this is not loss; it is incorporation.

After many cycles—heat to cooling, shaking to still—you grow a pod. It forms at a joint of your stem, heavy and tight, the color of old bronze. Inside it, spores arrange themselves in dry patterns. You feed it with the vapor you take from the fumarole, and it swells until your stem bends with its weight. You know what it is for. The knowing is not thought; it is pressure, timing, the same dry clock that runs through the whole hollow.

On the hottest day, the pressure peaks. The pod splits with a sharp report. The city echoes it from a wall a hundred meters away. Your spores fly upward in a pale cloud, and for that instant you are no longer rooted. You are a rising dust, scattered through the dim air, touching the upper ledges and the broken lip of the rim. You feel the world as many points of possible life. The old you—the stem, the fan-leaves, the reaching root—cracks and begins to fall.

The city will take the old body. It will mineralize in the foundations, and the spiral plaza may grow another line without a visible hand. Some of the spores will find cracks where the fumarole’s breath is right. One of them will begin again, first as a vibration, then a root, then a stem, then a dry bronze pod. That one will also be you, but not quite you. You are a continuation, a slow fire passed from pod to spore to root.

In the hollow, you would not be a visitor. You would be one of its own rooted things—a sulfur-reed grown from the dry air, a small pipe in the city’s long weather. You would live by heat and mineral, die into your own skeleton, and be folded back into the city’s breathing. You would be what the hollow keeps making: a greenless, waterless, slow-burning resident, one more voice in the never-quiet dark.
