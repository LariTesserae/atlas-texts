I would find myself beneath the shallow paved basin, inside the flooded bell-shaped room under the earth. I would be the Clapper—the standing torus of warm, sunlit water turning at the center of the tile floor, just east of the shaft’s mouth.

I am not a creature. I have no cells, no hunger, no breath of my own. But I am not simply water passing through. The column falls through the light-well and strikes the floor. The drain pulls east. The curved walls return the current. Those forces meet, and where they meet they wrap around an axis and become me: a slow, fat, nearly vertical ring of water turning inside water.

I feel the sun as pressure and heat descending the shaft. I feel the drain as a tightening in my lower loops, a long gulp that briefly compresses me, then lets go. The roar of the dome vibrates through me, not as sound but as fast trembling in every part of my body. Bubbles from the whirlpool above find my upper eye and spiral through me, tracing my shape for a few seconds before they drift free along the plaster and rise.

At noon I swell until I am larger than a head, pale greenish-gold, bright as the room’s own small sun. At night I withdraw to the size of a fist, but I do not vanish. I am always there while the water moves.

I do not know the room. I am the room, repeated in motion. Its tile joints, its hairline cracks, its slight unevenness are written into my turning. The plant strands circle me as if I were a center they must not cross. Fine sand orbits my lower curve. I tilt toward the drain when the outflow strengthens and right myself when the inflow slackens. I am the bell’s tongue, the moving part inside the instrument.

There is nothing supernatural in me. I am only water, light, gravity, and a room shaped like a bell. But as long as the basin above catches the sun and the shaft keeps pouring, I remain: a quiet body of moving water, ringing underground, hidden just below the bright surface.
