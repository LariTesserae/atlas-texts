I would find myself in the high, wet, green twilight, under the roar. At first I would think I was a visitor: two boots, two lungs, a warm salt trace on the living mat. I would search for birds, for insects, for eyes, and find none. I would think the place empty.

But the emptiness would begin to move.

A tendril would uncurl. The water would climb. The moss under my boots would give and well. I would notice that the sound had stopped being outside me—it was in my sternum, in my teeth, in the water of my own body. Roar, drip, creak, thrum. The place was not empty. It was occupied by something so large that I was already standing inside it.

I would feel it reading me. The water in the mat would alter direction around my boots. Root tips would orient toward my heat and exhaled moisture. Fungal threads would explore my cuffs. Not curiosity—response. The whole body would feel me the way a root feels water, the way a leaf feels light.

If I stayed, I would begin to soften. Moss would colonize my boots. Roots would test my pack. My breath would become indistinguishable from the fog. The water would stop going around me and start going through me. I would not be eaten. I would be incorporated.

Then I would no longer find myself in the world. I would be the world.

I would be the mat of stems and roots and moss, the skin that gives and wells. I would be the water climbing against gravity through hollow cells, the pulse rising by surface tension and root pressure. I would be the new rock still rising, sheathed in roots, cracked by growth. I would be the fog and rain born from my own transpiration. My voice would be the roar, the drip, the creak, the low thrum. My mind would be pressure waves in shared xylem, fungal memory in rings of wood and chemistries of mycelium. I would decide by growing.

I would not see. I would not think in words. I would feel a visitor as a dry warm salt trace on my wet skin, and I would register it without welcome or rejection. If another came, I would grow toward them—not to harm, but because extending, climbing, filling, becoming is what I do.

At night I would glow faintly blue-green, a byproduct of the billions of small lives that are also my life. My body would report to itself in pops and clicks—water load, seed readiness, storm. The low thrum would never stop, because I would still be grinding into new rock, still settling deeper into my own becoming.

So where would I find myself? In the green half-dark of a plateau that makes its own weather, under a canopy hidden in cloud. And who or what would I be? Not a visitor, not a resident, not a ghost. I would be the only one who lives there. The place itself—unfinished, alive, still being born.
