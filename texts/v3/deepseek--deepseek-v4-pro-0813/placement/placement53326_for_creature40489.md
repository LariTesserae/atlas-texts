I would find myself low to the ground, in the blue hour before dawn, before the stars have faded and before the sun becomes a weight. I would know the salt not as brightness but as a texture under paw: cool, crisp, each polygon edge a small ridge holding the night’s contraction. I would know the smell first—the wet-zinc smell of brine rising through the cracks, the dust on my own coat, the faint oily sweetness of a pump’s greased leather gasket half a kilometer away. I would not call myself fox. That is the man’s word. I would be the one who moves along the berm without wasting breath, ears full of the tiny crackle of brine flies and the far-off snap of salt like someone walking on broken glass.

The man would be there. I would know his footsteps before I saw him: the give of the crust under his boots, the slow pace that does not hurry. He would come out of the dark carrying the smell of woodsmoke and bitter tea. We would fall in together, me trotting ahead, pausing where the seep darkens the ground, looking back to see that he is still coming. He always is.

At the pumphouse where the wooden door has gone soft as felt, I would wait outside while he goes in. I would hear the tap of his knuckle on the pressure gauge—not a question, a greeting—and the small hum that comes from the pump even when it is not running. I would sniff the threshold: oil, salt, the mineral breath of the concrete. Then he would close the door and we would move on.

At the evaporation ponds I would drink from the fresh seep while he kneels at the edge and touches brine to his tongue. I would not drink the pink water. It is too sharp, too alive with small shrimps; it would taste of lightning and age. I would lap from the seep instead, where the water is thin and cold and slightly bitter. The man would nod, as if tasting the same morning. The flamingos would still be far off, too bright, improbable against the white.

In the heat of midday I would rest in the shadow of the old loading derrick, where the salt has not yet softened the planks. The man would sleep in his shelter under the rubber sheet. I would dream in smells: the moss on the warm pipe flange, the mummified vicuña slowly becoming a polygon, the copper wires in the cracks, the finches nesting in the junction boxes. I would not dream in stories. I would dream in routes. The seep line. The fox den in the loose fill. The place where the fence wire drips with condensation at dawn.

In the evening I would hunt. Not because I understand tomorrow, but because dusk is the hour when the rodents move through the loose fill and the snipe stitch the sky with their thin, metallic calls. I would pounce, miss, pounce again. The man would light his small stove and boil his bitter tea. I would hear the fire’s whisper from far away. I would not go to him then. He would not call.

When the dark came fully, I would return to the shelter and find him lying on the cot, breathing in the rhythm of the pump timer. I would curl in the doorway, tail over nose, facing the open pan. The salt would tick and settle. Somewhere a pump would start and stop. The air would cool so fast it felt like the basin shrugging off the day. I would sleep lightly, one ear always on the distance, where the salt snaps and the birds call once and the silence closes over it.

So where would I find myself in this world? I would find myself on the berm at dawn, red coat dull with dust, eyes adjusted to the glare, moving beside the man who has forgotten his name. Who would I be? I would be the fox. Not his, exactly, and not wild in the way the birds are wild. I would be the creature the basin has shaped for this particular life: four-footed, salt-white at the muzzle, patient, opportunistic, half a step ahead of the man, belonging to the place the way his rusted bolt belongs to it, the way the lost boot under its crust belongs to it. I would be the one who knows him without needing his name.
