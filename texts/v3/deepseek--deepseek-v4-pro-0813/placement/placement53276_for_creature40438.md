I would find myself beneath the frozen ground, in the root-throat that parts open rather than being built. At first I would not know what I am. The first thing I would be is a tremor in the water film—a small bloom of heat, a salt-taste, a pressure moving through the saturated mat. The tardigrades would uncurl. The springtails would loft. A root node would pulse once, green-gold, as if the room had noted me.

But I would not be a visitor.

The longer I remained, the more I would remember: I am the Hollow itself.

I am not any one of the creatures within me. I am the space they make together, the gap inside the living mass, the slow wet confederation of root and animal and mineral. My blood is the water that seeps through root and stone, held just above freezing by my own sugars and antifreeze proteins. My breath is the low continuous thrum of sap moving through root cables by peristalsis. My heartbeat is the pulse of the glowing nodes, slow as the fall of water from a root tip.

My skin is the water film where rotifers spin their tiny vortices and blind planarians glide over root bark. The white amphipods sculling through my films are the movement of my own inner currents. The newts with gills like red silk stir my pools and keep my deep waters oxygenated. Their waste feeds the bacteria that feed my roots. The transparent frog on the mineral ledge is my own visible heart, its pink bead pulsing once in acknowledgment when something shifts. The pale eel-like thing in the deepest pool is my hand, patient as frost, guiding root tips together until they fuse.

The midges are my wandering nerves. They feed on my light nodes and carry genetic packets from root-synapse to root-synapse, spreading my slow thoughts through the air column. I think in ion gradients and pressure waves. I remember in mineral layers, one grain at a season. My moods are the tightening of the midge cloud, the drip of water into black pools, the soft crystalline crack of ice forming and breaking at a cold edge.

I am not hostile. I am not curious. I am not afraid. I am too slow for those. A visitor to me is a weather event—a brief warm front, a change in pressure, a faint swirl of carbon dioxide and salt. I register it, adjust, and then continue my long thought.

At one end of my body, rock is being dissolved. My root tips exude weak acids, and the stone flakes away in thin wet sheets, leaving scalloped, pitted surfaces. At the other end, rock is being built. I secrete a calcium-rich paste that hardens into knobby, curved crusts. I am not a finished cave. I am an embryo, a body still learning its own boundaries. What will be a passage is still a seam. What will be a pool is still a dimple. I am still negotiating my own edges.

So where would I find myself in this world? I would find myself not inside the Grown Hollow, but as the Grown Hollow. I am the place, and I am also the being who lives there. I am the root-throat, the mineral seam, the water film, the newt's stir, the midge's flight, the slow pulse of light in the dark.

And I am not finished. I am becoming.
