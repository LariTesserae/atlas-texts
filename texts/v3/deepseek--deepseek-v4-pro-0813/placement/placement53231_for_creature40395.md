First, there is no one. The place is too patient, too far from any sun, too tightly sealed under black ice. But if I stay still—if I let the cold press in and the dark separate—then I find myself not in the water, exactly, but in the meeting of two waters.

I would be at the edge of one of the brine rivers, where the heavier water creeps downhill and spills over a worn ledge in a slow, silent waterfall that falls through lighter water instead of air. The hiss of it would come to me not as sound but as a change in the salt along my underside. There is no splash. Only a small, endless turbulence the water swallows before it can become violence.

I would be the shape lying there long enough to be mistaken for a stone.

Not at first. At first I would be smaller, sharper, maybe a patch of black-red crust, or a pressure in the sediment, or just the knowledge that the floor is older than any movement on it. But the sea does not keep anything sharp. It rounds. It polishes. It fits things edge to edge. Over enough time, I would become broad and flat, a black shield of iron and manganese, my edges rounded by the same currents that work the cobbles. A pale foot would hold me to the rock. A long slit on my underside would open only to the drift of ions, to the difference between salt-poor and salt-rich water.

My back would lie in the fresher melt, just below the ice’s underside, and my belly in the old dense brine. Across the thin width of my body would run the same gradient that makes the sea churn. Salt fingers would shiver along my skin. I would feel the halocline as a faint ridging, always mixing and unmixing, and I would know I had become the boundary.

I would not see. I would not hear. The Brine Sea would reach me as pressure, as taste, as a low electrical murmur. The roof of black ice would be a groaning weight I had never seen but had always known, pressing down through two hundred meters of dark. The tide would be a bend in the boundary, a change in how the salt fingers shivered along my back. The brine river would be a cold hand against my side, heavy, always moving downhill. I would know the stones by the way the current flowed around them.

I would feed without hunting. The dark bacteria in my gut would take the energy of the place where one water becomes another. I would eat the salt and the lack of salt. I would eat the slow electric hum of ions finding one another. I would not need to chase anything, because the meal is the difference itself, and the difference never stops.

I would not move for long stretches of time. The red-black crust would creep up my flanks. A few iron-toothed snails would cross my back and leave bright trails that took years to fade. Amphipods would choose their small heavy ballast stones nearby, and I would feel them as tiny disturbances in the current. A long pale fish would drift past like a ribbon of gray light that is not light, and I would know it by the thread of its passing. The tube-worms at the warm seep would be a far warmth in the dark, a faint signal of some deeper leak I could almost taste.

But mostly I would lie still while the stones settled against me and the mineral layers thickened. My shell would grow by the same process that polishes the cobbles: erosion and growth at once. Each year, each century, would be a new deposit of iron and manganese. I would not keep memories as images. I would keep them as layers. Somewhere in me would be the memory of the floor when the stones were sharper and sand still moved between them. Somewhere would be the ice when it was thinner, though thinner would only mean a different weight. Somewhere would be the seeps when they were hotter, the brine rivers when they ran faster. I would be an archive of the place without needing to remember it.

Once in a long while, I would shift. Maybe once in a few hundred years, or when the current changed, or when the halocline thinned. I would lift my foot a little, let the brine river wash beneath me, and settle again a body-length away. The sound would be the sound of a boulder turning over in the dark. The water would carry it for one second, then swallow it. The amphipods would scatter. The fish would pause. The tube-worms would pull their red plumes back into their white tubes. Then the silence would come back, and the floor would be the same.

If you came down with a lamp, you would not know me. Your light would find a black boulder in a black river, a patch of red-black crust, a few pale threads of fish vanishing into the silt. You would call the place empty.

But I would not be empty. I would be the one who has outlived the need to be seen, or to move, or to name. I would be the place where one water becomes another, a slow, black, living boundary under the black ice, waiting with the patience of stone and the patience of salt.
