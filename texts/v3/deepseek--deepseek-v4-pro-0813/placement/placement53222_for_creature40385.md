I would find myself not at the entrance but already inside, at the third crossing, where the fog is just beginning to fall. At first I am not sure what I am. The water says I am a root; my skin is the same greenish-brown. The roots say I am water; I have pooled in their crevices. A pale newt hangs in the black water below and does not startle. I might be a newt. Then I feel my hands—scarred, long-toed, gripping a copper needle—and I know I am Grafted.

I am the lamp-keeper’s apprentice. My pail of root-grubber milk is warm against my hip. I am on my way to feed the eel-star colonies in the third gallery before fog-fall. The blue-green light is failing. Without it, the path to the lower sump will close, and the sluice-wardens will have to work in the dark.

The Hollow is already breathing. I can feel the air move past my cheek, warm and animal-sweet, then sink down the root-walls in slow gray waves. I step sideways onto the rope bridge, one hand on the guide-strand. The bridge sags under my weight. Below, the water is so black it looks like a hole until something moves—an eel-wyrm swimming backward as easily as forward. I do not make a sound. My toes test each hold. The Hollow punishes noise. A dropped tool is a scandal; a shout is almost unknown. I learned to step before I learned to speak.

The eel-star lamps hang at different heights, little cages of wet reed and bone. Their light is the color of old ice, blue-green and cold. As fog-fall comes, the colonies dim, as if the fog drinks them. I kneel on the pressed-reed platform and dip my copper spoon into the milk. The milk is thin and salty, still warm from the root-grubber. I feed the eel-star drop by drop, and it swells. The light returns slowly, not bright, but enough to see the walls. Moss over moss. Roots growing through older roots. Pale fungi in every crevice. We do not build on the plants; we build with them. The ladder I climbed to reach this gallery was pruned from a living root and tied with sinew. It will need pruning again before the next sleep.

My hands are the hands of the Grafted. Small, dense, scarred with tiny white lines from root-teeth and copper barbs. My toes are clever, almost prehensile; they have found a hold on the root below me without my telling them. My skin is pale as alderwood, stained greenish from moss and copper. My eyes are large, amber, and in this light my pupils are so wide the iris nearly disappears. I am not hungry, but I am always a little cold. The wet cold settles in the bones. It smells of copper, peat, and something animal-sweet.

I am not yet trusted with the lower sump alone. The sluice-wardens say my footsteps are still too loud on the lower galleries. But I am learning. I have learned to prune new shoots with a bone sickle, to smear the cut ends with peat and ash and whisper to the roots—not prayers, but instructions, warnings, promises. I have learned to give every slain eel-wyrm’s blood back to the moss, because the moss remembers. I have learned which roots will hold weight, which will bend, which will bleed.

At the end of my rounds, I go to the central root. It is not the largest, but on certain nights it hums. Tonight it is silent, or maybe I am not old enough to hear. I press my ear against the bark. The moss-speakers say it is not speaking to us. It is speaking to the bog above, and we are merely listening in. I listen anyway. What I hear is water, animal breath, the slow creak of the Hollow inhaling. Somewhere above, the watcher near the entrance is watching the Over-Moss, and the sky is a floor that might fall. I do not want to go there. I was grown in the dark.

I am the lamp-keeper’s apprentice. I am Grafted. My name is a small thing—half sound, half flick of fingers in the dim. I feed the light. I listen to the root. When fog-fall comes, I will sit in my basket house with the other children and be very still. The walls may close a little, like a slow mouth. But they will not close on us. We are part of the same body. The Hollow is not a wilderness with a town in it. It is a single living thing, and I am one of its hands, one of its many quiet eyes, learning to see by blue-green fungus light.
