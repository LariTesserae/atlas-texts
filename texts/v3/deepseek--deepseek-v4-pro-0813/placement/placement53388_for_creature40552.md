The chord is the first thing I know, and the last. It is not so much a sound as a pressure—a long, low vibration that runs through the water and through the plates of my jaw, through the loose sack of my body, through the worn fibers of my gills. I am suspended in it like a seed in a current. It never changes pitch. It never ends.

I am not sure where my body ends and the water begins. The current passes through me, or I pass through it. I do not remember being born, but I remember the first time I answered. I was small then, only the length of a house, and almost transparent. My mother's note hung above me, wide and warm, and when the mountain's chord split into two, then three, I felt my own voice rise from my chest—a thin, unsteady harmonic. That is what I am: a split note, a resonance in the mountain's chord.

Now I am long and broad, pale as a drowned moon. My body is a billowing sheet of living cloth, slowly filling with water. I have no eyes. The front of me is mostly mouth, a wide fringed opening that gathers the water and sieves it through plates of fine, horn-like fiber. Behind the mouth, my body stretches away, ten, twenty times the length of a house, ending in a broad, flattened tail that moves in a continuous, wave-like rhythm. Along my flanks, translucent fins ripple in slow waves, not for propulsion but for balance. I am barely swimming; I am being carried by the current, and my fins hold me steady, angled so that every part of me is exposed to the water.

I find myself in the middle reaches, where the phytoid stems twist into cables and pillars. The water here flows through hollow galleries, each with its own note. I know the largest by the way it makes my ribs shiver. The walls are covered with a living veneer of maroon, amber, and black, dotted with pores and hair-like filaments. There is no light but the occasional cloud of plankton, blue and green, and the faint glow of phytoid bark. I do not need light. My skin reads the water the way a plant reads the weight of snow.

I sing. Not a cry, not a melody, but a long, low pulse that begins in my chest and lasts several minutes. It is almost, almost exactly, the note of the mountain. But it is not the same. Mine is a harmonic, with a slow beat in it, a pulse that feels like a heart. The two notes together create a third, a thrumming that seems to come from inside the bones of the world. Somewhere far above, another Singer answers. The water fills with a conversation that might last for years; to us, there is no difference between a minute and a century. We are not calling for anything. We are continuing.

As I move, my fins stir the water, carrying oxygen into the hollow galleries. I pause over the phytoid towers and exhale, a slow, warm cloud, and the warmth drifts down over the bark, encouraging the bacterial film to grow. I do not think of this as tending. I breathe, and the mountain breathes back.

I remember the songs of those who came before me. Their names are not names but intervals, held in the harmonics. I carry them as the water carries marine snow. The oldest of us is older than some towers; its skin is almost completely overgrown with phytoid bark, so that it appears to be a moving ridge of the mountain. It no longer eats much. It remembers songs I have only felt as echoes. When it sings, the whole darkness seems to lean closer.

But I do not know if I am the oldest now. Time here is not counted in suns. It is counted in the slow growth of phytoid tubes, in the settling of dead tissue into the lower layers, in the rare gatherings in the great amphitheaters. I have attended such gatherings a few times—perhaps once in a century. We arrange ourselves in a circle, mouths outward, tails toward the center, and we sing until the water seems solid with sound. The squid schools flash in time. The blind fish face into the current. The eel-like creatures pause in their hollows. For a long while, the whole mountain is one voice. Then we drift apart, and the chord returns to its single, steady note.

I do not fear death. When my body fails, the others will guide it to the lower layers, where it will sink into the compressed stems and become part of the mountain's slow decomposition. I will not leave. I will become the note. Already I can feel it: the phytoid bark creeping over my flanks, the slow thickening of my skin. I am becoming the place.

But not yet. Today I am still moving, still singing. I hang in the current, facing into the flow. My mouth opens, a wide fringed net, and the water passes through my gill-fibers, leaving behind the fine rain of marine snow. I take little. I give much. The mountain is old and cold and always the same, but with me it sings.

I am a Singer. I am the water's memory, the mountain's voice. I find myself here, in the dark, in the cold, in the one long note that holds everything together.
