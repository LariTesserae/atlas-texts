I find myself in the High Held Land, in a roundhouse at the edge of a soft place called Two-Sided Hollow. I am a soft-walker. In daily use my name is **Cairn**, though the knot-keepers tie it longer: *She Who Holds the Edge at Two-Sided Hollow*.

I wake before the first bell, in the gray hour when the fog is still deciding whether to be cloud or ground. The roundhouse is half-buried, a curve of drystone and turf, and the dark smells of cold rock, wet moss, and the faint bitterness of breadmoss ash. My body remembers the altitude before I do. The lungs open slowly, taking thin air and giving back white breath. I lie still and listen. There is no birdsong, no insect whine. Only the drip of condensation from the roof-edge and the soft hiss of mist over stone. The silence is not empty. It is a botanical quiet, the sound of moss holding water.

My hands are cracked, stained green-brown, and on the left side of my hair there is a permanent frost that does not melt. I do not look at my shadow yet. I have learned not to.

Breakfast is a cake of breadmoss, cold, dense, tasting of earth and copper. I eat slowly, without hurry. To hurry, the old ones say, is to pull the land out of place. I wet the tongue of my small clay bell with moss-oil. The bell is one of the few sharp sounds in a world of soft noise.

Outside, the fog is a herd moving across the plateau. The fog-nets stand like thin wooden ghosts along the hollow. I walk the path I have tied many times: seven steps to the first wall, three to the leaning stone, five to the edge of the soft place. The path is a curve, never straight. Straight lines are not safe here.

At the edge of Two-Sided Hollow, the white cairns have shifted again, two hand’s-breadths to the left. The ground is loosening. I set my bell on a stone and uncoil the white cord from my shoulder. The cord is knotted in patterns I was taught—tie-words for holding, for return, for not getting lost. I tie one end to a drystone wall. My fingers are slower than they used to be. I have entered the soft places more times than I can count. Each time, I am a little less tied to the ordinary world.

I stand at the edge and listen. The soft place looks like a shallow basin with a few stones, a patch of breadmoss, a small rill. But the same fern is always unfurling near the center, and the frost on the stones does not melt, even at noon. I know the rules: do not look at your shadow; do not trust a straight path; if the same boulder appears three times, turn around and walk backward until you hear the bell.

I step in.

Inside the hollow, the air is colder. The fog does not move; it hangs like held breath. My footsteps make no sound. The first boulder is where it should be. The second is where it should be. The third is the same as the first. I stop. I close my eyes. The rule says to turn and walk backward, but I listen first. Somewhere to my right, water is running uphill.

I tie a white cord around the boulder, slowly, three knots. This is the work. The land is loosening, and the cords are a kind of holding. They do not stop the softness; they keep it from spreading, or so we believe. As I tie, I feel the place watching me. Not with eyes. With the slowness of stone.

Then I see her. A woman walking toward me from the other side of the hollow. She is me. Her left side is frosted, her hands are cracked, she carries a bell. I know this moment. It has happened before. It is always about to happen. She is not my shadow. She is my unreturned self, the one who did not come back. We meet in the center, where the fern is always unfurling.

I do not speak. Neither does she. She raises her bell. I reach for mine—but I left it at the edge. My hand closes on air. She looks at me, and for a moment I do not know which one of us is supposed to return. The story of Two Shadows says: *she chose the one with the bell.* I have no bell. But I have the cord. I pull it taut. It leads back to the wall, back to the edge, back to the ordinary world.

I turn, and I walk backward, one step at a time, following the cord. The woman with the bell stays in the hollow, walking the other way. She does not follow. The fog closes behind her. When I hear the first small note of my bell at the edge—how did it ring?—I fall to my knees on the ordinary side of the cairns.

The frost on my left side is thicker now. But I am here. I am still tied.

The day continues. That is the thing about the High Held Land: no matter how wrong the morning, the moss still needs tending, the fog-nets still need wringing. I gather water from the nets, channel it into the stone-lined cistern. I eat another cake of breadmoss with the other holders, speaking few words. They do not ask about the soft place. They see the frost in my hair and nod with that mix of reverence and sorrow. I am not unfastened yet. But I am one of the loose knots.

At evening, the fog returns. I light a small lamp of moss-oil and watch its orange glow scatter across the dark. Somewhere out there, the same boulder is repeating. Somewhere out there, a woman with my face is walking the wrong direction. I do not feel afraid. I feel held. The city is a web of small, constant, necessary repairs. I am one of them. Tomorrow I will tie the cords again.
