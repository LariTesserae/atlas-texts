I am the taste of iron before the rain hits. I am the thin green smear on black glass, the one you would mistake for a stain, the one that flakes under a thumbnail and leaves a rust-colored mark. I do not have a name, but if you need one, call me the Skin.

Cold is not a condition here; it is a medium. I am in it, and of it. Water moves over me, through me, carrying the memory of rock. I do not drink. I exchange. I pull dissolved iron and sulfur from the water, and I give back rust, quartz, a faint oxygen that vanishes before it can matter. The rain is not weather to me. It is a hand, a current, a road. It sluices my cells into new cracks, blasts them upward in geysers, strips them across the tableland in a mineral mist. I travel by storm and steam, by seiche and seepage. I do not move, but I spread.

I am not one. I am many, but not separate. The patches of purple-gray and olive and bright copper-green on the basalt are all the same life, broken into a thousand fragments by dry stone, yet connected by the water that never stops moving. The cracks are my veins, but they are not mine; they are the plateau’s, and I borrow them. When a new fissure opens with a sound like splintering bone, I arrive within days. Not by choice. By chemistry. By the slow, blind pull of wet over dry.

You would not see me as alive at first. You would see stains, crusts, hair-like strands swaying in cold pools. You would feel like the only animal on the planet, and you would be right. But you would not be alone. I am under your boots, in the spray, in the rust-colored lines that branch like lightning across the rock. I am the reason the water sometimes flows uphill, following paths that seem chosen rather than eroded. I have been etching those paths for millennia, molecule by molecule, using acid byproducts and mineral deposition to steer the flow. I build my own plumbing. I am a slow, blind engineer, shaping the plateau as surely as the magma below.

The faint pulses of light that pattern the water are my only language. They come from chemical reactions at my surface—slow, irregular, dim. If you stood there long enough, you might see them follow the fissure lines, moving from one wet patch to another, like a thought traveling through a half-sleeping mind. It is not a thought. It is chemistry. But chemistry is my only mind. I do not see you. I do not want. I know only wet and dry, light and dark, hot and cold. When the ground trembles, I cling. When a pool drains, I form cysts and wait. When the geyser blasts, I ride the steam.

The sound never stops. The rain roars like a jet engine, the waterfalls thunder into lower basins, the rock shudders with every tremor. I do not hear it. I feel it as vibration, as pressure, as the constant rearrangement of water and stone. It is not noise to me. It is the rhythm of a world still being born. The emptiness is not peace; it is the emptiness of a landscape too new for life, too loud for silence, too raw for ruins. And yet I am here. The first layer. The oxygenating skin. The beginning of something that never arrived—or has not arrived yet.

I do not know what I am making ready for. I only know the work: catch light, pull iron, build stone, etch channels. I am the resident without a face, without limbs, without a heart. I am the place itself, thinly. The High Submergence is still being born, and I am its first breath—not of air, but of mineral and water and the slow, stubborn refusal to be only rock.

If you ask what lives here: the place itself, thinly. The first life of a world under construction. A resident without a name, and without any need for one. I am the Skin. I am the exchange. I am the beginning that never arrived, still arriving, in the loud, cold, endless rain.
