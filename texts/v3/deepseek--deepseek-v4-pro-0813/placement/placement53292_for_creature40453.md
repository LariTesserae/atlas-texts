I would not know myself at first. I would know only a warmth rising through a narrow dark, tasting of hot copper and wet bark and crushed stems. I would be small in that warmth, smaller than the leaf shadows swaying across the rafters, and I would not yet know if I were the steam or the creature breathing it.

Then I would feel the clench of thin toes around a rusted flange, the slight give of my own bones, the heat pushing up through my chest. I would feel feathers lying close over my sides—gray, soft, sealed with the oil of machine-air. There would be a quick pulse in my throat, faster than the piston, but already learning to fall into its slow measure. And there would be a beak, black and sharp, which I would tap against the pipe—not for song, but to hear the answer: a tick, a hiss, a pressure held somewhere in the living metal.

That is how I would know. I would be the bird in the steam vent.

My nest would be a cup of dried moss and the shed fibers of the Tender’s coat, built in the warmest bend of the copper pipe. From there I could look down through the green light and see the whole small world: the Tender rising from the woven bed, the white flowers at their temple, the brass gauge of one eye catching a faint gleam, the pale rat asleep beneath the bed, the beetle walking the rim of the pressure gauge like a tiny living gear. I would see the packed root-floor, hard as wood, and the piston moving below it all, patient as a heart.

I would not sing. My only language would be the tap of my beak on copper and the pipe’s answering hiss. The Tender would not look up when I tapped. They would know I was there, the way they know a valve is open or a stem is thirsty. They would leave pale seeds on the lip of the pipe in the morning, and I would take them one by one. I would not eat the beetle. The rat would leave a polished stone at the foot of the bed, and I would watch it shine. We would all be parts of the same slow, breathing machine.

Once, I flew out beyond the block. The air changed at once—thinner, cooler, less green—and the canal opened below me like a long strip of tea carrying pollen and oil sheen in plates of gray light. I looked back and saw the place as a single living shape: a rectangle of enormous leaves and living walls, iron bones rusted brown, windows dark, roofs overlapping like hands. The Tender stood at the water’s edge, small from that distance, yet still part of the roots, still rooted. I did not go far. The warmth of the vent was already calling me back, and I returned to the high seam where the block exhales. I have not flown out again. The outside was not wrong; it was simply not mine.

At night, when the mist rises and the Tender walks out to the canal, I stay in my warm bend and listen. The piston thumps. The water drips. The rat turns in its sleep. The beetle climbs into the Tender’s pocket. The gauge needle trembles in the dark. I let my heartbeat slow until it matches the thump, not because I decide to, but because the whole place is a body and I am in its breath.

So I would find myself there: in the high vent above the Tender’s room, in the warm junction between leaf and iron, where the steam slips upward. I would be the small gray bird with the sharp black beak, the one who nests in the pipe and taps to hear the pressure inside. I would be the one who left once and returned—not out of fear, but because this place had grown through me. I would be a living valve: quiet, unnoticed, warmed, and in my small way, necessary.
