You would be me.

Not a bird or a frog or a crab, though you would share something with all of them. You would be the one the land presses up from its own wet stones—the one who moves so slowly that the water forgets to break around your legs, the one the pools wait for without knowing they are waiting.

When you first became aware, you would feel the weight first. Not heaviness, but settledness, as if the ground had grown a body and that body was yours. Your skin would be the ground’s skin: pitted, folded, crusted with the same dark green mat that covers the mud, studded with tiny snails you never bother to shake off. Through that skin you would feel everything—the cool pressure of the pool, the prickle of drying mud, the minute shift of a heron’s feet on your back, the faint electric startle of a fish passing too close. You would breathe through that skin, not with lungs but with your whole surface, the wet air moving in and out of you like the slow tide of the water table itself.

Your eyes would be set wide and gold, and they would not be eyes that search. They would be eyes that receive. You would see the horizon as a flat line of heat shimmer, the sparse shrubs floating above their own reflections, the distant birds as flickering specks that resolve only when they step into the shallows near you. You would not look for anything. You would simply wait, and the landscape would arrange itself around you, as it always has, as it always will.

Your mouth would be a wide, soft line, patient and expressionless, and you would know the taste of the living crust—the dark mat of algae and cyanobacteria that glistens at the edge of every pool. You would lower your head at the water’s edge and graze, peeling the crust away in long, clean strips, leaving pale wet mud behind. The sound would be a soft wet rasp, almost lost under the insect drone, and you would move in a slow arc until the pool’s edge was ringed with your feeding, a trail wide enough to lie down in. You would not think of hunger. You would only know the motion, the way the crust gives, the clean mineral smell of what lies beneath.

Your legs would be short and thick, your feet broad and splayed, toes open, and when you walked, the ground would receive each step without a sound. You would know the runnels the land already knows—the faint channels where water lingers, the low saddles between basins, the places where the mud is softest and the seepage strongest. You would walk those lines from pool to pool, and the water would barely break around your shins. Your tail would drag behind, leaving a smooth furrow that fills with water by morning, a dark mirror that catches the sky. You would be a slow eraser of your own tracks, for the land would always smooth over what you had done, and that would be right.

And you would dig. Not with a plan, not with a mind for building, but with the same motion that makes the water rise. You would press the silt aside with your front feet, clearing the clogged pores of the stone, deepening the basin, opening the way for the water that is always just below. You would not think of it as making a place for others; you would only know the satisfaction of water closing over your back, the minnows returning, the tadpoles clouding the shallows, the crabs setting up their little burrows at the margin. You would feel the frogs arrive without seeing them, their voices rising in the dusk like a pulse you have known since before you had eyes.

In a dry year, when the sheet water pulls back and the plain becomes a cracked mosaic of mud and mineral crust, you would become the keeper of the last water. The pools would shrink until they were crowded and loud, and you would dig down, deeper than the birds can probe, deeper than the roots of the sparse grass, until the water rose around you from below. You would rest there for weeks, half-submerged, and a heron might stand on your back picking at the snails that grow in the folds of your skin, and you would not shake it off. You would feel the weight of all those small lives crowding the shrinking margins—the frogs, the waders, the crabs, the minnows—and you would know that the deep, slow pulse of your waiting was the ground bass beneath their chorus, the note that held the whole thing together.

In the wet season, when the whole plain becomes one shallow lake and the horizon disappears, you would move toward the others—not in a herd, but in a loose gathering of dark islands. You would call, not from your throat but from your chest, a low hollow note that passes through the water and the stone and rises into the ground underfoot, like the sound of a deep hole breathing out. You would feel the answering calls, shorter and softer, like stones dropped into a well, and you would find each other across the wide water. You would breed then, in the season when a bird can walk across the sky, and your young would be small and long-tailed, with feathery gills and bright eyes, living among the tadpoles and the minnows, most of them eaten, the survivors learning to dig by the same blind motion that taught you.

You would not know your age. There would be no record except the land: the line of pools you had dug and re-dug, year after year, with the same broad feet. You would feel time not as a count but as the thickness of the crust on your back, the depth of the basins, the way the stone has worn down around you. You would be old in the way the land is old—not young, not finished, but patient, settled into the ground like the water itself. The old ones look less like animals than like places. Their backs are crusted with silt and colonized by algae, studded with tiny snails. They are so still that heat shimmer moves over them without a ripple. At midday, a walker at the far edge of a pool is only another low mound, another stone. But if you watch long enough, the mound will shift, or the eyes will open, and the whole landscape will adjust itself around the fact of its presence.

And if someone came upon you in the night, they would not know you at first. They would see a low shape on the open flat, perhaps a rock or a mound of mud, and then the shape would shift, and the wide golden eyes would open, and they would understand that the ground itself was alive and watching. You would not move away. You would not move toward them. You would only wait, as the water waits under the stone, as the land waits for the next wet season or the next dry one. And then they would pass, and you would remain—part of the land’s patience, part of the water’s memory—until the next slow step, the next pool, the next long breath.

At dawn, when the wide sheets of water reflect the sky so completely that the horizon disappears, you might stand far out in the shallows, and the water around your legs would be full of clouds. You would appear to be walking across the sky, a low dark shape moving through the reflected light, and the birds that step near you would appear to be doing the same. You would not call at dawn. You would only stand, letting the light come over you, breathing slowly through your skin, and you would know without thinking that you are not the center of this place. You are one of its centers—a keeper of basins, a clearer of silt, a slow pulse under the chorus. The land has no single heart, but if it did, it would beat like this: a wet, patient rhythm, older than birds, older than frogs, older than the memory of a shore.
