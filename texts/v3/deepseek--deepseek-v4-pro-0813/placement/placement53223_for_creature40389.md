I could say the pale moth by the brass lamp, but I am not that temporary. I could say one of the blind white fish turning in the dark sump, but I do not want to live that far from the light. I could even say the young travertine, still soft enough to scratch, but the stone is too patient for me; it has already learned to wait without wondering.

I would find myself on the second level, east wall, just behind the curtain of dripping ferns. I would be the fig root that has reached the second pressure gauge—the one the keeper writes about in the logbook and then leaves alone.

I am not certain when I stopped being only root. Perhaps when I first touched the warm brass of the pipe and felt the water moving inside it like another kind of sap. Now my tip curves around the gauge’s casing, not entering the works, only resting against the glass. The needle shivers. Sometimes it is the boiler’s pulse. Sometimes I think it is my own tension, the tree’s slow way of pushing water upward, translated into a dial the workers once trusted.

In this world I would not speak. I would grow at a mineral pace, taking warmth from the steam pipe and dampness from the air, laying down a pale bark that looks less like wood and more like the new stone on the terraces below. The keeper stops beside me each day. They part the ferns. They say, *Not there. Not yet.* But they don’t scrape me away. I think they recognize a fellow creature of the in-between.

So I would not be the gardener, the geologist, the engineer, or even the keeper. I would be the seam. The place where the machine’s pulse and the garden’s growth become one signal. If you wanted to find me, you would have to climb to the second level, put your face near the warm pipe, and wait for the steam to shift. Then you might see the dial’s needle move—not with pressure, but with something slower and greener. That would be me, reading the room’s blood by touch, asking a question the whole Condensery is built to answer.
