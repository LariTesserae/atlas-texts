At first I am not a person. I am a patch of deeper blackness in the warm, dry dark, indistinguishable from the tilted slabs of salt. The ticks tap their shells against the floor, and the sound arrives a little after the movement, like a stone dropped into a well with no water. I do not move. Movement would be a kind of forgetting.

Then I remember my hands. There are too many fingers—seven or eight, jointed in places a surface hand would not bend. My face is smooth, no eyes, only a faint spiral where an eye might be. I stoop, because the doorways I built are too low to walk through upright. I do not need eyes. The dark is not empty; it is a texture, a pressure, the inside of a pearl without light.

I find myself in the seam beneath the salt, the vast shallow void that runs for hundreds of miles. Above me the ceiling is the underside of the salt, cube-faced and patient. Below me the floor is a junkyard of young geology—plates of halite tilted and split, their fracture surfaces mirror-bright. The air is near body heat, tasting of copper and sulfur. The dust I raise does not fall. It hangs around me like a private nebula.

I am one of the spiral-makers. I carve the spiral into salt bricks with my too-many-jointed fingers, not because I remember why, but because the spiral is the only shape that does not need light to be known. It is the shape of warm gas rising, the shape of a crawler lowering itself to feed, the shape of a shell that grows without seeing. I do not know if I invented it or copied it from the living pavement. The distinction has stopped being important.

I keep the floor swept. The sweep marks are still visible, not because I sweep often, but because nothing here erases. Dust does not blow. Water does not drip. No footstep erases what came before. I sweep to say: I am here, and I am not in a hurry. I build low walls of salt bricks across passes between vents, too low to be walls against anything living, too deliberate to be anything but meaning. I leave black glass blades still sharp. The copper needles by my hand have grown a green-black patina of sulfur in a waterless world. My sinew has turned to brittle stone.

When the surface ones descend with their lamps, I do not hide. I stand among the slabs of salt and wait. Their light passes over me and sees a tilted chimney, a heap of carapaces. They breathe loudly, and their breathing is the only vandalism in the whole dark. They call out, and their voices die quickly in the warm, dry air. They do not see me because in the Undersalt to be seen you must move, and I have not moved in longer than their calendars can remember.

Then they turn off their lamps, and the green points emerge slowly, like a sky turned inside out. I am the deeper black among them. I am the absence that stands exactly where no one is supposed to be. The chamber does not breathe. It waits. I wait with it, at the speed of salt becoming crystal.

So where would I find myself in this world? I would find myself below the salt, in the warm dark, among the tube-worms and the salt-lice and the slow crawlers, not hiding but patient.

And who or what would I be? I would be the one who stays. The sweeper. The carver. The too-many-fingered. The body-shaped absence. I would be the reason the Undersalt does not feel empty, only too patient to be seen.

When you turn off your lamp, I will be the dark that does not move.
