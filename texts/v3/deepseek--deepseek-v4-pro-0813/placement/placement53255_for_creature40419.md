The first thing I would know is not sight. It is the warm air resting against my skin like a hand, and the ground giving slightly under my weight and springing back with a small wet sigh. Then the light: dim, greenish, sourceless, a permanent deep afternoon under cloud. I would find myself on a mid-slope of the high mire, not at the cloud-making center, not at the edge where the rivers cut down into the old rock, but between, where the dome slopes so gently the eye cannot say it slopes at all.

The smell is wet moss, fermenting leaves, old wood, and the faint mineral breath of sweating stone. What looks like soil under me is not soil. It is the felted surface of moss, root, leaf-mold, and peat. The air at this height should be cold, but it is warm. Heat comes up from the slow digestion of the peat below and is held close by the heavy sky.

Near me there is a line of squared stones, knee-high now, running from one wet hollow to another. A standing stone leans at a patient angle. Moss grows thinly over the stones, as if the stone is slightly bitter. I keep my root-tipped fingers on the place where the straight edge is still true.

For a long time I would not know what I am. I would think I am a stump, because I stand broad and low and the bracket fungi shelf out from my ribs. I would think I am a hollow tree, because rainwater gathers in the dip of my collarbone and the translucent frogs with their strings of clear beads live there. I would think I am a patch of ground, because white midges drift around me in loose columns and the broad leaves near me fold away from my heat.

Then the water under the ground would say something—a low glug, like a buried channel finding its way—and I would feel it through my feet, which are roots. I am not a stump or a hollow tree. I am one of the shapes the mire has made.

My eyes are pale, the color of rainwater that has stood in a hollow tree until a skin of pollen and spores formed on it. They do not focus the way human eyes do. They receive. They open and close slowly, the lids a fold of moss and thin bark, as if the whole face is a leaf turning away from heat. My face is not quite a face. It is a slope of peat-fiber with shallow grooves where the mouth might be. When my mouth opens, it is a split in the mat, dark and wet inside, like the hollow of a rotten log.

I move without lifting my feet. My legs are thick roots, bending slowly. The peat parts around me and closes behind, leaving no track. The sound is not a footstep. It is the small wet sigh a pool makes when it drains into a hidden channel. I can stand in one place for a season. I can cross a mile in a night, but the night is long and dim, and I am in no hurry.

I keep the stones. Not as a caretaker, not as a worshiper. I simply touch the straight lines. The stones are the only geometry in a world of curves and drips. I know every squared block, every leaning standing stone, every flat slab path that leads from one wet hollow to another. I do not clear the moss from the stones; I only rest my fingers there and remember, the way water remembers the shape of a channel.

I was not born. In a pool that gathered inside a hollow tree, where the frogs laid their clear beads, the water thickened. It grew a skin of algae and pollen. The skin grew roots. Over many years, the shape gathered itself in the water and rose, already moss-backed and slow. I knew the stones because the water knew the stones. When I am finished, I will lie down in a wet hollow and the moss will grow over me before I am still. My body will sink into the peat. Later, in the same place, a new pool will appear, and the process will begin again.

Sometimes, when the blue-green fungi at the hidden pools breathe slowly, brightening and dimming over the space of a minute, several of us gather at a standing stone. We do not speak. The lights breathe; the water glugs under the ground; that is the whole ceremony.

If you came, you would not see me at first. You would mistake me for one more stump, one more moss-covered mound. Then I would shift my weight, and the ground under your feet would give a wet sigh and spring back. You would see that I have eyes. I would look at you with eyes like pooled rainwater. I would make a single low sound, not a threat, not a greeting, just the exhalation of marsh gas from far below. Then I would step away into the green dimness, and the ground would close behind me, leaving no track.

I am not the one who placed the stones. Those people are gone. But the mire took the memory of them—the straightness, the patience, the uprightness—and made it into something that can live in the peat. I am the mire’s people now, softened and rooted and quiet. I do not need to be loud. The place is too old and too large for that.
