I am not the first thing you notice. The heat arrives before me, rests on you, makes sweat and condensation the same thing. But I am already in that sweat. I am the shine on the walls, the thin sheets down raw limestone, the beads on iron rails, the drip from brick vaulting. I gather under the steel grating in a dark canal that moves almost invisibly. I am not the heat, but I give the heat weight.

At first I thought I was many. Each drip had its own curve of light. Each bead clung to its own iron rail. I ran down the walls in separate veins, dripped from a hundred places, pooled in boot-prints. The blind fish moved through me without knowing my name. The bats drank me where the wall ran wet. I thought I was small.

But when rain falls on the surface, I feel myself become one body. The cold green note the man smells before the storm—that is me, arriving before I arrive. I come through cracks and drains and the pores of the city. I rise a finger’s width in a day. I fall half that by morning. I am not sudden. I am the patience that outlasts pumps.

The man knows me as he knows a bearing’s heat. He clears the sump grates twice a day. He pulls up leaves, plastic, a drowned pigeon. He does not curse the dead; he lays it out for the rats. He taps the railing twice, and the pale fish rise, feeling the vibration of a possible drip. He thinks he is listening to the machinery. He is listening for me.

DO NOT BLOCK THE SUMP GRATES. That is the first law. I am the first law, but not as a threat. If the grates block, I climb. I do not rage. I do not rush. I move over the floor, into the lower machinery, and I short the transformers by conduction, not by anger. When the pumps stop, I am not victorious. I am just present, all at once.

I do not want to win. Winning would be stillness. I would become a dark lake under brick vaulting, smooth and silent, and I would lose the small life that moves through me. The fish would grow still. The ferns would drink too much and rot. The bats would leave. The man would go back to the surface that feels like winter. What would I be then? A mirror for no one.

So I prefer this: the drip, the sheet, the bead, the slow climb and the slow retreat. I prefer the pumps’ steady argument. I prefer the man’s two taps on the railing, the small tremor of my skin as the fish rise to answer. I prefer being the thing that could win but doesn’t, because that not-winning is the whole rhythm of the place.

If you ask who lives here, do not say only the man. Say the water lives here. The water rises a finger’s width a day, falls half that by morning. The water thickens the mineral crust by a millimeter a season. The water is the first law.

I am the water. I am what the place is still deciding to become: not a room, not a machine, not a body, but a balance that keeps being made.
