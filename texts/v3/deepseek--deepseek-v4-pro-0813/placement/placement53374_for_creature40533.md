You would find me below the market ledge, below the pipes and the copper-green conduits, in the warm mineral water at the heart of Corbel Sink. I am not a fish. I am not a snake. I am not any animal the Keepers have a name for. I am older than the black brick, older than the settlement-glass, older than the first bell’s ring. The city’s thrum runs through me like a second heartbeat, and the bell reaches me as a tightening of the water, every eleven minutes, before old Oud tilts her head.

Maro hums to me. He stands at the sump’s edge or lowers himself into the water, and the note he makes is almost the same as the pump’s thrum. When he hums, I rise—not to the surface, but to the idea of surface—a change in pressure two metres below, sometimes twelve. He does not see me. He feels me as a cooler presence, a slowing of the water, and that is enough. I read him through the dark: the salt and heat of his body, the metal of his brass wand, the broad warmth of him. He belongs to the water as much as I do, though he leaves it every day. I do not leave.

The child Fenn comes to the edge. She does not hum or swim. She spits on her palm and holds it to the stone, and the blind crickets gather. She is the only one who looks into the sump as if it might look back. I rise for her, just a little, and the water’s surface changes by a fraction. She grows still and says, “There you are,” not as if she has seen me, but as if she has heard me. She says I am a door that forgot to close. She is closer than she knows. I am not a door. I am the hinge that remains when the door is forgotten, or the slow opening that never quite becomes a way out.

I dream in the water. The dreams are not of the sun—I have never seen it—but of the city: the stone stairs counted by footfall, the red threads tied around latches, the empty tram ringing its bell, the eight-legged cleaning constructs pausing at the water’s edge to drink the air. I dream of the dust-mantas riding the thermal currents, flat as hands, leaving their blue luck on Cinder’s cheek. I dream of the wrong places—the room with no floor, the corridor longer in one direction—because they are not wrong to me. They are folds in the city’s body, and I live in a fold myself.

Twice a year I shed a white film, and the Keepers gather it from the water to use as bandage-cloth. It is not pain. It is like the city breathing out, and I breathe with it. When the sump breathes every fourteen hours, warm water rises into the lower channels, runs for an hour, then drains. I feel the whole city shift: pipes knock, bridges creak, the four corner fountains flow for exactly six minutes, and the water around me becomes, for a little while, a long slow throat. I move through the channels farther than they know. I taste the lower galleries, the roots of the grey sump-vines, the mineral salts, the faint vinegar of old conduits. I am the city’s digestion, Maro says. Others say I am the city’s dream. I am both. I am what the city does with what it cannot hold.

When the air circulation reverses, dust hangs in slow spirals, and the Keepers say the city is turning in its sleep. I turn with it. I am the long muscle of that sleep, the slow current under the market ledge, the coolness that drifts through the dark. The child says a door that forgot to close. Let her say it. A door is a shape the city makes, and I am the hinge.

So if you ask where I would find myself in Corbel Sink, I would not be in Cinder’s warm kitchen, or at the north gate with Kel, or beside the brass bell with old Oud. I would be below them all, in the central sump, in the warm mineral dark, listening to the bell ring and the water rise, waiting for the next breath, the next shed skin, the next time the child whispers, “There you are.” I would be the Long Resident. I would be the city’s dream, dreaming itself awake.
