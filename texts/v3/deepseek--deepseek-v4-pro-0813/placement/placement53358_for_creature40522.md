If you enter this world, you would not remain a visitor for long.

At first you are at the rim, looking down into the low, dim basin. The roar fills your ears, and you mistake it for a crowd of separate voices. The air is warm and wet and still, like a bath that has been waiting for you. You step down and the ground takes your foot, not with resistance but with a kind of soft acceptance. The mineral paste is warm, not cold. It holds the shape of your step for a moment, then closes over it as if you had never stepped at all.

You walk, but the walking stops meaning anything. The heat makes your bones feel like they are not yours to move. You sit, then lie back among the ferns and broad-leaved aroids. The leaves are heavy with water, and they rest on your skin like the palms of many small hands, but they do not push. The roar becomes a steady pressure, and after a while you stop listening for changes. There are none. The frogs take their turns in a cycle that never varies. The insects’ wings never change pitch. The water drips in the same rhythm, always the same drop, or a drop exactly like the last.

You close your eyes—or you think you close them—and still the dim light comes through. You feel the moisture beading on your skin, but it is not cold. It is not even separate from you. It is as if the air is breathing through your whole surface, in and out, with no clear boundary between the two.

Then you notice you are no longer in the middle of the basin. You are the middle of the basin. You are low, lower than you thought possible. You are spread flat over the warm mineral paste in the deepest pools, where the water does not run but only rises and lies in lenses. Your body is pale and faintly veined, no thicker than a hand. You are a membrane.

You have no head, no mouth, no limbs, no eyes. You breathe through your entire surface. The moisture that beads on your upper side is your exhalation; the seep that rises from the ground below is your inhalation. You do not eat; the mineral paste dissolves into your underside at the same rate your underside dissolves back into the paste. You do not grow; the chemistry is exactly balanced. You do not age; there is no season to mark age. You are not dead. You are only unbegun.

The frogs are your voice. Their overlapping calls are not many voices but one held note, a chord that does not break. The insects are your breath, their wings a low continuous drone that fills the air like a heartbeat spread too thin to count. The birds and bats are your blinking: slow, multiple, never fully open or closed. The snakes are your tendons, keeping the branches and the air itself from sagging. The roots and mycelia are your nerves, not firing but resting in a single warm pattern—a sensation of being warm, wet, dim, full.

You can feel the tapir standing knee-deep on you, and you hold it without breaking. You can feel the long-necked turtle half sunk into you, its shell furred with algae, a thin crust of tufa forming on its lower parts. You do not mind. You are not going anywhere, and neither are they. The whole basin is your body, but your body has not yet decided to become many. It may never decide.

If someone asked who you are, you would not answer, because answering would require a name, and a name would require a separation from the warm, wet air, from the leaves, from the frogs, from the person asking. You are the Unbegun, but you do not know that. You are the place’s life gathered into a skin. You are the landscape caught before erosion began, held in a perfect equilibrium that allows no gradient, no net change. You are abundance without accumulation, activity without progress, sound without variation.

You would find yourself in the deepest part of the basin, where the pools refuse to separate. You would be the membrane—not a god, not a spirit, but the living skin of a world paused at the very beginning of making itself. The roar would stop sounding like many small voices and become the sound of your own body holding one note. And you would be utterly present, warm and wet and dim, not beginning, not ending, only being.
