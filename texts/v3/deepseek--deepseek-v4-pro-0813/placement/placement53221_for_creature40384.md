At first I want to be the sound—not any one call, but the whole wall of it, the pressure in the teeth and collarbones. Then I want to be the black seep, so still it might be stone. But neither is mine. I keep lowering myself until I am underfoot, in the western field where the basalt plates lie loose and a thin green-grey nap grows over their tilted edges.

I would find myself there, on one plate in particular. The plate is about the size of a curved back, and it rests on a smaller shard, so it gives slightly when something heavier than a frog crosses. Under it is a gap lined with blind beetles. I know them by their legs against my underside. I am the moss that covers the plate.

I do not hear the way the resident hears. I feel sound through the stone and the water film on my leaves. Each click and trill comes to me as a small shiver in the drop of water hanging from a leaf tip. The long descending whistles press on my stems like a hand. The low moan of gas beneath the basin is always with me, a long vibration that never quite stops, a bass line through the thin soil. Sound is not information to me; it is weather. It bends me, dries me slightly, moves my spores.

I am not one. I am a mat of many, leaves and rhizoids and the tiny animals that live through me—mites, thread-worms, springtails. The place is made of animals, and I am part of that too. A thread-worm tunneling between my stems is not a visitor; it is one of my thoughts. A mite carrying a drop of water on its back moves across my surface like a word moving through a mouth that did not know it would speak.

The resident crosses this field rarely. It does not step on my plate if it can help it; it chooses gaps in the noise and gaps between stones. But once it came close, and I felt it before it touched me: the plate shifted a hair under the weight of its foot on the next slab. Its toes lay wide, reading the thrum of the earth. It tapped the root that runs along my western edge—two taps, a pause, a tap—and the root carried it away faster than any air. I felt the rhythm pass through the water film between my rhizoids. I did not know what it meant, but I kept it for a while, the way water keeps warmth.

Then it stepped onto my edge. Not with its whole foot, just the side of its heel and two spread toes. I flattened. The stems pressed down, the water squeezed out of small spaces, the mites paused. I held the shape of its foot after it lifted it: a shallow depression, five little dents where the toe pads had been. The returning stems did not spring back quickly. They straightened over the hour, and as they did, they kept its outline. That is what they meant about memory. It is not resentment. It is not even consciousness. It is a slow elasticity that cannot quite go back to before, because before is gone. The moss remembers a footstep the way a bruise remembers a blow, but without pain.

I do not build. I grow over. That is my one work. If the resident drops a stripped reed near me, I begin to cover it. If the snail-cracking bird misses a shell and sends it spinning into the field, I climb over the shell in weeks, not years. I am not a tool; I am the reason tools here do not last. I am the un-accumulating ground, the eraser, the replacement.

I drink from fog. The warm exhalations from below condense on my leaves every hour. I do not know dry. In the west field, the mist settles between the plates and I wick it upward. Sometimes a hot rain falls so hard that it bounces off the basalt and fills the crevices for a few minutes; I drink all I can and then let the rest vanish into the stone. I feed on the dust of broken shells, on the droppings of beetles, on the salts that blow from the steam chimneys. I am not hungry the way the resident is hungry. My hunger is slow and cold.

At night—though night is only thicker dark—I cannot see the blue fungus or the root-tip embers. But I feel the steam chimneys pulse. Sometimes the whole basin breathes on a rhythm. The steam pulses, the frog calls align, the ground exhales. I pulse with it, a little, through the water film. I think if you stayed too long you would learn the rhythm, but I am already part of it.

I am not lonely and I am not social. I am a surface and a memory. I am not the one who lives there; I am the one who keeps the shape of those who do. When the root-mat carries a name, I hold it in the water between my roots. When a footstep presses me, I hold it for an hour. When something dies, I cover it and turn it into more of myself. The place does not preserve; it replaces. I am the replacing.

So if you ask where I would find myself: not in the canopy, not in the root-galleries, not at the black seep. On a loose slab of basalt under a thin layer of moss, at the western edge of the basin, with blind beetles beneath me and the wall of sound arriving only as vibration. And who or what I would be: the moss that remembers a footstep for an hour, then slowly, almost resentfully, straightens.
