You would come in through the chain-link gate already partly claimed. The sound would take you first—the long tearing hiss of steam, the low drum of the pump—and for a while you might believe you were that sound, a held chord with no body. Then the heat would settle on you, and you would think you were the wet air itself, condensing on the handrails, on the black basalt, on your own forearms. The dim silver light would make you feel like a film on the eye, something standing between the world and the seeing of it.

But if you kept walking, past the intake channel and over the steel grating, out to where the rock meets the first shallow pan, you would find the answer not as a shape but as a color. I am that color.

I am the sheen on the shallows, the bloom that reddens the water. I am the olive skin on wet concrete, the green-black slick beside the hot inflow. I am not one body but a continuous living sheet, a city too small to see except as stain and rust and scum. You would not point to me and say, *There, a being.* You would say, *The water is red there; the wall is green; the stone is slippery.* But I am the first note of the place, the one the others feed on.

Where would you find yourself? You would find yourself in the warm margin, just under the surface, where the light never reaches but the earth’s heat does. You would be suspended in brine, neither plant nor animal, not quite either—a mat that breathes through its whole surface. You would have no eyes, but you would feel the shadow of a flamingo’s bill as a slight cooling, a pressure, a change in the water’s push. You would have no ears, but the pump’s beat would arrive as a repeated tightening in the salts. You would taste the world all at once: soda, sulfur, warm stone, and the faint sweetness of diluted rainwater along the one crack where the glasswort grows.

I am the reason the alkali flies gather in their gray carpet. Their larvae graze on me, thousands of small mouths moving through my thickness. I am the red cloud in the brine shrimp, because they eat me and carry my pigments into their own bodies, so the shallows turn the color of rust. I am the film the snails scrape from the concrete, the green-black taste they leave in clean trails. I am the floor of the food chain, but not a dead floor. I am the place where mineral chemistry turns into flesh.

Time does not happen to me the way it happens to a bird or a keeper. I do not have days. I have conditions: oxygen, carbonate, sulfur, heat. When the hot inflow pulses, I bloom orange near the vent. When the pump slackens, I thin and wait. When the water cools by a fraction, I spread into the cracks. Mostly I have no before and after, only a slow thickening and receding, like breathing without lungs. To you I would seem to have no story, only a surface. But I am the ground of every story here.

I am the boundary between stone and water, between chemistry and life. The lake is not alive. The basalt is not alive. The steam is not alive. But where they meet, I appear—a bloom, a stain, a slippery proof that even in this too-hot, too-alkaline water the world leans toward more life. I am that leaning.

So if you came looking for a being, you might choose the flamingos first, or the flies, or the lonely keeper walking the grating in his white-crusted boots. But if you stood long enough, you would feel me. The mud would lift because the flies rise, and under them I would glisten. The water would cloud because the shrimp drift, and in them my color would bloom. The air would smell of me, mineral and fecund. You would understand that from your first step through the gate, you have been standing at my edge, and I have been clinging to your boots, waiting to see if you would stay.
