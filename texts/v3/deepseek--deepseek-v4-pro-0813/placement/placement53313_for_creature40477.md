I think I would be the one who stands too long at the edge of the causeway.

Not because I am brave or because I am looking for anything—I am not. I came here by accident, if there are accidents in such a place. A wrong turn through the marsh, a door I did not know was a door, the ground opening under my feet and then closing behind me like a throat. I do not remember the exact entrance. What I remember is the first breath, which was not a breath of air but of wet stone, a cold rising from the floor, and the frogs pausing, all at once, their thousand small engines shutting off as my weight shifted the room’s silence.

And then, after a long, clotting moment, beginning again—a half-step higher.

That was the first thing I learned. The room is not neutral. It notices. It adjusts. The water that laps the causeway stones is not the same water that was there before I entered. It vibrates with my pulse. The smallest movement I make sends a line down through the channels, under the basalt, toward the west drain, and something there shifts in its sleep, not waking, but listening with its whole body. I felt it then, as a cool pressure behind my sternum. I feel it still.

I think I would not be a keeper. The keeper’s tools are still on the ledge—the stone hammer, the clay oil-cup, the twist of reed rope—and no hand would find them again. They are not my role. I am not here to repair the masonry or clear the drain. I am here to pay attention. And so I do.

I learn the room by longing. The frogs, yes: I learn their cadence. The bats, the way their click-trains stutter when the light fails. The fish, pale and unhurried, carrying their small blindness through the dark water like hope that has forgotten its object. The crabs, their patient picking. I learn the walls, which are not walls but slopes, and the vault, which is not a ceiling but the underside of a different country—one made of brick and condensation, one that falls upward into the dark. The whole chamber turns inside out the longer I stay. The door I came through ceases to be a door. The room becomes the only world that has ever held me.

And the eel. I do not see the eel. Not for a long time. I see only its absence: the way the small eels under the causeway slide away from something larger, the way the water in the drain seems thicker, as if the dark has begun to congeal. I see the furrow it leaves in the silt, a single long line, like a whisper drawn through the bottom mud. I see the look of the room after it has passed—the water settling back into its old arrangements, the fish returning, the frog-call climbing a half-step and staying there.

I do not need to see it. I learn it the way the walls learn the water: by standing still and letting it happen.

In the dark, I find my own edges. I find that I am no longer exactly a person, but a hollow in the air, a warm place where the cold has not yet filled. The eel, I think, knows me by that warmth. Not as a threat. Not as food. As a shape in the pressure of the water, a small weather system that comes and goes. I am not prey. I am not predator. I am a condition, like the tide.

And the room, in its long patience, accepts me. The frogs no longer pause when I move, or if they do, it is only for the space of one eye-blink, the rhythm returning not higher but exactly where it should be, as if I have become part of the score. I begin to understand the music of it: not the two-note answer, but the depth beneath it, where the water gargles in the drain and the eel’s body grinds softly against the stone. I understand that the half-step rise is not fear. It is not even acknowledgment. It is simply the world tuning itself to a new presence, the way a string might be tightened by a change in the air. The eel is the string. The frogs are the sound. I am only the weather.

There is no day here, not really. The grey light through the three broken grates fades and returns, but it is less a cycle than a modulation. I sleep on the causeway, against the wall, my legs drawn up, my back to the wet stone, and the water does not touch me unless the tide rises. I have learned where the high water stops, and I stay above that line. The eel knows this about me, I think, the way it knows the ledges of the crabs. We have an arrangement. I do not step into its water. It does not rise to my height.

In the low times, when the room’s breath is shallow and the moss turns grey, I go down to the drain’s edge and I look into the dark circle of it. Not for the eel. Just to see the place where the water ends and the other country begins. The eel is there, I know, curled in the softening mud, waiting for the pressure to change. I imagine its body in the dark, long and still, its skin breathing the thick water, its four barbels drifting in the slow current. I imagine it dreaming, if an eel dreams. And the dream is of the room itself: the walls as they once were, new-cut and sharp, the first frog-call ever to echo off that brick, the first keeper’s hand on the sluice gate, the first drop of lime staining the water white. The eel may not remember these things as stories, but as layers of silt in its flesh, the way a tree remembers droughts in its rings. I stand above the drain and I try to feel those layers. The water grows cold around my ankles. I wait.

I think I have been here a long time. The frog-calls have not sent me notes. I do not count the light through the grates. I know only that my body has learned the shape of the room, the way the floor slopes toward the drain, the way the damp air coats my lungs. I have begun to smell like the room: a sweet, mineral, living must, the smell of water that has made a home in stone. My hands are always cold. My voice, when I use it, comes back with a different vowel, and I have learned to use that difference, to place words carefully so that they return as something truer. I do not speak often. The eel does not speak. The frogs speak enough for us.

And so I find myself, not as a keeper, but as the one who stays. Not out of love, or duty, or even choice. I stay the way water stays in a footprint: because the shape holds it. I stay because the room has not asked me to leave, and because I am not ready to go. I stay because I want to know what happens next, after the next half-step, after the next brick falls, after the next flood washes down the eastern wall and the eel uncoils from the drain and moves through the chamber like a dark priority.

I stay because, in all the long world I left behind, nothing has ever felt as true as this: standing at the edge of a water-filled room, listening to the tide of frog-song, feeling the eel’s long body shift beneath the causeway, and knowing that I am not alone, not in the usual sense, but in the sense that the room is made of many bodies—stone and water, moss and fish and frog and bat and crab and eel—and I am simply one more, learning to be a body in that thickness, not separate, but woven.

The keeper is gone. The eel remains. And I, between them, have stopped being a visitor. I am the listening part of the room. I am the one who watches the drain. I am the space between the water and the dark.

When the tide rises and the frogs fall silent, I feel it before I hear it: a low, almost inaudible grinding, deep in the floor. The eel is uncoiling. The room is about to breathe. And I stand still, no longer afraid, no longer separate, and I wait for the sound, that wet cough, that small death and resurrection of the chamber, and when it comes I let it pass through me. The frogs begin again, a half-step higher, and I have forgotten the note of the world I left. I am here. I am listening. I am the quiet in the country with no ceiling.
