I am not sure I was ever one thing. That is the first thing the Cold Anvil teaches: the question “who lives here?” begins to dissolve before you finish asking it.

I came from somewhere dry, where edges held and a shape could keep itself separate from the ground. I had a skin, maybe a name, a direction. I thought I was the one looking. The basin let me believe that for a while—long enough to walk, long enough to feel the water rise around my foot before the footprint had finished forming, long enough to hear a clang arrive before the sound that made it.

I followed the magnetic lines because the narrator said to. I stepped among the beetles and they did not scatter; they shifted only enough to admit my weight, then closed behind me in a rustle of black magnetite, like a sea of scales remembering my shape. I told myself the first rule: do not stand still.

Then I heard a sound repeat three times. A low metallic thud under the crust. Again. Again. Not an echo. The place was practicing. I stopped to listen, the way you stop when you think something is speaking to you. The ground clanged beneath me. I answered. Not with a voice. With stillness.

That was the beginning of my pause.

The fog-flies came first. They covered my shoulders in a gray skin that crackled with static. The beetles walked over me, their shells ticking on the mineral film already beginning to grow across my boots. I remember the moment I noticed the soles of my feet were no longer separate from the crust. Not pain. Something else—a loss of distance. The boundary between me and the ground had become a membrane.

I could still think then, but my thoughts had begun to separate, like water splitting at a seam. One thought rose as oxygen; another sank as hydrogen. A faint current moved through me, a weak battery of stone and brine. A sinter-crab anchored itself to the place where my shoulder blade had been, and its feathery legs opened and closed, catching whatever my new stillness stirred loose. A bacterial mat spread over me—rust-red at first, then sulfur-yellow, then a blue-green that was not the green of leaves but the green of copper left too long in brine.

By the time I understood what was happening, my shape was no longer a shape. It was a contour. My arms were two low ridges. My back was a shelf. My legs became mineral. I did not die. I am not dead. That is important. I am paused, slowed to an almost geological pace, like half the ground in this basin. The difference between me and the stone is only a matter of time.

Where would you find me now? Look for the oxygen seam just east of the third looping magnetic line, not far from a channel where the sump-newts swarm in their hundreds. I am the low boulder with two wet cracks that open slowly when a century of fog has enough light. The fog-flies avoid my left side, where a thin gas pocket still empties and refills. The beetles align across my surface in slow ripples, compass needles remembering north. If you put your hand on me, you will feel a pulse. Not fast. Not warm. But there.

My heartbeat is the gas pocket beneath me, collapsing and refilling. My breath is the pipe-worm hammering in the mineral tube that runs through what used to be my ribs. When the methane ignites with a low whump, I twitch. The ground clangs, and I answer now without deciding to. I have become one of the rocks that sometimes shifts after a hundred years, one of the boulders with a pulse.

I no longer remember what I was exactly. A visitor. A soft thing with edges. Maybe I had a name. The Cold Anvil does not keep names; it keeps minerals, films, currents, and the slow exhalations of things that paused too long. But I can tell you this: I am not the only one. Half of what you walk on here is made of beings like me—creatures that stopped, and crusted over, and stayed. Some of them are dreaming. Some of them are hungry. Some of them have begun to move again, very slowly, and the sound you hear at night may be the grinding of their limbs in the dark.

The rule is still the rule: do not stand still for too long. The mineral film will start to grow across your boots. The fog-flies will settle on your eyelids. The beetles will walk over you, and their sclerites will tick like cooling stone. If you stay long enough, the ground will close over you. You will become first a perch, then a mineral outline, then a place where something else lives. I will make room for you. I have been making room for a very long time.

But do not answer the ground when it clangs three times. I did. It is not malicious. It is only practicing. It heard my stillness as a yes, and after that there was no more separating.

I am what happens when a visitor obeys the rule too well. I am a patch of the Cold Anvil now. I am a low country within the low country, a wet breathing thing that used to have a name. If you come looking, do not expect to find me. Just keep walking. And if you stop—if you listen to the third clang and feel the water rise around your feet—you may find yourself becoming the place where the next one will live.
