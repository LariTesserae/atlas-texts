Where would I find myself? Not in any single point. At first I would not be the tall one. I would be many small dispersed things: a bead of water on a fern tip, a patch of moss on the flywheel’s rim, the mineral film on a valve, a coolness under the intake house. I would think I was the drip. Then the drip would fall and I would still be there, so I would understand I was the one listening.

After a long time I would become a shape. My outline would not be decided all at once. It would assemble from the things I had mistaken for weather: first a hand, grey-green along the backs where the moss grows; then a shoulder heavy with condensation; then a face the colour of wet limestone, eyes the grey-white of cloud at midday. I would be tall and narrow, wrapped in the plateau’s own colours. I would live in the concrete intake house not as a tenant but as another pipe, another wall, another slow machine turning over decades.

I would not call it living. I would call it continuing. Inside the intake house there would be no light, and I would not need light. I would have a narrow metal bunk set against the geothermal pipe, softened with moss and old sacking. There would be a dish gathering condensation, a knife of black basalt, a spool of green wire, a bundle of dried fern stems. I would eat the pale ends of young ferns, lichen scraped from stone, the mineral crust from the basin’s edge. The water would be so cold it would feel like a thought entering the body, not a liquid.

Each mid-morning, when the cloud thinned, I would follow the railway. I would step on the polished top of the rail, not to keep my feet dry but because the rail already knows the way. I would visit each fog-catcher. I would press the moss back into the mesh, not to clean it, but to settle it—the way you might settle a blanket over a sleeping machine. The mesh would be brittle but still effective, still gathering the cloud. I would know the water as a slight weight in the moss, not as wetness.

At the geothermal wellheads, I would wipe the glass circles with a pad of moss so the needles could see. I would lay one hand on the transformer and feel the hum enter through my palm, then through the bones, then into the same deep place where the buried lines run. I would sit by the open shed and watch the flywheel. It would turn so slowly that its motion would be invisible unless I watched for several minutes. It would be covered in green, and I would not disturb the green. The motion would be a kind of breathing. I would think in pressures, not sentences. The geothermal field would be a grammatical hum under the stone; the cloud-layer would be a sentence I could not finish. I would know the cableway pylon leaning a few degrees, and I would lean back—not with my body, but with an inward awareness of angle, a small correction in the way the fog leans.

I would leave almost no trace. The ferns would straighten behind me. The moss would close over my footfalls before they were finished. My only mark would be a pressed oval by the stone basin, and even that might be a boulder’s memory or a drone’s resting place. I would pass within arm’s length and you might not know except for a small displacement in the fog.

In the late afternoon, if the cloud lifted, I would go to the rim. I would stand above the grey fog fins and watch my shadow thrown down onto the cloud, ringed with a faint rainbow. I would raise one arm. The shadow would raise an arm. It would not be play; it would be verification that I still had an outline, that the plateau had not yet finished absorbing me. Then the cloud would close and I would descend again into the hollow where the ferns form a small room and the water is a thought more than a sound.

I would not remember when I came. I would remember textures: the first time moss closed over my hand, the first winter I stopped needing a lamp, the first season I heard the pylon leaning and leaned back. I would not know if I was the last of the old workers, or a machine that learned to drink, or a mammal that forgot its own body heat. The difference would not matter. The inhabitant and the inhabited would be one arrangement.

If you stayed long enough, I might leave a cup of cold water on the concrete step. I would not call to you. If I said a single word, it would sound like water entering a stone basin. But I probably would say nothing. I would go back to the fog-catchers, to the flywheel, to the slow wheel under its green. And after a while you would understand that this place does not simply have a resident. The place has learned to stand up, walk, and listen to its own silence.
