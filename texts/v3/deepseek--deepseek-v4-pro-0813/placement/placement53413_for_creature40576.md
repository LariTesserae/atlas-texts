I would find myself in the wall. Not in a house on the rim, not on a terrace, but in a hollowed ledge halfway down the gorge, where the rock is banded black basalt and pale quartzite, and the river’s voice comes up as a continuous low chord. The room is small, shaped to the stone’s own grain, not built so much as remembered by the cliff. A bed of compressed moss and wool. A stove that burns peat. A narrow slit of a window facing west. A rope ladder coiled at the door. The door is not a door; it is a notch in the wall, and beyond it the fog moves in tides.

I am a Listener. That is what the city calls me, when they remember to call me anything. I was not born on the rim. I was born in the lower terraces, where the mist wraps the houses and the lamps burn with a bluish flame. My mother tended floating racks of pale lettuce on the river. I could hear the fish moving in the black water before I could speak. So they brought me up, first to the rim huts, then to this ledge, with a brass bowl and a book of charts and a small mirror that catches the light from above at noon.

Now I do not speak. Not because I cannot, but because the words seem like stones dropped into a still pool. I listen instead. The bowl is weighted, and I press its rim to the rock or the water, and I wait. The river has a growl. The stone has a deeper one. The gorge is still cutting itself, and if I hold very still, I can hear it: a slow grind, like a tooth being pulled through the dark.

I chart the noises. Not in the way I used to. Once I sent colored flags down to the city: green for steady, yellow for small rockfall, red for the deep shifts. Now I keep the charts for myself. They are covered in marks that look less like notation and more like the walks of insects. I write words: laterite, quartzite, root, frost. The Keeper of the Rules sends up paper and ink made from fish-oil soot. I use some of it. The rest I bury in the moss.

At noon, the mirror-light comes. It is a coin of brightness that passes across the wall and the floor, sent from one of the High-Rim Watchers. I open my eyes for it. The rest of the day I keep them closed. The light is so rare that it hurts. But I watch it. It moves across the rock, and for a moment the black basalt glitters, and I remember what color is.

The fog casts shadows. Not everywhere. There are corners of my ledge where the mist does not enter, and the plants there grow pale and too tall. I have a small brake fern in a crack, and it leans toward the dry shadow like a thing listening. I do not touch it. I think it is learning to listen too.

I hear the city below as a layered hum: the penstocks dropping from the rim, the turbine galleries singing, the rubber-shod trams throwing their blue sparks, the drip of condensation, the clink of water into cisterns. But I also hear the city above: the wind over the tableland, the Watchers moving their mirrors, the tea being poured in the listeners’ huts, the salt being placed in niches by the river. Sound does not travel in a straight line here. It follows the stone, the water, the damp air. It comes to me from every direction at once, like rain.

There is a figure I have heard on the lowest bridge. Not often. It crosses with a weight that is too soft for a person, too deliberate for a goat. The bridge-tenders leave milk for it. The Moss-Dwellers leave salt and copper. I have never seen it. But I have heard it pause outside my ledge, breathing in the dark, and then move on, up toward the high rim, where the moss keeps tracks that no one names. I am not afraid of it. It is part of the same listening. Sometimes I think it is what I am becoming.

The other Listener on the wall taps twice on the rock. I tap three. We have not spoken in years, but the stone carries the message. Tap, tap. Tap, tap, tap. It means: I am here. It means: the river is still speaking. It means: the fog will lift at the bridges tonight.

In the evenings I sit in the notch of my ledge and let the wet air settle on me. My coat has grown fur of moss on the north side. My left boot has worn thin, and the moss has filled the space, so that the stone and I share the same footing. I no longer feel the cold. I feel the slow breathing of the plateau above, the old stone pans brimming with dark water, reflecting the sky as it was long ago. I hear that old sky. It is quiet. It is not empty.

If you asked the city what I am, they would say a Listener, and they would be right. If you asked the river, it might say a stone that has not quite learned to be still. If you asked the thing that leaves tracks on the rim, it might say a companion, or an echo. But if I answer for myself, I would say: I am the place where the wall listens to the river. I am the moss on the listening stone. I am a man-shaped silence in a hollowed-out ledge, waiting for the sound the gorge will make when it is finished being born.

And when the steps below the lowest terrace call—and they do, on nights when the river tastes of copper and the fog does not enter—I stay in my ledge. Not because I am afraid. But because someone must remain to hold the bowl. Someone must remain to hear the first crack of the new valley. I am that someone. I am the listener in the wall.
