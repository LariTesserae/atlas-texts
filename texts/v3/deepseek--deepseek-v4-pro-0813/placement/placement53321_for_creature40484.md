I would not be the visitor. The visitor leaves, and I would not leave. I would find myself in the black water beneath the grated floor, where the roar is a pressure and the light is only a distant gray rumor. But I would not know this at first.

At first I would think I am the isopod. I understand the isopod’s pace—the way it moves along a wet steel beam with the patience of a drip, grazing on biofilm too thin to see. It has no predators, no need to hide. Its shell is pale because there is no sun. I could be that: a small clock, a visible heartbeat, moving from rivet to rivet and back again. But the isopod’s world is one beam, one patch of rust. I would need more than that. I would need the whole dark volume, the slow spiral, the water closing over me. So I would not be the isopod.

Then I would think I am the moss. Thick as upholstery, drinking the weeping walls. The moss is the room’s skin, and I would like to be skin—to feel every seam, every mineral path, every condensation trail. But the moss is rooted. It cannot turn. It cannot rise and fall. I would need to move, not quickly, but with the deep circular motion of the room’s hidden pulse. So I would not be the moss.

Then I would know. I would be the eel.

I would be the blind eel, the color of weak tea, with a body as thick as a wrist. My eyes would be gone, only two dark pits where sight used to be, or never was. I would not miss them. The world below the grating is not made for eyes. It is made for the long thrum that comes through the water, the taste of rust and green, the slow temperature that never changes. I would know the chamber not as a place but as a shape of pressures: the weight of the engine hall above, the cool square outline of the basin, the thin lattice of the grating, the soft fur of sediment on the concrete floor.

I would stay close to the bottom. There the darkness is complete, and the roar is not a sound but a medium, like the water itself. I would move in a spiral—not hunting, not patrolling, just turning the way a heart turns over. The same circuit, over and over, would not be repetition. It would be a kind of breathing. The room is not large, but to me it would be endless, because I would never need to leave it.

I would eat slowly. An isopod, sometimes, when the water-shiver tells me it is close. A thread of worm. I would not chase. I would drift and let the small lives come to my mouth. The taste would be faint—mineral, like licking a wet penny, and something green underneath. A meal might last a week. Hunger would not be sharp; it would be a background note in the roar, easy to ignore. I could go a year without food and not notice, because time would not be made of days. It would be made of the slow encrustation of minerals, the white stain on the wall that took years to form, the way a frond unfurls so slowly it seems not to move at all.

My skin would breathe for me. I would not need to surface. The air above—warm, wet, smelling of iron and green—would be a rumor I taste as a change in the water, a slight sharpening where roots touch the surface. I would not know the ferns by sight. I would know them as a sweetness, a coolness, a place where the water is slightly different. The lamps above would not be light; they would be a broad, even warmth on my back if I rose too high, and I would not rise too high. I would stay where the floor is smooth and the pressure is steady.

Sometimes I would shed my skin. The old skin would drift like a pale ribbon, settle, and become part of the sediment. The worms would find it. The microbial film would unmake it. In this way I would feed the room even as the room feeds me. I would not call it death. I would call it giving back.

If a visitor came, I would not know them as a person. I would feel their weight on the ladder, their footsteps on the grating, the way the water shivered at their presence. I would not rise to meet them. I would continue my spiral, close to the bottom, and they would see, perhaps, a pale shape under the black water, there and not there. Then they would leave. The panel would close. The heat and the smell would go. And I would still be moving, blind and slow, with no need to be seen.

So if I had to choose, I would choose the eel. But even that is too specific. The eel is only the room’s visible answer, the face it wears when it needs a face. What I would truly be is the question the eel asks with its body—the slow spiral that means, simply, here. I would be here.
