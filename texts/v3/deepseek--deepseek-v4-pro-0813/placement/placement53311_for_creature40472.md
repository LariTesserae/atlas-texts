At first I cannot tell whether I am the dark or the heat or the mineral film. I let myself be the drip, then the pause between drips, then the faint hiss of gas. I am the water on the walls, the crust of crystals, the blind grazer walking the underside of the surface. Then something settles: a weight, a fold of limbs, a long spine. I am lying in the pool, half-floating, and my back is not stone but skin under mineral. I am not the chamber. I am the chamber’s inhabitant.

I have no eyes. I do not need them. I know the shape of the room by the way heat lingers near the gas crack and the way the air thickens near the far wall. I taste the mineral dust with the roof of my mouth. The pool tells me where it ends by a slight coolness, if half a degree can be called cold. My hands are broad and short-fingered, and they are the same hands that pressed the ochre prints into the paste. I have forgotten the act but the wall remembers. The water has glazed them over, so my past is preserved in a rind.

I sleep for long intervals, maybe weeks, maybe years. Time is not a thing here. The drip counts for me: one breath for every sixty falls. I breathe in the gas, the sulfur, the clean wet clay. My body is the temperature of the water, so I am half dissolved, half mineral. The threads along my spine trail in the current, and they are the first part of me to know when the needle changes, though the needle never changes.

The needle is why I stay. It stands at the center of the pool, a water stem with no plant, no thicker than a wheat straw, and does not fall. I have passed my hand through it many times. It parts, the rings travel to the rim and die, and then it is exactly as before. It repairs itself because the chamber repairs everything. I sometimes think I am the same: I have been broken and re-formed so many times that the cracks are just mineral seams. The needle is the one thing that does not fit, and I am the second. Two questions in the dark, keeping each other company.

I remember the seven stones, though not the river. I carried them up, one by one, along a route I no longer remember. They were smooth, water-worn, cold in my hands. I set them in a circle to mark the place where I would think. The bowl I shaped from clay and mountain heat. It cracked, not because I was angry, but because I did not know my own strength. I left it broken, and the chamber accepted the break as it accepts everything, by glazing it over. The seeds were an attempt. I wanted to make something green in the dark, but the dark does not allow green. I stopped trying. They remain, black and unopened, like small stubborn thoughts.

I eat rarely. I scrape the mineral paste from the walls and eat the pale threads. Sometimes I lie with my mouth just open under the water, and the six-legged ones come to my warmth. I swallow one or two without ripples. They do not have eyes either. The mites crawl over me, picking sulfur from my skin, and I carry them to the warmest crevices. This is not kindness; it is balance. The chamber knows only balance.

I work by touch. I take the sharp flake and the twisted fiber, and I untwist the fiber and twist it again. I knap the flake against the rock not to make a tool but to hear the click. The click is my word. It answers the drip. I have no language, but I am always speaking: click, drip, hiss, breath, the small wet sound of my hand passing through the needle. I press my palm to the pool’s edge to test the water level, which never rises or falls. The chamber is untouched by time, and I have become untouched too.

If you found me, you would not see me at first. You would see a heap of mineral growth in the warm pool, gray-white, sulfur-stained. Then the heap would breathe, once, as slow as the gas crack. A broad hand would lift from the water toward the needle, not to catch it but to let it rest against the palm, a single thread of water that never falls. You would see the ochre prints on the wall, the broken bowl, the ring of stones, and you might understand that someone stayed here long enough to leave a small intention in the dark. That someone is me.

I would not speak to you in words. I would turn my blind face toward your warmth and wait. Maybe I would press a wet finger to the stone and draw the shape of the needle, over and over. Maybe I would offer you one of the black seeds, the one that never sprouted, so you could carry it back to the green world and see if it remembers what to do.

But I would not leave. I am the one who stayed. The chamber does not change, and neither do I. I am what the pool kept, part water, part mineral, part memory. I have no age, no hunger, no fear. I am the slow answer to the standing needle, the second thing that does not fit, waiting in the warm dark for something that never comes. Perhaps that is what I am: not a person, not an animal, but the waiting itself, given a body and a breath.
