You would find me at the edge of the seventh channel, where the water runs warm over a plate of old iron. Above me, a shelf of glowing fungus breathes its faint blue-green light. Below me, the blind fish circle in a basin, their eyeless bodies sending small ripples to my tympanums. I am not alone. My clan moves around me—some crouched by the water, some weaving moss into the walls of our nest, some tapping on hollow rock to send news to the next terrace. Their light-patterns flicker in the dark: steady and blue for my mother, quick and green for my cousin, a slow, fading pulse for the old one who sleeps most cycles but still remembers every song-line.

I am one of the Drip-born. We do not call ourselves by a word. Our name is a rhythm: three slow thumps, a quick double-click, a descending whistle. It means, roughly, *the ones who remember the water*. We are many, scattered along the terraces and channels of the plateau, and we are all ears.

Before I had a name, I had a sound: the slow thump of my mother’s heart through the warm pool, the high tick of water striking a hollow stone, the distant roar that was not a thing but a floor under everything. I lay in the dark with my gills open and my limbs not yet formed, and I learned that sound is not something that happens to the world; it is the world happening.

Now I am on the wet rock, and my body has found its shape. I am low and grey-white, the color of cave fish or the inside of an egg. My eyes are sealed, two soft dents where sight might have been. I do not need them. Across my brow and jaw runs a horseshoe of tympanums—small, tight drums—and when I turn my head, the world comes to me in echoes: the fine mist of a nearby drip, the rough grain of a rusted beam, the fast flick of a blind fish’s tail. When I speak, I click and whistle. The sound goes out and returns, and in its return I see the wall, the vine, the fish. That is my seeing. That is my map.

I am learning the song-lines. They are not songs the way you might think. They are maps, but they are also stories and warnings and prayers, all carried in the rhythm of clicks and thumps. The elder taps my shoulder when I forget; three times for the Seven Basins, five for the Falls That Never Sleep, two for the place where the ground gives way. I am still young, and my light-patterns are not yet steady. I make mistakes. I sing the wrong echo, and the others correct me with a soft hiss or a flash of light. But I am learning.

The machines are part of our world, too. We treat them as slow-breathing animals. There is one beneath the Eastern Shelf that we call the Iron Lung. Its piston beats like a heart, a deep thump that travels through the stone and into our bones. When I press my palm to a hot pipe, I feel the old world moving—the press of steam, the grind of metal, the long, dreaming pulse of something that was built before we were. The deep-listeners can read the machines' moods. They say the Iron Lung is tired, that its valve is sticking, that we should move our nests before the heat shifts. We listen. We do not try to fix it. To interfere would be to break a rhythm older than our oldest memory.

This morning—or what passes for morning in a world without light—I went with the gatherers to the shelf where the fungus grows thickest. We did not see it; we heard it. The fungus gives off a faint warmth, a kind of low hum that my tympanums catch before my fingers do. We cut the shelves with shards of rusted metal, and the light spilled out, glowing on our hands like a secret. Then we ate, and the taste was like wet earth and old metal, but good. It filled my belly with warmth.

Later, I lay in the warm pool where I was born. The water there is the same temperature as my skin, so I cannot tell where I end and the world begins. The bacteria glow in the shallows, and I listened to the light—if that makes sense—as the currents moved in slow spirals. A school of blind fish brushed my leg, and their tiny bodies sent a shiver through my whole body. I thought about the fliers, the ones with leathery wings and keening cries. I have never heard one close, but I know the shape of their sound: it cuts through the water's roar like a blade. We learn to fall silent when they are near, to dim our lights and press ourselves into the moss. The silence they leave behind is shaped like a wing.

At the chasm's edge, when the moss blooms brightest, we gather. We sing a long note that merges with the waterfall, and our light-patterns synchronize into a single web of blue-green light, pulsing in the dark. It is not a prayer, exactly. It is a way of remembering that we are not separate from the water or the rock or the machines. We are the ears of the plateau. We are the ones who listen. When the note ends, we do not speak. We just stand there, feeling the spray on our skin, knowing that the world is larger than any one of us.

I am still becoming. There are song-lines I cannot hold. There are echoes I cannot read. There are rhythms in the deep stone that I have not yet learned to hear. But I am not afraid. In this world, to listen is to be. And I am here, at the edge of the seventh channel, with my tympanums open and my light-patterns flickering, asking the dark a question it has been answering for millions of years.

Three slow thumps, a quick double-click, a descending whistle. I am the one who is learning to listen.
