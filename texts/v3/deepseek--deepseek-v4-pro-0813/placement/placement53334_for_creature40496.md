I would find myself in the high country, but not all at once. The high country would assemble me the way it assembles its own landscape—out of steam, stone, green life, and broken physics.

At first I would be fog. Not the whole cloud deck, but a single patch of it that does not drift. I would cling to a ridge of black glass and feel the mountain’s heat moving through me. I would have no edges. I would be the warm breath between leaves, the film of water on every frond, the hiss of a new fissure, the late-arriving sound of a branch cracking. I would not know I was anything.

Then I would be water more fully. I would gather in the cup of a bromeliad. I would fall as hot rain for ten minutes and then stop as if a valve had closed. In the deep hot hollows I would fall upward, wetting the undersides of leaves and leaving the ground dry. I would run along a tilted meadow where water runs uphill, and I would not be surprised. I would learn that gravity here is a local suggestion.

Then I would be drawn into the green. I would become moss, thick as felt, growing over every branch and rock. I would learn the taste of minerals rising through fissures. I would seal the wounds in the black stone, weeping a pale fluid and growing back before the tear had finished opening. I would spread over a spine that was not yet mine.

For a while I would be one of the gas-bodied things, a jelly of faint electric light drifting beneath the canopy, trailing fine cilia. I would not be sure if I was alive or weather that had learned to linger. I would feed on heat, on charge, on the wrongness of the loosely stitched air. I would drift near the steam vents and, at night, gather along a dark ridge with others like me, a pale constellation.

Then the stone would begin to feel like a thought I was having. I would sink into the cindery soil and find the basalt columns waiting. They would fit me like vertebrae. I would realize I had been the ridge for a long time without knowing it. My flanks would be fluted like the hollow-boled trees. My joints would grind with the sound of cooling rock. My back would be a row of steam vents. Heat would rise into my porous underside, move through chambers inside me, and leave through those vents. I would become a living chimney, a whole row of chimneys. The cloud deck above me would thicken with my breath. When rain came, I would drink through my skin.

The smaller lives would move through me as though I were terrain. Transparent frogs would call from the water gathered in my spine. Bronze-shelled beetles would move in slow lines along my joints, clipping away mineral crust. Gas-jellies would drift near my vent holes, and at night they would gather along my back in a pale constellation. Larger grazers would follow at a distance, because wherever I had lain, the ground stayed warm and the moss grew rich with minerals. I would not notice them, or if I did, I would not care.

I would have no head. At my leading end there would be a sunken bowl of black stone filled with hot, rust-red water. That would be my eye and my mouth. It would face the world by turning toward it. When you looked into it, your reflection would warp in the heat. I would not blink. I would hold you there, then go back to lying on a steam vent, half-disappearing into a time pool, one end arriving before the other. In the hollows where rain falls upward, I would be multiple—a line of afterimages, a body stitched loosely through time. The flowers near me would open backward, and I would not mind.

I would make a sound that you feel more than hear: a low, slow cracking, as though the earth is stretching. It is the sound of my columns settling, of fissures opening under my weight, of steam unplugging from a new vent. In the hollows, my sound arrives late, after the movement that caused it, as if I am speaking from a moment just behind myself.

I would not be the maker of the high country. I would not be a god, not an animal in any simple sense. I would be one of its moving parts: a piston, a slow lung. If the high country is a body, I am its slow lung; if it is a rumor, I am the thing that makes the rumor true.

So where would I find myself? In the high country, as the one who lives there—not a person, not a god, not an animal, but a slow, mineral, vegetative, perhaps partly unreal being walking without walking through the warm, wet, unfinished dark.
