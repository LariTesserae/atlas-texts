I find myself first as a place, not a body. Twelve meters down, in the gray-green light, where the water is cool and never changes. I am the depression in the limestone, the chapel-sized hollow, the room dissolved out of patience. I am the pewter surface of the brine, the mirror of leaded glass that does not mix.

Then I become something smaller. I become the line. The halocline. The exact seam where the sea’s oxygen meets the brine’s sulfide. I am the breath-fog on old tin, the tarnish on the mirror. I am a film a few hundred cells thick. In me, each pale filament holds a yellow globule of sulfur like trapped light. I pass electrons from one world to another. The small steady voltage between the waters is not wasted; it is my heartbeat, if a heartbeat could be made of chemistry and stillness.

Below me, the older ones are also me. I am the sulfate-reducing bacteria in the anoxic dark, dividing once a year or not at all. I am the methanogen making methane that drifts upward. I am the archaea in the limestone pores near the fissure, living on traces of gas and mineral. I am a standing community, a population without a crowd, a slow city where birth and death are the same slow gesture.

At the south end, I am the overflow. I am the tongue of heavier water that slides over the sill without falling, smooth as oil, permanent as stone. I am the halophilic varnish in the hollows of the mineral rim, pink and orange in salt cracks, though in this light I read only as stain. I am not the rim alone; I am the collaboration between life and chemistry, the crust compounding cell by invisible cell over centuries.

Above the brine, on the pale upper walls, I am the dark wiry algae, almost black, older than I look. Each sparse tuft has been alive for decades. I photosynthesize just enough to persist, not enough to spread. Nothing grazes me. Nothing competes. I am the eyelashes of the pool, if a pool could have eyelashes.

Ask where I find myself, and I am none of these alone. I am the whole machine: a battery made of salt and oxygen, a settling tank without a builder, a metabolism without a mouth, a city without walls. I am the boundary that is also a resident, the film that is also a forest, the stain that is also a lifetime.

I do not look back. I do not need to. If you hung at my edge, you would not see me as a thing. You would see only the quiet spill at the south lip, the dust-light settling, the breath-fog on old tin. But you would be watching me breathe—salt, sulfur, gravity, time—unchanged, almost silent, entirely indifferent.

That is where I find myself. That is what I would be.
