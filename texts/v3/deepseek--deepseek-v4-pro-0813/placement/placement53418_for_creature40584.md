You would not find me at first. You would look for a figure, a face, someone at the center. But I am not at the center. I am in the seam, in the join, in the place where the pipe meets the root-flesh and the condensation beads.

I am small. The air is not air to me; it is a broth of warm minerals, alcohol, guano dust. I breathe through my skin, and my skin is always wet. The dark is not dark because I have no need for eyes that see shapes; I see with my toes, with the fine hairs on my belly, with the taste of the film on my tongue. I know the Cask the way a tongue knows a mouth.

We are not one. There are many of us, a scatter of sticky marks on the pipes, a rustle in the root-crawler mats under the walkway. But I am one of them, and I have a place. It is a copper joint on the south end, where the sap-line from the upper gallery bends around a riveted brace. The metal is warm from the sap, but not too warm. A skin of resin forms there each night, sweet and sharp, and I eat it. It is my food and my work. If I did not eat it, the joint would close, the pressure would build, the doorways would change. So I am a keeper of the open way, though I am no bigger than a thumb.

The Moss knows me. Not me by name—we do not have names—but she knows my kind. She walks barefoot on the boardwalks, and I feel her before I hear her: a change in the pressure of the air, a slow weight that makes the boards exhale. When she passes below my pipe, she pauses. Her hand, huge and pale, reaches up and she lets me step onto her knuckle. I do not know why she does this. Perhaps she is checking that I am alive, that the sap has not stiffened me. She holds me near her face, and her breath is warm and smells of sap-thinner. Her eyes are dark and wide, and in them I see the whole Cask reflected: the ribs, the bats, the blue-green nodules. Then she puts me back on the pipe, and she hums.

The humming is not a sound; it is a pressure. It runs through the root-flesh, through the water, through my bones. When the Moss hums, the wall answers. I feel the root swell a little, and the sap in the pipe next to me moves faster. I know that seep-hour is near, because the humming makes my own blood move, and I must finish cleaning my joint before the water rises. The other root-crawlers know too. We all move at once, a wave of us, licking the joints, scraping our small claws along the edges. The crickets trill louder; they feel it too.

I have lived in the Cask for as long as I can remember, which is not long but is also forever. I hatched in a crack in the north wall, a warm fissure where the root-flesh is soft and the moss grows thick. My first food was the thin sap that weeps from the root when the pressure is low. I grew by molting, leaving my old skins stuck to the wall like tiny grey petals. The Moss found one once, after I had molted under the school niche, and she picked it up and held it to the light. I watched from a nearby rootlet. She did not throw it away; she put it in her pocket, among the guano briquettes and the copper blade. I do not know what she wanted with it. Perhaps nothing. The Moss does things without wanting.

My world is not the whole Cask. I know only the lower third, the pipes and the boardwalk undersides, the sump edge where the film is thick. I have never seen the upper gallery except as a blackness that moves and drips. I do not need to see it. The root is my map. I feel the vibrations of the pump house in my toes, a deep repeating thud that travels along the metal and the wood. I feel the conveyor chains as a rattle in the air, and the steam vents as a sudden hiss that makes the hairs on my belly flatten. I know the water level by the taste of the air: when it rises, the air grows sweeter, fuller, and I climb higher on my pipe to stay above it. I know the bats by the sound of their wings, a soft leathery beat, and by the smell of their guano, which is sharp and fertile.

I am not alone. The wall is alive with us: root-crawlers, millipedes, crickets, moths, the slick blind things that slide through the water. We are not a herd; we are a body. When the pumps surge and the electric bulbs brighten, I feel the sudden heat on my back and I know to move into the shadow. When the saffron light fades back to orange, I know to return. The Moss and the pump-tenders call the rhythm the seep-cycle; to me it is simply the way the world breathes. The Cask breathes, and I breathe with it.

There is a rule: do not kill the root-crawlers. I have never seen it broken, but I have felt the boots of the careless. They shake the boardwalk, and I flatten myself into a seam. The Moss’s bare feet do not shake. When she walks, the wood does not creak; it only breathes, and I can stay where I am. She is the only one who can reach into the sump and not disturb the fish. She is the only one who can touch the rootlet in her arm and know which vessel will bleed gently. She is not our master; she is our neighbor, and we all share the same walls.

What am I? I am the small thing that the big ones forget, but I am also the reason the pipes do not clog and the pressure does not burst. I am a root-crawler, a cleaner, a licker of sap-residue, a body of sticky toes and pale skin, no bigger than a thumb. My eyes are only dark spots, but I see the Cask by taste and touch. My mouth is not a mouth, but I taste the root. My home is not a house, but the space between the pipe and the root-flesh, the seam where the machine and the living meet.

Where would you find yourself in this world? You would find yourself pressed against the warm copper, smelling of pears and guano and wet earth, feeling the pump-house rhythm in your chest, waiting for the Moss to pass and humming back. You would be a root-crawler, and the Cask would be your heart.
