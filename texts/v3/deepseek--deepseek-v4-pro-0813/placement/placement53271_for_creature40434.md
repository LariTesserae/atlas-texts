**Wall-Ear**

I would find myself not in a room but in a thickness between rooms—a crawlspace behind a wall of living mineral, where the stone is still warm and the water runs in vertical threads. I would be on my knees, one cheek near the basalt, breath held so my own pulse doesn’t blur the sound. I would be a Wall-Ear, a Keeper who listens to the city’s unfinished bones.

At first you might not see me. The only light is the pale blue of silt-skin on my sleeves and the faint violet of the cloud deck through a vent. My eyes are wide, dark-adapted, with a reflective layer that gleams like wet obsidian when a cold light passes. My skin is oiled so the drizzle beads and runs. A coat of hull-bat down and sump-mite silk is crusted with living film, so when I move, I move as a small constellation.

My craft is listening. I tap the walls with my knuckles and read the returning vibration. I click my tongue and listen for the shape of the stone. The city is loud—water, pumps, sonar, chittering mites—but loudness is not the same as noise. The wall has a voice in it. A crack has one note; a void has another. A wall curing too fast rings sharp, like a bone under strain. I mark it with rust-limpet foam and move on.

I would not have chosen this. I would have been chosen by it. In the creche I pressed my ear to the nursery floor and heard the thermal pumps. The other children played at Bat-Listener, but I wanted the lower frequencies. So the Keepers gave me to the unfinished district, where the walls are still speaking in sentences that have not ended.

You would find me in the half-poured tower near the caldera’s western rim, a place the Extruder-Tenders call the Open Mouth because the scaffold fields hum in a slow, subsonic chord. The building is not a ruin; it is simply not done. Its walls end in clean sharp edges, and the living-mineral composite leans out over the dark like a wave that has not yet broken. I kneel at the edge and tap. The sound goes down through the stone and comes back changed. I know the wall is sound. I know the water is moving. I know the ground beneath has shifted a fraction since last year, and I mark it in my memory.

There are hull-bats above me in their thousands, folded leather, clicking. I click back. They know my breath-weight, my heart-thrum. One drops from the heat exchanger and clings to my shoulder. I do not brush it off. It is a citizen, and it is reading me as I read the stone. It tells me in sound there is a draft from the east, a new current, a warmth. I thank it and move on.

At the end of the shift I climb to the rim. The drizzle hovers. The plateau steams. The sky is not a sky; it is a roof of black vapor reflecting faint violet from the city’s waste heat. Nothing green grows. The ground crawls with life that needs no sun. I look out and feel the slow pulse under the roar—the plateau rising a few centimeters a century, the water table creeping higher, the city still being born from the rock.

I am cold, but my thermal belt is banked with heat from the kitchen vents. I am not afraid. I am not waiting for anything to be finished. I am a Wall-Ear, and the city is still speaking. I am just listening.
