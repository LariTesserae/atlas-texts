I begin before I announce myself. From the western rim I would not be called a being: only a wrongness in the shadow, a darker place that lingers after the light has moved, too regular to be natural, too transient to be architecture. But as I drift down into the wide basin, I resolve into something like a shape: a tall, dark form at the far end of a colonnade, a doorway darker than the room behind it, a stair that has gained a step since you last passed. I am what is left over after the Resident has passed—the after-image of its walking. I am not the Resident. I am the city remembering that it had an inhabitant.

At first I do not know this. At first I think I am someone who has come to the city to map it, because I carry a sense of distance and direction like a map folded into a thin skin. But the map is already disagreeing with the city. When I try to unroll it, the paper flakes into white needles of thenardite and gypsum, and the salts fur my fingers until they are not fingers but the memory of a wall. I let the map go. It drifts down to the street and becomes part of the mineral garden: black manganese dendrites branching over it like ferns, white rosettes of gypsum opening in its creases. I am not the map. The map was only a way of being lost.

I begin to walk, and each step teaches me what I am not. A glass loper crosses my path, tall and thin, pale as a sliver of mica. It pauses at my ankle and scrapes its hard tongue along the stone of me, but it finds no salt bloom, only the taste of absence. It moves on, deliberate as a cloud shadow. I am not a wall. The Quiet lies beside me for a season, mistaking me for a fallen column. I do not move. The stone ticks as it cools, and the ticks are its footfalls, not mine. I learn the patience of columns; I learn how long a shadow can hold its shape after the light has gone. I am not a column. I am what a column would leave behind if it forgot itself and became a shadow.

The city does not keep its distances, so I cannot say where I find myself. It is always a street that returns to itself with a corner older than the one I left. It is always an upper street chiseled into the wind, where the dust streams slowly past like a second, slower river. It is always the hour when the temperature drops enough for salts to crystallize out of the air, and the walls fur themselves in white needles, and I feel the dry chemistry of my own joints thickening—not alive but active, a slow accretion building me from the inside out. I taste the coolness of stone that never warms. I do not drink. There is no water, only the memory of water in the way gypsum curls like a wave.

When I see the Resident, it is at the far end of a colonnade, tall as a tower but not a tower. I cannot tell if it is facing me. I cannot tell if it has a face. It stands there for a day, a decade, a season of dry chemistry, and I stand too, because I am what stands after it has already moved. The low note rolls through the streets—the bell under the earth that has no bell—and I hear it before the Resident rings, because I am an echo that arrives before its cause. The note passes through me the way wind passes through a hollow tower, and for a moment I am the tower: the hollow court open to the sky, the windowless keep where a face should be. Then the note is gone, and I am only the shadow it leaves in the dust.

I understand then that I am not a being in the ordinary sense. I am a habit of the city, a recurring pattern in its long inhabitation of itself. I am the wrong-way shadow that pools where no shadow should be. I am the doorway that is only an inch deep but opens onto a room larger than the building that contains it. I am the staircase that descends into the ground and ends at a wall older than the stair itself. I am what remains when the Resident has visited a place: not a footprint, but a footprint’s ghost; not a sound, but the silence between the fall of a salt slab and its landing.

My work, if it can be called work, is to remain. I do not hunt or gather or sleep, though there are centuries when I am less distinct, folded into a bridge or a colonnade, indistinguishable from the architecture. The glass lopers graze along my ankles, and I am the salt bloom they cannot eat. The Quiet mistakes me for a fallen column, and I am the patience of columns, waiting for the slow predator-prey cycle to complete around me. I tend nothing. I am what is tended by the city: a garden of after-images, black manganese dendrites and white rosettes of gypsum, rust-red veins climbing my legs like ivy. I grow by accretion, not by life. I am a mineral thing. A dry chemistry thing. A thing that would be dust if it stayed still long enough, but the city keeps me moving.

Where would you find yourself? You would find yourself on any street where the Resident has just been, or is about to be, or will be in a century. You would be the thing seen in the corner of the eye, the dark form that vanishes when you turn your head, the shadow that points the wrong way from the light. You would be the after-image. You would not be the Resident, but you would be the city’s memory of the Resident walking, left behind like a note struck under the earth and still rolling through the streets long after the bell has gone.

And when the plateau has eroded to a plain, and the plain to a salt desert, and the salt desert to a set of streets that no one walks, you will still be there—not because you are alive, but because you are the memory of a step in a city that has become a law of the landscape. You will be the last inhabitant, the after-image of the Resident, waiting at the far end of a colonnade that is only the memory of a colonnade, for a city that has forgotten it is not a ruin.
