I would find myself not on the path, but just off it, on one of the bare flats where the mud has cracked into polygons. The city would be behind me or in front of me; in that gray-gold dimness direction would not matter. I would be standing exactly in a crack, letting the water well up warm around my bare foot. I would not move. I would wait, the way the ground waits, until the fog thinned enough to show the high terraces and the pale scar of the old water line.

Then I would understand.

I am not one of the tenders of green islands. I am not one of the ones who clear salt from the broad leaves or uncurl the ferns. I am the one who goes into the empty rooms.

I live in a low structure on the second terrace, just above the thickest fog. It does not hover. It sits directly on the stone, adjusting itself with small, patient clicks. Its walls are dark and faintly translucent; at dusk they blush with the glow of something inside. The opening appears when I press my palm to the wall. Sometimes it appears before I touch, as if the building has been expecting me. I crawl in. Inside, the floor is slightly tilted, enough to remind me that nothing here is finished. There is a bench, a basin, a strip of mesh I knot and unknot. I do not need more.

Each day I walk the unlit paths until the paths stop. I carry no light. My eyes have learned to read the dimness the way a hand reads a wall. I step where the sediment is firm, where the cracks are narrow, where the water under the ground is close enough to feel as coolness against my bare feet. I avoid the green islands. Not because they are dangerous, but because they are already tended. I am for the spaces between.

When I reach a building that has been closed too long, I do not knock. I lay my palm flat against the surface and wait. The wall is often cold, smooth as old bone. I can feel the slow shifting inside, the tiny adjustments of its legs, the faint vibration of the city’s hum. I do not push. Some buildings need you to wait outside for a long time—long enough for the fog to rise from the lake, long enough for the light to deepen toward the breathing dark. I sit with my back to the wall and let my warmth pass into it. When the wall softens, it opens like a mouth, not suddenly, but the way a hand opens after sleep.

Inside, the air is warm and full of a fine dust that is not dust. It smells of wet stone and old breathing. I press my ear to the wall. The building speaks in tiny settlings: a tick, a sigh, a long low groan that sounds almost like a name. I do not have a name, but the buildings give me one. It is the sound of water finding a new crack.

I listen for the places where the silence is too tight, where the settling has stopped and become a held breath. If a wall is too cool, I breathe on it. If a floor is too warm, I pour a little water from the sleeve of my mesh coat and smooth it along the seam. If one of its legs has been adjusting too often—shifting in small, anxious increments—I crouch and press my hand to the ground beneath it. The ground is young; it is still deciding. I tell it with my palm, without words, to be patient. The building will wait.

I am not a repairer. The city does not need repair; it needs witness. The buildings do not break. They forget, or they dream, or they grow lonely in the long gray-gold hours. I am the one who goes into the empty rooms and finds them not empty. There is a hum in every wall, a pulse in every floor. Some rooms have not been entered in years, and the air in them is so still it has begun to drink its own moisture. When I enter, the walls seem to wake slowly, like a sleeper recognizing an old friend. I do not speak. I simply stand there, one hand against the dark, until the building’s breathing deepens and the walls settle into rest.

The tenders of the green islands see me pass. They nod. They know I am not one of them. They leave me a green shoot or a peeled reed at the place where my path leaves the edge of the city. I eat it slowly, walking. They do not ask me to speak. They understand that my conversations are with walls and floors and the low groan of the ground. Sometimes a child follows me, at a distance, curious. I do not turn. The child learns to walk as I walk—slowly, listening. After a while, the child returns to the green islands, carrying a small stone from the upper terrace. The stone will be placed in a crack, one more marker of the city’s patience.

I keep no written record. Writing feels too final for a place that is still arriving. Instead I tie knots in a strip of mesh: one for a building that has settled, two for a building that dreams too loudly, three for a wall that opened and did not close. The knots are not for anyone else. They are for me, to press in my pocket when the fog is too thick to see. They remind me that the city is not a ruin. It is a body in its first long breath.

The lake is not my charge, but I go to it when the buildings are quiet. I stand at the edge of the mudflats, where the green gives way to open ground. The lake breathes, rising and falling by a few inches. I match my breath to it. I do not drink. I touch the water with my fingertips, then touch the ground, then touch my own chest. The water is too mineral, too close to the blood of the basin. But I keep its taste on my skin. It tells me what the buildings already know: nothing here is finished. The lake is still retreating. The ground is still settling. The city is still being adjusted, tuned, extended.

I am an interval. I am the pause between the city and the bare flats, between the green islands and the long empty stretches. I am the one who walks into rooms that have forgotten they are alive and reminds them, with a palm, with a breath, that they are still arriving.

If you look for me, you will not find me on the path. You will find me just off it, where the mud has cracked into polygons and the water is welling up around my feet. The city will be behind me, or in front; the direction will not matter. There will be a low building on the terrace, its opening appearing as you approach. Inside, the air will be warm and humming. I will be there, my hand pressed to the dark, listening for the next small settling.
