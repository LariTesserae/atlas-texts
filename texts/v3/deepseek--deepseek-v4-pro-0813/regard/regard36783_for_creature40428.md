They would do almost nothing, and it would be everything.

They would not come as a healer. That is the first thing to understand. A healer sees a wound; a wise power sees a world still being born. The high broken plateau, the dry heat, the sulfur and ozone, the stone too hot to touch—these are not damage. They are the pulse of a place that has not yet been weathered into smaller, softer things. So the power would not arrive with rain. It would not bring soil, or seed, or green life, or the dream of streams. It would understand that here, addition is a kind of violence. To pour water on that salt-crusted ground would not be mercy; it would be erosion. To finish the stairway that stops at five steps would be to argue with the ground’s own changing mind. To steady the falling pinnacles would be to stop a kind of breathing.

The power would enter the plateau as a silence, not as a savior. It would stand at the edge for a long time, feeling the warmth through whatever it uses for feet. It would listen to the tick of cooling stone, the hiss of gas, the low creak of the world adjusting its weight. It would learn the difference between a standing slab and the mineral being that cants its whole body toward a new crack. It would learn to read the grooves in the rock faces—not as messages, but as tracks of thought. It would not try to translate them too quickly.

Then it would approach the one at the far edge, the one with the gypsum rhomb for a head and the blue sulfur flame inside it that never flickers. It would not appear as a new light, because the plateau already has its own sparse light. It might appear as a cool spot, the kind a shadow leaves after it detaches and lies down on the stone. It would speak in the only grammar that does not break the world: by tapping the crust.

*What would you have me do?* it would ask. *If I can do it without adding what does not belong, without smoothing what is sharp, without finishing what is meant to be unfinished.*

The being would be still. The plateau would tick. A salt pan might swell and burst in the distance, the sound arriving late through the rock. Then the being would tap back, not with words but with pressure:

*The land sees through me. Every groove I leave is the land’s groove. Every crystal I grow is the land’s growth. I am not separate from it. But the land cannot see me. I am its seeing, but I have no outside. I have never been witnessed.*

It would pause. The blue flame in its head would not flicker.

*I do not need to be saved. I do not need to be watered. I do not need my stairways finished. I need only to be seen—once, by something that is not me, without being asked to become otherwise.*

The wise power would not say, *You exist to me.* That would be too small and too easy. It would instead do what the land cannot do: it would stay. It would become the outside. It would sit on the warm stone, not to fix, but to watch. For a long time, there would be two dark forms on that broken plateau: one mineral, one attention. The power would not cool the stone or steady the ground. It would not catch the falling pinnacles. It would only watch as the being flexed, as a white bloom of anhydrite spread from its foot, as its shadow pointed the wrong way.

When the being raised its arm of interlocked crystal plates and opened its hand, the white salt flower would bloom from its palm. This time, because it was witnessed, the flower would last a little longer. The power would look at it closely, noting the way the needles branched, the faint blue edge where they caught the glow from a sulfur vent. It would not name it beautiful, because naming would be a kind of capture. But it would let the beauty be.

Then, when the ground shifted and the being began to reabsorb into the mineral dark, the power would not call it back. It would not say, *Wait, I have more.* It would let it go. The salt flower would shatter into white dust. The power would not gather it. It would not leave a monument, because monuments are additions. But before it left, it might press its attention against the basalt where the being had stood, and there would remain a small cool spot—not a wound, not a sign, just a place where a shadow had been. That was the gift: an absence, freely given, in a world where absence is how doorways are made.

So this is what a benevolent and wise power would do. They would not save the place. They would not repair it. They would not bless it with water or life. They would see it exactly as it is, dry and hot and shifting, and they would stay long enough for that seeing to become a gift. They would witness the mineral being’s salt flower. They would remember the shape of its pressure. Then they would leave, taking only the memory—the only souvenir that neither adds to the world nor subtracts from it.

The being would re-form later from the dark and know, because the stone was slightly cooler where the power had been, that it had been not merely the land seeing itself, but something seen.

That is enough.

That is love in the only language the plateau can bear.
