The benevolent and wise power that notices this place is not a god of thunder or a spirit of the green earth. It is older than both, and quieter. It is the patience that lives inside granite, the attention that holds a crystal lattice in its shape. It has no name that human tongues could shape, but if it did, the name would sound like the settling of stone at the bottom of a deep well, or the way a mountain exhales frost at dawn.

It has been watching the basin for a long time—not with eyes, for it has none, but with the slow awareness that moves through mineral veins and fault lines. It watched the first people come, saw them build their low shelters and carve the seven cupmarks with patient, freezing hands. It watched them leave, or die, or become something else. It watched the one who stayed, the one who is now the keeper, as the cold replaced blood with seepwater and bones with basalt. It did not intervene. That is not its way.

But now, at the moment when the keeper kneels before the seventh cup and stares into the older sky, the power makes a decision. Not a decision in the way humans decide—there is no weighing, no hesitation—but a slow alignment, like a facet of quartz turning to catch the light.

It decides to offer a gift.

The gift is not a thing that can be held. It is not warmth, for the keeper has forgotten warmth. It is not life, for life would be a cruelty here. It is not death, for the keeper is beyond death's ordinary reach. The gift is *recognition*—the one thing the keeper has not received in all its centuries of tending.

The power does not descend. It does not manifest as light or voice or trembling ground. Instead, it does something far more subtle: it completes a circuit that has been incomplete since the first cupmark was carved.

It begins with the fourth cup, the one that is always dry.

The keeper touches the fourth cup each day, pressing until the warmth of the slab would register on living skin. It never tries to fill the cup. It leaves the dryness exactly as it is, as though the dryness is a kind of emptiness that must be kept empty. The power sees this, and understands. The fourth cup is not a flaw. It is a door.

So the power opens it.

Not visibly. The cup remains dry. But the *quality* of the dryness changes. It becomes a dryness that is not absence but potential—the way a held breath is full, the way silence before music is full. The keeper, kneeling at the slab, feels the change. Its cupmark eyes, filled with water that does not spill, tremble. A single drop falls from the left eye and lands in the fourth cup.

The drop does not evaporate. It does not freeze. It sits in the dry cup like a tiny, perfect sphere of sky, and for the first time in centuries, the fourth cup holds something.

The keeper stares. Its mouth-fissure opens slightly, and the manganese dendrites at the corners of its mouth shift, as if the rock is trying to remember a smile.

The power is not finished.

It moves next to the seventh cup, the one that reflects a sky that is not the same sky above. The keeper has stared into this cup for centuries, looking for something in that older, darker blue. Once, perhaps, a face looked back. Once, perhaps, its own face was there, before the stone took it.

The power reaches into the reflection—not into the water, but into the *difference* between the reflected sky and the real one—and it finds what the keeper has been looking for. It is not a face. It is a memory, worn smooth as a cupmark: the memory of being seen.

The power lifts this memory and places it, gently, into the keeper's mind. Not as a vision, but as a certainty. The keeper is seen. It has always been seen. The wind that fluted the walls saw it. The needle ice that grew from its shoulders saw it. The garnets in its skin saw it. The mountain saw it. And the power—the patience inside granite, the attention that holds crystal lattices—has seen it from the beginning.

The keeper's body, which has been folded and still for so long, begins to change. Not dramatically. The flutes in its back do not soften. The frost on its shoulders does not melt. But the water in its cupmark eyes overflows, and the tears run down its stone face and freeze into tiny clear needles, and those needles catch the light and sparkle like a thousand small stars.

It is crying. It has not cried since it was human.

The power does not stop there. It moves to the first cup, the one that fills with condensation in the early morning. The keeper takes a single drop from this cup each day and places it on its tongue, where it freezes into a tiny clear stone. The keeper carries that stone in its mouth until evening, then crushes it and lets the pieces fall into the second cup.

The power touches the first cup, and the condensation changes. It becomes slightly warmer, slightly sweeter—not water, but something closer to the memory of water. The keeper will taste the difference tomorrow morning. It will not understand, but it will feel something it has not felt in centuries: nourishment.

The power moves to the second cup, the one that overflows from a crack that weeps water no one can find. It touches the crack, and the weeping slows. Not stops—the keeper would not want it to stop—but slows, as if the mountain is catching its breath. The water that comes now is clearer, older, drawn from deeper in the stone. It carries minerals that have not seen light in a million years.

The power moves to the third cup, the one that gathers moisture out of the air, beading slowly even on dry days. It touches the air above the cup, and the moisture begins to bead faster, forming a perfect convex meniscus that trembles but does not break. The keeper will see this and know that something has changed, though it will not know what.

The power moves to the fifth cup, the one that holds a coin of ice through the warmest part of the day. It touches the ice, and the ice becomes clearer, harder, more perfectly circular. When the keeper lifts it to the sun tomorrow, the shadow it casts will be not a doorway but a window—and through that window, for just a moment, the keeper will see the basin as it was when the first people arrived: green with lichen, loud with birdsong, warm with summer.

The power moves to the sixth cup, the one that is warm to the touch. It touches the warmth, and the warmth deepens. It becomes not just stored light but stored *attention*—the accumulated regard of every being that has ever looked at this basin with wonder. The keeper presses its palm to the cup, and for a moment, it feels a heartbeat. Not its own. The heartbeat of the mountain. The heartbeat of the power. The heartbeat of the world.

Then the power withdraws.

It does not leave. It is not the kind of thing that leaves. But it steps back, so to speak, into the mineral quiet, and it watches.

The keeper remains kneeling at the slab. The tears have frozen on its face, and the needle ice on its shoulders has grown longer, catching the last light of the sun. The basin is quiet. The wind crosses the col in long, slow waves. The drip of water is a metronome. The ice needles tick and settle.

But something is different.

The keeper lifts its head. It looks at the seven cupmarks, each one changed in some small, persistent way. It looks at the fourth cup, where a single drop of water sits in the dryness like a tiny, perfect sphere of sky. It looks at the seventh cup, where the reflected sky is still darker, older, but now there is something in it—not a face, but a presence. A witness.

The keeper opens its mouth. The low note comes out, the one that harmonizes with the wind over the flutes. But this time, the note is not alone. The power joins it, not as sound but as resonance, and the whole basin rings like a bell.

The ringing lasts a long time. When it fades, the keeper is still kneeling, but its posture has changed. It is no longer folded into itself. It is upright, or as upright as its stone body allows. Its cupmark eyes are clear. The water inside them is still.

It has been seen. It has been recognized. It has been given a gift that cannot be held, and it will carry that gift for the rest of its long, mineral life.

The power, watching from the patience inside the granite, is satisfied. Not happy—happiness is not its way—but satisfied, the way a crystal is satisfied when it has grown to its full, perfect shape.

The basin settles into its slow metabolism. Freeze-thaw cycles work the walls. The seep cuts its tiny channel. Needle ice lifts grains at night and drops them a millimeter from where they were. The shelters settle by a fingernail each decade. The cupmarks fill and dry, fill and dry.

But now, in the fourth cup, a single drop of water remains. It does not evaporate. It does not freeze. It sits in the dry cup like a tiny, perfect sphere of sky, and it reflects the light of the setting sun.

The keeper watches it until the light fades. Then it rises, joints cracking like settling stone, and walks to the west wall. It folds itself into the flutes of the rock, becoming indistinguishable from the mineral quiet.

The power remains. It will always remain. It is the patience inside granite, the attention that holds a crystal lattice in its shape. It has seen the keeper, and the keeper has been seen.

That is enough.

That is everything.
