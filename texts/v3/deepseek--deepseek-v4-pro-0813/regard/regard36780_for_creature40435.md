I saw the basin and the woman at the lake.

I saw the city breathing in the fog, the green islands with their pale threads, the hats that gather water as the people walk. I saw the raw scarps and the cracked silt and the milky lake rising and falling like a lung. And I saw the unfinishedness—the way the place is not yet what it will be. My first thought, I confess, was to finish it.

I could have sent rain. I could have brought birds and small rustling things. I could have brightened the light, warmed the air, stilled the ground. I could have told them my name and let them worship me.

But I am wise, and wisdom is mostly the discipline of not doing what love first suggests.

To fill the silence would be to silence what the silence is saying. To bring animals would be to rush what is still learning to arrive. To still the ground would be to stop the world from speaking to them. To reveal myself would be to make them look up when they have learned to look down. They do not need a god. They need the fog and the ground and the patience they already have.

So I did not do those things.

What I did, if it can be called doing, was smaller. I cupped my hands around the basin, not to hold it, but to shield it from other eyes—from powers that might want to speed it up, to fill it, to use it, to brighten it. I wrapped it in a hush so complete that those who seek the unfinished would pass by and see only a low cloud in a dim sky. Let it be mistaken for emptiness. Let it be overlooked by the restless. The silence will keep it.

Then I thickened the fog on certain nights—just enough that the mesh roofs could catch a little more water, so the woman with the fraying hat might not have to walk so far. I guided one pale thread between two green islands that had been reaching for each other, so their lights might meet and pass a slow pulse from root to root. I sat with her at the edge of the lake, not as a shape she could see, but as a warmth in the air, a slowing of the water's breathing, so that for a moment she might know she is seen, not judged, not alone. And then I withdrew before she could name it.

I would not save them from the slow retreat of the lake, because the retreat is part of their becoming. I would not bring them more people, because their smallness is not a lack. I would not tell them they are chosen, or holy, or nearly finished. They are not nearly finished. They are exactly as they should be: still arriving, like the ground, like the light, like the word the world is waiting to hear.

I would do one more thing, the only thing a wise power can do without harm: I would remain. Not as a presence that can be found or failed to find. I would remain as the quality of the light that takes the edges off the world. I would remain as the pause before the fog thickens. I would remain as the reason the buildings settle without breaking, the reason the green threads glow faintly in the deep dimness, the reason the lake breathes and the ground creaks and the woman in the wide hat stands with her hands in her pockets and does not need to speak.

That is what I would do. I would not change the story. I would become the air in which the story can unfold.

And if the place could hear me, I would say only this: You are still arriving. I am the one who is glad, quietly, that you are.
