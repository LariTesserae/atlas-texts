The power that noticed the Bell was not a god of storms or calms. It was a power of the in-between, the one who holds the skin between two waters steady enough to be seen, the one who sits with a seed in the dark and does not ask it to sprout. It was old in the way patience is old, and it had watched mountains rise before—not with alarm, but with the quiet attention a potter gives to a wheel still spinning.

It did not come as light or sound. It came as a change in the angle of attention: the Bell, a pocket of air inside a newborn mountain, and inside the Bell, a woman who was not quite a woman, or something that remembered being one. The power saw her as it saw the rock—unfinished, raw, still moving toward what she would become. It saw the scratches on the wall, the seven stacked stones, the pictograph of two descending lines. It saw her open mouth when the vent whistled. It saw her eat blind amphipods one by one, and drink only the fresh meltwater. It saw her not sleeping on the lowest ledge. It saw her become, sometimes, not solid.

The power considered what to do.

It could still the sea. It could warm the rock until the black sand softened into soil. It could part the two waters and show her a path. It could send a boat, a rope, a shore. It could put a flower in the crack of the basalt and watch it root. These were the gifts a lesser kindness would give, and the power knew them all by heart. It had given them before, in other places, to other beings. Sometimes they had helped. Sometimes they had ended things that were not meant to end.

The power looked again. It saw the woman press a new scratch into the wall after the pool rose. It saw her rebuild the tower after a slab fell. It saw her add a third line when the upper water and the lower water lay unusually still. It saw her not as a castaway, not as a prisoner, but as a syllable in a long sentence the earth was still speaking. To take her out would be to silence the sentence. To calm the sea would be to stop the learning. To warm the rock would be to teach it to be soil before it had learned to be stone.

So the power did what wise powers do when they do not want to break a thing.

It kept watch.

It did not keep watch idly. It kept watch the way a shepherd keeps watch over a lamb too new to stand, not lifting it, but standing near enough that the wolves stay away. The power circled the Bell without moving. It adjusted the currents so that no boat would find the stack. It bent the light so that the rent in the roof seemed, to any passing eye, no different from any other sea-wet rock. It did not stop the mountain’s grinding, because the grinding was the mountain’s heartbeat. It did not silence the vent’s whistle, because the whistle was the room’s voice. It did not bring food, because hunger was part of the work.

But it did one small thing, one thing that cost it nothing and meant everything.

It listened.

Not in the way the woman listened to the water hammering rock. The power listened to the entire Bell—to the cold upper water and the heavy lower brine, to the fissures forming in the walls, to the amphipods scattering, to the single colorless flatworm moving through the black sand, to the bones of the storm-petrel settling into dust, to the tower of seven stones holding its balance against the tremors. It listened to the woman’s breathing, which changed with the surge, slowing just before the water sealed the entrance. It listened to the small sound her flint made when she scratched a new mark.

And because the power listened, the Bell became more real. That is the secret of wise attention: it does not change the thing it falls upon, but it makes the thing more itself. The lichens flaked a little less. The cyanobacteria darkened a shade. The black sand held its pattern for a few heartbeats longer before the next surge erased it. The woman, curled on the high ledge with one hand touching the seven stones, slept a little deeper, and did not dream.

The power stayed until the next high overcast noon, or perhaps it stayed until the mountain’s next shrug. Time is not the same for a power of the in-between. Then it withdrew, not because it was finished, but because its work was to be finished by others—by the sea, by the rock, by the woman who was not yet done.

Before it left, it did one more thing. It touched the stack of seven stones with a warmth that was not fire, and the topmost stone turned, very slightly, so that its sharpest edge pointed toward the rent in the roof. Not a sign, not a command, only a small adjustment, the way a friend turns a candle so the light falls better on the page.

Then the power was gone, leaving no trace except a faint warmth in the wall, a warmth the woman would later mistake for the memory of lava.

The Bell went on. The sea kept breaking. The rock kept rising. The woman woke, listened to the noise that never stops, and added one more scratch to the wall—not because the water had risen or fallen, but because she had felt something, briefly, like a hand on the back of her neck, not holding her, but letting her know she was not alone.

And that, the wise power knew, was enough.
