The attention arrives without entering. It does not part the haze or turn the heat or still the tide. It is simply there, the way the low sky is there, the way the water table is there: a vast, slow, patient presence that takes in the city all at once and makes nothing move. It is not a being in any shape the city has known. It is more like a pressure change, a thickening of the air, a light that is not light but a quality of watchfulness, as if the brown sky had opened a single, colorless eye and was looking down with neither pity nor alarm.

The woman feels it first as a change in the rain. The afternoon storm comes as it always does, hot and heavy, but the drops are different. They are still rain, still salty from the sea, but they fall with a kind of deliberation, each drop knowing exactly where it lands. She stands on the roof, her rubber coat steaming, and holds out her hand. The rain pools in her palm. It is warm, but not feverish. It smells of the sea and of stone. She drinks it without thinking, and for a moment the heat in her throat loosens, and she can breathe without effort. She is so unaccustomed to relief that she mistakes it for weakness, and sits down on the wet concrete. The city below her—the cranes, the tank farm, the flooded streets—wavers in the steam like a reflection on water. She feels a pulse in the roof, not the pump station, but something slower, deeper, as if the whole building had a heartbeat. She puts her palm flat on the concrete. The pulse is there. She has never noticed it before. But then, she has never sat still in the rain with a warm handful of water and no fear.

That night, the tide rises as it always does, six inches higher, then falls. But as it falls, the water in the flooded stairwell of her building clears. Not all at once, but by morning she can see the bottom step, then the second, and the eel is a dark ribbon of surprise, twisting against the sudden visibility. She sits on the edge of the stairwell, her boots in the water, and the eel rises to the surface, not for food but to look at her. They regard each other across a distance that is not distance. The eel's eye is black and wet, and in it she sees the same watchfulness that has been pressing on the city. She reaches out a hand, not to touch, but to acknowledge. The eel hovers a moment longer, then sinks back, but the water remains clear. That evening, when she lowers her plastic jug into the cistern, the water that comes up is cool and almost sweet. She drinks it slowly, sitting in the dark, and for the first time in years, she does not feel the day's heat still burning in her chest.

The power does not speak. It has no voice, no form, no name. It is not the woman's idea of a god; she has no such idea. It is not the city's idea of a machine; the machines are all dead or dying. It is something else, something that has been growing in the margins of the world's attention—in the bacterial mats, in the shell hash, in the slow accretion of salt and rust—a patience so old and so complete that it has become almost indistinguishable from the landscape itself. And now it is here, not to repair, not to save, not to transform, but to witness. To see this place as it is, without wishing it otherwise. To see the woman as she is, without needing her to be more or less. And in that seeing, without lifting a finger, it begins to change things, because attention of that kind is never passive. It is a form of care, and care, even silent, even still, rearranges the world.

The first green appears three days later. Not in the streets, not on the concrete, but in a crack in the roof where the woman dries her bacterial paste. A single shoot, pale and thin, pushing up through the salt crust. It is not a weed she knows; it has no name in her memory. It is the color of old copper, then yellow-green, then a green so dark it is almost black. She does not pull it. She waters it with a spoonful of her clear cistern water, and the next day there is another shoot, and the day after that a third. They grow not toward the sky, which is still brown, but toward the water, their roots reaching down through the cracks toward the flooded stairwell. The woman sits on the roof in the afternoons and watches them, and the eel swims in its clear water, and the crabs climb over her boots, and the mussels on the far pilings open and shut with their tiny weather. The city is still hot, still wet, still half-drowned, but the heat has a different quality now. It is no longer a fever. It is more like the warmth of a body at rest.

The woman begins to talk. Not to anyone, not to herself, but to the roof-grass, to the eel, to the rain. She says the names of things she has never said aloud: *conveyor*, *cistern*, *catwalk*, *culvert*. The words taste like salt on her tongue. She says her own name, which she had nearly forgotten, and it comes out cracked and soft, like a mussel shell opening. The power hears. The power has been hearing. It does not answer in words, but the city answers: the pump station thrums, the pipes tick, the water in the canal reverses, and she feels the answer in her feet, in her chest, in the roots of the unnamed grass.

She does not become less alone. The city is still empty of people, still hollow, still quiet. But the quality of her aloneness changes. It is no longer the emptiness of abandonment, but the emptiness of a held breath. She is no longer the last person in a dead city; she is the first person in a new one, a city that has never fully died, only changed its mind about what it wanted to be. The power, whatever it is, has not fixed her. It has not sent a rescuer, or grown a garden overnight, or turned the sea back. It has done far less, and far more: it has stayed. It has paid attention. And in the economy of that attention, nothing is wasted—not the rain, not the rust, not the woman's cracked hands, not the eel's dark eye. All of it has been seen.

One evening, the sky briefly parts. Not all the way, but a rent in the brown clouds opens like a seam, and through it she can see a few pale points of light. Stars. She has seen them before only in memory, or in dreams. She stands on the roof, the grass shoots cool against her boots, and looks up. The eel stirs below. The crabs pause. The mussels open. The power does not speak, but the woman feels its attention on her like a hand on the back of her neck, warm and steady. She is not afraid. She is not alone. She is not saved. She is simply *seen*, and that is enough. She closes her eyes, and the world continues, in its slow, water-slow way, and she is part of it. The attention remains, not as a visitor, but as a resident. The city has another inhabitant now, one that asks for nothing, takes nothing, and gives only the gift of being noticed. That is the power's action, the whole of it: to notice, and to stay. And in that staying, the woman finds that she can breathe the thick air, and find it sweet.
