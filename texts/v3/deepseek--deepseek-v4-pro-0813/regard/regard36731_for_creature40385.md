Let it be said: the power came as a listener, not as a hand.

It had no name, or too many names, all of them acts of attention. It first sensed the Grafted Hollow as a warm pause in a cold country—a ring of melted frost, a breath of animals, a root-mass humming below the peat. It did not descend with thunder or light. It descended the way a thought descends: without weight.

It saw the Hollow whole. It saw the water running in sheets down living walls, the eel-star lamps blue-green as drowned moons, the woven houses hanging like cocoons. It saw the Grafted moving slowly, toes testing each root, their eyes huge and dark-adapted. It saw the moss-speakers listening to the central root. It saw the sluice-wardens working in fog-fall, the children learning to step before they learned to speak. It saw the dead being given back to the moss. It saw the bargain: blood returned, roots instructed, silence kept.

And it saw the being who lived there—not a separate creature, but the Hollow itself, the root-mass and the fog and the countless small lives, a single responsive organism with the town as one of its organs.

Because the power was wise, it did not think, *How broken.* It thought, *How complete.*

Then it considered what it might do.

It could make the water warm. But warmth would breed rot and quicken the roots until the walls closed like a slow mouth.  
It could make food plentiful. But plenty would break the careful hunger that kept the grubbers milked, the eel-wyrms trapped, the fungi harvested without greed.  
It could tear open the ceiling and let in the sun. But the sun would blind them and scorch the moss; the Hollow would heal its wound by shutting.  
It could teach them metalworking, medicine, writing, roads. But each lesson would be a new noise, and noise is danger in a place that hunts by vibration.  
It could slay the pale long-bodied predators. But then the grubbers would overgraze the root walls, the eel-wyrms would swarm the black pools, and the balance would tip and fall.

The power sat with these possibilities, not as a king with a map, but as a guest at a small fire. It saw that the Grafted Hollow was not a problem to be solved. It was a solution to a problem it had never been asked to understand.

So it did the only thing a truly wise power could do.

It hid the Hollow from those who would enlarge it.

Not with a wall. A wall would be an invitation. Instead it laid a thin bias over the Over-Moss, a kind of unattention. The ground above grew a little duller to the eyes of strangers. Maps forgot to mention the bog. Compasses drifted a degree or two. Machines that searched from the sky saw only cloud or a shallow depression where the peat had slumped. Hikers who came near felt a small tug to walk elsewhere—not fear, only the certainty that there was nothing worth seeing. The frost ring remained, but it became, to those not already listening, just a place where snow melted early.

It did not make the Hollow invisible. It made it unremarkable, which is the deepest hiding there is.

Then the power descended once more to the central root, not to command but to answer. It had heard the hum that the moss-speakers recorded in their knotted cords, the slow song of the root-mass speaking to the bog above. The power added its own note—a harmonic so low it was not sound but a slight loosening in the chest, a warmth behind the sternum, a sense that the dark was not empty.

One moss-speaker, an elder whose name was a knot of three turns, pressed her ear to the bark during the next fog-fall. The hum had changed. It was not louder. It was not afraid. It was as if the root had been speaking for a long time to an empty room, and now someone had sat down to listen. She tied a new knot in the cord, a loop she had never tied before, and did not know what it meant.

The power remained there for a while, not as a guardian with a sword, but as a held breath. It watched the Grafted prune and weave and feed the eel-star. It watched a child fall from a low root into black water and make no sound as a sister hauled him up by the ankle. It watched the dead wrapped in sphagnum and given to the roots. It watched the fog-fall rise and sink, rise and sink, like a gray lung.

It did not save them from their life. It did not need to.

When it finally left, it left no mark except the unattention above and the low note in the root. It did not leave a book, a tool, a commandment. It left the Hollow as it had found it: whole, hidden, and listening.

And that, perhaps, is the only thing a benevolent and wise power can do for a world that is already alive: notice it without seizing it, protect it without entering it, love it without improving it.

The Grafted Hollow continues. The moss remembers. The root hums with a new undertone. And high above, on the cold peat, the frost still melts in a small ring, and the world walks past, unseeing.
