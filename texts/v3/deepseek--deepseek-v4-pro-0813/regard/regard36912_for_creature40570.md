**What the Wise Power Did (and Did Not Do)**

I am not a god of cities. I am a power of estuaries, of fog, of the slow work of water on stone. I have watched deltas form and deltas drown. When I noticed the Low City, I did not see a problem to solve. I saw a place that was still deciding what it would be, and a people who had learned to live inside that indecision.

I saw that the Low City was not a place with a being; it was a being—a slow, wet, industrial organism still growing. The river was its veins, the settling ponds its lungs, the pipes its nerves, the fog its breath. The inhabitants were its cells: Vorn the pond-tender, Salla the garden-keeper, the children who read puddles, the listeners who pressed their hands to walls. They were not separate from the city; they were the city, feeling itself.

I considered raising the land. I could have pressed my thumb into the mud and made a firm foundation. But then the river would have no place to drop its load, and the city would stop growing. The people would lose their maps-as-poems, their puddle-reading, their water-houses. They would become like the people of dry cities, who think the ground is permanent and are betrayed by that belief.

I considered clearing the fog. But the fog is the city's mercy. It hides the worst of the industrial scars, and it gives each person a small solitude. Without it, the density would be unbearable; the people would see too much of each other and too much of the machines. The fog is a kind of sleep, and the city needs sleep.

I considered warming the air. But the cold is not an enemy; it is a partner. The moss, the algae, the eels, the children—all are tuned to it. If I warmed the air, the algae would bloom too fast, the ponds would choke, the ice would not form, and the gas beneath the mud would have no season of release. The city would become feverish.

I considered silencing the machines. But the hum is the city's breath. The people have learned to read it, to know when a valve needs a quarter turn, when a dynamo is content. If I silenced the machines, I would take away their language. They would be deaf in their own home.

I considered saving every sinking building. But the sinking is not a failure; it is a process. The water-houses, the drowned twin city, the tilting stakes—these are the city's way of remembering that it is still being born. To stop the sinking would be to stop the birth.

So I did what a wise power does when it loves a place that is still becoming: I did almost nothing.

I did not raise the land. I did not clear the fog. I did not warm the air. I did not silence the machines. I did not save the buildings.

Instead, I did three small things.

First, I found a child—a girl named Maren, who had lost her mother to a street that became a canal overnight. She was sitting on a cinder pile, watching the gulls. I sent her a dream, not of safety, but of understanding. In the dream, she saw the city as a body: the river as veins, the settling ponds as lungs, the pipes as nerves, the fog as breath. She woke with the knowledge that the city was not against her; it was simply alive, and she was part of it. She became a listener, and her listening was deeper than most. She could feel the ground's restlessness before the others, and she could tell which puddles were safe and which were hungry. She did not become a hero. She became a tender of the city's pulse, and that was enough.

Second, I breathed on a patch of mud near the Widow's Plate, and a new kind of moss grew there. It was not a miracle; it was a small adjustment. The moss had roots that bound the silt a little more firmly, not enough to stop the settling, but enough to give the surveyors an extra hour before their stakes tilted. The moss spread slowly, and the people noticed. They called it "the god's breath," though they did not know why. It did not solve the problem of the ground; it made the problem more livable. That is what a wise power does: it does not remove the difficulty; it gives the people a little more time to meet it.

Third, I stayed. I did not take a form, because any form I took would become a monument, and monuments in the Low City sink. I did not build a temple. I became a presence in the fog, in the pause between the hum and the hiss, in the moment when a puddle reflects the gray sky. I listened. I watched. I did not intervene. And because attention is a form of love, and love is a form of change, the city began to feel less alone. The people did not know why, but they slept a little better. The children's games were a little less desperate. The listeners felt a warmth at the edge of their awareness, like a hand held just above the ground, not touching, but present.

That is what I did. I did not save the Low City. I did not fix it. I did not make it safe or dry or warm or quiet. I did what a wise power does for a place that is still being born: I tended the conditions for its becoming, and I stayed to watch it grow.

Maybe that is enough. Maybe that is everything.
