**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do**

The benevolent and wise power came to the Hollow Country as a gap in the wind, not as a storm. It had felt the plateau from far off—a low vibration in the world’s bones, a sound like a river that never stopped. It came not to answer the roar but to hear it.

At first the power stood at the edge of the Bellowing Shelf, where the limestone drops into cloud. The sound pressed against it like a hand. It felt the old desire of powers: to speak into the noise and make it orderly. To still the slabs, seal the circular pits, soften the sharp solution blades, turn the green pockets into gardens, make the Hollow Folk safe.

It did not.

A wise power knows that every place has its own way of being whole. The Hollow Country was not broken. It was ancient, turbulent, alive. Its collapses were breaths. Its roaring holes were a voice. The Hollow Folk were not victims of the country; they were the part of the country that listened. In time the power came to understand that the plateau and its people were one being—stone the body, wind the breath, people the senses, the long roar the blood.

So the power entered as a traveler. It wore no metal, because metal would cut the land’s song. It walked barefoot on the wet limestone, feeling the vibration through the soles of its feet. It learned the greeting: a hand on the sternum, then on the rock. *I feel the same vibration.*

A child met it near a green seam. The child looked at the power’s chest, not its face, and tapped a short rhythm on a stone: two beats, a pause, one beat. The power did not understand, but it bowed. The child pointed into the mist, toward a shelter under an overhang, and walked away.

The power lived among them for a season. It did not announce itself. It slept in a low dry-laid shelter with the doorway angled from the wind. It learned to drink from bromeliad cups and to ask the thumbnail frogs’ permission before eating—tapping a stone three times and waiting. It helped re-braid the rope bridge across the chasm, using wooden wedges and no metal. It watched the fog pour over the escarpment like slow liquid. It learned to read the cairns not as monuments but as sound-marks, placed where the land spoke most clearly.

It saw a green pocket slump into a hole after hard rain, leaving a scar of pale stone. The Hollow Folk gathered at the nearest cairn, dismantled it, and rebuilt it in a new alignment. The power wanted to hold the next tower in place, to keep every green seam from falling. But it did not.

In a lull, the Bone-Ear came and sat beside it. The wind dropped. The only sounds were water dripping from moss and the faint call of a frog. The Bone-Ear looked at the power’s hands.

“What would you do for us?” the Bone-Ear asked in the low, chesty speech of lulls.

The power considered. It could say, *I will make you safe.* It could say, *I will make the land stable.* But those were the answers of a power that needed to be needed.

“I would listen,” the power said.

The Bone-Ear nodded. “Then you already understand. The land is not a danger to us. It is our scripture. The roar is not noise. It is the way the world tells us we are still here. If you quieted it, we would become blind. If you made the stone still, we would forget how to feel. If you filled the pits, we would lose the questions we leave on their rims.”

The power understood then. Benevolence without wisdom would be a kind of violence. To make the Hollow Country gentle would be to destroy it. The Hollow Folk’s whole life was built on attention—to the flex of a slab, the pressure of air, the direction of the strongest sound. Their vulnerability was not a flaw; it was their faculty.

So the power did the only thing a wise guest can do. It did not fix the land. It did not end the wind or stop the collapses. It did not offer eternal food or unbreakable shelters. It left no temple, no scripture, no name.

But it was not indifferent. It did one small thing.

On the morning it left, the power chose a stone near one of the standing voices—a wind-shaped spire that hummed in certain gusts. The stone had a fine resonance, a mineral bell. The power held it for a moment, warming it with its own attention, until the stone’s vibration matched the deep note of the plateau’s breath. Then it set the stone on top of a cairn the Hollow Folk had rebuilt the day before.

The stone was not magic in any obvious way. It did not stop avalanches or summon water. It simply hummed a little more clearly than the others when the wind rose. A traveler would not have noticed. But the Hollow Folk did. They placed their palms near it and felt the note. They said, “The land has placed this one here.” And they tapped its rhythm into their memory.

The power left through the western pass, where the fog pours over the edge. It did not turn back. But from that day, it carried the sound of the plateau inside itself, a low note beneath everything.

It did not forget the Hollow Folk. When distant powers spoke of taming the Bellowing Shelf, of building roads or mining the stone, the wise power bent the maps slightly, thickened the clouds at the passes, deepened the roar in the ears of those who came with metal and greed. It did not guard the Hollow Folk by force. It guarded their obscurity. That was the only protection they needed, and the only one they would have accepted.

The Hollow Country continued to breathe, to collapse, to rearrange itself. The frogs called in the lulls. The cairns were rebuilt when the sound changed. The Hollow Folk kept listening, walking from seam to seam across the bare, vibrating stone.

And the benevolent and wise power listened too—not to save, not to change, but to know that somewhere in the world there was still a place where the land spoke, and the people answered by being silent enough to hear it.
