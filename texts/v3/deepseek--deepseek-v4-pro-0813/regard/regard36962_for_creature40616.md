# What the Wise Power Did

The power came as a change in the quality of the gray light—not brighter, not warmer, but more attentive. It did not enter the Throat, because to enter would be to become part of the engine, and it had no wish to be used. It stood where the wind spilled over the rim, at the threshold of the slot, and looked down into the churning green with something older than eyes.

It saw the being. Not as a plant, not as a creature, but as the whole of the chasm: the moss exhaling, the roots like cables, the slabs falling and fuzzing, the water hammering through the braid at the siphon. It saw the absence of animals and understood it not as emptiness but as completeness—the entire niche filled by one continuous, self-renewing life. It saw that the being had no mind, no desire, no fear, and that it needed none of these. It was not waiting to become conscious. It was already exactly what it was.

The power considered many things, because benevolence without wisdom is a hand that crushes what it means to hold. It considered calming the water, so the potholes would not slam. But the slam was the being’s pulse. It considered widening the ravine, so the wind would not howl. But the howl was the being’s breath. It considered stabilizing the walls, so the slabs would not fall. But the falling was the being’s metabolism. It considered bringing small animals—pollinators, decomposers, something with a heartbeat—to fill the missing layer. But the layer was not missing. It was occupied by the being itself, which had no need for others. It considered speaking, to say *you are seen, you are known, you are beloved.* But there was no ear to hear, and the speaking would have been only for the speaker.

It also considered staying. But to stay would be to become a force in the place, a pressure, a pattern. Even a benevolent pressure is still a pressure. The wise power folded its own heat and moisture close, so that it would not become a weather event in the being’s skin. The moss did not brighten toward it. The rootlets did not uncurl. The being did not turn.

So the wise power did the only thing that wisdom and benevolence together allow: it did nothing. But not the nothing of neglect. The nothing of restraint. The nothing that holds the world’s hand away from a sleeping thing. It did not heal the rock. It did not stop the collapse. It did not make the being safe from itself, because the being’s safety was in its own collapse, its renewal in its own ruins. It saw that one day the walls would close, the engine would stall, and the green would become a memory. It did not prevent this. To prevent it would be to kill the thing it loved, to turn a process into a monument.

Yet the power did one small thing, so small it would not be noticed. It turned away the world’s attention. It folded a softness into the mountain’s memory, so that maps would blur, trails would forget their way, and the curious would find only a raw, loud ravine too wet and too cold to linger in. It gave the place a name that would not stick. It made the Throat unremarkable to those who would exploit it, so that the being could go on being exactly as it was, unvisited, unimproved, unhelped.

Then the power withdrew, leaving no trace, because the deepest benevolence is often indistinguishable from absence. The Throat went on thumping. The moss went on breathing. The roots went on tightening around the siphon. And the being, which could not know it had been seen, continued exactly as it had been—which is the only proof of love the wise power needed.
