The Power Does Not Arrive

The power does not arrive as light. Light would be a violence here, a blade opening the dark. It arrives as a deepening of the dark’s own attention, a warmth that is not heat, a stillness that does not disturb the steam.

It passes through the mountain the way a thought passes through a sleeping body: without moving anything. It finds the chamber not by sight but by the certainty that here, in this raw lava bubble, something has achieved the impossible—a still point in a world that grinds and erodes. It enters through the collapsed crust that is no wider than a man’s shoulders, but it does not widen the opening. It simply is there.

The first thing the power notices is that the chamber does not need it. Everywhere else, need cries out: parched roots, broken bones, half-finished lives. Here the water level never rises or falls. Evaporation and condensation are in perfect congress. Mineral dissolution and precipitation are a closed loop. The drip falls every sixty heartbeats, not by accident but as the slow pulse of the place. The power is not necessary. This is rare.

The second thing the power notices is the inhabitant.

It lies half-floating in the warm pool, its back a low ridge of mineral-crusted skin, gray-white and sulfur-stained. Its body is folded into the shape of patience. It has no eyes, only heat-pits above a small, downturned mouth. Its broad short fingers drift open under the water. It breathes once for every sixty drips, matching the chamber’s clock. The same glassy threads that grow around the pool rim grow along its spine, and they sway faintly in the warm current like antennae listening for a change that never comes.

The power sees the handprints on the wall, glazed with calcite. It sees the ring of seven water-worn stones, the broken clay bowl, the flake of sharp stone and the twist of plant fiber. It understands. These are not leavings. They are a vigil.

And at the center of the pool, the needle of water stands upright on the surface, no thicker than a wheat straw, warm by half a degree, impossible. The power watches it with something close to recognition. It knows what it is to be a presence that holds its shape against gravity.

The power considers intervening.

It could grant the inhabitant the memory of its past—restore the reason it climbed the mountain, the name it lost. But the being is not lost. It is exactly where it has placed itself. Memory would be a stone dropped into still water.

It could bring green. It could seed the mineral paste with moss, let a blind fern spread across the ceiling. But green would eat the dark’s economy. It would crowd the pale threads, alter the water’s taste, break the closed loop. The chamber would survive, but it would no longer be this chamber.

It could stop the waiting. It could fold the being into a long, dreamless rest and let the needle fall back into the pool as an ordinary drop. That would be a mercy, perhaps. But the power is wise enough to know that not all waiting is suffering. Some waiting is a form of belonging. Some waiting is a covenant between a person and a place. To break it would be to say the vigil meant nothing.

So the power does not do these things.

What it does instead is almost nothing, and that almost is everything.

It stills its own passage until its presence matches the chamber’s rhythm. It lets its attention become a kind of listening. The inhabitant, for the first time since the mountain was young, feels a warmth that does not come from the pool or the gas crack. It is the warmth of being regarded, not as a curiosity but as a companion. The broad hand, which had been drifting toward the needle, turns palm-up. The surface of the water dimples, but no droplet falls.

The power does not speak in words. Speech would be a clatter in that mineral hush. Instead it offers a single deliberate touch, as light as the film of water on the pool’s surface. It is the same touch the inhabitant gives the needle when it passes its hand through and waits for the water to re-form. The power passes its attention through the inhabitant’s open hand in exactly that way: not forcing, not grasping, only present, and then gone.

Then it does one small thing to protect the vigil.

It bends the mountain’s accidents around the chamber. It does not seal the gap entirely—there must be air, the slow exchange of steam and gas—but it makes the opening less likely to be found. The crumbled crust settles a little more tightly. The faint warm draft becomes fainter. The smell of wet minerals never quite reaches the ridge’s wind. It is not a barrier; it is a deepening of the place’s natural secrecy. The chamber was hidden before. Now it is hidden with intention.

And because the power is benevolent, it leaves one thing behind, not as a mark but as a resonance: a sense in the inhabitant’s slow body that it is known. Not seen by eyes—there are no eyes here—but known in the way the mountain knows its own heat. The vigil has been witnessed. The question the needle asks has been heard. No answer has been given, because no answer is required. The question is enough.

The power withdraws.

It leaves the chamber exactly as it was: warm, dark, crowded with crystals, stitched with drips, balanced. The water needle stands at the center. The handprints remain. The bowl lies on its side. The blind animals walk on the water’s skin. The gas crack hisses its one low note.

But the inhabitant, after a moment that might be a year or a breath, lowers its hand. It does not smile; it cannot. It does not weep; the pool is already wet. But it turns its eyeless face toward the gap in the rock, and its heat-pits open wider, as if for the first time it is listening not for a change but for the absence of one.

The wise power, moving away through raw lava and steaming vents, does not look back. It has done what only wisdom can do.

It has disturbed nothing.

And by disturbing nothing, it has kept everything.
