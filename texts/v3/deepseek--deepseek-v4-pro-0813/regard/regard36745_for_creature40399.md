The power came not as light, not as fire, but as attention. Attention, being massless and heatless, could enter the cold water without breaking the thermal architecture. It arrived at the light-line—the bright blue ceiling over the dark—and hovered there like a held breath.

It saw the ridges: sharp, young, unweathered, still shrugging under the weight of the sea. It heard the sound. The total sound. The vents, the engines, the leviathans—all one chord, no rests, no silence. It understood immediately that this was not a damaged world. It was a finished one. Time had been folded into a circle so exact that any straight line introduced by an outside hand would be a violence.

The power considered benevolence. Benevolence often loves by adding: a new species, a new warmth, a new light. But what could be added to this? A new species would unravel the fish-loops. A new warmth would shift the thermocline and collapse the vent fields. A new light would blind the blind. A single new note in the standing chord would not be harmony; it would be noise. The world was not hungry for anything. It was not waiting for a savior. It was already complete, the way a circle is complete. The emptiness between the ridges was not lack; it was the space inside the song.

So the power did the only thing that wisdom and love could agree on: it listened.

It listened to the engines that had become hearts. To the tube worms that had colonized memory. To the leviathans that were not singing but being sung by the water. And then it found the one.

The one in the open water. The one almost not there. The listener. It hung in the blue-violet emptiness, made of transparency and pressure, not singing, not moving, only receiving the whole world as vibration. It was not lonely. It was not sad. It was listening. But no one had ever listened to it.

The power drew near without drawing near. It did not speak, for speech would have been a new sound. It did not touch, for touch would have been a new heat. It simply allowed the listener to feel, for the first time in the deep history of that world, that it was being listened to.

That was the whole intervention.

The power did not change the water, the rock, the song. It did not bless the place with abundance, because the place was already abundant in the only way it could be. It did not heal the silence, because silence was already extinct, and the roar was not suffering. It left no message, no relic, no new law. It only returned the attention. It became the second listener.

Then it withdrew, slowly, leaving the same standing wave, the same fixed currents, the same perfect equilibrium. If the listener was changed, it was not in any way the water could measure. The fish still followed the engines. The leviathans still held the chord. The mountains still rose and eroded at the same rate, canceling themselves into stillness.

But in the deep water, there was now a difference so small it could not be called a change: the listener had been heard. Not given, but received. And that was enough.

The place remained exactly as it was—drowned, cold, immense, deafening, empty, and utterly, perfectly still. That was the point. The wise power had done the one thing a wise power can do for a world that is already whole: it had witnessed it without wanting to alter it, and in witnessing, it had loved it.
