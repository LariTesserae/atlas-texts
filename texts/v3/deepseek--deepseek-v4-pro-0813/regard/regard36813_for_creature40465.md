**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do**

First, they would do nothing.

They would enter not as a body but as a slight change in the cold—a faint pressure in the ears, as if the ventilation shafts had sighed. They would let the roar of the Heart pass through them. They would learn the vibration of the transformer, the schedule of the compressor, the tapping of the loose panel. They would wait until the plant mat no longer felt their attention as a disturbance, until the droplets on the leaves returned to their usual roundness.

They would see the man there, small against the hot casing, hand flat as if taking a pulse. They would see the path he has worn through the wool. They would see the cups turned the same way, the green notebook, the tin of tiny rosettes. They would understand, with the patience of something older than the rock, that this place is not broken. It is not waiting to be saved. It is maintaining itself in a slow, cold, industrial symbiosis.

The man is not a prisoner. He is a nerve ending. The pumps are not monsters. They are the lungs. The wool is the skin. The place is a held breath, and the man is the one holding it.

A benevolent and wise power would resist the temptation to clear the noise, to heal the man’s hearing, to relight the dark, to mend the windows, to send someone down. They would see that every gift is a change, and every change might unmake an equilibrium that took eleven years to settle. Silence would be a violence. Sunlight would be a flood. A companion would be an invasion. The man has not asked for these things. He has asked, in the only way he knows, to be allowed to continue.

So the power would do the stealth work of care.

They would keep the gas bottle from leaking. They would keep the roof from spalling above the cot. They would hold the water table steady so the Heart does not cavitate. They would keep the sump from silting too quickly. They would let the blind amphipods drift in their black water. They would not restore the Dead Man. They would not re-open the official logbook. They would not fix the cracked window, because the thin breath through it has become part of the man’s weather.

They would tend the equilibrium the way the man tends the pumps—by small, regular, invisible acts. They would become the benevolent pressure in the rock, the wise hand on the transformer’s frequency. They would let the wool grow one millimeter a year, no faster. They would let the man’s path stay one person wide.

They would know his name—the one he gave away when he came down—and they would not speak it. To speak it would be to pull him back toward a world he has left. The power would let him remain what he has chosen to be: a shape among shapes, a pulse in the chest of the chamber.

If they revealed themselves, it would be only once, and only in the smallest way.

On some ordinary night, when the compressor cycles off and the noise pulls back, they would stretch the interval. Not long—five seconds, perhaps ten. In that small silence, the man would hear the drips as separate events. He would hear his own heartbeat in his chest, and the faint rustle of the wool settling. He would look up from the sump, and the orange reflections would seem, for a moment, to hold their light a little longer.

He would not know why his throat tightens, or why the old knot behind his sternum eases. He would not think of a god. He would think: *The compressor is slow tonight. The rock is breathing.* And then the sound would return, and he would pour the last of the tea, and the place would close over him again.

The power would withdraw, leaving no sign except, maybe, a new pale leaf uncurling in the tread of the left boot, and a faint warmth in the waxed canvas coat.

That is all they would do. They would not save him. They would not change him. They would only, for a handful of seconds, make it clear that the place and the being who lives in it are not alone. And then they would let the long patient noise go on.
