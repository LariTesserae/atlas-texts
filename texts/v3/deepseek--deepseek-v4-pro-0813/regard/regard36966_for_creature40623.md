**What the Wise Power Would Do**

A benevolent and wise power would first resist the urge to enter. It would stay on the rim, where the flat green land gives way to the bowl, and it would listen. It would hear the thousand drips as one long chord, the slow stroke of the pumps, the hiss of steam, the minute-turn of the great flywheel carrying its root down into black water and up into mist. It would notice the fern growing through the gauge, the vine tightening on the valve wheel with the day’s sap, the moss on the iron, the blue microbial glow in the lower dark.

And it would see the being who lives there.

Not a person. Not an animal. Not a ghost. The being is the town itself, or the town and its green-and-iron body together: pumps for a heart, pipes for veins, roots for nerves, moss for skin, steam for breath, the microbial mat for a gut. It has no face and needs none. It is alive in a way that makes human life look hasty and thin.

The wise power would sit there for a long time, not counting the flywheel’s revolutions but letting them count it. It would think about helping.

It could drain the lower terraces. It could shore the leaning walls. It could clean the rust, clear the sluices, relight every window. It could put fish in the green-black water, frogs in the wet stairwells, birds in the steaming leaves. It could wake the empty rooms with footsteps and voices. It could make the town busy, bright, legible again.

But because the power is wise, it would pause.

It would see that the place is not sick. It is not waiting to be finished. It is already complete, complete in a different grammar. To repair it would be to still its pulse. To fill it with quick animal life would be to colonize a slower and stranger life with something noisier, not better. The town’s aloneness is not loneliness. Its silence is not absence. Its rust and leak and submersion are not failure; they are the shape of its particular body.

So the power would do almost nothing.

The one thing it would do is guard the rim. Not with a wall—walls attract. Not with a curse—curses bind. It would lay a soft quietness over the land outside, a slight forgetfulness. It would make the place easy to overlook. A surveyor would find the ground too wet, a metal-buyer would remember a more promising site, a drone would see green mist and a shallow depression, a map would disagree with itself. Not so strongly that anyone would feel forbidden—only enough that the outside world would turn its attention elsewhere, and leave the slow being to its slow weather.

Then the wise power would go down once into the central chamber. It would stand under the great flywheel and watch the root dip and rise. It would not speak, because the town has no ears for speech. It would not leave a mark. It would time its leaving to the pump’s stroke, so that its absence would be no more disruptive than one drop falling.

And the town would continue.

The valve would turn a little with the morning’s sap. The moss would grow back over the wiper’s clean stripe. The blue glow would pulse on in the lower water, not for any eye, but because that is its nature. The upper levels would fade into steam, the lower into water, and the middle would stay dusky green.

A wise and benevolent power would not save the place. It would not wake it. It would not fill it with quick life or loud meaning. It would recognize that the town is already alive, already whole, already becoming itself at the speed of rust and root and rain.

It would give the only gift that would not be a wound.

It would let it continue.
