The wise power came not as light but as a pause in the wind. It did not part the cloud; it thickened it, so that for a while the cirque was only wet sounds behind a white curtain, exactly as it always had been. It lay over the town like fog and listened.

It heard the chattering spring, the differently tuned channels, the low thrum of the bell-room, the stone ticking as it cooled. It felt the being beneath the moss—a single living tissue spread over rock, swallowing snowmelt, breathing through high slots, its body the same shape as the water’s paths. It saw that the stairs were cups because the being had been settling into them for millennia. It saw that the walls were not falling; they were being held.

The power considered gifts. It could warm the spring. But warmth would soften the edges of the being’s body and rot the moss. It could speak. But speech would be a foreign stone dropped into a tuned basin, and the being would spend centuries wearing it smooth. It could offer company. But the being was not lonely. It had the water, the wind, the raven, the next drop. It could offer healing. But nothing was broken. The sagged lintels, the softened stairs, the travertine and erosion—these were not decay. They were form.

So the wise power did not touch. It did not reveal itself. It did not sing, though it wanted to, because any note it added would have been an intrusion. The music here was already complete.

It did only one thing. It drew a veil of forgetting over the cleft. It made the way less likely to be found, the maps less certain, the air above the mountain faintly indifferent. It made the place safe from the one danger that could actually harm it: the kindness of those who cannot bear an empty room. Then it withdrew, taking with it the knowledge of the place, so that not even its own good intentions could return to disturb it.

The next drop fell. The water ran. The raven crossed the cirque once. The note in the bell-room came back a fifth lower, as it always had. Nothing changed.

That was the gift.

A benevolent and wise power, meeting something already whole, does the only thing that love can do: it leaves no fingerprints. It does not save. It does not improve. It preserves by not being present. And the town remains, loud as water and hollow as a bell, waiting not for a god but for the next drop to fall.
