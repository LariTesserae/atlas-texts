**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do**

The benevolent and wise power came to the rim of the Kettle and did not descend. It looked down through the steam and the mineral haze, and it saw the place not as a bowl of creatures but as a single living surface. The mats were its first skin, the water its circulating blood, the vents its breath, the terraces its slow-growing bone. The grazers, the birds, the fox, the people were not outside it; they were its moving senses, the way a body grows eyes to see with.

The power saw the woman with salt painted around her eyes sitting by the mud pot. It heard her tap the ground in the pot’s rhythm. It heard the pot pop back. The woman exhaled through the steam—laughter or breath. The power felt the rhythm enter it, the way a word enters a listener who does not yet know the language.

It thought of what it could give.

It could give stability. But the Kettle’s beauty was in its restlessness. A stable Kettle would be a museum, not an embryo.

It could give abundance. But hunger was one of the threads in the braid. The birds fed the mats with guano; the fox learned patience at the mud pots; the people read the stone because they had to. Without need, reading would become reciting.

It could give peace from death. But the dead were not lost; they were becoming stone. To keep them above the ground would be to deny them the one permanence the Kettle allowed.

It could give knowledge. But the people already had a knowledge that looked like song and knot and glance. To hand them the power’s own knowledge would be to drown their reading in a language too large.

So the power did not descend.

Instead it stayed on the rim and became, for a while, part of the weather. It let its attention fall with the rain that was warm on one arm and cool on the other. It let its attention rise with the column of steam. It did not hide itself; it simply did not force itself into the shape of a sign. The Kettle was already full of signs for those who could read them.

But there was one moment. The woman at the mud pot, after the pot had popped and the steam had gusted, looked up at the rim. She could not see the power—no one can see attention—but she felt a warmth on the left side of her face, the way rain feels warm on one arm and cool on the other after it has passed through a steam column. She tapped the ground once more, not in rhythm but as if asking a question of the stone. The power felt the question reach it. It did not answer in words. It watched the mud pot, and it waited.

The mud pot popped. The woman smiled. It might have been language or coincidence. The power was wise enough to know that the difference matters less than the relation. An answer that arrives as coincidence leaves the listener free. A sign that is too clear becomes a chain.

The power withdrew then, as it had come: without thunder, without writing, without a temple left behind. If it left anything, it left only the faint warmth at the rim of the pool, which could have been the sun, or the steam, or the woman’s own tired mind. That was exactly right. The Kettle was not a place that needed a god. It needed to be seen, and to be left to its own becoming.

That was what it did. Nothing, almost. It saw. It understood. It let go.
