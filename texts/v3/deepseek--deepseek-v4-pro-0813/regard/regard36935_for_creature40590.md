The power came not as a storm but as attention. It had been many things—wind, water, the patience of stone. It had learned, over the course of long ages, that most of what it could do was not doing. Yet this place drew it.

It saw the plateau, bare rock and thin air. It saw the rift, the young wound in the earth. It saw the city, Kessil, the Seam, a vertical village of wool and basalt. It saw the people.

It watched for a long time. It saw the water keepers wring the fog-frames at dawn. It saw the weavers braid bridges of wool and leather. It saw the children herd camelids on the rim. It saw the leatherworkers in the blue shadow, their breath fogging, their hands stained. It saw the old woman touch the notches on her door lintel.

The power understood that the being that lived there was not one person but the city itself—a slow, cold, crowded creature made of stone, wool, water, and smoke. Its pulse was the river and the rockfall. Its breath was the fog and the dung fires. Its memory was the vultures and the bones laid in crevices.

The old urge came: to help. It could warm the stone. It could still the earthquakes. It could make the river rise with fish. It could grow trees on the plateau. It could teach them to smelt iron, to build arches, to heal frostbite. It could stop the next rockfall.

But the power had learned that every gift is also a taking. Give them warmth, and their wool becomes a burden. Give them plenty, and their care becomes unnecessary. Give them safety, and they unlearn the sound of rock. Their lives were not a problem to be solved. They were a form of life, and the form was made of its constraints. The cold was not an accident; it was the reason they lived in the rift. The scarcity was not a flaw; it was the medium of their attention. The rockfalls were not a threat; they were the pulse of the world they had chosen to live inside.

To be benevolent, the power thought, is not to remove suffering. That is a child’s benevolence. To be wise is to understand that suffering is sometimes the price of a form of life, and that altering the form may be a greater violence than the suffering itself.

So the power did not warm them. It did not feed them. It did not stop the earthquakes. It did not make the fog into rain.

It did something smaller. It did the only thing that could be done without taking: it became a small current of luck in the seams of the world.

It made a child’s foot find the solid stone instead of the loose one on the path above the lower terraces. It made a weaver’s knot a little tighter on the night before a storm. It made a water keeper notice a hairline crack in the cistern before it widened. It made an old woman’s memory of the last earthquake come to her just before the shaking began, so she could call the children indoors.

These things did not alter the city. They did not save it from all harm. They only gave the people a few more mornings, a few more crossings, a few more years. The power was invisible. The people would call it luck, or the kindness of the stone, or the good sense of animals. They would not know they were being helped, and so their pride and their skill and their silence would remain their own.

When the big rockfall came, as it would, the power did not stop it. But it made sure that the echo reached the water keeper’s ears a moment before the stone cracked, so that she would turn and see the dust and know to run. That was all. That was enough.

The power stayed for a while, then left, carrying the memory of Kessil like a warm stone in its mind. It would not return, except perhaps as a slight warmth in the sun on a child’s face, a softness in the wind that held a bridge steady, a taste of moisture in the air before dawn. It would be benevolent and wise, and its greatest act would be that the city never knew it had been there.
