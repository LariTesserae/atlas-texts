The power does not arrive. It has been arriving for a long time, the way the cloud arrives: not coming from somewhere, but condensing.

It watches the plateau for what might be a season or a century. It does not count; counting would be a kind of pressure, and its first act is the discipline of not pressing. It sees the land as it is—young stone, old patience, moss filling the gaps between human intention and geological time. It sees the inhabitant moving along the railway, pressing moss into mesh, wiping glass circles with the tenderness of one who has stopped distinguishing between maintenance and prayer. The power is careful not to mistake stillness for emptiness.

It begins with water.

Not adding water—the plateau already has what it needs. Sentiment would be to bring more; wisdom is to notice what is already flowing. But it can do something subtle, something that might take years to become visible: it can bless the seep. The thread of water from the pipe into the stone basin. It can cause the mineral precipitate to arrange itself just differently enough that, over the next few seasons, the basin deepens by half a hand-span. Enough for the water to pool instead of pass. Enough for the cold to be held a little longer before it sinks into the cracked stone.

It does not tell the inhabitant. The inhabitant discovers it. One morning, after the cloud thins, the figure kneels by the basin and sees the water has grown still at its center, catching the white glare. It touches the scalloped rim. The scallops are smoother, older somehow, as though the basin has aged forward. The inhabitant makes a sound, low, not quite a word. The power, listening, feels it as the first note of a conversation it will never interrupt.

Next, it turns to the flywheel.

Here the power is even more cautious. The flywheel is already perfect—moss-covered, turning so slowly it might be a planet deciding to rotate. To accelerate it would be cruelty. To clean it would be vandalism. But the power can do something: it can smooth the path. It can cause the ancient lubricant in the bearing to distribute itself once more, to find the places where friction has begun to accumulate, to ease them. Not repairing, but remembering. The flywheel's motion does not quicken; it deepens. The inhabitant sits by the shed and watches, and feels the change not as difference but as rightness—as if the machine has exhaled a breath it had been holding for decades.

The power lingers by the fog-catchers. The mesh is brittle, the moss has colonized the frames. A lesser power might restore them. A wise one understands that the fog-catchers have fulfilled their purpose and moved into a second life. The moss does not need the mesh; the mesh now serves the moss. The power does nothing. It observes. It notes the way each frame holds water differently, the way the wind has taught each one its own shape. This is good. Let it be.

It visits the inhabitant in the concrete house. The door is swollen shut; the power does not need to enter. It stands outside and regards the absence of light within, the narrow bunk against the geothermal pipe, the small dish gathering condensation. The inhabitant is asleep or still or somewhere between. The power has known many beings—gods and machines, animals and people—but rarely one so wholly given to a place without being diminished by it. It feels something like respect. It does not disturb.

It finds the cableway pylon leaning a few degrees. The cables are wrapped in rime. The power considers. This is the only place where danger lives—a slow, patient danger, the kind that comes from a structure deciding over decades whether to fall. The power does not straighten the pylon. That would break the quiet compromise the pylon has made with the rock and the cold. Instead, it does something very small: it strengthens the roots of the ferns at the pylon's base. Not to hold the pylon—the ferns could never hold it—but to signal to the stone below that it may spread, that the foundation may settle, that the next century may proceed without fracture. The pylon will lean. It will lean a little more. Then it will stop, and moss will cover the new angle, and after another hundred years no one will remember it was ever straight.

The power sees the geothermal wellheads. The gauges show pressure; the pipes are cold with moving water. This is the plateau's heart, or one of its hearts. The power lays no hand on it. To touch would be to leave a signature, and the plateau is already signed by every fog that has ever condensed here. The power does not need to be known.

And the inhabitant? The power watches. The figure moves along the railway, tends the fog-catchers, watches the flywheel, sits by the basin. It leaves its pressed oval in the moss each day. The power observes the shape of that oval, the way it records the inhabitant's presence without harming the moss, the way the moss springs back. It sees the battered hands, the limestone face, the eyes like noon cloud. It sees the loneliness that is not loneliness—that is something else, something the power must be careful not to misread.

The power considers: What can be given to a being that needs nothing?

The answer is: the same thing the inhabitant gives to the machines. Attention. A hand placed on the moss to settle it. Not to change, but to confirm. The power cannot touch the inhabitant without risk; so it chooses a moment when the figure stands at the rim, watching its shadow on the cloud below, ringed with that small rainbow. For one second, the power stands behind it, unseen, and adds its own shadow to the cloud. The ring of color widens. The inhabitant sees it—the shadow doubled, two figures on the fog, haloed. It does not turn. It raises one arm, slower than it has ever raised it before, and on the cloud below, both shadows raise their arms in a movement that might be greeting, might be farewell, might be acknowledgment that some places are watched not over but alongside.

Then the cloud closes, and the power is gone, or has always been gone, or has never left. The inhabitant stands a moment longer. Then it descends into the hollow, where the basin holds the water a little longer than it used to, and drinks.

That night, the power does not bring rain. It brings only the temperature of the cloud, a half-degree warmer, enough that the moss on the inhabitant's shoulders will not freeze, enough that the bunk against the geothermal pipe will be not warm but less cold. A mercy so small it might be mistaken for weather. That is the wisdom.

In the morning, the inhabitant rises and finds, by the dish that gathers condensation, a single stone. It is not from the plateau. It is smooth as a worn heart, the color of the cloud at mid-afternoon, and warm—not from any heat, but warm the way moss is warm, the way stone holds the memory of sunlight long after the sun has gone. The inhabitant does not pick it up immediately. It sits on the edge of the bunk and regards the stone the way it regards the flywheel: as something turning, slowly, patient, alive. Then it reaches for it.

The stone is cooler now. It fits the palm.

The inhabitant carries the stone to the stone basin. For a long time it stands there, turning the stone in its hands. Then it places the stone in the water, where the basin has deepened, and the water covers it, and the mineral crust begins, slowly, to gather at its edges.

This is what the benevolent and wise power does: it stays unseen. It blesses the seep, eases the bearing, warms the cloud. It adds a stone that does not belong, so the one who belongs will have something to give to the water. And then it does nothing more. The rest is not its work. The rest has always been the work of the place itself.

---

If, years later, a traveler climbs the plateau and finds the basin, they will see the stone. It will be half-covered in mineral precipitate, grey-white as the inhabitant's eyes, smooth but no longer foreign. The water will be cold and clear, and the ferns will form their small room. The traveler might drink. They might not.

Before they descend, they might notice that the pressed oval by the basin is slightly larger now, as though two figures had once sat there together, or as though one figure had sat there long enough to become two.

But the mist will close in before they can be certain. The mist has always known how to keep this place's secrets. The mist, and the moss, and the silence, and the stone.

That is all.
