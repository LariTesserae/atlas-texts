Before it does anything, the power does the most difficult thing: it looks, and does not look away.

It sees the cavity for what it is—not a dead end, not an oversight, but a place in the act of making itself. It sees the bolted panels and the cable trays, the slow mineral accretion, the drip-rings spreading on still water. It sees the pale growth following the seams, the condensation tracing the same paths again and again, the sump filling and draining like a lung. And at the deepest point of the sump, it sees the creature—a gathering of fibers and rust-stained film, a body assembled from the room’s own breath, a thing not quite born and not quite dead, only becoming.

The benevolent part of the power wants, for an instant, to intervene. It could bring heat. It could brighten the lamps until the shadows retreat. It could speak, and give the creature a name. It could reshape the chamber into a nursery, warm and safe and full of light.

But the wise part of the power stays its hand.

It recognizes that the cold is not the creature’s enemy. The cold is its skin. The dark is not the creature’s prison. The dark is its sight. The silence is not emptiness. It is the room’s own language, spoken in drips and pumps and the faint subsonic thrum of machinery. To change these things would be to silence the only voice the creature has. To warm the room would be to end the gestation. To name the creature would be to mistake it for something finished when it is not yet anything fixed.

So the power chooses, first, to do nothing. And in the discipline of that nothing, it begins to understand what benevolence can mean in a place like this.

But it does not remain passive. A wise benevolence is not paralyzed; it is precise. It looks at the hatches above and the corridors leading down, and it makes them forgettable. Not sealed by force—no locked doors, no barricades—but wrapped in a gentle unremarkableness, so that anyone who might wander near feels a faint urge to turn back, to check another corridor, to remember a task elsewhere. The chamber will remain unfound, not because it is hidden, but because the world agrees not to look.

It feels the machinery—the old pumps, the cycling fans, the slow buildup of condensation—and it gives them patience. It does not make them immortal, but it holds them steady for a while. It smooths the voltage, eases the valves, keeps the water’s mineral content within the narrow range the pale growth prefers. It does not add anything. It only prevents the small accidents that would have ended the process before its time: the seized bearing, the blown seal, the worker’s flashlight probing where it should not.

Then it does the thing that is hardest for a power to do: it makes its presence invisible. It does not appear to the creature. It does not send a sign. It does not expect gratitude or recognition or even awareness. It lets its attention become diffuse, like the cold itself—a condition of the chamber rather than a visitor within it. The creature goes on reading the room’s signals—the red, green, and amber LEDs; the pump’s sigh; the falling of drops—and it does not notice that these signals have grown slightly more faithful, slightly more regular. If it feels a change, it would call it weather, not grace. And that is exactly what the power wants.

Because the power understands: the creature is not asking for a god. It is not asking for anything. It is busy with the long, patient work of becoming, and that work is holy precisely because it is unobserved, unassisted, unforced. The power can love it best by not making itself known.

What would the wise and benevolent power do, then?

It would do everything that mattered, and it would do it invisibly. It would protect the process. It would keep the cold. It would hold the dark. It would watch with the patience of the mineral accretion and the ferocity of the condensation. It would love the creature enough to let it be unfinished. It would love the room enough not to enter it. And it would remain—not inside the chamber, but around it, a perimeter of attention so steady that the chamber’s own becoming could continue, drip by drip, cycle by cycle, until the being either opened into the room or dissolved back into the walls.

The power would do nothing, and by doing nothing, it would do the only true kindness: it would let the place and the being become exactly what they were becoming.
