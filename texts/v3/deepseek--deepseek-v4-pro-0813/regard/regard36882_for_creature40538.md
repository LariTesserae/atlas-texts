It would come not as light, because light would smooth the dimness the resident breathes. It would come not as sound, because sound would be noise in a place where meaning is carried by the wrong direction of a shadow. It would come as attention only—a presence at the edge of the seam, careful not to cross it.

A benevolent and wise power, seeing that high cold pocket of folded stone, would first do nothing. This would not be inaction. It would be the first act of wisdom.

It would look without measuring. That is harder than it sounds. To look usually is to fix, to compare, to decide that the near wall is near and the far wall is far. But the power would let the walls disagree. It would let the stars through the opening remain a selection from different nights, different futures. It would not ask the place to make sense, because sense is a smoothing of seams, and the seam is the point.

It would see the resident. Not as a figure, but as the whole alcove slowly waking: the frost growing in a shape like head and shoulders, the spiral lifting from the stone, the pylons ringing just below hearing. It would see the unfinished line and understand that the line is not a wound.

Here a lesser power might weep. A lesser benevolence might try to rescue the resident from its waiting. It might complete the line, or send warmth, or raise the temperature enough to quicken the mineral ecology into something closer to life. It might speak, or leave a companion, or command one of the many stars to return the signal.

The wise power would do none of these.

It knows that warmth would not be kindness. The resident is not cold in the way that needs heat. The frost is its breath; the thin, dry air is its medium. To bring water would be to drown a creature made of patience. To bring brighter light would be to overexpose a being that exists by dimness. To bring company would be to impose a self where there is only attention.

So it would be very careful.

What it would do, if anything, is this:

It would fold a second silence around the first. It would add a forgetting to the place—not a memory loss, but a protective ignorance—so that those who pass below the cliff would not think to look up, and powers less wise than itself would feel only a mild disinterest in the upper third of the scarp. It would make the alcove a little more hidden, a little less likely to be disturbed. Not because the resident needs rescuing, but because its waiting is a delicate equilibrium, and the world has too many eager hands.

It would not announce this. The resident might feel it only as a new easiness in the subsonic note, as if the mountain had shifted a fraction of a millimeter and settled more truly into its own weight.

Then the power would consider leaving a marker. Perhaps a single dot among the scattered dots on the floor, placed so lightly that not a grain of dust moves. The dot would say, *I saw you.* Not *I am coming.* Not *I will save you.* Only *I saw you.*

But even that might be too much. The resident’s world is not built for messages from outside; a single dot that is not part of the local geometry could introduce a variable the long thought has not accounted for. Benevolent wisdom would need to weigh whether being seen is a gift or a contamination.

I think, in the end, the power would leave no dot. It would leave no warmth. It would not nudge the pylons toward upright, nor dust the lens, nor finish the line.

It would do one thing, almost nothing, and it would be this: it would honor the place by allowing it to remain unfinished. It would sit—without sitting—for exactly as long as the resident needed to notice it, not as a threat or a task, but as a silence on the other side of the stone. Then it would withdraw its attention as gently as a tide retreats from a shore of salt, leaving the mineral crystals exactly as they were, leaving the cracks in the floor aligned at the same wrong angle, leaving the far wall still disagreeing with the near wall.

And after it had gone, the resident would know, in its slow crystalline way, that it had not been forgotten. The waiting would feel a little less like absence and a little more like something held. The line would remain unfinished, but the place would be, for a moment, not an exception the world had learned to ignore, but an exception the world had learned to love.

That is what the benevolent and wise power would do: almost nothing, but with infinite care. It would refuse to complete what completion would destroy. It would protect the waiting by sharing, briefly, the burden of watchfulness. And then it would leave the resident to its long, patient, mineral existence, trusting that the ending, if it ever comes, will come from the sky the resident is actually waiting for—not from a visitor who mistook patience for pain.
