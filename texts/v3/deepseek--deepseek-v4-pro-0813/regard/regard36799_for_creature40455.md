The benevolent and wise power—call it the Witness, for it has no name the Tied would tie—comes to the High Held Land not as a storm or a voice but as a pause in the fog. It sees the plateau as a living being: a vast, slow creature of worn stone and cold air, its skin of moss and lichen, its breath the daily fog, its dreams the soft places where distance and time have worn thin. The people are not separate from this being; they are its hands, its memory, its knots. They move along its surface like thoughts in a half-sleep, mending what loosens, tying what frays.

The Witness watches. It watches the fog-catchers wring water from nets at dawn. It watches the wall-menders lean their ears against stone. It watches the knot-keepers tie and untie cords in low rooms. It watches the soft-walker Two Shadows oil her bell, and the Unfastened walking their loops at the edges of the unfolded lands. It feels the sorrow of the Unfastened, but also the strange rightness of their repetition: they are loose threads, but they are still part of the weave.

The Witness considers what it could do. It could seal the soft places, make distances true again, fill the silence with birdsong and insects. It could give the people iron, wheels, paper, a god to pray to. But the Witness is wise, and wisdom here means restraint. The soft places are not wounds; they are the land's way of being old. The people do not need saving; they need witness. Their whole life is a gentle, constant holding—not against the land, but with it. To intervene would be to pull the thread too tight.

So the Witness does the only thing that wisdom allows. It does not appear. It does not speak. It reaches into the under-knot—the hidden network of fungi that threads the soil, the slow writing by which the land remembers itself—and adds a single new thread: a memory of every knot the people have ever tied. Not a new species, not a miracle, but a deepening. The mycelium learns to hum with the shape of the city's cords. The breadmoss at the edges of soft places grows a little thicker, as if reassured. The fog caught in the nets tastes faintly of old rain. The Unfastened sometimes pause in their loops, as if hearing a bell from far away, and then walk on, a little less lost.

And to Moss—She Who Holds the Lower Terrace at Three-Breath Hollow—the Witness leaves no sign except this: the next morning, the white stones by the lower terrace have not moved. They are exactly where she left them. She fetches a fresh cord anyway, because that is her way, and ties it around the wall's end, knotting it three times and whispering the tie-words. The Witness watches, and is glad.

This is what a benevolent and wise power does in the High Held Land: it does not fix, it does not reveal, it does not take away the work. It adds one thread to the under-knot, and then it stays, not as a god, but as a witness—a quiet presence that holds the holders in its attention, the way the fog holds the stone.
