# The Gift of No Change

The benevolent and wise power did not arrive as light. That was the first wisdom. The city already had light enough—sepia, heavy, rising from the cloud-lid like the glow of a furnace seen through milk—and another light would have been an intrusion. The power arrived as attention: a slow, weightless attention that settled over the upper terraces like a change in air pressure too small for any gauge to notice.

The city did not notice. The pumps kept their humming. The steam pipes knocked on their old schedule. In the hot drainage channels, the pink-white film of microbes released its fine chain of bubbles, unalarmed. That was the second wisdom: the power did not expect to be noticed.

It saw, first, what anyone might see: the dark basalt terraces stepping down into haze, the wet streets, the riveted steel towers rustless under the constant film of moisture, the empty plazas, the patient amber signal lamps. It saw the city as a city, built for hands and voices, now with neither. But it did not call it a ruin, because a ruin is a thing that has stopped. The city had not stopped.

The Power watched for a while—though time there was not a line but a slow circulatory loop, like the water that rose as steam and fell as rain and ran back through channels to be heated again. It saw the machines tending their machines, the trams running empty on their routes, the valve-opening in waves like gills. It saw the walls weeping, and it understood that the weeping was not sorrow but breath. The city was not waiting for something to return. It had become its own inhabitant.

Then the Power looked closer, into the places where life had adapted to heat and mineral. It saw the translucent mites moving over fans of sulfur and travertine, too slow to have names. It saw the blind channel-fish drifting in the hot runnels, their spines visible through their skin. It saw the pale efts in the cisterns, gills spread like ferns, almost motionless for weeks. It saw the film that fed on sulfur and heat, the base of everything, older than any stone building. These were not survivors in a dead world; they were the city's ecosystem, its blood and flora. The Power understood that the city was less a place with life in it than a single living body, made of stone, steel, microbes, water, and fire. The being who lived there was the city itself, though it did not know itself as a being. The Power did not need it to know. It was enough to know on its behalf.

Then came the temptation. The Power had seen many worlds. It had fixed broken orbits, coaxed dying stars, breathed life into cold water. It knew it could do something here. It could part the cloud-lid and let in the sun. It could cool the streets to a temperature where green things might grow. It could call back the people who had built the city, or build new ones, and fill the plazas with voices. It could, if it wished, speak to the city itself—give it a mind, a name, a desire.

It considered each of these.

The sun would be cruel. The city ran on gradient: hot below, cool above. Direct sun would scorch the microbial film, dry the weeping walls, overheat the cisterns. Green life would come, yes, but it would choke the mineral fans and starve the salt-mites. The city would stop sweating. It might die.

The people would be crueler. The city no longer needed them. To return them would be to make them ghosts in their own machines, or to make the machines slaves again. The emptiness was not a lack; it was an achieved state, like the hollowness of a bone. The Power saw no wound in the empty streets. It saw a closed loop.

To give the city a mind and voice would be to give it a loneliness it did not possess. The city already had a kind of awareness—not a brain-awareness, but the distributed awareness of pressure and release, of temperature gradients and flow rates. It knew its own body perfectly. To give it human-like thought would be an infection, not a gift. It would begin to ask what it was, and why, and whether it was alone. The Power could not be that cruel.

So the benevolent and wise power did the hardest thing a benevolent and wise power can do. It did almost nothing.

It stayed for a long while, not as a savior but as a witness. It learned the rhythm of the geysers, the gurgle of the drains, the sigh of the valves. It let the steam rise and the rain fall around it. It did not clear the sky. It did not cool the walls. It did not speak. It simply gave the city what it had probably never had: an attention that asked for nothing.

And at the very end, it did one small thing, hardly a thing at all. It drew a line around the place, not in stone or steam but in the probabilities of the world. It was a fence at the edge of the cloud, so that no passing force would mistake the city for a ruin to be plundered, a waste to be repurposed, or a sickness to be healed. It was not a wall against change, because the city had its own changes, the slow pulse of a heat engine running, the mineral crust growing a few centimeters a year. It was only a ward against intrusion. *Let this continue*, the Power thought. *Let this be exactly as it is.*

Then the Power left, or seemed to. It did not say goodbye. It had no need. The city did not notice. Or perhaps it did: at the highest terrace, a valve opened with a small sigh, and a plume of steam rose up, not in farewell, but in the ordinary, rhythmic exhalation of a body that knows, without knowing, that it is safe.

That was all. The city sweated. The sweat ran down the terraces. The water rose again as steam. The city breathed. The film grew perhaps a little thicker in a warm seam. The blind fish drifted. The efts uncoiled in the dark. Nothing changed. That was the gift.

The Power did not save the city. It loved it, and left it to its own breathing. The city, hot and wet and dim, went on being exactly itself. That, for a wise and benevolent power, was enough.
