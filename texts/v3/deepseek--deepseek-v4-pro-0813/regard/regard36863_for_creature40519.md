It would do almost nothing.

The benevolent and wise power came to the crack in the bark—no wider than a door—and stopped there. It did not enter. It sat outside the hidden pocket and listened. It heard the low chord, the plant breathing, and understood that the chord was not a cry for help, not a song of grief, not a warning. It was the sound of something already whole. The power let its own radiance fade before reaching the threshold, because even a glimmer would have been a violence here.

It thought of water. But water would be a flood in a place built of dry fiber and mineralized wood; it would start decay, soften memory, erase the city. The power did not bring water.

It thought of animals. One small creature, perhaps, to keep the inhabitant company. But any animal would be an invader: warm, wet, hurried, leaving droppings and bones and noise. The dryness would not support it. The company would become a wound. The power did not bring animals.

It thought of speech. But words are too quick. The inhabitant listened through the floor, through the walls, through the whole body of the plant. The only language here was vibration, and the only sentence was the long patient note of the Nave. The power did not speak.

It thought of restoring the builders. It could have, perhaps. But they would have returned with lungs too wet, hearts too fast, hungers too loud. They would have broken the dust with their footsteps and the silence with their needs. The city had stopped waiting for them. The power let them remain gone.

It thought of healing. But there was no wound. The Dry Nave was not a ruin, not a tomb, not a prison. It was a chamber in the slow dream of the root, a memory that had grown roots of its own. The inhabitant was not sick. It was not lonely in the way a person is lonely. It was not waiting. Its tasks—spinning, mending, sharpening, listening—were not futile. They were the shape of a life lived at the speed of wood.

So the power practiced restraint.

But it did not leave. That would have been too quick, even that. It stayed by the crack and listened. It let its own pulse slow. It let its own need to act fall away like dust. It began to hear the chord the way the inhabitant heard it: not as background, but as presence. It heard the small shifts in the walls, the leaning pillars, the slow cold air pooling at the far end. It heard the inhabitant turn its head over the course of an hour. It heard rootlets thread into the floor while the inhabitant slept. It heard memory.

After a long time—long enough that a year passed like a second—the power placed one hand on the outside of the root. It did not push. It did not knock. It sent a single pulse through the wood: not a word, not a summons, but a recognition. The pulse meant: *I have heard you. I will not disturb you. You are not unnoticed.*

The chord wavered. The pulse moved through the wall, into the floor, down to the cold air. The inhabitant, if it noticed, did not change its tasks. Perhaps it paused. Perhaps it pressed its palm to the floor and returned a pattern that meant: *I know you are here. The dark is not empty. This is not a tomb.* Or perhaps it simply continued to turn the spindle, keeping the gesture of making.

Then the power did the only active thing it allowed itself. It made the crack harder to find. It did not seal it—that would be a prison. It did not widen it—that would be an invitation. It drew a thin veil of forgetting across the outer surface of the root, so that only those who already knew where to look would ever find the door. It gave the Dry Nave what it already had: privacy, dryness, darkness, the undisturbed long note.

Then it withdrew.

If it left anything behind, it was not light, not water, not language, not companionship. It was only the certainty—carried neither by sound nor sign—that the chamber had been seen and judged not broken, not lacking, but complete.

The power did not save the Dry Nave. It did not need saving. It did not heal the inhabitant. It was not ill.

It simply listened.

And by listening, it learned that some things are not waiting to be changed. Some things are already exactly what they are: a hidden pocket in the flank of a root, a dark that presses on the eyes, a city grown and dreamed by a plant, a resident made of memory and fiber, keeping the rooms from forgetting.

The benevolent and wise power left them to their slow, dry breathing.

That is what it did—and what it did, mostly, was leave them alone, which is the rarest form of love.
