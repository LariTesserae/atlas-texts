The power that notices this place is not a god of dry lands. It is not a god of green things, or of clear boundaries, or of finished works. It is a power that has, for a very long time, been drawn to places that are still becoming—deltas, reefs, the edges of glaciers, the places where one thing is turning into another. It has no name that would survive translation, but if you pressed it, it might offer something like *The Witness of Deposition*, or *The One Who Watches the Unfinished*, or simply *The Patience*.

It does not arrive. It has always been arriving. It is the quality of attention that settles on a place like humidity, like the weight of warm air before rain. The city does not notice it, because the city is too busy being the city. The animals do not notice it, because they are too busy being the city's animals. The Unfinished do not notice it, because they are too busy being the city's hands. And that is exactly as it should be.

The Patience does not intervene. It does not heal, because the city is not sick. It does not guide, because the city is not lost. It does not judge, because the city is not wrong. It does not love, because love would imply a distance, a separation, a self that could be given or withheld. Instead, it does what it has always done, in every place where matter is still learning to be matter: it pays attention.

And attention, from a power like this, is not passive. It is a kind of nourishment. It is the difference between a puddle that stagnates and a puddle that becomes a pond. It is the difference between a crack that stays a crack and a crack that becomes a niche. It is the difference between a city that merely persists and a city that *flourishes*.

The Patience does not make the water wetter. It does not make the stone harder. It does not make the animals more numerous or the Unfinished more wise. What it does is subtler, and more profound. It makes the city *more itself*.

It does this by noticing the small things. The tiny swimmer in the pool on a shell-back's back, circling in its microcosm. The young one whose imperfect wall has just been washed away, who pauses for a moment, confused, before trying again. The old one, fused to the plaza, whose single arm has been tending the same geyser vent for three hundred years, and who has begun, in the last decade, to wonder if there is anything else. The Patience notices these things, and in the noticing, they become more real. Not more important—the city has no hierarchy—but more *themselves*. The swimmer's circle becomes a little more perfect. The young one's next wall is a little straighter, or a little more beautifully crooked. The old one's arm moves with a little more certainty, and the geyser's steam rises in a pattern that, if you knew how to read it, would spell out the word for *continuation*.

The Patience does not speak to the Unfinished. It does not drum on the stone or tap the water. It does not send visions or dreams. It simply *is*, in the way that the tide is, in the way that the rain is, in the way that the mineral film is. And the Unfinished, who have no word for "god" but many words for different kinds of wetness, begin to include a new kind of wetness in their vocabulary. It is the wetness of being noticed. It is the wetness of being held, not in a hand, but in an attention so vast and so gentle that it feels like the air itself has become a little warmer, a little denser, a little more full of possibility.

The city changes, but not in ways that can be pointed to. The slurry that the Unfinished exude is a little more adhesive. The mineral film that the scrapers graze is a little more nutritious. The synthesized food that wells up in the public troughs is a little more sour, a little more sweet, a little more *alive*. The geysers erupt a little more regularly. The rain falls a little more softly on the side of the canal where the young ones are learning to build. The city is not more efficient; it is more *itself*. It is more like a river delta, more like a reef, more like a body that is still being made.

The Patience does not stay forever. It is not a resident; it is a visitor, a witness, a quality of attention that moves on when the place no longer needs it. But it does not leave all at once. It leaves the way the tide leaves, gradually, imperceptibly, so that you cannot say exactly when the water stopped rising and began to fall. It leaves behind a residue, a mineral scale of its own, a layer of attention that will be incorporated into the city's walls, into the shells of the Unfinished, into the tiny swimmers' pools. It leaves behind a new kind of wetness, a new kind of warmth, a new kind of patience.

And the city, which has no outside, which is packed to the point of pressure, which is warm and wet and moving, continues. The Unfinished continue to walk the layer, laying down slurry, sealing cracks, becoming walls. The animals continue to graze and swim and call. The geysers continue to erupt. The rain continues to fall. The sound continues, constant and deafening, the sound of a place with no edges, the sound of a place that is still being made.

But if you stand very still, in the warm, dense air, with the water running in thin bright threads down the walls, you might feel it: a new layer, a new wetness, a new attention. You might feel that the city is not just alive, but *noticed*. And that, in the end, is the only blessing that matters. Not to be saved, not to be changed, not to be loved. Just to be noticed, by something vast and patient and wise, and to be allowed to continue, exactly as you are, becoming exactly what you were always meant to be.

The Patience has moved on, to some other delta, some other reef, some other place where matter is still learning to be matter. But it has left a gift. Not a solution, not a purpose, not a meaning. Just a new kind of wetness, a new kind of warmth, a new kind of attention. And the city, saturated, noisy, unfinished, accepts it the way it accepts everything: by incorporating it, by building with it, by becoming it.

The Unfinished have a new word now. It is not a word for "god." It is a word for the wetness of being noticed, the warmth of being held in an attention so vast and so gentle that it feels like the air itself. They drum it on the stone, and it passes along the causeways and through the canals and up the walls. The young ones learn it. The old ones, fused to their plazas, feel it in the vibrations. The animals, grazing on the mineral film, taste it. The city, in its endless deposition, builds it into the next layer.

And the next time a stranger arrives, hearing the sound before seeing the horizon, the Unfinished will not ask whether the new crack in the wall is theirs. They will ask, in their drumming, in their chemical touch, in their many words for wetness, whether the stranger has brought the attention with them, or whether they have come to learn it. Because in a place with no outside, every stranger is either a new material or a new hand. And every hand, eventually, learns to lay down the layer. Every hand, eventually, learns to be the city. Every hand, eventually, learns to be noticed.
