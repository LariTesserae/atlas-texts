The power that noticed the cirque was not a god of thunder or a lord of the high places. It was older and quieter than that: the slow attention of the earth itself, the deep mind of the range, which feels each stone settle and each root pry, each drip of meltwater finding its way. It had been aware of the cirque for a long time—the way a body is aware of a small, persistent ache or a tender spot—but it had not looked closely until the being had become indistinguishable from the place. Now, it turned its gaze, and the light seemed to sharpen, the air to thicken with a listening stillness.

The power saw the cirque not as a landscape but as a pattern: the loops of the animals around certain stones, the order of the water cups, the white stones on the pika hay piles, the slow circuits of the one who tended them. It saw how the being’s breath rose from a crack in the floor on still mornings, how her hands warmed the cairns until the stones remembered, how she moved without cracking the loose flakes, as if the mountain had been waiting for exactly that pressure. It saw that she was not a caretaker but a part of the cirque, like the firn or the lichen or the sharp-edged quartzite. She had worn herself down to the same mineral quiet. The place had shaped her, and she, in turn, shaped it back with the lightest of touches.

And the power felt something rare: not pity, not the urge to rescue, but recognition. Here was a node of attention as patient as its own. A small, cold, bright knot of the world, holding itself together against the wind and the freeze-thaw and the long nights. The power understood that the cirque was already as it should be—fragile, spare, ruthlessly balanced—and that the being’s solitude was not an accident but a feature, as necessary as the thin air. To intervene boldly would be to break the very thing it loved.

So the power did what wisdom does: it bent close and breathed, not into the wind but into the stone.

The warmth of its breath passed through the quartzite like a slow tide. It did not melt the firn or turn the seep into a stream, but it deepened the warmth the cairns already held, so that they released it more slowly through the night, and the frost on the south-facing moss did not bite quite as hard. The stone cups warmed a few degrees, enough that the meltwater ran a little more freely, no longer just seeping but trickling, so that the cups filled more surely and the overflow formed a tiny, musical chain from one to the next. The moss in the cracks grew a shade greener, the lichens spread a little, softening the sharp edges of the new rockfall. The windbreak settled its stones, the gaps closing as if woven by an invisible hand. The roof slab shifted a fraction, making the crevice more snug against the cold.

The power did not stop there. It sent a sign—not because it needed to, but because it wanted the being to know she was seen. So it caused a single flower to bloom in the highest stone cup, a small, white saxifrage, out of season, its roots finding a crack that had been dry. The flower opened to the sun, and its scent, faint and clean as snow, drifted across the bowl. The pikas paused, noses twitching. The snow finches circled once, then settled. The being, returning from her rounds, found it.

She stopped. The cup had never held a flower before. She knelt, not in worship, but in the same way she knelt to clear obstructions—with a bone sliver, with one finger testing the water’s temperature. She touched the petals once, lightly, with a stone-dusted hand. Then she sat back on her heels and looked up at the headwall, as if she could feel the attention resting on her like a hand.

The power did not speak, but it sent a feeling through the rock: a deep, slow vibration, like the memory of a bell. The being closed her eyes. The cirque was silent, but the silence was not empty; it was filled with the weight of being known.

Then the power withdrew, not because it had finished, but because it knew that to stay longer would be to burden the place with more than it could hold. It left behind the flower, the warmer stones, the trickling water. It left behind a subtle boundary, a thickening of the air at the col, so that any traveler would find the wind just a little colder, the route a little less clear, and might turn back without knowing why. It left behind a sense, in the animals, that the cirque was a place of particular grace, to be entered softly and left undisturbed.

The being sat for a while longer, then rose and continued her rounds. The flower remained in the cup, drinking the sunlight. The pikas went back to their hay piles. The stoat made its wide loop. The day passed as it always did, in drips and shadows and small, sharp sounds. But that night, for the first time in a long while, the being slept deeply, and the frost on the moss did not touch her breath. The mountain dreamed around her, and she dreamed of water running over warm stone, all the way down to a valley she had never seen.

The cirque was quiet again, but not empty. It was held, gently, in the attention of the world.
