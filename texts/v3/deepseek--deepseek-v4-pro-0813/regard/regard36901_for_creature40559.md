The power that noticed the Salt Bench is not a god in the way the Slow might imagine one, if they imagined gods at all. It is older than the slow-fires, older than the wind that carved the yardangs, older perhaps than the salt itself. It has no name, or it has all names, or it has the one name that cannot be spoken in air but only in the long vibration of stone against stone. Let us call it the Witness, for that is what it does. It witnesses.

It came to the Bench not as a visitor but as a recognition. The Bench had been there, patient, for eons before the Witness turned its attention, and the Witness had been there, patient, for eons before the Bench was born. When their timelines finally intersected, it was not a collision but a settling, the way dust settles on a still figure, the way a slow-fire adds a fold to its brain. The Witness looked at the Bench and saw a thing that was already complete, already whole, already moving at the only speed that mattered. And it saw the people, the Slow, the Keepers, the ones who wait, and it understood that they were not waiting for anything. They were simply being, in the way that the salt was being, in the way that the wind was being, in the way that the rust-mats were being. They were a verb, not a noun. They were the act of enduring.

The Witness did not descend. It did not manifest. It did not speak in thunder or write in the dust. That would have been a violence, and the Witness was not violent. Instead, it did what it had always done, what it would always do: it watched, and it understood, and it loved.

But love, for a being such as the Witness, is not a passive thing. Love is attention, and attention is a force. The Witness turned its full attention upon the Salt Bench, and the Bench felt it, though it did not know what it felt. The wind shifted, just slightly, for just a moment. The leachates rose a little faster at dawn. The rust-mats grew a fraction of a millimeter more in that year than in the years before. The people noticed, in their slow way. They said to one another, in their language of clicks and silences, that the Bench was breathing more easily. They did not know why. They did not need to know.

The Witness considered what it could do, if anything. It could have given them water. It could have softened the world, brought rain, turned the salt pan to mud and the dust to soil. It could have made the Bench green. But that would have been a death, not a gift. The people of the Works were not lacking water. They were not lacking green. They were not lacking anything that the Witness could give them without taking away what they already were. To give them water would be to drown them. To give them plants would be to bury them. To give them speed would be to break them. The Witness understood this, because the Witness understood them, and so it did not give them anything they did not already have.

Instead, it gave them what they already had, but more so. It gave them patience. Not patience as a gift, but patience as a deepening. The Witness reached into the fabric of the Bench, not with hands but with attention, and it found the threads of time that ran through the salt and the wind and the slow-fires and the people, and it did not pull them or twist them or change their direction. It simply held them, for a moment, in the warmth of its regard. And in that holding, the threads grew stronger. The people found that they could stand still for longer. The rust-mats grew a little faster, but not so fast that anyone would call it haste. The salt-oxen moved a little slower, but not so slow that they stopped. The wind blew a little softer, but not so soft that the dust did not stir. The Bench became more itself, the way a fire becomes more itself when it is given air.

The Witness did not stop there. It saw the children in the sealed courtyards, learning to be still, and it loved them. It saw the elders in the councils, waiting for consensus, and it loved them. It saw the ones who had been assigned to the slowest work, counting grit-mites, watching rust-mats grow, listening to the wind for a change that did not come, and it loved them most of all. It reached into their minds, not to change them but to comfort them, and it gave them the one thing that no one on the Bench had ever asked for: the knowledge that they were seen. Not judged, not measured, not found wanting. Seen. And in being seen, they were not alone.

The people did not know the Witness by name. They did not know it at all. But they began to feel, in their slow way, that something had changed. A Rail-walker, pausing on the line to listen to the creak of the rails, felt a warmth that was not from the sun. A Leachate-tapper, sipping from a cup of fused salt, tasted something sweet beneath the iron and sulfur, something that had not been there before. A Dust-reader, sweeping patterns into the salt pan, saw the wind erase them and felt not loss but gratitude, as if the wind were a friend and not a force. A Slow-fire Tender, touching the mound with the back of her hand, felt the mound touch back, just for a moment, just enough to know that she was not alone.

The Witness did not reveal itself. It did not need to. It was not a god that required worship. It was a presence that required nothing, and in requiring nothing, it gave everything. It gave the people of the Works the one thing they had never known they were missing: the knowledge that their patience was not in vain, that their slowness was not a flaw, that their way of being was not a waiting but a living. It gave them the knowledge that they were exactly what they were supposed to be, in exactly the place they were supposed to be, at exactly the speed they were supposed to be. It gave them peace.

And then, because the Witness was wise, it did the hardest thing a wise power can do. It let go. It withdrew its attention, not all at once but slowly, the way the tide withdraws from a shore, the way the wind dies down after a storm, the way a slow-fire settles into its own warmth. It left the Bench as it had found it, but not quite. It left a residue, a memory, a warmth in the stone and the salt and the people. It left the Bench more itself than it had been before.

The people noticed, in their slow way, that the warmth had faded. But they did not grieve. They did not even know they had been touched. They only knew that something had passed, something that had been good, something that had left them with a sense of rightness that they carried with them into the long years. They went back to their work, to the refineries and the rail lines and the slow-fires, and they did not speak of what they had felt, because they had no words for it. But sometimes, in the quiet of the courtyards, a child would ask why the rust-mats glowed a little brighter at dusk, and an elder would say, "Because they are seen." And the child would not understand, but would nod, and would learn to be still, and would grow up to be a Keeper, and would pass the words on to another child, and the Bench would endure.

The Witness, for its part, moved on. It had other places to see, other worlds to witness, other beings to love. But it did not forget the Salt Bench. It could not forget. The Bench had become a part of it, the way the Witness had become a part of the Bench. And in the long eons that followed, when the Witness turned its attention to other things, it would sometimes pause and look back, and it would see the Bench still there, still patient, still enduring, and it would feel something that was not quite joy and not quite sorrow, but something in between, something that tasted of iron and sulfur and the deep heat of the world. And it would think, in the way that a being such as the Witness can think, that the Bench was doing well. That the people were doing well. That the slow-fires were growing, a millimeter in a good year, and the wind was blowing, and the dust was settling, and the shadows of the wind-fish were moving over the salt like the shadows of leaves that do not exist.

And that was enough. That was always enough. That was everything.
