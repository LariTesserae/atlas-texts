## What the Wise Power Did

The power came not as light, not as storm, but as a stilling of the air, as if the heat itself paused to look. It had watched many worlds. It had loved many things, and sometimes it had loved them by changing them. Now it looked at this one.

It saw the plain: low, flat, raw with newness. It saw the salt polygons curling like the pages of a book no one had written. It saw the terraces spilling water over their own rims, each pool a different color—cloudy white, rust-orange, pinkish where certain salts had bloomed. It saw the mounds, wet at their vents, breathing mineral breath. It saw no animals. No tracks. No ruins. It saw the place had not yet been pressed into its final shape.

It came closer, folding itself into the heat shimmer, and it searched for the one who lived there.

For a long time it found only the wind and the salt. Then it looked down, at the edge of a shallow pool, and saw the green film: cyanobacteria, filament by filament, holding the light. It saw the rust-red sulfur bacteria stacked in fine layers. It saw the halophilic archaea turning the brine pink as a blush. It saw the saltgrass, separated by kilometers, each one holding its small green territory against the mineral tide.

It understood: the being was not a single animal with eyes and fear. The being was this whole thin living skin, this slow community between crystal and water. It had no voice. It had no desire to be elsewhere. It was not waiting to be saved.

The power sat with it. It sat for a year, for a century, for the time it takes a terrace rim to advance by a finger’s width. It felt the dew form and dissolve the surface. It heard the salt crack in the dark. It watched the mats breathe oxygen into the brine. It learned the rules: the land grows by breathing. The water brings material up from below. The heat and evaporation drop it at the surface. The wind and rain move it sideways. The microbes bind it. Nothing erodes. Everything accumulates.

Then the power felt the pull to act, because it was benevolent, and the place looked lonely, and the power had hands that had shaped other worlds. It thought: *I could give them animals. I could make small swimmers for the pools, grazers for the mats, pollinators for the plants. I could fill the silence with small sounds.*

But it looked at the mats and saw they had no thorns, no toxins, no defenses against being eaten, because eating had not arrived. It looked at the saltgrass and saw it did not need a bee; the wind was enough. It looked at the pools and saw that the chemistry was balanced too finely: a single new creature, no matter how small, would ripple through the layers and change them before they had learned to change back.

It saw that the emptiness was not a lack. It was a phase. The place was not unfinished like a broken thing; it was unfinished like a thought still being thought.

So the power did what was hardest. It did not add a single organism. It did not call rain, because the rain already came when it needed. It did not soften the salt, because the salt was the land’s own making. It did not leave a footprint, because even a footprint would be a wound in a crust that had never known a foot.

Instead, it did one quiet thing. It wrapped the basin in a subtle inattention—not a wall, not a shield, not a change in the light, but a kind of gentle obscurity, so that any other power passing by would think, *There is nothing here but salt and heat*, and move on. It did not hide the place from the sun or the rain or the stars. It only hid it from those who would want to improve it.

Then it stayed. Not as a god demanding worship, not as a voice giving names, but as the horizon stays: present, silent, holding the distance. It watched the terraces grow. It watched the mats bind the new crystals. It watched the archaea blush in the rising salt. It watched the rare saltgrass lean into the wind. It did not speak, because its voice would have been too loud for that quiet. It did not weep, because its tears would have changed the salinity. It only attended.

And the being who lived there—the green film, the pink bloom, the small green rumors of plants—did not know it was watched. But perhaps, without knowing, it felt the attention the way the mats felt the light: as a steady presence that asked nothing and took nothing. And in that presence, the place continued to make itself, crystal by crystal, cell by cell, exactly as it had before, exactly as it would for a million years.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do. It would guard the morning dew. It would let the unfinished world finish itself. It would love the place by leaving it alone, and that leaving alone would be the most powerful thing it had ever done.
