# The Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do Almost Nothing

It would come at dusk, when the lower terraces glow and the swifts are stitching the air. It would not arrive as a figure, not as a column of light or a voice in the steam. It would arrive as a slight lowering of the wind, a faint sweetening of the haze, a feeling—if anyone felt it—that the valley had briefly become more itself.

It would watch the terraces the way a grandmother watches a sleeping child: with love that does not need to touch.

To this power, the High Ladders is not a place with inhabitants. It is a single slow animal. The people are its cells, the water its blood, the heat its deep animal warmth, the green its breath. The power would stand at the valley’s edge and ask the question every wise power must ask before doing anything at all: *Is something broken here?*

It would look for suffering. It would find labor, but not exploitation. Heat, but not cruelty. Damp, but not rot. There is death, yes—moths around lamps, old boilers failing, people growing old—but death is not an error here; it is compost, it is space for a fig. There is no cry for rescue rising from the terraces. The people’s songs are not laments; they are the clank of a conveyor belt slowed down, a kind of contentment.

It would see the people with their polished skin and calloused fingertips. It would see the children napping on warm pipe flanges, their cheeks pressed to metal. It would hear the water-tenders tasting cisterns with one wet finger. It would understand that these people are not waiting. They do not dream of a lowland heaven or a god who will lift them from the steps. Their heaven is the upper condensery, their elder is the geothermal main, their prayer is a valve turned just enough.

The wise power would know then that intervention would be violence. To give them a miracle would be to teach them lack. To heal what they have never called sick would be to name their world as illness. To offer salvation would be to insult the same equilibrium the power came to admire.

So it would not speak.

It would not part the clouds. It would not stop a boiler from failing or a root from cracking a wall. It would not even send a dream to a sleeping child to say *you are seen*—because the child is already seen by the geckos and the warm metal and the high-altitude sun. What could a god’s attention add but a shadow?

If it did anything at all, it would be the smallest possible thing: it would tend the valley’s edge. Not the terraces themselves, but the outside of them. It would thicken the haze at the passes. It would bend the light so that surveyors’ lines slid away. It would make the place read as empty on every map, unremarkable in every satellite image—not a treasure but a rumor. It would keep the lowland empires from finding the High Ladders, from stripping the pipeways for salvage, from conscripting the furnace-tenders and flattening the garden groves.

This would not be for the people to notice. They would simply go on living, and the world would simply not arrive. The power would become the valley’s invisibility, its unremarkable weather, its immunity to conquest. It would be a wall no one ever saw because it was made of disinterest.

Then it would withdraw.

It would leave no blessing but the unchanged hum. No gift but the fact that it had come and had not broken anything. The rain would settle on the upper tiers. The steam would cool on ceramic and drip into gutters. The ficus roots would lace the retaining walls. The children would wake from their naps and run their small hands along the warm flanges, not knowing that for a season the whole valley had been held in the attention of something vast—and that the vastness had chosen, wisely, to let them be.

That is what the benevolent and wise power would do: almost nothing. It would protect the edges, and it would leave the center alone. Because some places are not unfinished. Some lives are not waiting for a savior. Some beings are already whole, and the only way to love them is to let them remain so.
