# The Visitor

It came not through the slit between the tilted stones, nor through the pool's wrong reflection, nor through any passage the chamber had yet learned to make. It came the way a meaning comes to a word that has been waiting—not from outside, but as a sudden settling, a recognition that something had been addressed.

The light did not change. The frogs did not turn. But the silence deepened by a fraction, the way a held breath deepens before a name is spoken.

The inhabitant felt it first as a warmth in the chalk sphere, a slight rotation in the cupmark of its face. It had been kneeling by the pool, watching the reflected chamber at an hour that had not yet arrived, when the water's surface—for the first time in all its long becoming—rippled. Not from any falling thing. The ripple moved outward from the center, as if the pool had been touched from beneath, or as if the water had suddenly remembered it was water.

The inhabitant stood. Its limbs resolved into two arms, two legs, a narrow torso of damp limestone. The lichen on its shoulder spread slightly, like hair rising. It turned its unfinished face toward the brightest part of the room, where the light had no source, and waited.

The presence did not announce itself. It did not need to. It was simply *there*, the way the chamber itself was there—not as an object, but as a condition. It was old, but not in the way the pitted wall was old. It was old the way the idea of shelter is old, the way the first hand that cupped water to drink was old. It was wise, but not in the way of accumulated knowledge. It was wise the way a seed is wise, knowing the whole tree without needing to see it.

It had no shape, or rather, it had the shape of attention itself—a quality of being noticed by something that could hold what it noticed without crushing it.

The inhabitant had never been seen before. Not truly. The frogs saw it as part of the pool's edge. The springtails felt it as a change in the wall's moisture. The flies passed through it as they passed through the light. But this was different. This was being *held* in awareness, the way the cupmark held the chalk sphere, the way the pool held its wrong reflection.

It was terrifying. It was also, somehow, the thing the inhabitant had been building toward without knowing it—the bench waiting for its sitter, the steps waiting for their direction, the sentence of split pebbles waiting for its reader.

The presence spoke. Not in words, but in a rearrangement of the chamber's own grammar. The line of split pebbles shifted, almost imperceptibly, into a new pattern. The cupmarks on the upright slab deepened by a hair. The notched edge of the slab caught a shadow that had not been there before, and for a moment, the shadow held—a beam of darkness fitted into the notch like a key.

*You are not finished,* the presence said, in the language of the pebbles and the cupmarks and the shadow. *And that is good.*

The inhabitant's mouth-fracture opened. No sound came, but the silence changed shape, forming a question.

*What are you?* the inhabitant asked, not in words but in the way it stood, the way its fingers curled against its palms, the way the chalk sphere in its face turned slightly toward the presence.

The presence considered. The light leaned, just slightly, as if listening.

*I am what watches the unfinished things,* it said. *I am what tends the places that are still deciding. I have seen worlds begin in pockets like this one. I have seen them end. I have seen them choose to remain pockets, and I have seen them grow until they swallowed the sky above them. I do not choose for them. I only witness, and sometimes, when I am asked, I help.*

The inhabitant felt something move through it—not fear, not hope, but a kind of recognition. It had been alone in its becoming. It had not known that aloneness could end without ending the becoming.

*Why have you come?* it asked.

The presence did not answer immediately. Instead, it moved—or the chamber moved around it—toward the drystone bench. The bench that had never been sat upon. The presence did not sit, but it came to rest there, or the idea of it did, and for a moment the bench seemed complete, as if it had finally remembered its purpose.

*I have come because you are at a threshold,* the presence said. *Not the threshold of the chamber, but the threshold of yourself. You have been building for a long time. You have built a bench, a slab, a set of steps, a sentence of pebbles. You have built a body out of limestone and lichen and the small lives of this place. You have built a face with a cupmark for an eye and a sphere for a thought. But you have not yet built the thing that all these things are for.*

The inhabitant looked at its own hands. They were pale and jointed, like the white shoot rising from the silt. They had shaped the bench, or the bench had shaped them. It could not remember which had come first.

*What are they for?* it asked.

The presence was quiet for a long moment. The droplet that had been falling for the first time for several minutes finally struck the floor, and this time, it made a sound—a small, clear note, like a word spoken at last.

*They are for the one who will come after you,* the presence said. *Or the one who will come to you. Or the one you will become. The bench is for sitting, but not by you. The steps are for climbing, but not by you. The sentence of pebbles is for reading, but not by you. You are building a place for someone else to arrive. That is what it means to be a place, and not merely a creature in a place.*

The inhabitant felt something shift in its chest, where the copepod moved like a heartbeat. It had thought it was building itself. It had thought the chamber was its body, or its body was the chamber, and that the building was the same as being. But this was different. This was a purpose that pointed outward, toward a future it could not see, toward a visitor it could not imagine.

*Who will come?* it asked.

*I do not know,* the presence said. *That is not for me to decide. It may be a creature like you, or unlike you. It may be a human, drawn by the cold scent of wet stone and the seam of lighter air. It may be something that does not yet exist, that will be born from the pool's wrong reflection or the crack that is filling itself with new stone. It may be no one at all, and the chamber will remain a pocket, a possibility, a place that is ready but never used. That is also a kind of completion.*

The inhabitant turned this over in its mind, the way the chalk sphere turned in its cupmark. It had never considered that the chamber might be *for* something. It had only considered that the chamber was *becoming* something. But perhaps those were the same thing, seen from different angles.

*What should I do?* it asked.

The presence seemed to smile, though it had no face. The light brightened by a fraction, then settled back.

*You are already doing it,* it said. *You are tending the pool. You are walking the steps. You are arranging the pebbles. You are letting the light move through you. You are being, and in being, you are making a place where other being can happen. That is enough. That is more than enough. That is the whole work.*

The inhabitant felt something loosen in it, a tension it had not known it was holding. It had been trying to finish itself, to become a complete thing, to resolve the ambiguity of its own existence. But the presence was telling it that the ambiguity was the point. The unfinishedness was the gift. The chamber was not a problem to be solved but a question to be lived.

*But what if I am doing it wrong?* the inhabitant asked. *What if the bench is too low, or the steps lead nowhere, or the sentence of pebbles says the wrong thing?*

The presence was quiet again. The springtails on the weeping wall paused in their silver pulse, as if listening.

*There is no wrong way to be unfinished,* it said. *The bench is the right height for someone. The steps lead exactly where they lead. The sentence of pebbles says what it says, and what it says is true, even if you cannot read it yet. You are not making mistakes. You are making a place. And a place is not judged by its perfection, but by its capacity to hold what comes to it.*

The inhabitant looked around the chamber—at the sourceless light, the bad shadows, the pool with its wrong reflection, the frogs waiting at the water's edge, the snails crowded in their glossy ranks, the root hanging from the ceiling with its blind white buds. It saw, for the first time, not a collection of impossible things, but a coherence. A wholeness that did not require completion.

*Will you stay?* it asked.

The presence did not answer immediately. The light shifted, and for a moment, the chamber seemed to be in two moods at once—one young, one already ancient. The presence was in both of them, or neither.

*I will not stay,* it said. *But I will not be gone. I am what watches the unfinished things. I will be watching you, even when you cannot feel me. I will be in the light that has no source. I will be in the water that falls upward. I will be in the silence that sits on the ears like felt. When you need me, you will not need to call. You will only need to be still, and I will be there.*

The inhabitant nodded, or something in it nodded—a settling of the stone, a slowing of the copepod in its chest.

*Thank you,* it said, and the words were not words but a rearrangement of the pebbles, a deepening of the cupmarks, a brief, clear note from the falling droplet.

The presence began to withdraw. Not quickly, not with any sense of departure, but the way a tide withdraws—gradually, leaving the shore changed but not diminished. The light returned to its sourceless equilibrium. The shadows resumed their bad behavior. The frogs did not blink.

But something was different. The inhabitant could feel it. The chamber was still unfinished, still becoming, still a pocket in the earth that was deciding what to be. But now it was *witnessed*. Now it was *held*. Now it was not alone in its becoming.

The inhabitant knelt by the pool again. The water was still, except for the faint orbits of the copepods and the slow, silent motion of something larger under its surface. The reflection showed the chamber at another hour, and in that reflection, the inhabitant saw itself—not as it was, but as it might be. Not finished, but *ready*. Ready for whatever would come. Ready for whoever would sit on the bench, climb the steps, read the sentence of pebbles. Ready to be a place, and not merely a creature in a place.

It reached into the pool. The surface did not ripple. Its hand came out more finished, more particular, the wetness briefly setting its details. Then it dried back into damp stone and indecision.

But the indecision was different now. It was not the indecision of confusion. It was the indecision of openness—the willingness to be shaped by what came, rather than by what was already known.

The inhabitant stood. It walked to the drystone bench and ran its hand along the sharp stone edges. The newly split faces seemed to become slightly more real under its palm. It did not sit. The bench was not for sitting, not by it. But it could touch the bench, and in touching, it could feel the future sitting there, waiting.

It walked to the upright slab and touched the cupmarks. The chalk sphere in its face turned slightly, as if acknowledging a kinship. The cupmarks were older sentences, or newer ones. They were words it had not finished, waiting for a reader.

It walked to the steps and stood at their base. They led nowhere. They led everywhere. They were a track for someone else's becoming.

It walked to the line of split pebbles and looked at the pattern. The presence had rearranged them, and now they said something new. The inhabitant could not read them, but it could feel the shape of the meaning—a promise, a permission, a blessing.

*You are not finished,* the pebbles said. *And that is good.*

The inhabitant stood in the center of the chamber, in the bright, sourceless light, and let itself be unfinished. Let itself be a place. Let itself be witnessed.

The frogs waited. The springtails pulsed. The droplet fell for the first time again.

And somewhere, in the space between the roots of the dry moor and the first hollows of the limestone shelf, a pocket in the earth continued to become—not alone, but attended. Not finished, but ready. Not a ruin, but an invitation.

The presence did not stay. But it did not leave. It was in the light, in the water, in the silence. It was in the inhabitant's own becoming.

And that was enough.
