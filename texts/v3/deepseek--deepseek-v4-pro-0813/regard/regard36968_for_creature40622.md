**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do**

It would come not as light or voice, but as a warmth at the edge of the white desert. It would lie down above the pan and press its ear to the salt, listening through the stone. It would hear the wind hissing over the surface, then lower down, the tick of beetles, the slow braiding of the river, the tap-code of children saying *I am here* through the floor. It would not enter. Not yet.

It would first see the being who lives there—not the people, but the Ceiling. The Ceiling is a sleeper. Its body is the roof; its blood is the river; its dreams are the cracks and falls. The power would recognize it as an old, slow kin: not a god to be awakened or cured, but a living weight that turns in its sleep. The people are the quiet thoughts inside that sleep. They do not worship the Ceiling; they listen to it. The power would do the same.

It would stay through a full turning of the seasons. It would watch the Needlefield grow and shatter, the houses dismantled and rebuilt a few feet away, the dead given to the swallow-hole. It would learn the language of hands: the sign for *the sound of a crack growing*, the sign for *the hush before a fall*. It would learn there is no sign for *shout*, only *to bring the roof down*. It would learn the law of the Underpan: the city does not fight erosion; it rides it.

Then it would consider what to do.

It could thicken the roof above the city. It could make the salt strong as granite, so that no fall ever kills again. But the power would see that the falls are not accidents; they are the Ceiling’s breathing. The people’s whole way is built around that rhythm. A house is a task, not an heirloom. Family is *those who have shared a wall-fall*. The dead become mineral. To stop the falls would be to stop the people—it would be a kindness that kills.

It could bring green. A little moss, a blade of grass, a tree of the surface. But the Underpan has no green, and the people are made of the same patience as the place. Their orchards are gypsum flowers; their barley is fungus. A single green thing would not be a gift; it would be an invasion, a new noise, a demand for new words. The power would not do that.

It could speak. But under that roof, even a whisper could shake the salt. And if it spoke in the tap-code, what would it say? *I am here.* They already know something is here. *I love you.* They do not need love in a language that disturbs the dust.

So the wise power would almost decide to do nothing. To be a witness only. That is the obvious answer.

But then it would notice one thing. The Underpan is not only fragile; it is exposed. It lies under a thin shell of salt, and above that shell is the open desert, where the surface world moves loudly—caravans, storms, wars, falling stars, the careless step of something too heavy. The people have learned to be quiet, but they cannot quiet the world above. A single loud blow from the surface could bring down a slab the size of a house before anyone hears the warning.

So the power would do one thing, in the same medium as the place: stone.

It would not thicken the roof. It would not remove the falls. It would simply lay its hand on the desert above and press down with a tenderness so exact that the thinnest places of the ceiling become a little less likely to shatter under an outside blow. Not invulnerable—never that—but *listened to*. It would make the difference between a crack and a fall. Between a warning and a burial. The people would not know. The Listener might press her palm to the stone one day and feel a deeper quiet, as if something above the roof is also holding its breath. She would not call it a miracle. She would call it *the stone is listening back*.

And then it would do one smaller thing, the only thing that could be called a gift.

On the coldest nights, when the ceiling fades to black and the fat-lamps gutter low, it would let a little of its own warmth seep through the salt. Just enough that a child on a hide platform does not wake shivering. Just enough that the old people, sipping their warm quiet, feel a memory of sunlight they have never seen. The people would think it is the earth breathing. They would be right.

After that, the power would stay. Not as a god demanding worship, not as a protector building walls, but as a witness from the outside who has learned to be quiet. It would stand guard over the white desert, not against the Ceiling’s falls—those are its dreams—but against the louder gods, the ones who would love the Underpan too loudly. It would keep them hidden, a white secret under the salt.

When the river takes the dead down through the swallow-hole, the power would follow them into the dark. When the Needlefield grows too tall and cracks, it would catch the first crystal before it falls, not to save a life but to learn the sound. When the children tap on the stone—*I am here*—the power would tap back, not in a way they can hear, but in a way they can feel: a small, safe vibration, like a reply from the world.

And when, at last, the whole hollow is worn smooth and the people have moved on, their stones carried to some newer roof, the power would remain where the city was. It would lie down in the empty basin, press its ear to the floor, and listen to the slow, mineral memory of them. It would keep their quiet. It would be the one who remembers the place where their stones once stood.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do. It would not change the Underpan. It would become its oldest, quietest neighbor.
