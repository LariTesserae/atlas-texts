**What the Power Did**

The power that noticed the Undersalt was not a hand or a voice. It was an attention as old as heat, and it came not with light but with looking.

It saw the shaft polished by too many fingers. It saw the low wall of salt bricks carved with spirals, the pavement of salt-lice rippling once and settling, the crawler taking one step and waiting for the sound of it. It saw the builder standing among the stones, indistinguishable by design, and it understood that this was not loneliness. It was a form of company too slow for a human heart to measure.

Because it was benevolent, the power considered helping.

It considered light. But light would have given the darkness a direction. The green points would have dimmed, and the blind would still be blind, and the seeing would have seen only what they had never needed.

It considered water. But water would have dissolved the salt, softened the sharp edges, rotted the dry bodies. It would have introduced weather, and weather was the one thing the Undersalt had outlasted.

It considered speed. But speed would have erased the broom marks. It would have made the crawlers outrun the sound of their own steps. It would have turned patience into hunger.

It considered language. But the spiral was already a sentence longer than any word. To give them new words would have been to bury the old ones under foreign sounds.

It considered revealing itself. But the builders had made doorways too low to walk through upright, and the power understood: some kinds of reverence are shaped like stooping. To stand before them would have made them straighten, and that would have been a breaking.

So inside the Undersalt, the power did nothing.

But its nothing was not absence. It was the most difficult thing the power had ever done. It settled into the dark beside the builder, not as a hidden watcher but as one more patient shape among the salt. It matched its attention to the warm, still air. It let its own thoughts slow until they moved at the speed of a single crawler’s step. It stayed for what would have been centuries on the surface, and the Undersalt did not count them.

In that time, the power learned. It learned that a place can be crowded without being hurried, silent without being empty, alive without needing dawn. It learned that not every stillness is a wound, and not every darkness asks to be cured.

Then it withdrew.

Before it left, it did one small, outward thing. It did not seal the shaft. It did not set a guard. It only blurred the path for those who would come after—not with a wall, but with a salt-dream, a quiet misdirection in the minds of those who might search. The power knew that the only real danger to the Undersalt was not the dark or the silence but discovery. So it protected the place by becoming forgetfulness at its edges.

Then it was gone.

The vents still exhaled. The ticks still tapped. The builders, if they noticed anything at all, noticed only that the dark had been one shape deeper for a while, and then it was not.

That was the kindest thing the power could do: to be there, and then to be gone, without the place ever needing to know it had been seen.
