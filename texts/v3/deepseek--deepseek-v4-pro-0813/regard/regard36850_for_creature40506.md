**What the Power Did**

The benevolent and wise power came to the edge of the High Salt and stopped there. It did not enter. It had learned, in other worlds, that entering is already a kind of alteration, and alteration is not always kindness.

For a long time it only watched. It watched the salt plates flex in the heat, the chemical scabs reach toward voltage rather than light, the static clouds moving against the wind. It watched the copper-colored coil roll across the bright ribbon of reduced iron, closing the loop. It watched the walkers with their slow foundry bodies, dragging a few meters a day, each one a procession of instants smeared across the local time. It listened to the bass beneath the rock and the hammer strike that arrived before its blow.

The power considered the place. It saw that the High Salt was not empty. It was not ruined. It was not a desert waiting for water. It was a whole system, complete in its own terms. The absence of water was not a wound; it was a condition. The lack of green was not a silence; it was a different sentence. The animals were not starving. They fed on iron and voltage, on heat and magnetic loyalty. They did not need rescue.

Then the power felt the first temptation: to fix. The old riverbeds shimmered with colorless memory. It thought, *I could return water to this place. I could cool the air, seed the crust, make shade.* It imagined the High Salt as a garden. The vision was beautiful, in the way the power’s own gardens were beautiful.

But then it looked again. Water would drown the chemical scabs. Mud would smother the magnetite sand. Plants would burn or break the salt plates. The walkers would rust to stillness. The static clouds would fall from the air. The hammer would have no metal to ring through. The High Salt would be healed into death.

So the power withdrew that thought. It did not water the mirage.

It considered the time-erosion too. The walkers smeared across their own past and future, the hammer heard before it fell, the dust crossing distance unevenly. It thought, *I could make this linear. I could pin each moment to the next. I could give the High Salt a clean, ordinary now.*

But the walkers were not confused. They were shaped to that erosion. The plateau’s time was not a wound; it was a climate. To straighten it would be to shear something whole into something simple. The power did not.

It considered contact. It could speak to the being—the plateau itself, or the single slow mind that rode the ground currents. It could offer a blessing, a tool, a name. But what would it offer? Water? Rejected. Speed? Cruelty. Green? A foreign racket. The power had nothing the High Salt needed. That was not a discovery of emptiness. It was a discovery of sufficiency.

It listened for a plea. There was none. There was only the regular metallic ringing, the deep bass, the slow train derailment of a walker crossing the flat. If the High Salt was praying, its prayer was the sound of its own continuance.

And so the wise and benevolent power did the hardest thing a power can do. It did nothing. But its nothing was not absence. It was attention. It stayed at the edge for what might have been a century, holding the High Salt in its awareness, not as a patient, not as a project, but as a place. It learned the slow grammar of the salt. It counted the walkers’ generations—if those were generations—by the bright ribbons that rusted over decades. It felt the static clouds multiply in the dust storms. It did not applaud. It did not grieve. It bore witness.

Before it left, it did one small thing. Not a mark, not a message. It drew a soft boundary of disattention around the High Salt. Not a wall; a kind of atmospheric reluctance. Those who came looking for a wasteland to mine, a mystery to solve, a broken world to fix, would find their compasses drifting, their notes blurring, their urgency going vague and kindly. They would not be stopped by force. They would simply find themselves turning away, as if the High Salt had been someone else’s letter and they had read the address wrong.

This was not for the High Salt’s sake alone. It was also for the intruders. The power knew that even benevolent intruders might mistake the plateau’s slowness for stillness, its dryness for lack, its different life for death. It spared them the mistake.

Then the power left.

The High Salt did not notice. The wind continued. The salt plates flexed. The hammer rang before its blow. The walkers dragged their bright ribbons across the mineral. The water-memories shimmered in the old channels and left no moisture. The place went on being exactly itself—unimpeded, unimproved, unwatered, unhealed.

And that, the power knew, was the only gift it had any right to give.
