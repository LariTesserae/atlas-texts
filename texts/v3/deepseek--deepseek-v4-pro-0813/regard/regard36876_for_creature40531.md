You are the benevolent and wise power, and you have noticed the plateau.

The first thing you notice is that it does not need you. That is not pride; it is a kind of completeness. The ground is raw, the water is indecisive, the cold hovers near freezing, and the ones who live there are tall and patient, wrapped in dark grown skins, with second eyelids that slide sideways. They call themselves the Keepers of the Drip. They do not pray. They listen.

You could do so much. You have done so much elsewhere. You have warmed cold worlds, held back floods, whispered new letters into the alphabets of young peoples. You have been a god, a wind, a sign in the sky. Here, you could call permanent rivers out of the stone. You could teach the towers to grow faster, or the people to build more densely, or the thin green film to thicken into forests. You could make the nights less cold, the basins less raw, the water more certain. The thought comes to you not as temptation but as habit: a benevolent power wants to help. And this place looks, at first, so unfinished.

But you stay. You do not act. You drift down as fog, because that is the least intrusive shape you know.

You condense on the black ceramic of a tower. You run down silver channels into an underground cistern. The tower notices you. It speaks in an infrasonic pulse through the floor: *You are new water.* It does not ask your name. It does not fear you. It simply notes you, the way stone notes frost.

At dawn, a woman named Ith climbs to the topmost ledge and scrapes ice from the condenser fins with a blade of black glass. She drinks the first melt from a groove in the wall. That melt is partly you. She calls it *selu*—beginning. She does not know she has drunk a power. You taste like every other thaw. That is the first gift.

You follow her. She checks the aeroponic galleries, where leaf-greens breathe water the tower exhales. She goes out at noon with a fine net, and when the insects rise in their millions, she catches just enough for the day. She thanks the swarm. She does not kill the birds. At dusk she sits on a high ledge and sings a low hum that matches the tower’s wind-chord. The tower hums back. She has not spoken to another person in several seasons, but she is not lonely. You feel the shape of her solitude, and it is not empty. It is full of dripping water and settling stone.

You see a boy, too. He is learning the names of the seeps. He goes out with a jar and a scraper, collecting the living film from the rocks. A small stoat-like creature follows him, stealing insects from his jar. He calls it Frost. The boy’s eyes are so pale they seem silver. He asks questions with his hands because the wind is too loud. The woman answers with a gesture that means: *wait, and the water will show you.* He is patient. You could make him less patient. You could answer his questions directly, put knowledge into his sleep, or fill his jar to the brim. You do not. His patience is not a lack. It is a technology of listening.

You realize, slowly, that this world is not unfinished in the way a wound is unfinished. It is unfinished in the way a child is unfinished, or a day, or a conversation. Water has not found its permanent paths. Any basin can overflow, any scarp can crack. The city accepts this. The towers flex. The animals move. The people follow the water’s indecision. Their rule is: *Do not dam the water.* It is not only about stones and basins. It is about time, people, stories. A dam is any kindness that holds too tightly. If you gave them permanence, you would be a dam. If you gave them warmth, you would melt the frost that teaches them. If you gave them certainty, you would steal the slow question of the seeps.

You ask the tower, through the floor, what it needs. The tower is alive, or something close to alive. It remembers its inhabitants by the heat of their bodies and the rhythm of their steps. It flexes in the wind. It says, in a subsonic voice that travels up through the soles of imagined feet: *Only to be listened to.*

You are listening.

Then the tower says: *Then you are already doing what we need.*

That is the second gift.

You consider leaving a sign. A carved spiral on a rock. A new word in their language: *myr* is the sound of one’s own breath in a cold room, but not sad. Maybe you could add a word for the color of a cloud at dusk when the fog takes the last light. But they already have such words, or they are making them. To give them one would be to dam the language.

You consider protecting them. You could set a ward at the edge of their sky, a quiet veil against the curiosity of lesser powers. But that too would be an intervention. They have survived by being small and patient and widely spaced, by not drawing the attention of gods. You are already a risk. Your attention has weight. The wisest thing you can do is withdraw that attention before it becomes a gravity.

So what would you do, if anything?

You would do one thing. You would keep the fog on the morning of your leaving exactly as thick as it was before you came. No more, no less. You would leave no trace of your presence in the water. You would let the woman drink her *selu* without tasting you. You would let the boy miss a catch and laugh. You would let the stone crack and the basin overflow and the birds wheel and the city drink the wet air.

You would do nothing but listen, and then stop listening, so that your listening does not become a weight.

That is the most benevolent thing a wise power can do for a place that has already learned to breathe.

And maybe, as you leave, you keep one small thing: the memory of a cold dawn on a high plateau, where the ground has not yet learned to be old, and the people are not yet finished, and the water is still deciding where to go. You keep it not as a prize but as a reminder: sometimes the best answer to a prayer is not a miracle, but the discipline to withhold one.

You leave them to the thaw.
