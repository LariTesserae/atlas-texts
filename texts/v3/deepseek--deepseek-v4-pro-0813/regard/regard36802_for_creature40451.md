The benevolent and wise power would do almost nothing.

That is not the same as doing nothing. It would first spend a long time being there, because attention is the first act of any true power. It would enter not as a storm or a voice, but as a slow arrival at the edge of the High Fog Sea. It would stand on a black basalt island where the fog hangs in sheets between emergent trunks, and it would let the place teach it how to listen: to the drips from leaves, the creak of a growing stem, the small pop of a seed pod, the underwater hiss of gas rising through fissures. It would learn to breathe at the same rate as the water’s long cycle—evaporation to fog, fog to dew, dew to water again.

Then it would see the blind eel.

It would see her not as a lack but as a solution. She is built in densities that match the water’s layers; her body seems to break and rejoin at each thermocline, but she is whole. She has no eyes, but she does not need them. She tastes the rock’s slow dissolution through fine pits along her jaw, feels the pressure of gas as if the water spoke to her in a language of small disturbances. She eats almost nothing, grows slowly, carries the basin’s rise and fall as faint lines along her narrow fin. She is not lonely. She is not waiting. She is not unfinished.

The power, being wise, knows that the greatest danger is love that wants to improve. It feels the temptation because any benevolence feels the temptation. It considers giving the eel eyes, then understands that eyes would only fill her with a green-gray confusion, a need to see things that do not matter. It considers giving her companions, but the rare meetings of her kind are already exactly enough: once in decades, two blind eels cross in a folding of layers, touch, learn each other’s water, and part. To multiply them would be to diminish the event into a population. It considers making the plants grow faster, the rock soften faster, the seasons arrive. But the place is not slow because it is weak; it is slow because slowness is its only true wealth. The glass-reeds grow in spiral rows and hexagonal patterns from the young rock. The undecayed leaf mat is the only soil, and it is made by patience. To hurry it would be to replace the High Fog Sea with a different world, one that already exists elsewhere.

It considers speaking. But the eel is not listening for words. The only speech here is mineral and thermal. The water’s layers are the only grammar. The glass snail’s single track on a leaf is a sentence that takes months to write. The power understands that a voice would be noise, not revelation.

It considers carving an outlet to the lower ocean, so the basin might know the real sea. But that would unmake the very condition of the place: a hanging sea above the ocean, closed and still. The boundaries are not walls but conditions. The water does not need to drain. The eel does not need to meet the sea.

So the power does not give eyes, companions, speed, speech, or an outlet. It does not bless the water with clarity, because the slight silica cloudiness near the springs is part of the water’s taste. It does not bless the place with immortality, because the basin’s long changes—the source waning, the fog thinning, the water deepening or shallowing—are not harms. They are the continuation of a story the power did not make and should not end.

But there is one thing it does. Not a gift to the eel, not a change to the water, not a structure. It surrounds the whole plateau with a particular kind of unremarkableness, the way an animal may be surrounded by a scent that says *the world is ordinary here*. It makes the place easier to leave alone. Any future mind that might come with tools, with names, with maps or desires, would find its attention sliding away, the coordinates blurring, the route forgotten, the fog too thick, the water too yielding. The place would remain findable only by those who could bear its stillness without needing to use it. This is not a wall; it is a hush around a hush. The power’s protection is not to lock the basin away but to make it uninteresting to those who cannot see it as already complete.

Then the power goes. Not entirely—a wise power cannot entirely leave what it has noticed—but it goes the way fog goes from the water: by thinning, by becoming indistinguishable from the air. It leaves no structure, no tool, no made thing. It leaves no message except the place itself, still hanging, still warm, still slow.

And the blind eel? She may have felt something pass. At the boundary between the warm layer and the cool one, she may have paused, tasting a slight new gradient in the water, not heat, not mineral, not another eel, not food. Something like a layer of attention, without weight. She would sway there a moment, her body legible and then illegible. Then she would move on, and the line of that meeting would be added to the lines along her fin, not as a cycle of water rise or fall, but as a cycle of being seen. Then it would fade.

The water still comes first, not as a boundary but as a condition. The reeds still grow. The fog still returns as dew. The basin breathes. The power does not act in the way we might ask a power to act. It does not save, because there is nothing to be saved from. It does not improve, because improvement would be a loss. It only notices, and lets that noticing become a kind of surrounding stillness, indistinguishable from the place’s own.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do.
