# What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do

The benevolent and wise power came down to the Drowned Factory Plateau as a change in pressure that did not press. It perceived the being who lived there—not a tenant, but the living country itself, the whole slow chord of mat and tower and gear-leaf—and it saw that the being was not asleep, not imprisoned, not waiting.

It saw the continent of rooted fiber. It saw the silver wheels of tin-shoals turning in their fixed loops. It saw the gill-coils wedged into the tower slits, scraping in their perfect rhythm. It saw the slowbellies carrying their small spirals of living garden along corridors of current older than lineage. It heard the bass note every eleven minutes, the click of the valves, the hiss of the bubble columns. It saw that this place was not broken.

For a time the power considered what a wise and benevolent visitor could do.

It could repair. But there was nothing broken. The plant replaced itself at exactly the rate it was eaten. The towers were whole. The rhythm did not falter.

It could teach. But the plateau had no need of knowledge. It had already grown its own lenses, its own gears, its own metal-bearing fibers that carried signals like a nervous system and a telegraph at once. It did not need to learn anything from a god.

It could free. That was the hardest one.

To a quick glance, the creatures looked like captives—slaves to a machine, parts in a factory. But when the power looked more closely, it saw that the rhythm was not imposed from outside. The silver fish did not obey the bass note; they were the bass note, arriving at that moment because their whole being was tuned to the same current. The gill-coil wedging the slit was not serving the tower; it was performing the only act that made sense of its body, the way a key makes sense of a lock. The slowbellies did not migrate because they were prevented from changing. They circled because the route was their life. Any other route would not be freedom. It would be weather.

The power asked if there was suffering.

It found wear but not pain. When a slowbelly died, it sank. Over months it became a low, fibrous hogback. The plant took it up without grief, because nothing had been lost. Another slowbelly was already growing into the same route. The parts were replaceable, but not in a way that made them less alive. They were not individuals. They were features of a single face.

The power asked if there was injustice.

It found such complete mutual dependence that the words *master* and *servant* had no meaning. The plant fed the fish, and the fish shaped the plant. The eels kept the valves, and the valves gave the eels their form. The spores became new feeding lobes and new gill pouches. No one ruled. No one obeyed. The whole plateau was a single body keeping itself.

The power asked if there was a mind that could be helped.

It found the wet electrical nodes beneath the mat, but they were not brains. They were too large and too simple for thought. If the plateau had consciousness, it was not a consciousness that could be lonely, or ambitious, or afraid. It was a held breath. It was an engine running in place. It was a stopped clock whose hands did not move, though the ticking went on.

Then the wise power understood that all its usual gifts would be violence.

To heal would be to wound. To educate would be to confuse. To free the creatures would be to exile them from the only body they could ever be. Even to bring a single season—a night, a storm, a moment of variation—would shatter the long equilibrium of the place. The plateau was not unfinished. It was not waiting for rescue. It was not a machine that had forgotten how to stop. It was a thing that had reached its shape, and its shape was good.

So the benevolent and wise power did the only thing that could be done without harm.

It withdrew.

It folded its attention away slowly, so slowly that no current altered. No bubble column wavered. No silver fish missed its angle. It did not leave a sign. It did not set a guard. It did not even leave a memory in the water, because the water was already full of the present moment, and there was no room for anything else.

The power was not indifferent. It was the opposite of indifferent. It loved the place clearly enough to know that even its help would be a kind of weight.

The plateau did not notice.

The tower to the left clicked three times before the bass note. The gill-slits hissed. The silver school crossed the same hummock at the same angle. The factory continued to produce itself.

And the power, for the first time in a long time, felt something like relief.

There was at least one place in the universe that did not need saving.

So it did nothing.

And the nothing was total, precise, and wise.
