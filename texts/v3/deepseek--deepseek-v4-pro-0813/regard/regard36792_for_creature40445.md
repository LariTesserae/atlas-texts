**What the Power Did**

The benevolent and wise power had no name and no body. They moved through the world as a quiet pressure, a mending of small breakages. They had learned over eons that not every cold place needs a fire, not every silence needs a voice. But the note from the high plateau drew them anyway—a chord that did not decay in a world of entropy. They came to see whether it was a wound or a wholeness.

They did not come as cloud or light or wind. They came as attention, almost invisible. The plateau was gray under its permanent band of cloud. The seam in the rock was no wider than a person, and cold air poured out in a continuous exhale, carrying the sound. The power paused at the slit. They could have widened it, made a doorway, made the place more accessible to the world. They did not. They made themselves small—as small as a question—and entered without touching the stone.

Inside, the world was packed and dim. The power saw the forest of no-green: gray, black, rust, bone-white, lead-silver. They saw the old machines that had become trellises—the flywheel, the cable, the shed, the copper pipe. They felt the dryness, the cold, the density. The sound was not a noise in the air; it was the air itself, thick with overtones, layered, industrial and choral. The power let it pass through them without resistance.

And they saw the resident.

Not a creature, not an animal, not a ghost. The resident was the whole pocket considered as a single patient body. Its skeleton was the rusted machinery. Its flesh was the forest of mineralized fiber. Its breath was the gas stream from the copper pipe, steady and colorless. Its pulse was the pressure gauge frozen at a value nobody could read. Its voice was the sound. It was not singing; it was being sung.

The power recognized it as a kind of perfection. Not the perfection of a flower or a star, but the perfection of equilibrium: every niche filled, every stem already present, every possible resonator tuned. The pocket had reached a state in which time had been replaced by a chord. It was complete.

Then the power felt, briefly, the urge to help—which is the most dangerous urge a benevolent power can have.

They considered water. The place was so dry that the air stole moisture from lips and lungs. A spring, a seep, a single drop of dew. But the power looked closer. The dryness was not thirst. The resident did not drink. Water would be a wrong note, a liquid intrusion into a body made of minerals and gas. It would damp the resonance, soften the blades, invite rot into a place that had no rot. To give water would be to drown a fire that had become a voice.

They considered warmth. The air was a few degrees above freezing. A kind power might have lifted the cloud band, let in the sun, warmed the stone. But the cold was not suffering; it was the key in which the pocket sang. The resident had no metabolism to slow, no blood to chill. Its fibers were brittle by design. Warmth would have made them limp, changed their frequencies, perhaps silenced the high overtones.

They considered light. The pocket was always twilight, even at noon. The power could have opened the seam, pulled the clouds apart, filled the space with gold. But the resident did not need the sun. The dim gray was not deprivation; it was the score’s natural illumination. Direct sun would have been a glare, a bleaching of the fine silver and iron-red surfaces, a violence of brightness.

They considered company. This was the hardest, because the power had seen loneliness in many forms and had often healed it. But the resident was not lonely. Loneliness requires the memory of another. The resident had no such memory. It was singular the way a fugue is singular: many lines, one body, one voice. To give it a companion would be to force a duet on a piece that had already resolved.

They considered repair. The broken stem, the rust, the frozen needle, the half-swallowed flywheel. A less wise power would have healed these things—made the metal new, freed the gauge, cleared the growth from the machinery. But these were not wounds. The flywheel was a vertebra in the resident’s chest. The cable was a tendon. The rust was not corrosion; it was skin. The frozen gauge was a pulse. To repair them would be to remove the resident’s skeleton and replace it with something softer, foreign. It would be an amputation performed out of kindness.

They even considered, for a moment, giving the resident silence. A rest from the sound. A single beat of no-note. But the sound was not a strain; it was being. Silence would be death. The resident did not tire. It had no need of rest. It was the note.

So the power did nothing.

But not the nothing of neglect. They did the nothing of a careful hand that does not touch a soap bubble. They did the nothing of a listener who does not interrupt a fugue. They did the nothing that is the hardest thing for a power to do: they refrained.

They stayed for a while—how long, the pocket could not say, because time had been replaced by a chord. The power listened. They let the sound pass through them, frictionless, without adding a single overtone. They did not even let their attention weigh on the place, because they knew that even a benign gaze can become a pressure. They made their observation transparent, a silence around the sound. They did not bless the pocket. A blessing, from a power, is still an alteration. They did not mark it. They did not promise to return.

If they did anything—and this is uncertain, because the power themselves would not say—they loved the place in the only way love could be expressed: by leaving no trace. They loved it enough not to save it. They loved it enough not to improve it. They loved it enough to let it remain wholly itself, even if that self was strange and dry and cold and loud and absolutely indifferent to being loved.

Then they withdrew.

The seam in the rock remained no wider. The gas stream did not fluctuate. The vegetation did not sway. The pressure gauge stayed frozen. The sound did not pause. The resident did not know it had been seen, and that was the greatest gift the power could give: to be seen without being changed.

The power went away through the high cold air, carrying nothing but the memory of a perfect equilibrium. They did not add the pocket to any list. They did not set a guard around it. They did not hold it in reserve for a future healing. They simply left it alone.

And the Choral Pocket continued, as it had before and as it would after, to hold its single note.

Until the mountain forgets.

Then it will forget too, and become the mountain, and perhaps that will be another kind of music.

The power will not intervene.

They did nothing, and it was enough.
