The wise power did not come with thunder, and it did not come with silence. It came as a small pause in the weather—a thinning in the white lid of cloud above the plateau, a brief lessening of the mist in the streets. It looked at the city of water and stone, and it saw it all at once.

It saw the thousand thousand pools, each overflowing into the next. It saw the terraces rising like wet lace, the mineral domes leaking their slow springs, the narrow alleys where the only light was reflected upward from the water. It saw the living films that coated every rim—gray-brown, olive-dark, rust-red at the edges—pulling dissolved rock from the water and laying it down in sheets and scallops. It saw the blind amphipods stirring the silt in the deep pools. It saw the springtails on the wet moss, the tardigrades curled in their slow, patient barrels, the rotifers spinning at the edge of sight. It saw the whole metabolism of the place: water, mat, stone, air, water.

And it saw the being.

The small slate-colored bird, no larger than a hand, walking underwater in a fast overflow channel. The one with a face and eyes. The only resident that seemed to look back, if only for a second, before diving again.

The power was benevolent. Its first impulse was to bless. To warm the cold air so the city would not freeze so hard in the night. To make the pools clearer, sweeter, more welcoming. To give the bird a song, a flock, a companion. To lift the moss into soft green gardens. To ease the labor of the mats and the slow, blind work of the amphipods. It could have done any of these things. It could have made the city more beautiful, more alive, more legible to anyone who might come after.

But the power was also wise. And wisdom is the long memory of consequences. It looked at each gift and saw the future it would create.

If it warmed the air, the water would hold more dissolved rock, and the mats would build too fast. The pools would seal themselves into hollow stone, the channels would close, and the bird would have no fast water left to walk in. If it cleared the water, the amphipods would lose their milky cover, and the bird would eat them down to nothing in a season. If it fed the amphipods, they would multiply and graze the mats to bare stone, and the city would stop growing. If it gave the bird a song, the silence would be broken—and the silence here was not absence. It was the city’s own music, the sound of water inside stone, the sound of a world holding its breath. A song would fill that space and leave no room for the city’s own voice.

If the power spoke to the bird, even once, the bird would become something other than what it was. It would carry the memory of being addressed. It would look for the voice in the water. It would wait, or wonder, or yearn. And this place did not need a creature who yearned. It needed a small, silent walker in the fast channels, a top predator with no song, a soft wet tick that closed back into the air almost before it was heard.

The power considered protecting the city from future harm—from human roads, from deeper winters, from the slow turning of the continent. But even protection would be a kind of leash. The city was not fragile. It had survived by being exactly itself: indifferent, self-maintaining, already in balance. To protect it would be to decide that its own balance was not enough. And that decision, once made, would become a wall around the plateau, and then a gate, and then a name, and then a map, and then the city would no longer belong to itself.

So the power did the hardest thing a benevolent and wise power can do.

It did nothing.

Not the nothing of neglect, but the nothing of restraint. It made its attention smaller and smaller, until it was no more than the shadow of a cloud moving over the terraces. It did not speak. It did not touch. It did not leave a sign. It allowed the city to continue exactly as it had before, as if the power had never looked at it at all.

The clouds closed back over the high shoulder of the continent. The mist gathered again in the narrow stone streets. A sheet of water poured over a high rim with a low hiss. Somewhere, in the shaded margin of a pool, a springtail jumped and disappeared into the mist. Under the water, the bird stepped down into the current and gripped the mineral crust with its fine dark toes. The mats kept breathing and building. The city kept making and unmaking itself, pool by pool, layer by layer.

The power withdrew, and in withdrawing it gave the only gift that would not become a wound: it left no trace of its love.

The bird dove. The water ticked. The silence closed back over the sound, and the city went on, complete, unburdened, at the edge of the sky.
