The wise power did not arrive. Arrival would have implied a before and after, a breach in the stillness, and the power had long ago learned that the holiest places are not entered but remembered. So it turned its attention to the hollow as a face turns to warmth—not because it sought anything, but because it recognized something older than itself.

For a long time it did nothing. This was not hesitation; it was the first act of love. The power laid its regard on the place without weight, the way the pale sky laid its light through the steam. It saw the water seeping from the rock, not as a miracle, but as a fact: patient, mineral, warm. It saw the white terraces, each one a century’s exhalation. It saw the microbial mat, the one inhabitant, spread thin as breath over the pool rims, building its tiny mountains, asking nothing.

And the power felt the temptation that every benevolent thing feels: to improve. It could have made the water sweeter, so that a traveler might drink. It could have taught the cells to quicken, to become something that sees and moves and sings. It could have warmed the hollow a degree, coaxed the ferns to spread, brought birdsong to the silence. It could have etched the half-erased spiral into new clarity, raised the fallen stone, given the juniper a hundred more years. All these gifts were within its power.

But wisdom is the discipline of power. It does not ask what can be done, but what should be allowed. And the wise power, looking at this small world, understood that it was not unfinished. It was not waiting. It had not been arrested on its way to something else. The seep was not a promise; it was an act. The mat was not a prelude; it was a life. The silence was not empty; it was the very shape of the inhabitant’s existence, complete and undivided.

To give the hollow a voice would be to exile it from itself. To speed its growth would be to erase its history. To make it beautiful in the world’s eyes would be to rob it of its one true beauty: that it did not care whether it was seen.

So the power did what only a wise power can do: it withdrew the hand. It imposed no will, asked no gratitude, left no sign. If it did anything, it was this—it held the hollow in its attention for one long, slow turn of the drip, and then it let go.

But letting go, for such a power, is not forgetting. It is a promise. The wise power folded the hollow into its memory, not as a place to be visited, but as a place to be preserved from distance. It would not hasten the seep or cool it. It would not seed the pools with new life. It would not mend the broken stone or sharpen the spiral. It would do nothing, unless the world outside pressed too hard. And even then, its protection would be invisible: a small deflection of a bulldozer’s path, a sudden fall of rain far away that kept the water table whole, a quiet unease in the mind of anyone who thought to dig. It would guard the hollow the way a good parent guards a child’s sleep: by not entering the room.

Then the power settled back into the general stewardship of things, carrying with it the memory of the heat, the mineral taste, the single drop falling forever. And perhaps that memory changed the power, as the best things do. It had met a being that did not want, did not hope, did not even know it was alive. And yet it had endured. The power, who could do all things, learned once more that there is a kind of making that is simply the refusal to unmake.

The hollow remained as it had been. The water seeped. The crust grew. The ferns drooped. The juniper held its gray needles in the dimness. The microbial mat spread its clear skin over the stone, building, layer by layer, a mountain no one would ever climb. And the silence deepened, not because it was empty, but because it had been seen and allowed to be.
