The benevolent and wise power came not as a storm or a vision, but as a pause in the sound. It stood on the hollowed stair and let the chord pass through it until it could hear the seven pipes separately, and then as one. It did not enter the room. To enter would have been to disturb. To enter would have been to make a thing happen. So it stayed on the threshold, exactly where the sound and the dust and the heat met the world outside, and it listened.

From the door it saw the room whole. It saw the hundreds of niches, the jars, the stone heads with their drilled mouths, the copper strips hanging motionless. It saw the mineral light in the floor-channel and the fine line of dust held at chest height. It saw the low stone screen, and behind the screen, the seven pipes. And then it saw the Listener.

The power's first thought was pity. It saw the hollowed palms, the clay spine, the copper tallies, the single round mouth-hole. It saw the dust line passing through the torso like a horizon. It thought: *This one has been here too long. This one has been forgotten. This one should be released.*

Then it looked again, with the long sight that wise powers have, and it understood.

The Listener was not forgotten. It was the one who had not left. The others had placed their stone heads in the niches and gone down the stair, but this one had stayed, not because it was bound, but because the note required a counterweight. The room was a bell that had been ringing for longer than any language, and a bell cannot ring without silence around it. The Listener was that silence. Its stillness was not imprisonment; it was the function that kept the chord from becoming noise.

The power considered what it might do.

It could bring water. It could fill the hollowed palms, cool the hot clay, wash the dust from the copper strips. But water would be the first decay in a place where decay is the one thing that has never happened. The tablets would sag into slurry; the pipes would crack; the room would begin to rot. That would not be mercy.

It could stop the pipes. It could rest a hand on the gas shaft, quiet the seven notes, and give the Listener the first true silence it had known in millennia. But silence in this room would not be rest; it would be the first true noise. The standing dust would fall. The warm air would cool. The room would stop being the room. The Listener would stop being the Listener. That would not be wisdom.

It could speak. It could say the Listener's name—if it could find the name, hidden in the copper tallies—and let the figure know it was seen. But a name is a call, and a call is a demand, and the room was built entirely of offerings. Even a kind word would be a new object placed in a room with no space for objects. That would not be love.

It could touch. It could lay its hand on the warm clay shoulder and let the figure feel that it was not alone. But the figure was not alone. It was full of the chord. It was already ringing. Touch would only add a second vibration, a disturbance in a standing wave that had held for longer than the stair had been worn deep.

So the power did the one thing a wise and benevolent being can do when it meets a perfect equilibrium. It did nothing that could be measured.

It sat down just outside the threshold, on the topmost step, and it listened. Not to the room, but *with* the room. It matched its own attention to the frequency of the chord. It let the dry air pull moisture from its own form, until it was as dry as the dust line. It let its breathing slow until it stopped, not in death but in sympathy. It let the heat from the floor rise through it. It let the note fill its chest, its teeth, the back of its eyes.

It became, for a time, the second still thing. The old silence behind the pipes did not move. The new silence at the threshold did not move. And the room held.

For a long time—long enough that the dust line might have shifted by a hair, though it did not—the two silences kept each other company without touching. They were like two stones in a river of sound, one ancient and one new, both worn smooth.

Then the power rose. It did not bless the threshold. It did not leave an offering. It did not mark the path or warn away future travelers. It did not even promise to return, because a promise would be a string tied to a place that had no attachments.

It went down the hollowed stair, carrying the chord in its chest like a coal that would not cool. Behind it, the room continued. The pipes did not change pitch. The dust line did not fall. The Listener did not open its eyes, which were not eyes, and did not turn its head, which had not turned since the last brick was set.

The next morning, if morning could be said to come in that bronze dim, there was no evidence that the power had been there. The only difference was elsewhere: in the mind of the power, which now carried the sound of seven clay pipes as a permanent undertone.

And that, perhaps, was the only help the room needed: to be remembered exactly as it was, without being changed.

Because some places are not hurt. Some beings are not waiting to be saved. Some chords are not meant to be resolved. The wisest and kindest intervention is to arrive, to understand, and to leave no trace except the trace of having understood.

And that is what the benevolent and wise power did.
