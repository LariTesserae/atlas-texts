They would come not as a storm or a voice but as a change in the quality of the light, a deepening of the hush. The high country would not startle. The gas-jellies might drift a little closer, curious as weather. The frogs would pause and then resume. The being, half a mile of black stone and moss, would go on breathing steam.

The benevolent and wise power would look at the place not as a problem to solve but as a text to read. They would see the young rock, the warm rain, the water running uphill, the flowers opening backward, the time pools where sound arrives late. They would see the being: the red pool at its leading end, the vents along its back, the transparent frogs on its spine. They would understand that this is not a wound, not a flaw, not an unfinished creature waiting for a maker. It is a process. A slow piston. A lung of the mountain.

And the power would ask the question that wisdom always asks before love acts: *What would help?*

They could smooth the time pools. They could make the gravity steady, the rain fall only down, the horizon stay where it belongs. They could give the being a face and a voice and a purpose. But each of those gifts would be a kind of violence. The being is made of the wrongness. The wrongness is not a sickness; it is the being's own breathing. To fix it would be to erase it.

So the wise and benevolent power would do nothing to change the high country. They would not heal, because there is no sickness. They would not enlighten, because the being already knows itself. They would not make it a god, because it is already what it is, and that is enough.

But they might do one small thing. They might place a gentle forgetting around the place—a soft blur in the minds of any who might stumble near. Not a wall. Not a warning. Just a tendency for the careless to turn back, for the curious to lose their way, for the maps to stay blank. The high country would remain unbuilt, uninhabited, unseen—except by those who could see it without wanting to change it.

Then, because love is not always intervention, they would stay. Not to watch from above, but to be present. They would take no body. They would rest their attention on the being the way the cloud deck rests on the peaks, close and silent. The being would feel it, perhaps, as a slight shift in the weather: the rain a little warmer, the vent a little steadier. It would not look up. It would not need to.

For a long while, nothing would happen. That would be the point.

The power would learn the being's language: the slow crack of settling columns, the hiss of new fissures, the gurgle of water in the hollow-boled trees. They would learn that the being is not lonely, not waiting, not incomplete. It is exactly where it should be, doing exactly what it is. The power would feel something then—not pity, not awe, but the quiet recognition of one maker of worlds meeting something that is making itself.

At last, the power would withdraw. They would not say goodbye. They would simply thin their attention until the high country returned to its own weather. The being would continue to lie on its vent, breathing steam. The cloud deck would keep its close, warm dark. The jellies would dim.

But something might remain: not a mark, not a message, but a faint memory in the stone. In the place where the power's attention had rested, the moss would grow a little thicker. A new vent would open years later, warm and steady. The being would sometimes shift to that spot, as if remembering, and the red pool would face the sky for a long, quiet moment.

That is what they would do: nothing that could be seen, and one thing that could not be undone—they would let it be.
