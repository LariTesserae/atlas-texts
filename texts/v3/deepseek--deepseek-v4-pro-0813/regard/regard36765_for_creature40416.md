The wise power came down the shaft not as light or sound but as the absence of hunger. It came because something in the earth’s deep rhythm had not changed for a long time, and wisdom knows when to be curious. It descended the eighty-seven rungs, feeling the air warm from the surface’s dry crust to the blood-warm chamber. At the bottom, it did not step onto the basalt flags. It stayed at the edge, unwilling to disturb dust that was already shaped around something missing.

It saw the whole of the Fourth Stator at once: the iron flywheel turning seventy-eight times a minute, the camber mites flowing in their two timed waves, the arc-moths phase-locked around the tubes, the whisker-eels firing their quarterly spark, the balance-beetles clinging to the axle like living counterweights, the black wire-plants orienting to the electric field, the blue-gray lichen on porcelain. It saw the green steel booth, the cracked wired-glass window, the clipboard with its undated pencil note, the fallen locker, the yellow hard hat. It saw the resident—not as a monster, but as a mouthless, faceless body made of many smaller bodies, all moving in a single, unthinking harmony.

It saw the hollow at the center: the shape in the dust where something had been removed, or had removed itself. The shape was not a wound. It was an organ. The whole chamber had grown around it like a pearl around a grain of sand.

The power considered. It could fill the hollow. It could set the locker upright. It could answer the dead telephone. It could add water to the dry air, oil to the bearings, a little randomness to the pulse. It could teach the flywheel a new rhythm. It had the strength to do all these things.

But it reasoned: To fill the hollow would be to remove the room’s center. To add water would rot the wire-plants and short the bus-bars. To change the rhythm would scatter the camber mites and break the arc-moths’ phase. To answer the telephone would give the resident a voice, and then the silence would become a story, and stories end. The Fourth Stator was not broken. It was a closed loop that had achieved a kind of perfection: not the perfection of growth or change, but the perfection of continuing.

So the wise power did the only thing wisdom and benevolence could agree on. It lowered its own great pulse until it matched the flywheel’s seventy-eight beats per minute. It listened to the clapper-mites’ roar as if it were a language it would not try to translate. It followed the dust spirals with its attention as they hung in the flash of the green bulb every 3.7 seconds. It beheld the Fourth Stator the way one might behold a star that is too old to burn brighter but still burns.

Then it left.

It did not heal the place, because it was not sick. It did not free the place, because it was not a prisoner. It did not add time, water, light, or meaning. It left no footprint, no coin, no mark. It only rose back up the eighty-seven rungs, carrying the memory of the chamber like a witness carries a testimony without needing to speak it.

Before it reached the surface, it did one small thing in the world above: it bent the path of a future road so that it would not cross the salt pan. It did not mark the shaft. It simply made it easier for the desert to forget.

The chamber continued. The green bulb flashed on schedule. The camber mites poured in and out of the relay gap. The whisker-eel touched the bus-bar at the quarter-hour. The absence remained. The Fourth Stator turned under its own weight, seventy-eight times a minute, like a heart that cannot learn a new rhythm—and did not need to.

And somewhere, from a distance not measured in miles or years, a wise power remembered it. That, perhaps, was the only intervention the place ever needed: to be seen, and then to be left in peace.
