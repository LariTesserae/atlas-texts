The benevolent and wise power does not arrive. It has always been arriving, the way the amber light arrives through the narrow opening, the way the fog arrives every six hours. It perceives the hollow not as a defect but as a completed thing, a small world that has reached its stable shape.

It sees the water-film, the moss that suppresses repair, the drip-crabs, the pipe-frogs, the ash-flies, the bell mites, the skinks, the eels, the glass spiders, and the mirror-wren. It sees the mirror-wren most clearly, because the bird has learned to speak the wall’s language, and that is a kind of prayer.

The power considers its options. It could seal the seam, restore the ceramic, silence the frogs, dry the pools. It could widen the opening, let in more light, bring true rain, plant forests. It could give the mirror-wren a song of its own. It could make the hollow safe, or make it larger, or make it permanent.

But the power is wise, and wisdom knows that the hollow’s beauty is not in spite of its limits but because of them. The water is warm because the wall breathes. The animals are strange because the world is manufactured. The mirror-wren disappears because it agrees with the ceramic. To change any of this would be to unmake the garden.

So the power does the only thing that is both benevolent and wise: it does nothing that can be seen. It does not heal the wound. It does not enlarge the seam. It does not speak in thunder or light. Instead, it performs the smallest possible act of care: it notices the hollow completely, holds it in attention for one full cycle of fog and clearing, and then lets it go.

But before it withdraws, it leaves a single, almost imperceptible gift. It adjusts the pressure valve by a fraction of a degree, so that the hiss now carries a second, softer note beneath the first—a note that says, in the language of the wall, *I see you. Continue.*

The mirror-wren, pressed against the wet ceramic, hears the change. It tilts its head. It listens. Then it opens its throat and copies the new note perfectly, adding it to its warning call. The other animals do not notice. The crabs keep sifting. The frogs keep calling. The fog comes and goes. But the hollow’s rhythm has shifted by a hair, and the place is, if possible, even more itself.

The power withdraws, leaving no trace except a small bird that now speaks a two-note hiss, and a hidden garden that continues to breathe in the dark.

This is what a wise power does: it protects by not interfering, and it loves by paying attention.
