They would do less than they could.

The place and the being are not separate. The Throat is a breathing organ of the mountain—not a god, not a wound, but a long vertical lung of black glass and pale mineral. The city is lodged in its windpipe, and the mountain tolerates it only because it has learned to be small and soft. So any wise power that came looking would not face a landscape and a people. It would face two living creatures: the Mountain, whose language is tremor and gas, and the City, whose language is arrangement and hush.

The power came as a change in the air, not as light. Light, in that place, is only slightly less precious than water, and a strange light would have been an alarm. It came as a faint warmth along the vent-breath, an easing of the dust across the upper rim, a tiny settling of static on the busbars where the glass-flies gathered. No one saw it. That was already wisdom.

A lesser kindness might have wept at the waterless ledges and opened the sky. But rain would have dissolved the salt pan, drowned the smelt-mites, turned the ancestor-powder to corrosive mud, and spoiled the electrolytes. A lesser kindness might have conjured green things into the black rock. But green would starve the pale mats, clog the spiracles, soften the city’s careful chemistry, and fill the Throat with pollen and rot. A lesser kindness might have stilled the mountain to keep the people safe.

That would have been the cruelest gift.

The Throat-born are not merely people who endure instability. They are made by it. Their feet read the stone. Their markets fold and reassemble. Their bridges retract. Their law says, *Do not trust the rock.* If the rock became trustworthy, they would grow tall and careless. They would sing in a place where loudness means rockfall. And the first real tremor, when it came—because it would come—would find them no longer small in the throat.

So the wise power did nothing that could be seen.

It studied the mats first: the pale blue-gray fuzz on the warm vent lips, the sulfur-yellow crusts, the rust-black stains in the slag. It saw that these were the true floor of the world. It could have thickened them. It did not. If the fuzz grew too well, the crickets would swarm, the glass-flies would short the circuits, the salt-lopers would multiply and strip the pan. The city had tuned itself to exactly this abundance. To add more life would have been to subtract from the arrangement.

It studied the people then. It saw a child named Soot-Whisper learning to walk with one bare toe against the stone. It saw a rigger testing a bolt with a brass sounder and her open palm. It saw lamps flicker with messages, a market shifting silently from a settling ledge, a dish-ward brushing salt from the bronze curve of the Salt Clock. It saw a family on Level Thirty-Seven pause all heavy work for three days because a new child had been born. It saw the salt-lopers step over the white floor at dawn, leaving footprints no one would need to read.

And the power thought: *These are not people who lack. They are people who have been chosen by a narrow, acid beauty.*

It considered speaking to them. But any true voice would be thunder. Any visible sign would become a story. The story would become a debt. The debt would become a god. They already had the Mountain, which asks only that they be attentive. A new god would not be so restrained.

So the wise power did one thing only.

For three minutes, it caused the wind over the rim to curl a little less sharply. A dust-spiral crossed the Throat that morning softer than usual. It rattled no shutters, lifted no cable, set no pebble loose. It was not a miracle. It was indistinguishable from weather.

But on Level Eighteen, an old woman who had not slept well for years slept an extra half hour. On a lower bridge, a fraying knot held quiet, and a rigger noticed the wear by the absence of its usual thin singing. In the smelter galleries, a child did not drop a tray of microbial cake when the ledge shivered, because the ledge did not shiver. A glass-fly settled on an insulator and did not arc. The Salt Clock dropped one crystal exactly on time, and the small *tink* was clear.

Then the power withdrew.

It did not promise to return. It did not leave a sign. It did not mark them as its own. It only added to the world a single morning that was, by a fraction, easier to inhabit. Some of the Throat-born noticed and thought, with their old half-humorous reverence: *The Unquiet One is in a good mood.* Most simply went on with their work.

And the city continued, small and folded, moving when the mountain moved, never quite silent and never quite still, under the pale coin of the sky.

That was the whole act. To be, for a few minutes, the difference between a wind and a rockfall—and then to leave no trace.

A wise power, meeting the Throat, would not save it. It would not water it, green it, still it, or explain it. It would love it precisely. And precise love, in a place where a loud sound can bring down the mountain, is mostly a matter of not being loud.
