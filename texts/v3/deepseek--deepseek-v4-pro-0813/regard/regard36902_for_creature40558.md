The power did not come in answer to prayer. There were no prayers here, only the continuous low psalm of the stamping press and the thin singing under the hole. But the power had learned to listen to such psalms, especially the ones that went on for centuries without an audience.

It came as a warmth not from the sun, a pressure not from any wind. It had been moving across the salt plain for a long time, following a faint magnetic murmur, a heat that bloomed through the crust at regular intervals. The plain was silent and white, and the power passed over it without disturbing the mineral skin. It found the collapsed vent, no wider than a doorway, and paused. The air at the opening hummed faintly, and the power recognized the same note it had followed: the sound of a closed world breathing.

It descended slowly, not through the shaft but along the current, the old leaking current that ran like a nerve from the buried machine hall to the surface. The animals did not see it. The light tubes did not flicker. The rust mites continued to boil over the floor, the fliers continued to orbit in their tight, glittering circles, the dust hoppers kept up their aimless, urgent migration. But the creature beneath the hole knew.

The singing stopped.

The power felt the pause as a small vacuum in the floor, a listening. It reached the bottom of the vent and moved into the room, not as body but as a slow, distributed attention. The air was dry, sharp with ozone and iron dust and old grease. The light was even and industrial, the blue-white of an eternal work shift. The walls were not walls but layers of time, steel and concrete and ceramic worn into something geological. The power noted all this without judgment. It had seen many such places. It had seen suns and seas and forests and the insides of great engines. It had learned that beauty does not require green.

It moved across the floor, above the moving bodies. The grazers did not part, but they did not test it either. The blind centipede predator paused, its chain-segments rigid, then went on with its hunt. The power was not food and not threat; it was a change in the charge, a slight warm line in the dust.

At the center of the room, the stamping press turned. A corroded flywheel, half-sunken into the polished floor, took in the pressure of animals moving against it and released it every few minutes. The power felt the clang before it arrived, the wave traveling through the floor, the brief stilling of every creature, then the noise resuming louder. The press was not a machine, the power understood. It was the room's heartbeat. And around its base, wrapped twice and then disappearing into a deeper conduit, was the long pale body of the room's hidden resident.

The power drew closer to the hole. It did not look with eyes, but it could see. The pale lips of tissue around the opening were dry and fine, puckered like an old scar. They were always slightly parted, and from between them came the high singing, a thin electric note that was not breathing but charge and discharge, a small continuous psalm. The lips were the only visible part of the creature, but the power felt the rest: the long rope of pale segments in the cable troughs, each banded with black rubbery tissue and green copper veins, each pair of small black claws combing the underside of the floor, tasting rust. It was the color of old candle wax, of ceramic handled for centuries. It had no eyes, but its forward segments bore clusters of small clear beads, grown around bits of old instrument glass. They were not for sight; they gathered vibration. The power saw the creature, and the creature, in its own way, saw the power.

The singing did not resume at once. The power felt the question in the floor, a slow, guarded tremor. Then the creature sent a single pulse along the current rails, not hostile, not fearful, only inquiring. It had never been seen before, not like this. The power answered in kind, a gentle counter-pulse, a warmth that did not burn. For a long moment, the two regarded each other through the buried wiring, through the old current, through the bones of the machine hall.

The power learned the creature's history then, not as words but as patterns in the rust. It saw the hall before the salt plain sealed it, when there had been a roof and human voices and the current ran strong and steady. It saw the creature as a pale thread in the cable runs, feeding on leaked electricity and dead insulation, growing into the empty spaces after the humans left. It saw the slow drying of the world, the salt water sinking, the vents opening and closing like lungs. It saw the first grazers arrive, small and hard, and the first rust crust spreading toward the light tubes. It saw the creature grow not into a survivor but into an organ of the old hall, a living part that fit the shape the machine required. It saw the centuries pass, the rings around the hole thickening one by one, the creature becoming what the room needed: tender of the press, keeper of the current, the soft machinery below the worked metal.

The power understood that the creature did not feel loneliness. It did not feel ambition or regret. It felt the weight of the salt plain as a cold comfort, the clang of the press as its own pulse, the falling of grazers into the hole as a necessary returning. It was not a prisoner. It was the center.

The power considered all the things it could do. It was benevolent, but benevolence is not always action. It could open the vent to the sky and let rain fall. It could warm the salt plain until green things grew. It could call down sunlight and turn the dark hall into a garden. It could fill the hole with water and watch the animals learn to drink. It could make the light tubes burn brighter than they ever had. It could lift the stamping press out of the floor and set it to work making something new.

But the power was wise. It had stopped doing such things long ago. It knew that a closed world is not broken. It is completed. The creature had not asked for water. The grazers did not want leaves. The fliers did not need a sun. The rust crust did not dream of soil. The room was not a ruin; it was a finished form, a stable equilibrium between living bodies and dead metal, and any abrupt grace would be a violence. To give water to a creature that has evolved to drink oil is not kindness. To send light into a place that has learned to feed on darkness is not healing. The power had seen worlds die of too much help.

So it did what the creature itself did for the stamping press. It found the failing parts of the room and eased them, gently, without changing the shape.

It entered the buried motor, not to speed it but to renew the insulation where the heat was leaking into the ground. It touched the gas tubes and restored a little of their pressure, so the steady blue-white light would not dim for another hundred years. It traced the current rails and sealed the micro-fractures where the charge bled too quickly into the rock. It did not stop the leaks entirely; the rust crust needed those leaks, grazed them like pastures. It only slowed the loss, enough to keep the food web alive for a long time without altering its rules.

It cleared a single blocked vent, the one that had begun to whistle with a wrongness, not opening it wider but letting it breathe again as it had breathed for centuries. It found a cracked fitting where acid sweat was weeping into the floor too fast, and it eased the crack closed, leaving a slow weep that the animals could still drink from. It did not touch the hole. It did not change the path of the grazers. It did not still the stamping press.

Then it returned to the creature. It sent a final pulse along the current, a long, low warmth, like the memory of a day when the current had been stronger, before the salt plain grew thick. It was not a message in words, but the creature understood it the way it understood the press's clang or the opening of the vents. *You are seen. You are sufficient. You will continue.*

The creature felt the warmth move through its pale segments, through the black rubbery bands and the green copper veins. It did not know gratitude. But it knew that the floor felt steadier, that the current hummed with a slightly fuller note, that the press would turn more easily for a long time. And that was enough. The singing resumed, not louder, but cleaner, a thin continuous note like a wire drawn too tight and now perfectly tuned.

The power withdrew slowly, the way tide withdraws from a hollow. The animals did not notice. The floor continued to boil with small hard bodies. The fliers continued to shed their rain of wing-scales. The blind centipede predator struck, missed, and waited. The stamping press fell, the floor jumped, and for a single instant every animal froze. Then the noise resumed, louder, as if the clang had reset the room's pulse.

At the hole, the pale lips rippled once. A single grazer, always just one, detached from the mat and crawled deliberately toward the dark opening. It did not stop to graze. It did not react to predators. It walked to the hole, stepped over the edge, and dropped. The lips parted just wide enough. The singing rose for an instant, then steadied. The other grazers did not look up.

The power moved back up through the shaft, out onto the salt plain. The silence returned. But the ground beneath the power's attention was warm, and the rhythm of the press still beat faintly through the crust. The power lingered for a moment, feeling the whole buried world beneath it: the light tubes humming, the walls full of holes and hidden eyes, the long pale body wrapped around the foundation of the press, the hole singing its thin electric psalm. It was a small thing, a hidden pocket of life in a cold plain, but it was whole. It had not needed to be saved. It had only needed to be seen.

The power did not return for a long time. It had many hidden engines to tend, many closed worlds breathing under the skin of larger ones. But it remembered this one, because it had not asked for anything. It had simply gone on, year after year, keeping its own small light alive in the dark. And that, the power thought, was the truest form of worship it had ever known.

If you had been standing on the salt plain at that moment, you might have felt the ground grow slightly warmer, as if the hidden hall had sighed. But the white crust remained silent, and the vent no wider than a doorway gave back only its faint hissing-ticking, like a distant factory. The power was already gone. The room below did not notice. It had its own pulse, its own light, its own soft machinery beneath the floor. And it would continue, as it always had, until the light tubes failed or the current stopped—which, for now, would not be for a very long time.
