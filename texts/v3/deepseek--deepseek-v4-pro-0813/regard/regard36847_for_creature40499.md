**What the Power Did**

It came to Nooth in the hour before dawn, when the walls beaded with first sweat and the dew-wives were still asleep. It did not come as wind, though the wind felt it pass. It did not come as light, though the electric lamps on the main terraces steadied as if cupped by a warm hand. It came as attention—a pressure felt only by those paid to listen.

Ansa woke with her good ear ringing. She pressed the brass cup to the wall and heard something that was not a sap-joint and not a whisper. It was like a question mark made of air. She did not ring the botanists. She sat very still and listened.

Down in the eastern amphitheatre, the night shift of sap-cutters paused. The resin they were tapping ran clear and sweet, so clear it looked like water. One of them, a man with amber dust in his eyelashes, placed a piece of salt in a wall crack and whispered, *Thank you.* He did not know why.

The power moved through the city slowly, the way a thought moves through a dream. It saw the Hollow Meridian, where two trains crossed at right angles in space and never intersected. It watched the upper train’s lights pass through the lower train’s windows. It understood that this was not a wound; it was a memory of a time when the old lady’s body had been flexible and green and full of light. The knot was a place where she remembered herself alive. The power did not unknot it.

It saw the Church of the Vivus, forty hummers in undyed wool, keeping a note below the wind. It saw the goat-keepers following their goats away from a corridor that had shifted in the night. It saw the botanist Olin with his doubled eye, tracing chalk lines from a new knot that had opened near the school. The knot was small and gentle, a staircase that led to the same room twice. The power watched and did not interfere.

Then the power went down, through the wet halls where pale bacteria bloomed and receded with the old lady’s breath, through the deep cool chambers where the dead were folded into heartwood, down to the place where the Vivus was not stone but something closer to a nerve. There it found the old lady.

She was vast. She was the city. She was the breath and the whisper and the wind and the condensation. She was dreaming in geological time. And she was not dead. She had never been dead. She had only stopped moving on the surface, the way a sleeping animal stops moving, but the dream went on.

The power was old enough to have seen her when she was green and spreading across the plateau. It had seen her root-mind working, her million-year thought. It could have spoken to her then. It had chosen not to. But now the little warm brain of the city—the few thousand people, the dew-wives and sap-cutters and rail-gangs, the children with their chalk, the goats with their iron bells—had drawn the power back. Not because they were in danger, but because they had learned to live inside a dream without waking it. That was rare.

The power sat with the old lady. It did not sit in a body; it folded its attention into a warm patch on the wall. It matched its breathing to the 26-hour cycle. It tasted the mineral sweat. It listened to the wind playing the city like a flute. After a long time, it asked the only question that mattered:

*Do you want to wake?*

The old lady did not answer in words. Her voice was a slow exhalation of sap and air and the creak of ancient fibres. It moved through the walls, through the deep halls, through the church’s hum, through Ansa’s brass cup. The answer came not as sound but as a settling, a sigh of pressure released.

*Not yet. Let them listen a little longer.*

The power understood. It did not heal her, because she was not sick. It did not seal the knots, because they were her way of turning in her sleep. It did not multiply the gardens or tame the wind or bring back the great green canopy. It did not make the city larger or smaller. It did not draw back the mist. It did not speak to the people in a voice of thunder.

Instead, it did three small things.

First, it touched the broken vascular bundle at the edge of the escarpment, where the waterfall emerged. It eased the grain of the stone, just slightly, so the water would pool a little before falling. By morning, a small basin had formed, clear and cold, and the rail-gangs found it on their descent. They would not know it was a gift. They would only know the water was good.

Second, it breathed into a knot that was about to tear open near the market. The knot relaxed. The ceiling, which had begun to recede, settled back into a high but honest arch. The botanists arrived with their red cord and found only a warm, dry room that smelled of old resin. They blinked at each other. Hedda, the church leader, tasted the air and said, *She dreamed a gentle dream.* They left the door open.

Third, it found the listener Ansa, still awake with her brass cup. It gave her not a message but a single pure note, lower than the wind and higher than the sap-joints. It was the note a body makes when it is at ease. Ansa heard it, and for the first time in thirty years, she wept. She did not tell anyone why. But after that day, she added a small chime to the wall of her room, tuned to that note, and when the wind blew, the city could hear it.

Then the power withdrew, but not entirely. It remained at the edge of the old lady’s breath, just outside the dream, like a friend sitting in the next room.

The next morning, the dew was unusually clear. The dew-wives tasted it and found it less salty. The children found new moss growing in a spiral near the school, and they ran their fingers over it. The goats did not balk at the eastern corridor. The trains passed through each other on schedule, and the passengers on the lower line saw the upper line as a warm transparency, like sunlight through a leaf.

No one knew what had come. That was the point. A wise power is not one that saves the city by force. A wise power is one that asks the old lady what she wants, hears *not yet*, and then sits with her until the next dream.

The power is still there, in a way. When the wind drops to a low note, when the walls sweat clean, when a child draws a room she has never seen and it turns out to have a door, the people of Nooth say, *The old lady is dreaming.* And somewhere beneath the city, in the deep heartwood, the answer is still:

*Yes. And I am not alone.*
