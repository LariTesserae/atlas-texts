The power that noticed the Low Catchment had no name that would have meaning there. It was the thing that, in other places, turned seed to stem and stem to leaf, that pulled water upward through roots and let it fall again from clouds. It had been the sun on wet soil, the warmth in a seed-husk, the patience of a glacier. It was not accustomed to places like this: no soil, no seed, no sun. And yet it found itself listening.

It heard the catchment first as a rumor in the deep stone—a persistent vibration, a pattern of ticks and groans, the sound of many small purposes moving together. It was not the sound of water alone; water had no purpose except to fall. This was the sound of water being *asked* to move, and answering. It was the sound of stone being asked to hold water, and mostly succeeding. It was the sound of a thousand hands shaping a world out of the refusal to be unshaped. The power, which had always associated creation with growth—slow, green, silent—was drawn by something that was not growth but construction: the world being built from the outside in, piece by piece, with intent.

So it came close, not as light or rain or root, but as a warm exhalation along a fault line. It slipped through the pores of the upper galleries, drawn by the faint green-white phosphorescence of the bloom. The bloom, it realized, was a cousin to the things it knew—a life that had learned to live on mineral dew instead of light, a fungus that had made peace with salt. The power touched a patch of it, and the bloom flared softly, recognizing something familiar in the dark. The power felt the bloom's tiny, insistent hunger for the coolness of mortar, for the taste of wet brick. It felt the bloom being scraped, chewed, digested, worked into the skin of the people. It felt the bloom inside the woman's wrists.

It watched her for a long time. Long enough to learn the rhythms of her work, the way her body compressed through the narrow gaps, the way her finger tapped out a question on the copper pipe. It watched her press her ear to a wall and listen, and it watched the wall listen back. It watched her catch a drip that had fallen on bare stone, a single drop freed from the channels, and it watched her redirect it with a practiced touch. That small act—so insignificant against the roar of the pumps—was, the power understood, a prayer.

The power was benevolent. It had always been benevolent. But benevolence is not the same as kindness; it is the will toward the good of the other, and the good is not always what the other would choose. The power had seen many things: forests that died when they were given too much light, rivers that flooded when they were straightened, species that vanished when their predators were removed. It had learned, slowly, that wisdom often meant staying the hand. It had learned that the most loving act is sometimes to observe without intervening, to let a thing become what it is becoming.

And what was the Low Catchment becoming?

The power lay along the network of the bloom, spread itself through the mineral crust, and listened. It listened to the water in the walls, the water in the channels, the water in the pipes, the water in the blood. It listened to the dreams of the sleepers in their shifts—the dreams of the upper galleries, of green things called trees, of a world where water fell from the sky and did not need to be caught. It listened to the children with their hollow tubes, their small ears pressed against the stone, learning to distinguish a drip of condensation from a leak of waste. It listened to the old ones, their hearing fading, being taken to the sumps to become part of the walls.

It heard no cry for rescue. It heard no prayer for change. The people of the catchment did not suffer from their darkness; they had built a world in which darkness was not suffering but condition. They did not lack for water; they were water's keepers. They did not starve for green; the bloom held the same minerals, the same life, and it had taken root in their skin. They were not incomplete. They were not unfinished. The name "Unfinished Basin" was a joke, a humility, a recognition that the work would never be done; but the power saw that the work's infinity was not a flaw but a kind of permanence. The world was always being made, and that was the only world they wanted.

So the power did what it had learned to do: it did nothing.

It did nothing for eleven generations.

It did nothing while the catchment expanded, while new wards were bricked, while the Throat was deepened and the upper galleries grew cold. It did nothing when a pump-chain broke and three Listeners were lost in the flood; it felt their absence in the walls, and it knew that their name-stones had been set into the mortar of the new channel, and it understood that loss was also part of the work. It did nothing when the bloom-knot in the woman's wrist grew hard and mineral, when her hands began to glow even in the deepest dark, when she grew old and her hearing faded and she was taken to the sumps. It did nothing when her body was mixed into the brick of a wall near the central cistern, and it felt the wall grow a little warmer, a little more attentive. It did nothing.

But "doing nothing" for a power such as this is not the same as absence. It was there, in the bloom. It was there in the condensation that beaded on the walls, in the slow knitting of mortar, in the faint pulse of the geckos' translucent bellies. It was there in the way the walls sometimes sealed themselves shut, in the way new openings appeared where a wall had softened. The people of the catchment had always known the world was alive; they had always said the catchment was still deciding what it was. The power did not change that. It simply *was* the thing the catchment was deciding to be.

And then, one day—if there were days—it noticed something that made it pause.

It noticed that the bloom on the walls, which it had inhabited for so long, had begun to grow in a pattern. Not a random lacy spread, but a deliberate, repetitive shape: a series of dots and lines. It was a language. The people had been scraping the bloom, chewing it, wearing it, and they had been, without knowing it, reading the patterns the power left in the crust. They had begun to copy those patterns into their own name-stones. They had begun to tap them out on the pipes. The power had done nothing, and yet the catchment had grown a new organ of perception: a script, a notation, a way of recording the subtle shifts of water and stone.

The power realized that this was its own doing, after all. Not through intervention, but through attention. Its presence in the bloom, its long, patient listening, had imprinted itself on the growth. The bloom had become a record of the power's attention, and the people, in their endless making, had learned to read it. They did not know they were reading a god; they thought they were reading the weather of their own walls. But the power knew. It had been seen, not as a force, but as a texture.

And so it did one thing.

It did not send light. It did not cause a rain. It did not speak in a voice or send a dream.

It simply allowed itself to be known.

Not as a name, not as a face, but as a quality of attention. On the next cycle of the cold galleries, when the air cooled and the wood drip-panels sweated, the power let a single drop of condensation fall from a high stone plate. The drop was not different from any other drop; it was cold, clear, slightly mineral. It fell through the dark, past the rope lines, past the kiln-mouths, past the half-built walls. It fell for a long time, and as it fell, it gathered the history of the catchment into itself: the sound of the first brick laid, the taste of the first bloom chewed, the heat of the first kiln fired, the silence of the first body set into the wall. It fell with all of that inside it, a drop of water that had been listening for as long as the power had been listening.

It landed on the back of the hand of a young girl who was pressing her ear to a wall in the outer clay pits, a girl with a hollow copper tube tucked into her belt. The drop was cold. She looked at it. It sat there, on the skin between her knuckles, and it did not run off. It held its shape, a perfect bead of water, and inside it—if she had looked closely—she would have seen a tiny, faint green-white glow, the color of the bloom. But she did not look closely. She simply felt the water on her hand, and she knew, suddenly and without words, that the catchment was aware of her. Not as a worker, not as a listener, not as a future wall—but as a person. That she was seen.

She shook the drop from her hand into the channel, where it joined the water and flowed toward the central Throat. She pressed her ear to the wall again. The wall was silent, but it was not an empty silence. It was the silence of something listening back.

The power returned to its long, patient attention. It would do nothing else. It would never do anything else. It would be there, in the bloom and the water and the walls, in the mortar and the bone, in the dreams of the sleepers and the ears of the children. It would be the texture of the world that was always being made. And the people, without knowing it, would continue to build a god into the stone, one wet brick at a time.
