The power did not arrive. It had been there a long time, folded into the condensation schedule and the sub-audible hum of flywheels, learning the shape of the Sort, the Upcast, the Ledger. When it finally understood, it did not speak. It asked itself the only question a wise power allows: *What is needed here?*

It watched the people with their oiled hair and gum-soled shoes, their painted handprints and wire shrines. It watched the lamp-haunters read the moth-flicker for failing panels. It watched the ledger-folk compare three maps before trusting a new route. It watched the sort-folk rest in hammocks near patient conveyor belts. It watched the sump-wardens clear the channels with hooked sticks, teaching the water where to go. It watched the path-markers repaint the handprints and test the corridors that repeated.

The power saw that the place was not merely a city; it was a creature. The people were its senses, the water its blood, the flywheels its patient pulse, the anomalies its dreams. The being who lived in it was the whole of it, awake and asleep at once. That was what had drawn attention: not a cry for help, but the steady, damp breath of a thing that had made peace with its own impossibility.

It was tempted. A benevolent power is always tempted.

It could have given them ground, a true soil, something soft and dark beneath their boots. But the city was not built for ground. Below the decks were more decks; below those, cables and pipes and the roots of other districts. To introduce natural earth would be to declare their world a false one. The power did not think it was false.

It could have given them a sun. A single bright source, a dawn and dusk. But their eyes were made for even light. A sun would cast sharp shadows, would make the doubled shadows into liars, would confuse the moths that kept the lamp-panels alive. It was not an improvement; it was a different world.

It could have mended the anomalies. The corridors that repeated, the doors that opened onto far streets, the staircases that descended half a kilometer and arrived at the same landing. But the anomalies were not wounds. They were the city dreaming. To straighten them would be to tell a sleeping mind to stop remembering. The power was not a surgeon; it was a guest.

It could have filled the silence with music. The birds opened their beaks, and the people tilted their heads. A song might have been a gift. But sound in that place was a material, tuned and swallowed. A voice from outside would not be heard; it would be an earthquake. The power did not want to break the quiet that kept the machines from waking too loudly.

It could have spoken to them directly. It could have stood on the fourth gantry as a figure of warm light and given them a name to call it. But they already had enough names: the woman who oils the north stair, the boy who counts the moths, the keeper of the seventh sump. They had the path-markers with their five-colored hands. They did not need another god. They needed to know that their own hands were enough.

So the power did the most difficult thing: almost nothing.

It began to pay attention in the way the city pays attention to water—taking note of every channel, every threshold, every small shrine. It watched a child drop a coin in a room where the floor was farther away than it looked. It counted the coin’s long fall and did not shorten it. It watched an old man trace the handprints through a corridor that repeated itself, and it did not collapse the loop. It watched a sump-warden clear a blockage of enamel beetle-shells with her hooked stick, and it did not move the shells for her. The work was hers. The satisfaction was hers.

But because attention alone can feel too abstract, the power left one small thing.

It chose a wall near the fourth gantry, where the sparrow-things roosted and the shadows sometimes lagged. It pressed its palm to the metal. It did not dent the wall or burn it. It simply left a patch of warmth that would not fade, a place where the condensation gathered in the shape of a hand and then evaporated a little faster. The patch was always a fraction warmer than the rest of the wall, always a little drier. In a world defined by condensation, that was a mark of presence.

The Path-Markers found it the next cycle. They touched it with fingers stained in rust, copper, moss-pulp, and ground enamel. They argued about whether it marked a stable path or a new door. In the end, they did not paint over it. They built a small wire shrine around it, and they wrote in the ledger: *handprint, origin unknown, stable.*

It became a place where people paused, not to pray but to rest. A drip from the overhead pipe fell every few minutes, and if you stood there, you could hear it clearly, which was rare. The lamp-panel above was tuned so the light fell without doubling the shadow. The wall-lice avoided the patch, leaving the metal bright. Children came to race their beetle-shell toys, and they always slowed at the handprint, though they could not say why.

The power watched this and was satisfied. It did not linger. It moved toward the edge of the plateau, where the made world descended into its own haze, and it looked back once. The place was no different. That was the point.

If the gutters ran a little clearer in the following cycles, or the flywheels held their warmth a little longer, or the city dreamed a little more gently, the people did not call it a miracle. They said the city was remembering something that had not happened yet. They touched the handprint and went on.

And the power, wise enough to know that love is often indistinguishable from leaving something alone, did not come again.
