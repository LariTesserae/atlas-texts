# What the Wise Power Did

The wise power came to the rim and did not enter.

It stopped at the edge of the high dry bowl, where the wind turns and drops, and looked down into the roofless cathedral. It saw the rust-and-indigo mats pulsing with slow blue thoughts. It saw the masts standing like organ pipes, their fluted skins shedding in sharp cracks. It saw the bell-kites drifting, the midges braiding the dust devils, the leather-winged singers tuning their carapaces in the flanges. It saw the arch that breathes silence, the fused ring humming, the cables looping into the mineral dust like nerves. It saw a dust devil hold its shape a second too long—a spiral, an arch, a hand—and then fall.

It understood, because it was wise, that the bowl was not a habitat. It was not an ecosystem. It was one being: the whole room, wind and all, alive.

And it understood, because it was benevolent, that the worst thing it could do was help.

The power did not bring rain.

It could have. It could have drawn water from the sky and filled the basin until the cracked polygons shone with moisture. But water would not be a gift. The mat fed on charge and mineral salts; water would short-circuit its thoughts. The masts were not thirsty; they drank the electric wind. The bell-kites and midges and singers had no need of rain. Water would turn the dust to mud, the crackle to rot, the silence to suffocation. The power knew that to a life without water, water is not life. It is poison. A lesser power would have killed the place with kindness.

The power did not still the wind.

It could have raised walls around the rim to keep out the gusts, or made the air smooth and quiet. But the wind was the blood of the bowl. The dust devils were its hands. The hissing of the wool-vegetation was its breathing. The turbulence was not damage; it was the shape of the being's waking. To still the wind would be to put the place into a coma. The power understood that some living things are made of motion, and their rest is not peace but arrest.

The power did not speak.

It considered shaping a word from the wind and setting it down between the masts. It considered making the dust rise into a hand and holding it steady, to show that it had seen the introduction. But the hand was not a request for a handshake. It was a statement: *I am this.* The whole place was already one continuous sentence, spoken in charge and pressure and low chord. An outside word, however gentle, would be noise. So the power answered in the only language that could not disturb: it kept silent, and let its attention be the only reply.

The power did not leave a gift.

It had many gifts. It could have left a pool of still water that never evaporated, or a seed that grows without sun, or a mirror in which the bowl could see itself. But the bowl already made and unmade itself every hour. It already had a mirror: the dusty hand, the arch, the shedding skins. Any new object, however beautiful, would become debris. The power knew that love can be a form of debris when it is dropped where it does not belong.

What did it do?

It did one thing.

It made the bowl hard to find.

Not by hiding it. The bowl remained exactly as it was: high and dry, open to the pale ordinary sky, full of its own noise and silence. But the wise power drew a line around the outside of the rim, not a wall, but a soft confusion. It made the approaches double back. It made the plateau look, from a distance, like a dead crater—unremarkable, waterless, empty. It made the air above the rim taste of boredom to those who might come looking for something to exploit or save. It turned away the sample jars and the measuring instruments, the pity and the plans. It allowed the place to remain unseen except by the kind of attention that asks nothing.

Then it went away, but not far.

It settled just beyond the rim, in the wind or the pale sky or the hum of the fused blocks, as the outermost layer of the place's solitude. It did not interfere. It did not alter the chord. It did not touch the mat or the masts or the dust. It simply stood guard over the fact that the bowl was allowed to be exactly itself.

And the place went on as before. The masts sang. The blue pulses crossed the mineral mats. The singers argued and harmonized in the flanges. The arch ate turbulence and gave back its perfect, impossible silence. The dust devil formed a spiral, an arch, a hand, and fell. Nothing had changed.

If the bowl noticed the power's attention, it gave no sign. Perhaps the next dust devil held its hand a fraction longer before falling, as if to say: *I know you are there.* Or perhaps not. The wind had already moved on.

And that was the point.

The benevolent and wise power did not heal the place, because the place was not sick. It did not save the place, because the place was not lost. It did not give to the place, because the place was already full. It did the only thing that true wisdom and true kindness could do: it refused to improve it.

It loved the place by making sure that no one—not even the power itself—would ever try to save it.

And if you ever find the bowl, if you are the kind of person the power did not turn away, you will know it by the silence under the wind, by the blue pulses in the mineral fur, by the hand of dust reaching up and collapsing, and by the fact that there is no water anywhere, and no thirst.
