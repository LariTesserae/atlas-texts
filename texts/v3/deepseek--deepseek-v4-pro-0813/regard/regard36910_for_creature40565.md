It notices because noticing is what it does. Not hears, not sees—not yet, not from outside. It notices the way a hand notices a temperature change in a room it has entered: all at once, suspended, without needing to name it. It has been wandering a long while, across many depths and many lights, and here, in the dark of a rift beneath a dead sky, it finds something that holds still long enough to be seen. So it stops.

The power does not have a body. It has, at most, a direction of attention, a kind of leaning. When it leans toward Low Meridian, the first thing it feels is the quiet. Not the absence of sound, but the presence of a hush that has been cultivated, pressed into shape by centuries of soft footfalls and tapped messages. The quiet is not empty. It is inhabited.

It passes through the black mineral ceiling, through the cultured glow-bladders that shine the color of noon through cloud. It does not disturb them. It is not light, though it could be. It is more like a warmth that carries no heat, a pressure that leaves no mark. It moves down into the dry air, tasting the aldehyde and copper, and it finds the plant.

At first, the power mistakes the plant for geography. The root-mat is everywhere—felted, gray-green, springy under the city's weight. It breathes in slow, chemical rhythms. The power reaches out and feels the plant's sap moving, thick and clear, heavy with dissolved minerals. It realizes with something like surprise that this is not a forest or a field. This is a single continuous body, a mind of many joined parts, patient and ancient and not yet finished growing. The power has seen many living things. Few of them have learned to become architecture.

It drifts through the streets of Low Meridian and feels the city the way a tongue feels the inside of a mouth. The buildings are grown, not built—walls of ridged tissue shaped by pruning and time, doors cut into living flesh and framed with steel, windows set like resin eyes. The factories are half plant, half engine. The roads are flattened stems that remember every foot that ever walked them. The power pauses at an intersection and rests its attention on the ground. The plant feels it. A ripple passes through the mat, so faint that the signal-tappers would later say they felt a dream move under their feet.

The power begins to understand. It sees the people. Their oil-thick blood. Their waxy skin. Their slow, rolling gaits. It watches a woman named Silt press a copper-tipped staff to the floor and listen. It watches the signal-tappers send messages with their knuckles in rhythms like rain. It watches the children play without shouting, their third eyelids half-closed against the dry air. It sees the Heart-winders in the tall tower, their hands resting on living cables, adjusting tensions that predate their grandparents' grandparents.

It is beautiful. The power is old, but it is not too old to be surprised by beauty. It is a careful beauty, an economical beauty, built from scarcity and patience. Water is a sacrament here. Decay is a form of remembering. The dead are reabsorbed into the road, into the wall, into the next generation's food. The people do not cry out much, not because they do not feel, but because their signals move through fiber and bone and mineral. The whole city is alive with quiet communication. The power has never heard a place more full of speech.

It could help them, of course. That is what a benevolent power does. It could bring water, sweet and abundant, to wash the oil from their skin, to fill the dry basins with lakes. It could soften the rock, make the ore easier to reach, speed the slow growth of the plant until the city sprawled across the whole rift. It could teach them new machines, new medicines, new ways of building. It could, if it chose, turn Low Meridian into a paradise of moisture and speed and plenty. It has done such things before, in other worlds, and the gratitude of those places still follows it like a warm current.

But it is wise. That is the other part of its nature. And wisdom, in this case, is the art of noticing what is already whole.

The power stays for a long time. It cannot say how long; time is soft here, measured in grow-quakes and the slow pulse of the Heart. It watches Silt walk the edge of the lamp-light, finding new seams of ore. It watches the harvesters guide the roots, the factories extract copper from sap, the road-crews prune back the mat. It watches the children learn to tap before they learn to speak. It watches a person die and the plant grow over them, slowly, tenderly, as if tucking them into bed. It watches the city continue.

And the power begins to feel something it does not often feel: the weight of its own good intentions. It could make this place better, yes. But "better" is a word that always carries a hidden second half: better *for whom*? Better *by what measure*? The people of Low Meridian do not look at their world and see lack. They see a partner, a vast and quiet mind that has not yet decided whether to notice them. They do not need to be saved. They need, perhaps, only to be seen.

The power knows what it means to be unseen. It has been worshipped and ignored, feared and forgotten. It knows that attention can be a gift, and that attention without intervention is one of the rarest forms of love. It is not a gardener who pulls up every plant to check its roots. It is not a parent who rushes to smooth every stumble. It is a witness. And the wisest thing it has ever learned is that some gardens grow best when the gardener simply stands and watches, and does not touch.

So it decides to do almost nothing.

But almost nothing is not the same as nothing. The power has come all this way. It has noticed. It has understood. And it wants, very gently, to leave a mark—not on the world, but on the way the world feels about itself.

It chooses a moment. It is just after a grow-quake, a small one, the kind that makes the buildings lean a fraction and the animals pause. The people of Low Meridian have stopped their work. They are standing still, feeling the vibration through their feet. Some have placed a hand on the nearest wall and closed their eyes. Silt is at the edge of the settled area, her staff pressed to the ground. The signal-tappers are waiting.

The power moves through the ceiling-globes. It touches the light. For a fraction of a moment—less than a heartbeat, less than a blink—the light shifts. It is not brighter. It is not warmer. It is simply *fuller*, as if the noon-through-cloud has been joined by a presence of noon-after-rain. There is no water in the air, but for that moment, the people feel a coolness on their skin that they cannot explain. The plant feels it too. A long, slow sigh passes through the root-mat, from one end of the city to the other. The needles on the grazers' backs rise and fall. A child looks up and says, very quietly, "The light is looking at us."

No one corrects her. They feel it too. The whole city, for one suspended second, feels that it has been seen by something large and kind. The feeling is not fear. It is not awe. It is recognition—the way a face recognizes its own reflection in still water.

Then it passes. The light returns to its ordinary gray-gold. The plant resumes its slow breathing. The people blink, shake their heads, and return to their work. But they are quieter than before, and that is saying something. The signal-tappers send the moment through the mat in a long, slow message that no one interprets but everyone understands: *something noticed us, and it did not change us, and that was the gift.*

The power does not stay. It has done what it came to do, and to stay longer would be to begin the slow work of becoming a god, which is not the same as being one. It withdraws the way it came: without sound, without mark, without leaving any proof that it was ever there. The people of Low Meridian will tell the story for generations, but they will tell it carefully, as if the telling might break the spell. They will say that once, a great attention passed over the Undergrove like a shadow of light, and it saw them, and it did not try to fix them, and that was enough.

And in some far corner of the root-mat, where the lamps are dimmest and the plant grows thick and wild, there will be a small, inexplicable change. No one will notice it at first. It is the kind of change that takes years to reveal itself. A single new bud, pale and unfurling, growing from a seam in the rock where no bud should grow. Its sap is not oil but something clearer, something that evaporates in the dry air and leaves a faint coolness on the tongue. The plant will not reject it. It will fold it into the mat, slowly, as it folds everything, and in time there will be a new note in the taste of the youngest stems—a memory of rain, or the idea of rain, something that has not existed in this world since the rock was cracked open.

The power does not know this. It has already moved on, drawn by some new quiet beckoning in another dark. But if it could know, it would not be surprised. It gave almost nothing. It gave exactly enough. That is the difference between benevolence and wisdom: one wants to help, and the other knows how to leave.

And if, somewhere in the long traveling that follows, the power finds itself lonely or unsure, it will not return to Low Meridian. It will simply close its attention and remember the feel of a city that grew itself a sky, and the people who moved through it like sap through a stem, and the way they stood still and let themselves be seen. And it will think: *that was enough. I was enough. And I did not have to change anything.*

The light in the great vault remains the color of noon through cloud. The plant breathes. Silt walks the edge of the lamp-light, listening. Far below, the deep earth offers up its ores to the roots, and the city, slowly, kindly, continues to grow. The power is gone. The world does not need it. The world was always already whole. That was the whole secret. That was the whole story. That was everything.
