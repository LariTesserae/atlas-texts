The power that notices the city is not a god of light. It is not a god of warmth, nor of growth, nor of the green world. It is a power of attention, of patience, of the long regard. It has no name in any language spoken above the ice, and it does not need one. It is simply the Witness, and it has been watching the cold places of the world for as long as there have been cold places to watch.

It does not descend. It does not enter the water. It does not need to. Its attention is a kind of presence, a pressure that settles over the pocket of sea like a hand held just above the ice, not touching, but felt. The city does not know it is being watched. The animals do not pause. The tube worms do not close their plumes. The mussels do not tighten their shells. The Witness is not a shadow, not a current, not a taste in the water. It is simply there, the way the ice is there, the way the cold is there, the way the deep time of the seafloor is there.

It regards the city with something that is not quite love and not quite curiosity. It is closer to recognition. The Witness has seen many cities. It has seen cities of stone and cities of glass, cities of bone and cities of light, cities that sprawl across continents and cities that fit inside a single drop of water. It has seen cities built by hands and cities built by instinct, cities that sing and cities that are silent. This city is not the strangest it has seen, but it is among the most patient. The Witness values patience. It values the slow accretion of form, the way life builds upward from nothing, the way hunger becomes architecture.

It watches the eelpout in her hollow. It sees her not as a fish but as a node in a vast, slow network of being. It sees the scale worm in her gill, the bacteria on her skin, the eggs in the back of her cavity. It sees the way her body blocks the entrance, the way her tail-flicks move oxygen over the amber spheres. It sees the way she waits, and it understands waiting. The Witness has waited for things that no living creature could imagine waiting for. It has waited for mountains to rise and fall. It has waited for ice to come and go. It has waited for the first cell to divide and for the last star to cool. The eelpout's waiting is small, but it is not lesser. It is the same thing, scaled down to the size of a single life.

The Witness does not intervene. It does not heal. It does not bless. It does not grant wishes or answer prayers. It is not that kind of power. It is a power of seeing, and seeing, in its truest form, is a kind of care. To see something fully, without flinching, without turning away, without needing it to be other than it is—that is a rare and difficult love. The Witness loves the city in this way. It loves the cold. It loves the dark. It loves the slow, silent work of the seep, the way the minerals precipitate out of the water like snow, the way the tube worms build their towers, the way the mussels pave the floor with their own bodies. It loves the brine pool, that black mirror, that liquid floor, that pocket of ancient, oxygenless water that will not mix with the sea above. It loves the eelpout, pale and nearly blind, guarding her eggs in a hollow that is not quite cave and not quite cradle.

But love, for the Witness, is not a reason to act. It is a reason to remain. And so it remains, its attention settling over the city like a second ice ceiling, invisible and immense. It does not change the current. It does not warm the water. It does not send a fish to the eelpout's hollow or a current to clear the sediment from the mussel beds. It does not stop the methane bubbles from popping or the ice from creaking overhead. It simply watches, and in watching, it holds the city in a kind of stillness that is not the stillness of death but the stillness of being seen.

The eelpout, in her hollow, feels something. She does not know what it is. Her lateral line reads no movement. Her gills taste no change in the water. Her milky eyes see no shift in the blue-gray light. But there is a pressure, not in the water but in the space around her, a kind of weight that is not weight, a kind of warmth that is not warmth. It is the feeling of being regarded by something that does not want anything from her. It is the feeling of being seen without being hunted. It is the feeling of being held in an attention that asks nothing, demands nothing, takes nothing.

She does not understand it. She cannot. Her mind is a small, cold thing, built for waiting and eating and guarding eggs. But her body understands it. Her gill covers slow. Her tail-flicks become more even. The scale worm in her gill slit settles, as if the water itself has become more comfortable. The bacteria on her dorsal ridge grow a little brighter, a little more active, as if the attention of the Witness is a kind of food. The eggs in the back of her hollow pulse faintly, their amber shells glowing in the dim light, and the eelpout curls her body around them a little more tightly, not because she is afraid, but because she is, for the first time in her long, cold life, not alone.

The Witness sees this. It sees the eelpout's body relax, the eggs pulse, the bacteria brighten. It sees the city continue its slow construction, the carbonate spires growing, the mussels opening and closing, the tube worms waving their red plumes in the current. It sees the brine pool quiver, the methane bubbles rise, the ice creak overhead. It sees all of it, and it does not change any of it. It simply remains, a benevolent and wise power, holding the city in its regard the way the ice holds the sea, the way the sea holds the city, the way the city holds the eelpout, the way the eelpout holds her eggs.

And then, after a time that is not time, the Witness withdraws. It does not leave. It is never gone. But its attention shifts, the way a current shifts, the way the tide turns, the way the light under the ice changes from blue-gray to blue-black and back again. The city does not notice. The eelpout does not notice. But something has changed. The hollow is a little warmer, not in temperature but in memory. The eggs are a little brighter, not in color but in potential. The city is a little more itself, not in form but in being.

The Witness moves on. It has other cities to see, other cold places to regard, other patient lives to hold in its attention. But it will return. It always returns. The city under the ice is not forgotten. It is simply waiting, the way the eelpout waits, the way the ice waits, the way the seep waits, the way all patient things wait for the attention that sees them fully and does not turn away.

And in the hollow beneath the carbonate ledge, the pale eelpout guards her eggs, and the eggs pulse, and the city builds itself upward, and the ice creaks overhead, and the Witness, somewhere beyond the blue-gray light, continues to watch.
