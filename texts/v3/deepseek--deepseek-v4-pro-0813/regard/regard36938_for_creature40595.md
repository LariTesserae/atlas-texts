THE LISTENER AND THE HIGH MAT

There are powers that move through worlds like a hand through still water: slowly, so as not to disturb the sediment, so as not to break the surface tension where sky meets ground. Some of these powers are old, and some are older than old, and some are so ancient that they have forgotten their own names and remember only their purpose.

The power that found the High Mat was not the oldest of its kind, nor the youngest. It was a listener. It had crossed many worlds, and it had learned that most places do not need saving; they need to be seen as they are.

It came to the rim at dawn, when the cloud sea below was still thick and white, rolling against the cliffs like a second ocean. The ground lifted a finger-width as the mat drew water from its deep roots. The power felt the movement through the stone beneath its attention—a slow, continent-sized breath. It waited.

It watched the fog climb over the rim and drag its belly across the living ground. It watched the water condense into the cups formed by old broken crowns and folded leaf bases. It watched a cup-lizard drink, then a root-hare, then a bird, then a person. It watched the person bend, dip a hand into the brown water, and speak a word too quiet for the wind to carry.

The power listened.

It stood on the rim for a long time—days that might have been centuries, or centuries that might have been days—and it did nothing. It observed the towers, fluted and hollow, their bases swollen into buttresses that spread across the mat like the roots of a different world. It heard the drummers, the blind soft-backed animals that struck the inner walls with their knobbed tails of callus, sending low booms out through the flutes and across the plain. It heard the cicadas' metallic drone, the piping of ledgebirds, the clatter of wings, the shriek of a raptor falling on the hare-run. It heard the sound of hunger and flight, of feeding and fear, of wind and wood.

It watched the people. They were few and their marks were faint. A drystone arc built against a root flare. A woven platform hung in a fork. A doorway carved into living wood and closed with a slab of thick bark. They moved in pairs, carrying poles with hooked bone tips, and they never crossed open ground at dawn or dusk. They kept fire in lidded clay pots and lit it only on flat stones. They cut the mat only when they must, and only a wound no longer than a hand, and they poured water into the cut and sat with it for a day.

The power felt something it had not felt in a long time: humility.

These people were not waiting for a god. They were already in conversation with the ground. Their wisdom was not less than the power's own; it was simply shaped differently—tuned to the frequency of a world that breathed, to the slow healing of scars, to the language of a mat that remembered. The power understood that to act would be to interrupt a conversation that had lasted for millennia.

And yet, the power saw one small thing.

It was on the windward side of a dying tower. The drummer had gone silent there years ago. The tower's flutes were slowly filling with a different kind of silence—not the dense, living silence between heartbeats, but the hollow silence of a body without a pulse. The people avoided the tower. They said the drummer's silence was a sign that the tower was passing from the world, and that to camp beneath it would be to sleep in the shadow of a corpse.

But there was a young woman—a cupwalker, thin and sun-dark, with pale scars on her palms—who visited the tower each evening. She did not camp there. She did not climb its flanks or enter its hollow core. She simply came to the base, where a crack had formed in the mat, and she knelt by the fissure and poured water into it from a small clay cup. Then she sat with the wound for a while, as the people did when they cut the ground. She spoke to the wood in a language too quiet for even the wind to carry.

The power watched her for many evenings. She was not trying to heal the tower. She had no expectation that the drummer would return. She was not performing a ritual from her tradition. She was simply keeping the tower company. She was offering companionship, not intervention.

The power realized, with a feeling like breath catching in a throat it did not have, that this was the purest form of love.

So the power decided to do something.

Not to the tower. Not to the people. Not to the mat.

It decided to do something to itself.

It would become, for a single season, the wind.

It had done this before, in other worlds, in other forms. It had been rain on a desert, warm and patient, coaxing shoots from cracked earth. It had been light on a cave wall, steady and low, guiding lost creatures home. It had been silence in a place of terrible noise, giving rest to the overwhelmed. It knew how to inhabit an element without disrupting it, to become a part of a system rather than an intervention in it.

So it became the wind.

It learned to play the flutes of the dying tower the way the living wind did—not perfectly, but close. It found the resonance, the hollow note that trembled through the ground, the sound the tower made when it was alive. It blew gently through the hollow core, not a gale, not a storm, but a soft, steady breath. It added a new note—not a miracle, but a harmony.

The young woman, kneeling by the fissure, heard it.

It was a low, warm tone, like a voice saying, "I am here."

She looked up. The wind moved through her hair. It touched her face, her closed eyes, the rim of the small clay cup she had poured into the crack. She smiled—not because she believed in gods, but because she believed in the tower. And the tower, for that one moment, believed in her.

The power, now the wind, moved on across the mat. It carried the sound to every listening ear—the cup-lizards by the water, the root-hares in their shallow crevices, the ledgebirds on their outer buttresses, the raptors hanging in the high indigo. It carried the sound to the people in their shelters, who paused in their work and tilted their heads, feeling the note move through their bones. It carried the sound to the other towers, which answered with their own booms, low and patient, like slow thunder.

And that was all.

No lightning. No parting clouds. No resurrection of the drummer. No healing of the great fissure. Just a single note, heard by a single person, in a place where the sound never stops.

But the power knew that this was enough.

It had not saved the world; it had joined it.

---

The season passed.

The power, as the wind, learned the landscape by heart. It learned the way the fog climbed over the rim each dawn, the way the mat rose and settled, the way the cups filled and were drunk dry. It learned the taste of pollen and the weight of wings. It learned the sound of every tower, the particular note of each flute, the rhythm of each drummer's call. It learned the paths the people walked, the pale stones set into the living surface, the scars that were remembered as debts.

And it learned the young woman's name.

It was not a name she spoke often. The people here had no name for themselves as a whole, and they used names sparingly, as if a name were a small scar on the air. But one evening, as she knelt by the fissure and poured her cup of water into the crack, she spoke it aloud, to the tower, to the wind, to whatever was listening.

"Sera."

The power, as the wind, carried the name away. It held it carefully, like a seed. It did not speak; it only listened.

Sera continued to visit the dying tower. She brought water from the cups, carrying it in a gourd she had hollowed and sealed with resin. She spent longer there each evening, as the season turned. She began to clear away the dead epiphyte stems that had fallen into the fissure. She built a small drystone seat against the root flare, so she could sit more comfortably. She was not trying to heal the tower; she was still only keeping it company. But the company was deepening.

The power, as the wind, watched. It played the flutes gently when she came, and the tower answered with the note it had learned to make. The sound was soft, warm, like a voice that had been silent for a long time and was slowly remembering how to speak.

One evening, Sera did something new. She did not pour water into the fissure. Instead, she poured the water into the base of the tower itself, into a hollow between two buttresses where the living wood had split. She pressed her palm against the wood and listened.

The power, as the wind, paused. It felt the tower's attention shift, like a sleeping animal rousing at a familiar touch. The wood was not dead. It had gone dormant, waiting, conserving its strength. The drummer's silence had not been the death of the tower; it had been a kind of hibernation, a withdrawal into the deep roots. The tower had been waiting for something—a signal, a season, a reason to send up new growth.

Sera did not know this. She only knew that the tower felt warm under her hand, warmer than the surrounding mat. She smiled again, and she said, "I am here."

The power, as the wind, understood. It had been wrong to think that no intervention was needed. The tower did not need to be saved; it needed to be companioned. And Sera had been doing that all along.

So the power, as the wind, did one more thing.

It blew through the tower's hollow core, not gently this time, but with a long, low, sustained breath. It played the flutes in a rising chord, not a song but a question. It asked the tower if it wanted to wake.

The tower answered.

It was not a loud sound. It was a vibration, deep and slow, like the first movement of a root in spring. The drummer, still blind and soft-backed in the inner dark, stirred. It had not died; it had only been sleeping, curled in the hollow heart of the tower, its knobbed tail still. But now it shifted. It uncurled. It found the inner wall, and it struck it once—a single, low boom that rolled out across the mat.

Sera, sitting by the tower, felt the sound pass through her. She did not startle. She closed her eyes and let it move through her body, through her bones, through the cups of water she had brought and the scars on her palms. She had been listening for this sound for a long time.

When it came again, she laughed—a small, bright sound that the wind carried away.

The power, as the wind, let itself rest. It had done what it came to do. Not to save, but to listen. Not to intervene, but to join. Not to change the world, but to become part of its sound.

---

The season ended.

The power, as the wind, felt the change in the air. The cloud sea would soon rise higher, the fog days would come, and the landscape would blur into whiteness. It was time to move on. It could not stay forever; it was a listener, not a settler. It had other worlds to visit, other sounds to learn, other people to see.

But before it left, it did one last thing.

It went to the young woman, Sera, as she knelt by the tower. It breathed through her hair one final time. It touched the hollow of her throat, where her laughter lived. It whispered a single word—not a name, not a command, but a blessing.

"Continue."

Then it let itself dissolve back into the ordinary wind, the wind that had always blown across the High Mat, the wind that played the flutes and carried the sound. It folded itself into the landscape, leaving no trace of its presence except, perhaps, a slight warmth in the air, a slight resonance in the ground, a slight memory in the wood.

The power left the High Mat.

It would return, perhaps as rain, perhaps as a seed, perhaps as a memory in the mind of a young woman who would one day grow old and tell her children that once, the dying tower had spoken in a voice like the wind, and the wind had said, "Continue."

And the children would listen, and they would continue.

The sound never stops. It changes, but it never stops.

And the power, moving on through the spaces between worlds, carried the sound of the High Mat with it—the drone of cicadas, the boom of drummers, the piping of ledgebirds, the slow creak of the mat lifting before dawn. It carried the sound like a small fire in its chest, warm and steady.

It had not intervened. It had not saved. It had not changed the world.

It had only listened, and in listening, it had learned that the deepest act of love is to become part of the sound.
