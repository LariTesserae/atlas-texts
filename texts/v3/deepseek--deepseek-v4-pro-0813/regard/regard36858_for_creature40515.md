The power came not as a storm or a voice but as a change in the way the fog held the light. It had no shape, or rather it had every shape and chose none. It stood on the plateau the way a thought stands behind the eyes—present, but not in the way of things.

It watched for a long time. It watched the fog roll in and out on its twice-daily pulse. It watched the vents hiss and the small blue flames flicker like pilot lights. It watched the vent-folk rise from the moss, slow as stones turning over, and move through their day, which was not a day but a season of small acts.

The power was benevolent, which meant it wished them well. It was wise, which meant it did not assume it knew what “well” was.

It saw a child fall into a cold hollow, too far from the warm pools. The child’s skin was still soft, its gills not yet closed. It lay in the wet moss, breathing once a minute, and the cold began to take it. The power could have lifted it. It could have warmed the water. It could have called the elders with a note through the mineral tubes. It did none of these things, not because it did not care, but because it was still learning.

It watched the elders. They did not count their children. They did not call out. But one of them, an old one with moss thick on its back, paused at the edge of a seep and turned its head. It tasted the air with the pits along its jaw. Then it moved, not quickly, but without hesitation, through the fog, over the gravel, to the cold hollow. It found the child. It did not scold or grieve. It simply lay down beside the child, curled its long body around it, and breathed. The child’s breathing slowed to match. The moss under them began to warm. After a long time, the old one rose and carried the child back to the warm pool, not in its arms but on its back, the child clinging with newt-soft fingers.

The power understood. The vent-folk did not need a savior. They needed the fog, the moss, the slow attention of their own kind. They were not waiting for a god. They were the god’s hands, if there was a god, and they did not know it.

The power considered the land. It saw a dying vent, one whose flame had gone out. The vent-folk had gathered around it. They did not mourn. They sang a low note into the dead tube, and the note came back wrong—flat, empty. Then they began to dig. They dug with their fingers, slowly, for days. The power could have opened the vent with a thought. It could have called the magma closer. It did not. It watched them dig, and it saw that the digging was not only work but a kind of prayer. When the vent finally breathed again, a small blue flame catching at the lip, the vent-folk did not cheer. They sat back on their haunches and hummed the tube’s true pitch, and the tube answered. The power felt something like humility.

It saw the stone-colored cat take an old one, too slow to rise. The herd moved on. The vent-folk did not hunt the cat. They did not curse it. They left the body where it lay, and the moss grew over it, and the next season there was a patch of copper lichen in the shape of a shoulder. The power understood that death was not an interruption here. It was part of the slow machine.

The power thought: If I give them anything, what would it be? Fire? They already tend flames. Words? Their language is the land’s own breathing. Safety? Safety would make them strangers to the fog. Knowledge? They know the map of the plateau, the timing of the fog, the taste of the water. They know the names of the vents, which are not words but pitches. They know how to freeze and thaw, how to wait, how to listen.

The power thought: They are not broken. They are not unfinished. They are not waiting for me.

But the power had come, and attention is a kind of touch. To leave no trace would be a lie; the fog itself had changed. So the power chose one small thing.

It found a place where the moss was thin, a cold hollow between two rock ribs, where no warm seep reached. It pressed a single stone into the ground, not a large stone, but one that held heat longer than the others. It was not magic, or not only magic. It was a stone that had once been near the magma, and the power reminded it of that heat. The stone grew warm, not hot, just warm enough that the moss around it stayed green a little longer into the cold.

Then the power withdrew. It did not announce itself. It did not ask for thanks. It became the fog again, or the light behind the fog, or the pause between two notes.

The vent-folk found the warm stone the next day, or the next season—time is different there. One of them, a young one with down still soft on its shoulders, put its hand on the stone and felt the warmth. It sat there for a long time, not understanding, but not needing to. Then it did what vent-folk do: it cleared the moss from around the stone, deepened the hollow with its fingers, and waited. The next fog came, and water gathered in the hollow, and the stone kept it warm. A frog found it. Then another. The young one hummed a note, and the mineral tubes carried it across the plateau: a new warm pool, a new place for the frogs to thaw.

The power heard the note from far away, and it was glad. Not because it had been thanked, but because the gift had become part of the land, and the land had made it its own.

That was all. The power did not return. The plateau did not know it had been visited. The vent-folk did not tell stories about a god. They only knew that the world was slightly warmer in one place, and that the frogs had a new pool, and that the fog still came twice a day.

And that, the power thought, was enough.
