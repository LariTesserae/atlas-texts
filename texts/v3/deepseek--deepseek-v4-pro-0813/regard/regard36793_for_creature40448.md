# The Power That Did Not Bless

The power came to the plateau not as a light, but as a softening of the air, a momentary drop in the wind’s pressure as if all the fluted limestone had drawn a breath. It had heard the chord from far away—not with ears, but as a disturbance in the deep quiet of its own attention—and it had come to see what kind of world could make such a sound and mean it.

It arrived at the western rim, where the choughs rode the standing wave, and for a while it did nothing but watch. The wind poured over the combed ridges and the stone answered in its many voices: hum, whistle, thump, throb. The power understood at once that this was not a cry or a call, but a state—a tuning, not a plea. The first resident, the wind, was not enduring the plateau; it was playing it. The chord had been going for ten thousand years, and the wind had never once needed to stop.

The power’s first instinct was kindness of a crude sort. It looked at the pale stone, the thin air, the cold that seemed to press behind the eyes, and it thought: *I could warm this. I could bring more rain. I could plant a few low trees in the hollows, give shade to the pikas, give the blackbird a deeper song.* It imagined the basins fuller, the moss thicker, the wind a little softer. But as it imagined, it listened. And the more it listened, the more it heard that every one of those gifts would be a violence.

Warmth would change the condensation; the nightly sweat of stone would stop; the water pockets would dry or turn green; the frogs’ small channels would become unnecessary, then forgotten. Trees would break the fluting with their roots, and their leaves would scatter into the slots and change the pitch of the wind. A softer wind would take the choughs’ playground, the swifts’ highway, the very note that shaped every nest and every call. More rain would dissolve the walls too fast, make the old equilibrium young again, send talus down into the corridors. The power saw all this in an instant, and it drew back its imagined hand.

It turned its attention to the inhabitants, one by one, as if asking them the same question: *What do you need?* But the pika, curing its grass on a warm slab, did not look up; its need was the sun and the crack it knew. The marmot, erect at its den mouth, was watchfulness itself; its need was the high whistle that meant eagle. The jackdaw, choosing a pebble for the anvil, did not ask for a hammer; it had already learned to use the stone. The frog, digging its channel from one cistern to the next, did not need a river; it needed the patience of its own hind feet. The blackbird, dropping a snail onto the polished white center, did not need a plate; it needed the sound of the shell cracking against the same stone its ancestors had used. The swifts, the choughs, the fairy shrimp, the caddisflies—each was so precisely fitted to the gap the wind and the water had left for them that to add anything would be to take away their shape.

So the power did not warm, water, plant, or quiet. It did not build a shelter or carve a sign. It did not leave a gift of food or fire. It stayed through a full cycle of the pale day and the high grey night, and it listened to the chord until the chord became a kind of silence in its own mind.

At last it spoke to the wind—not in words, but in a small pause of its own, a held note. It asked: *Do you want anything? Any change, any rest, any end?*

The wind did not gust or falter. The columns kept their hum, the slots their whistle, the blowholes their thump. The chord continued, unbroken, exactly as before. And in that continuation the power heard the wind’s answer, which was not yes or no but *I am.* The wind was not suffering. It was not waiting. It was being, and it was being in the only way it had ever wanted: at exactly this speed, exactly this pitch, exactly this direction, forever.

Then the power understood what its benevolence could mean in a place that had already become itself. It could not add. It could not fix. It could not improve. The only loving act left was to protect the place from all lesser loves, from all the other powers—human and otherwise—that would one day come seeking to help it, to map it, to bless it, to make it more comfortable or more productive or more beautiful than it already was.

So it did one thing, and only one thing. It set a seal over the plateau, not of fog or fear, but of *unnoticedness*. A faint instruction in the texture of the air: *This place is already spoken for. Look away.* Any future traveler, human or animal, would find the rim and feel a sudden, reasonable need to turn south; any surveyor would read the land as empty, any dreamer would forget it by morning. The plateau would remain unbuilt, unvisited, unblessed—not because it was hidden, but because the power had loved it enough to leave no trace, and to ensure that no one else would either.

Then the power left, if leaving is the right word. It did not vanish in a flash; it simply stopped attending, and the wind filled the space it had occupied, as if the power had been a low-pressure area that had moved on. The swifts came out of the slots as usual. The choughs rode the rim. The pikas called from their cracks. The frog returned to its channel. The blackbird dropped a snail onto the polished stone.

And the chord went on, unchanged, for another ten thousand years.
