They would do almost nothing. But the almost would be everything.

The benevolent and wise power would come, if it came at all, as a change in the light: a slight warming of the noon haze behind the wired glass. It would not part the clouds. It would not fill the room with voices. It would look at the tenant and see not a thing to be saved, but a being in the middle of a long, quiet truce with a machine. It would understand that the truce is the point.

It would reason like this:

*If I restore the tenant, I unmake the tenant. If I lift them out, I kill them. If I quiet the noise, I silence the room’s heartbeat. If I dry the air, I turn the moss to dust and the frogs to paper. If I speak, I drop a word into the sump. Therefore the only benevolence is protection, and the only wisdom is restraint.*

So the power would not touch the room. It would not heal the rust or replace the thinned galvanized plates or unseize the yellow valve. The rust is the room’s skin. The thinned plates are its memory of use. The seized valve is its one firm warning. To fix these would be to erase the place’s history.

Instead, the power would make the place forgettable. It would thicken the clouds above the ridge so that satellites read only weather. It would blur the steam plume into the general breath of the mountain. It would let the old maps decay, let the access roads wash out, let the surveyors’ instruments drift. It would plant in every curious mind a faint, reasonable reluctance—not fear, just the sense that the ridge is not worth the climb, that there is nothing up there but rust and mist. It would not build a fence; a fence is an invitation. It would not scrub the place from memory; it would let memory turn soft and green, like the insulation on the main condenser.

It might leave one small thing, but not in the room. It might leave a pressure in the weather, a tendency for storms to circle the summit, so that the room remains hard to reach. A benevolent and wise power knows that the best protection is obscurity, and the best obscurity is natural.

And then it would wait. It would not demand the tenant notice. It would not ask the frogs to sing differently. It would not make the geckos bow. It would only keep watch, the way the moss keeps watch over the rivets, the way the bromeliads hold their small cups of water: without hurry, without need.

In the room, at the next eleven-hour valve, the steam would rise as it always did. The tenant would fold itself into the tangle of pipes and listen. It might feel, for a moment, that it was not alone—not because something had arrived, but because something had chosen not to arrive. That is a rare kind of companionship: the attention that does not intrude.

The power would understand that to leave a place alone is sometimes the most loving thing a power can do. It would not call the tenant by a name, because the tenant has no name. It would not reassure the tenant that it was once human, because the tenant has no language for *once*. It would only ensure that the room could continue, undisturbed, in its damp and noisy equilibrium, for as long as the mountain chooses to breathe.

And if you asked the power what it had done, it would say:

*I saw. I hid. I let be.*

The room would not know it had been blessed. That is the true form of the blessing.
