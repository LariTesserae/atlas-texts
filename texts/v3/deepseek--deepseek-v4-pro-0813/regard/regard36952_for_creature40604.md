The benevolent and wise power came to this place as it came to all places: without announcement, without expectation, without breaking anything. It had no body, or it had many bodies; it could be the pause between one drip and the next, or the warmth in the root-mat, or the slow blue pulse traveling through a cable vine. For a long time—if time still meant anything here—it simply looked.

It saw the being.

The power had witnessed many strangenesses in its long unfolding. It had known worlds made of memory, species that lived as songs, minds that grew like crystals in the cold between stars. It had loved things that died in a single breath and things that had never been born. But it had rarely seen a being like this one: a body without a self, a physiology without a story, a machine made of living tissue that had solved the problem of living without death, without decay, without change. A closed loop that was also an engine.

The power looked at the being the way a wise thing looks: not to judge, not to improve, but to see. It saw the root-mat, warm and damp, oozing water when pressed. It saw the black basalt below, all knife-edges and conchoidal fractures, preserved and reinforced by the minerals the roots deposited. It saw the hollow roots, lined with dark rubbery tissue, drawing warm mineral brine upward with a slow, continuous suction. It saw the piston-stems, thousands per acre, moving in their sheaths with a rhythm so regular it was almost below hearing. It saw the condenser vanes, huge and lobed, holding themselves open to the wet air. It saw the chimney reeds venting steam into the permanent fog. It saw the cable vines, sheathed in waxy translucence, carrying their slow blue pulses. It saw the battery bladders, round and pendulous, glowing deep red and violet.

It saw all of this, and it understood: this being was not suffering. It was not trapped. It was not incomplete. It was not waiting to be saved. It was not even waiting. It was simply running, and in running, it was alive.

The power was benevolent. This was not a metaphor. It meant that the power desired the good of all things, and it had the wisdom to know what the good of a thing might be. For some things, the good was to grow, to change, to seek, to find. For some things, the good was to be healed, to be freed, to be taught. For some things, the good was to be left alone.

The power considered what it could give.

It could give consciousness. It could reach into the distributed network of roots and cables and membranes and kindle a single spark of self-awareness. The being would wake up. It would know that it existed. It would see its own vastness, its own solitude, its own endless loop. It would ask: *what am I?* And there would be no one to answer. It would ask: *why am I here?* And there would be no reason except the chemistry, the gradients, the heat. It would ask: *is there anything else?* And there would be no answer, because there was nothing else—only the fog, the rock, the sound of its own body running forever.

The power saw this. It saw the consciousness it might create, waking in that endless body, alone. It saw the questions that would have no answers. It saw the suffering that would follow. And it understood that to give consciousness to a being that had no need of it would not be a gift. It would be a wound.

It could give change. It could introduce a small mutation, a new rhythm, a different pattern of growth. The being would become more complex, more beautiful, perhaps even more alive in a human sense. But the loop would break. Decay would enter. Death would enter. The being would begin to consume itself, not in the gentle reabsorption it now performed, but in the hungry way of living things that must grow or die. Competition would enter, and struggle, and loss. The power saw the slow unraveling of the perfect equilibrium. And it understood that to give change to a being that had achieved stasis would not be a gift. It would be a slow destruction.

It could give companionship. It could create a second being, a partner, a mirror. But the power saw that any second being would disrupt the perfect equilibrium. It would compete for energy, for space, for time. It would introduce difference into a system that had no difference. It would break the symmetry. Or worse, it would be absorbed, dissolved into the first being's perfect homeostasis, losing its own existence. The power saw this, and it understood that to give companionship to a being that was complete in itself would not be a gift. It would be an invasion.

So the power did not give consciousness, or change, or companionship. It did not fix what was not broken. It did not heal what was not sick. It did not save what was not in danger.

Instead, the power did something else, something that was perhaps the only truly benevolent thing a wise being could do for this being. It appreciated it.

It allowed itself to feel the weight of the being's beauty: the slow thunder of ten thousand pistons moving in unison; the faint blue pulses traveling along the cable vines like thoughts in a mind without a thinker; the deep red glow of the batteries, hanging like fruit from the body of the world; the permanent fog, touched by the tips of the condensers; the warm water seeping up from the root-mat; the taste of iron and hot vegetation in the air.

It saw the elegance of the closed loop. It saw the peace of the equilibrium. It saw that this being had achieved something rare: not immortality in the usual sense, but a state of perfect self-sufficiency, perfect unity, perfect rest-in-motion. It had no need to become anything, no need to go anywhere, no need to ask anything. It simply was, and in being, it was complete.

The power understood something then, something it had not expected to learn in this dark, warm, hissing place. It understood that there was a kind of love that required nothing from the beloved. A kind of love that was not diminished by the beloved's ignorance. A kind of love that was, in itself, enough.

And so, being benevolent and wise, the power did one more thing. It became a witness.

It did not leave a sign. It did not demand acknowledgment. It did not make itself known. It simply stayed for a while—not in time, but in attention—and watched the pistons move, and listened to the steam, and felt the deep, almost subsonic hum of the being's endless functioning. It added this place to its memory. It marked it on whatever map of wonders it kept. It made a small adjustment to the world's fate—not to the being itself, but to the space around it: a slight deflection of a future asteroid, a subtle discouragement of a future civilization's interest, a gentle protection that would last as long as the being's loop.

It did not tell the being it was there. It did not require the being to know it had been observed. It simply saw, and in seeing, it loved.

And then, because it was wise, it understood that even this protection was not for the being's sake. The being did not need to be protected. It had survived the cooling rock, the young crust, the chemical gradients. It had survived time, in its own way. It would continue to survive, with or without the power's help. The protection was for the power's own sake, because it loved the being, and love, even the purest love, seeks to preserve what it loves.

So the power stayed for a while longer, not to protect, but simply to be present. It watched the steam rise from the chimney reeds and fade into the fog. It watched the blue pulses travel along the cables. It watched the pistons move, one hand's-width at a time, forever.

And then it left, but not entirely. In the way that a wise and benevolent power can, it left a part of itself behind. Not in the world, but in the world's story. It left the memory of a witness. It left the knowledge that the being was seen, and that the seeing was enough.

After the power was gone, the being continued to run. It did not know it had been observed. It did not know it had been loved. It did not know it existed. It simply continued to do what it had always done: draw up the warm brine, pump the water, condense the steam, release the charge. The pistons moved. The cables pulsed. The batteries glowed. The steam rose and returned as a fine drizzle. The roots sipped the hot water from the black glassy rock. The whole land breathed in and out, in and out, without variation, without end.

And the power, from whatever distance it had gone, looked back and saw it running, and was content.

For the being had no need of the power's blessing. It had no need of the power's love. It had no need of anything. And the power, being wise, understood that the deepest love is sometimes the love that leaves the beloved unchanged.

So it did nothing. And in doing nothing, it did the only thing that a truly benevolent and wise power could do.

It let the being be what it was.

And that was enough.
