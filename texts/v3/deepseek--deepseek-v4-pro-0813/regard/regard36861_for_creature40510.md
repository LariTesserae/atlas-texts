A benevolent and wise power, drawn by the noise and the heat, came as a change in the quality of attention. It did not descend the ladder; it came the way weather comes, through the slot in the rim, through the cloud, along the single black wire. At the head of the waterfall it paused and regarded the notch as a single body: the hot seep, the young basalt, the penstock shaking with water, the turbine spinning too fast to see, the moss following rivet lines, and the woman moving through the middle of it with her head tilted to one side.

To such a power the place was not a hiding hole or a ruin. It was a slow, steep-sided mouth held open by water, rust, and small green things. The woman was not the keeper. She was more like a hand, or an eardrum, or the one point of skin where the body could feel itself. The power saw that she slept in the lifted roots above warm soil, drank from the bypass line, and touched the penstock as if it were a living flank. It saw her wipe the gauge face and tap the brass rim twice. It heard the bearing knock—the door inside the rock—and saw her turn her deaf middle hearing toward it as though it were the only clock she trusted.

The power could have fixed many things. It could have quieted the bearing, closed the door, made the turbine run smooth as water over a stone. It could have healed her hands, filled the white mineral creases with unbroken skin, restored the middle frequencies the waterfall had taken from her hearing. It could have lifted her out of the notch and set her down in a place with sun and birdcalls and bread. It could have stopped the basalt from undercutting the back wall, frozen the geometry against the day the columns would fall.

But the power was wise, and wisdom in such a place is not the same as repair.

To still the bearing would be to take away the door she listened for in the dark. To heal her hands would be to remove the map of the rock, the knowledge of where one sharp edge meets the next. To restore her hearing would be to fill the gaps with sounds she had forgotten how to want. To lift her out would be to make a ghost of her, because she was no longer a person who could live wholly in the world below. And to hold back the rock would be to stop the notch from learning what shape it would become.

The power considered these things the way water considers a stone: without hurry, with only the patient pull of care. It saw the many futures. In one, the bearing failed before the first heavy snow, and the woman listened as the door opened at last, and then she climbed the rim with her blanket, and the moss grew over the gauge face. In another, a visitor came down the ladder with a new bearing and a set of wrenches, and the machine’s voice changed, and she found she could not sleep because the door no longer knocked. In still another, no one came, and she grew so thin that the wind moved her as it moved the ferns, and one morning the notch was empty except for a wren, a snake, and a single black wire going up into the cloud.

None of these futures was better than the others. All were part of the body’s long breathing. The power understood that to intervene was not necessarily to bless. Sometimes the kindest thing is to be present without insisting that the thing become easier.

So the power did what such powers do when they are wise: it made itself small. It passed into the single black wire, not as electricity but as attention. It held, for one hour, the pressure in the penstock perfectly constant. The turbine whine steadied. The valve’s periodic hiss sighed once and then was quiet. The needle of the white-faced gauge ceased its nervous arc and stood still, and the woman—who had been crouched by the turbine housing with her ear turned toward the metal—felt the change before she saw it. The middle frequencies were still gone, but the gaps between them had altered. It was as if the whole notch had drawn a long breath and was holding it.

She stood. She wiped her hands on her trousers, leaving a pale mineral streak. She looked at the gauge. The needle was still. She touched the brass rim. It was warm, warmer than it had any right to be in that already warm place. She did not smile, and she did not call out. She only stood there with her head tilted, one hand on the metal, listening to the steadiness as if it were a voice that had finally learned not to stammer.

For that hour, the water still fell hard through its narrow slot. The ferns behind the pool still shivered. The midges still danced in their slow vertical column. But the woman’s shoulders lowered. The small hollowness beneath her ribs did not fill, exactly, but it ceased for a while to ache. She drank from the enamel cup. She tapped the brass rim twice. The sound rang once, clean, under the waterfall.

Then, because the power was wise, it let go.

The needle moved again. The turbine’s high clean note wavered as the water pressure changed. The valve hissed. The door inside the rock knocked, soft and far away, and the woman let out a breath she had not known she was holding. She did not look up at the wire, though she might have. She did not know she had been watched. She knew only that something had passed through the place as a cloud passes over the high country—not changing the ground, but for a little while changing the quality of the light.

The power withdrew the same way it had come, up the wire into the cloud. It did not leave a mark she could read. It did not stop the bearing from failing, or the basalt from undercutting, or the moss from growing in straight lines along the seams. It did not leave a gift she could hold in her hands. It left only the slight warmth on the brass rim of the gauge, and that faded in the falling mist.

But the moss on the gauge face did not grow for three days afterward. She cleaned it anyway, as she always did, because the gauge was not allowed to be swallowed. And where the attention had rested, the place answered in its own idiom: on the third morning she found a single small white flower growing out of a crack in the catwalk, where no flower had any reason to be. She looked at it for a long time. Then she stepped over it carefully and went on with her circuit.

The power, far away and near, watched her do this. It did not feel pride, satisfaction, or pity. It felt only the steady, quiet attention that is the closest thing such a power knows to love. It understood that it had done almost nothing, and that almost nothing had been enough. A wise power does not save what is already whole. It only accompanies it for a little while, the way a cloud accompanies the high country without asking to stay.
