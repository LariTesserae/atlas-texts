The wise power came to Vess not as a light, because light would have been an insult. It came as a listener.

It stayed for a long time above the cloud deck, or beneath the basalt, or in the pause between one drip and the next. It let the city’s sound pour into it: the roar of falling water, the click-chitter of sump-mites, the sonar patter of hull-bats, the low subsonic thrum of thermal pumps. It heard the Keepers before it saw them—their knuckles tapping the walls, their low voices, the weight of their breath. It understood that in Vess, sound was not noise; it was the city’s blood.

It studied the world. It saw the raw basalt, the pale chemotrophic mats, the fungal spires, the blind pipe-eels. It saw no green, no sun, no open sky. And it understood that these absences were not wounds. The dark was the condition of sonar; the cold was the slope down which heat moved; the absence of plants was the space where other life could grow. The city was not a place without light; it was a place where light was not needed.

So the power did not give light. A small sun, a break in the cloud deck, a season of brightness—these would blind the bats, wither the silt-skin, scatter the glass midges, and force the Keepers to close their eyes against what they had never needed. It did not give green things, because a leaf is a debt that must be paid in photons, and there were no photons. It did not give warmth, because heat was the only currency that mattered, and to flood Vess with free heat would beggar every thermal cell, hollow out every gift between neighbors. It did not reveal itself in force, because the Keepers already had a covenant with the dark, not with gods. They were still building the city; they did not need a revelation to build around.

For a while it considered doing nothing. To love without touching. To observe from a distance and never disturb. That was the most tempting, because the power was wise enough to know that any intervention could cascade, could flatten a nuance, could turn a slow process fast. But absence is not a gift, and the power was benevolent, not indifferent. It wanted to offer something that would not injure.

So it found the lowest note of the city. It listened for the fundamental frequency of the wet basalt, the resonance of the thermal pumps, the standing waves in the water channels. It waited until the city’s own rhythm opened a small gap—a pause between two sonar clicks, a moment when the drip of water was exactly between one drop and the next—and then it did the only thing that felt right: it vibrated.

It did not shout. It did not even make a sound that could be heard by human ears. It struck the stone with a note that was less a sound than a memory of sound, a harmonic that joined the city’s existing resonance without overriding it. It chose the frequency with care: low enough to travel through wet basalt, high enough to be felt in a Keeper’s sternum, in the fine bones of a hull-bat’s ear, in the slow chemical pulse of a rust limpet. The note was not a command. It was an answer. It said, without words: *I hear you. I am still hearing you. Keep going.*

The rock carried it. The water carried it. The pipes carried it. Over days and weeks and months, the note spread through Vess like a new root—not a green root, but a root of resonance. The city, still being made, absorbed it. The extruded walls cured a fraction more evenly at certain stress points. The water channels sang a little more clearly when they began to clog. The frost that formed in microcracks loosened more easily, because the note matched the frequency of ice trying to split stone. It was not a transformation. It was a tuning.

Sere felt it first while tapping the wall near a drain. She had been listening for the hunting clicks of a pipe-eel, checking that the channel was clear. Her knuckles struck the wet basalt, and the returning vibration came back with a new overtone—a low, slow wave that settled first in her palm and then in her sternum, like the memory of warmth. She stopped. She tapped again: three short, one long, the Keeper query for *who is there?* The wall did not give words. It gave the same note, longer this time, as if the stone had exhaled.

She called the others, not with her voice but by tapping the doorframe in the rhythm for *new thing, come listen.* Soon a small cluster of Keepers gathered in the corridor, their breath steaming, their silt-skin giving off faint blue-green constellations. They took turns tapping the wall. Each one felt it. The Bat-Listeners tilted their heads; the hull-bats overhead stirred and began to weave the new frequency into their sonar, adding it as a quiet undertone.

“It’s not a void,” one Extruder-Tender said. “It’s a wall that knows its own song.”

“It’s like a heat-gift,” a Water-Warden said, “but in sound.”

They did not call it a god. They did not build a shrine. They added the note to their tapping vocabulary, a glyph for *the deep listener*, and taught it to their children. The bats learned it too. The pipe-eels, in the sump water, began to cluster where the note was strongest, sensing a current that was not water. The rust limpets, slow and patient, adjusted their calcification by a fraction of a millimeter, as if the new resonance made the metal more welcoming.

The power watched. It had not changed the dark, the wet, the cold, the slow rise of the plateau. The city was still being made; the animals still outnumbered the Keepers a thousand to one; the stone still spoke in incomplete sentences. But now the stone had one more word in its vocabulary.

And the power stayed. It did not rule. It did not need gratitude. It listened to the city’s sound with its note woven through, and it found it not less beautiful but more—the way a harmony does not erase the melody, but holds it.

The dark remained dark. The water kept moving. The animals kept their slow cycles. The Keepers still scraped silt-skin, still traded heat, still slept in warm piles, still tapped the walls to find their way. But now the walls tapped back with a note that meant: *you are not alone.*

That was what a wise power did. Not to save a world from itself. Not to heal what was never broken. Not to plant a green thing where no green thing could take root. It listened long enough to learn the city’s song, and then it added one note that the city could keep.

And then it kept listening.

The city, wet and black and loud and cold, kept on—and the note kept with it.
