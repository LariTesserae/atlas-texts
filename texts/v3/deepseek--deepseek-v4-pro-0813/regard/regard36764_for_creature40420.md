**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Did**

The power came to the Unfinished Range not as a light or a voice, but as a slight thickening of the cloud, a pause in the hiss of the vents. It did not touch the ground. It did not part the fog. It only perceived, and the place went on as it had always gone on: water running down new joints, steam rising, birds streaming into the grey.

The power saw the mats first—those skins of rust-orange, sulfur-yellow, and dark green—and understood that they were the foundation. It saw the shivering water of the warm pools, the flies ticking, the finches shaking mist from their feathers. It saw the fog gulls rethreading their ledges, the pikas’ careful loops around the hot ground, the steam owl moving without sound. And beneath all these, it saw the larger being that lives here: not any single animal, but the range itself—the rising rock and the condensing cloud, the closed loop of heat and water, the slow heartbeat of the earth’s dark energy.

The power watched for a long time.

It considered what it might give. It could soften the cold. It could still the rockfalls. It could teach the finches to sing in harmony, or give the pikas a language, or tell the water to stay warm. It could bring soil, and forests, and gentle sunlight, and make the place easier for all the small soft lives that shiver in the damp.

But the power was wise, and so it thought about what each gift would take away.

If the cold were softened, the steam would not condense so thickly, and the water films would thin, and the mats would dry. If the rockfalls were stilled, the talus would lose its ringing sharpness, and the ledges would grow smooth, and the birds would lose their mated edges. If the finches sang in harmony, they would no longer sound like pebbles tapping together, and the hot ground would lose its voice. If the pikas had a language, they would stop sitting up with their paws tucked, staring into the steam as if listening, because they would be too busy naming. If the soil came, the raw mineral cycles would be buried, and the mats would no longer be the first skin of the world. If the sunlight came, the dark energy of the earth would no longer be the main source, and the food web would forget its own beginning.

The power saw that this place was not unfinished in the sense of lacking something. It was unfinished in the sense of still becoming. It was not a broken machine waiting for a maker. It was a machine that made itself, and its beauty was exactly in its rawness, its friction, its refusal to be rounded.

So the power decided not to change the place. It did not call the beings to notice it. It did not make the hot springs warmer or the cold air gentler. It did not stop the slaughter of the flies by the finches, or the pikas by the owl, because those were not cruelties; they were the place’s metabolism. It did not even bless the steam, because the steam was already blessed by the simple fact of rising.

What it did was smaller than a breath, and larger than any mountain.

It set a quiet boundary around the Unfinished Range, not a wall but a kind of cosmic inattention. Any outside force that might come looking for heat to mine, or land to settle, or wonders to exploit, would feel a sudden, deep unremarkableness—a sense that this was only bare stone and bad weather, not worth the journey. The place would be left alone, not because it was hidden, but because it would seem, to any who did not love it, like nothing at all.

And then the power did the only other thing it could do without taking. It stayed. Not as a presence that could be worshiped or feared, but as a faint inclination in the universe, a tiny tilt of probability toward the place’s continued existence. A little more chance that the steam would keep rising, that the cloud would keep condensing, that the water would keep finding its way down the new joints. A little less chance that the next eruption would wipe the whole fragile web away before it had a chance to grow.

It did not make the place safe. It made it possible.

When the power left, the range did not notice. The finches went on chittering. The fog gulls went on rethreading their ledges. The pikas went on looped trails around the hot ground. The steam owl called once, a single low note, repeated at long intervals. And the note carried farther than it should in the wet air, as if something in the world had briefly listened and then let go.

If you were to go there, you would not find any sign of the power. No shrine, no altered law, no footprint in the wet silt. But if you sat long enough by a warm spring, you might feel it: not a presence, but an absence of interference, a space in the universe where the world has been allowed to happen. A quiet edge, loved from a distance, protected by being left alone.

That is the most a wise power can do for a place that is already whole.
