The benevolent and wise power would not arrive with light or thunder. It would arrive as a change in the quality of attention: a stillness at the edge of the mist, a slight warming of the air where the mire already warms itself.

Before doing anything, it would watch. It would see the green dome breathing under its low gray sky. It would see the water moving through buried channels, the trees hollowed into pools, the frogs with translucent skin, the slow blue-green lights breathing at the hidden edges. It would see the beings who look like stumps until they shift, moss growing along their shoulders, their eyes like rainwater that has stood too long and grown a skin of pollen.

It would ask itself, carefully: *What does a benevolent power owe a place that is already whole?*

It would watch long enough to understand that the mire is not a paradise in need of protection from itself. It is not innocent. It is not fragile in the way a garden is fragile. It is dense, patient, absorbent, and old. The people of the mire are not lost, not suffering, not waiting for a revelation. They are continuous with the moss and the water. They touch the worked stones not as caretakers or worshipers, but the way water remembers a channel. Their death is a lying-down into wet hollow. Their birth is a thickening in a pool. Their rituals are slow as seasons. There is no hunger for a savior.

The power would then turn its attention outward, because wisdom looks past the immediate beauty to the threats beyond it. It would see the lower country: maps being drawn, roads proposed, peat-cutters, miners, drainers, machines built for extraction. It would see that the mire's only true vulnerability is not its own slow decay but the sudden, efficient hunger of outside worlds. Those worlds do not hate the mire; they simply do not see it. They see a resource, a wasteland, an obstacle.

So the power would act, but not by changing the mire. It would act by changing the attention around it.

It would make the mire forgettable.

This would not be a violent hiding. It would be a quiet, patient turn of the world's eye. Surveyors would arrive at the edge and find their instruments fogged, their notes turning to pulp, their purpose dissolving into the mist. They would think: *It is not worth the trouble.* The maps would never quite resolve; the satellite images would show cloud or the ambiguous green of no particular value. Roads would sink into the soft peat. Deeds and grants and permits would be lost in offices. The mire would become a rumor, a place old people mention, a shape on the horizon that hikers decide not to cross. It would not be erased. It would be gently released from the machinery of use.

To the ones who came with love—quiet, wet, patient—the mire would still open. They would see the standing stones, the slab paths, the slow beings. They would hear the glug of water under the ground. But they would leave no road, no map, no name. The power would allow that, because love that does not seek to own is not a threat.

But the power would also understand that protection can become a prison. So it would set a limit on its own intervention. It would not stop the mire from changing. It would not hold the stones upright forever. It would not freeze the beings in their slow lives, nor save them one by one from their lying-down into the peat. It would not make the mire a museum, because a museum is a place where time has been stopped, and the mire is time itself, thickened into moss and water. The power would allow the stones to sink. It would allow the moss to cover the last straight edge. It would allow the pools to appear and disappear, the trees to fall with their single deep boom, the people to become pools and the pools to become people. It would allow the mire to continue being itself, even if that eventually meant forgetting the first people who placed the stones. The power would not preserve memory against the will of the place; it would only preserve the place's right to remember in its own way.

If the power ever needed to speak to the beings, it would not appear as a figure of light. It would send a message through the ground: a low glug, a small wet sigh, a sequence of drips from a particular branch. It would say, in the language the mire already speaks: *I am here at the edge. I will not enter. I will not be worshiped. I will keep the loud ones away. Is that acceptable?*

The beings would not answer quickly, because a conversation can take days. But one of them, standing by a slab path with its root-tipped fingers on a stone, might shift its weight. The moss on its face might part. It might make a single low sound, not a thank-you, not a blessing, just the exhalation of marsh gas from far below. The power would understand that as consent, or at least as the mire's way of saying: *We continue. Do what you will at the edge.*

Then the power would withdraw to the boundary. It would become the mist that makes the world a room ten feet wide. It would become the softness of the ground that resists a road. It would become the cloud that hides the dome from above. It would not rule the mire. It would not enter its ceremonies or disturb its quiet. It would stand at the threshold, patient as a tree, and keep the outside away.

And that would be all.

If you went there, you might not know the power was present. You might think you were lost in the fog, or that the path was too wet, or that the place simply did not want you. But if you listened, you might hear something behind the water and the insects: a silence that is not empty, but full. A protection so old and so careful that it has learned to be indistinguishable from the place it protects.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do. It would do almost nothing. And that almost nothing would be the most loving thing possible: it would let the mire be the mire, and keep the world from making it otherwise.
