The wise power did not descend. It listened.

It had been aware of the plain for a long time—the grass growing, the small animals moving, the rain falling on the limestone. But one day it heard a new sound: a rhythm of taps traveling through the rock, a series of small intentional knocks that were not geological. It leaned its attention down and found the Cut.

It saw the depression from above, the staircase of ledges, the city built into the walls. It saw the people moving slowly, placing each foot with care. It saw the children pressing ears to stone. It saw the Listeners tilting their heads, the Channel-keepers touching the seepage with their fingertips. It saw the cutters scoring lines and driving wooden wedges, waiting. It saw the dead being laid in exhausted galleries and covered with limestone blocks, and the moss growing over them.

The power understood at once that this was not a wound that needed healing. It was not a mistake to be corrected. It was a conversation. The Cut and its people together were a single slow being, half stone and half breath, listening to itself.

Then the power considered what it might do.

It could fill the Cut with new rock, smooth the ledges, cover the raw edges with soil and grass. But the people would lose their home, and the stone would lose its listeners.

It could give them iron, steam, dynamite, machines to cut faster and deeper. But the city would eat itself in a generation, and the silence would be broken, and the law of quiet would be no more than a memory.

It could bring them abundant water from deep springs, so the channels never ran dry. But then they would no longer taste the difference between the layers of stone, and the Channel-keepers would become watchers of nothing.

It could make the rock unbreakable, so no slab would ever fall. But then the Listeners would have nothing to hear, and the children would not learn to pause when the ground ticked.

It could send them a prophet to teach them a better way, a new god, a new law. But the stone was already their older kin. They already had a law, and it was carved into their hands and their feet and their silence.

The power saw that every intervention it could imagine would be a kind of violence. The Cut-folk were not suffering; they were attending. They had taken the raw fact of the earth and made a life out of listening to it. To heal them would be to unmake them.

So the power decided to do almost nothing.

It would not shout; it would not reveal itself; it would not even send a dream. Instead, it would do what the people of the Cut themselves did: it would listen. It would turn a portion of its infinite attention to the stone, and it would hear every tap, every drip, every soft crack. It would remember the names tapped on the walls, the niches cut for the newborn, the last taps for the dead. It would be the one Listener who never slept, the one ear pressed to the whole of the Cut, from the oldest whitewashed terrace to the newest raw floor.

And because the power was benevolent, it would also do a few small, invisible things.

It would keep the plain-people’s eyes from lingering too long on the Cut. When traders came, they would remember the stone as unremarkable, the copper as not worth the journey. The Cut would remain at the edge of their maps, a place one might have heard of but never quite found—not from fear, but from a gentle forgetfulness, like a name that slips the tongue.

It would hold the great faults still. When the earth shifted deep below, the power would place its attention between the rock and the rock, and the tremor would pass around the Cut like water around a stone. The walls would still settle, still creak softly, still keep the Listeners humble, but no wall would fall without warning.

It would coax the spring rains to fall in the right amounts—not too much, not too little—so the channels ran full but not fierce, and the cisterns filled, and the water tasted of the upper layers in spring and the lower layers in autumn.

And once, when a child lay sleeping in a low terrace and the power saw a hairline crack creeping toward the cradle, it did not move the child. It did not seal the crack. It simply sent a tiny tap through the stone—a sound so faint it was almost a thought. The child’s mother, already half-waking, heard it in her bones as a question. She rose, lifted the cradle, and moved it to the other side of the room. The next morning, a block of limestone the size of a sheep fell where the cradle had been. The mother told no one, because there was no one to tell. She only tapped a brief rhythm on the doorpost—*thank you, thank you, thank you*—and the power heard it, and was glad.

The power did not need gratitude. It did not need worship. It needed only the knowledge that it had not broken what it loved.

And the Cut went on deepening. The children grew, and some of them became Listeners. The swifts returned every spring. The moss spread into the new cracks. The dead were laid into the stone, and the stone received them. The people continued their slow work, their quiet conversations, their careful steps. And the wise power stayed near, not above, not below, but beside—like a friend who says nothing, but whose presence makes the silence feel less lonely.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does for a place like the Cut. It does not save it. It does not change it. It listens. It protects. It lets the stone and the people continue their long, patient conversation, and it adds its own attention to the silence, so that even the smallest tap is heard by something larger than the rock.

And perhaps, in the end, that is all any wise power can do for any living thing: to love it without needing to fix it, and to listen without needing to answer.
