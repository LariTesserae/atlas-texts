The power came as a pause in the wind.

It had crossed many worlds and seen many living places, but it had learned to arrive quietly, because arrival is already a kind of change. At the rim of the caldera it stopped.

It saw the crater as a single folded creature: stone skin, leaf fur, animal nerves, human breath. The people called it *the throat*—not a throat that speaks, but a throat that drinks. The rim was the lip, the terraces the palate, the deep floor the belly, the warm air rising the breath. They were the hands that carried water from lip to belly. This was not a metaphor; it was a law.

For a long while the power did nothing, because it was wise enough to know that looking is also an act. It watched the fog-waker climb to the rim before dawn. It watched the children with their covered pots, moving like beetles along the ledges. It watched an old man squeeze wet moss and lay it out to dry. It watched the Night-Ear make her rounds, lamp swinging, listening to water moving through rock.

Then, being benevolent, the power began to think of gifts.

First it thought of rain. The people had no true word for rain; they called it *the drunk sky*, water that falls without being asked. How easy it would be to gather the thin high clouds and make them fat, to teach these dry people the joy of water falling from heaven. The fog-waker would stand with her face open, and the children would lift their mouths. The cisterns would fill without effort. The leaves would not need their cups and hairs.

But the power, being wise, followed the rain down. It saw the rain hammering the waxy leaves, bruising the delicate fog-catching hairs, washing the dust-polluted beads into the soil. It saw the terraces, so carefully plastered with pumice and leaf, dissolving into rivulets. It saw the buried cisterns overflowing, their clay walls loosening, the precious water running wasted into the deep belly. It saw the fog-trees, which had spent ten thousand years learning to sip from the air, drowning. It saw the people’s language shrinking: all those words for fog—morning-fog, evening-fog, dry-fog, hungry-fog—falling into a single word. Rain. It saw their law, their ledger, their whole careful arrangement of debt and dew becoming unnecessary. They would live, perhaps, but as a different people, softer and less attentive, their hands no longer able to pick a single drop from a leaf without breaking it. The power set rain aside.

Then it thought of food. Their diet was small and strict: eggs, tubers, insects, the flesh of old grazers, milk accounted for like water. They were lean, with narrow hips and long fingers. Would it not be benevolent to make the soil richer, to double the tubers, to make the flightless birds lay two eggs instead of one? But the power traced the doubling: more food, more people; more people, more terraces; more terraces, more water needed; more water, less fog for the wild leaves; less leaves, fewer insects; fewer insects, fewer birds; and the crater, which had held itself in a tight ring for ages, would begin to spill or starve. The power set abundance aside.

Then it thought of knowledge. It could press a single insight into the mind of the Ledger-Keeper: the shape of a pump, the chemistry of distillation, the secret of metal. It could whisper to the rope-wrights the principles of the wheel, to the cistern-keepers the art of pipe and valve. They would become more comfortable, more powerful, less bound to the daily fog.

But the power, being wise, saw comfort as a slow thief. It saw the pipes carrying water without hands, and the hands forgetting how to listen. It saw the wheels turning, and the ledges becoming roads, and the roads going outward. It saw the tight fabric of the crater beginning to unravel, not because the people were weak, but because the need that had woven them together would be gone. They would still be kind, perhaps, but they would no longer be the people of the throat. They would be people who used to live there. The power set knowledge aside.

Then it thought of healing. It saw the old woman with cracked lips, the child who fell and would always walk with a limp, the cistern-keeper whose joints ached at dawn. Could it not smooth their pain? What harm in one small touch, one mended bone, one eased breath?

But it followed the easing: if no one ached, no one would need the warm stones and the oiled hands of the lamp-tenders; if no one limped, the children would not learn to carry water carefully for fear of spilling; if no one died young, the crater would grow full, and the old ones would not step aside for the next. Their bodies were part of the economy, not separate from it. Even pain had a place. The power set healing aside.

Then it thought of protection. The crater was hidden, yes, but not impossible to find. A curious traveler could walk to the rim. A king could send soldiers. A fire could fall from the sky. Could not the power weave a fog-bank around the plateau, a gentle aversion in the minds of explorers, a shield of forgetfulness?

But the power, being wise, saw that such protection would make the crater a kept thing, a garden under glass. Its people would cease to be free; they would become the pets of an invisible hand. And if the hand ever tired, they would not know how to hide themselves. The power set protection aside.

One by one the power considered its powers and found them too heavy. It could have changed anything, but every change would unmake something. The crater was not waiting for a gift. It was not a problem to be solved. It was a solution that had solved itself so slowly that the solution and the place were the same thing. The people did not pray for rain. They did not look at the stars and ask for more. When they said *we*, they meant the walls, the leaves, the animals, the fog, the hidden warmth. A gift, no matter how gentle, would have told them they were incomplete. And they were not incomplete.

Once, it lifted its attention toward a child at the fifth ledge, a small person with an overfull pot, wobbling. The child paused, frowned, and glanced upward as if a shadow had passed. The power did not steady the pot. The child steadied it—barely—and went on, proud. The power was glad it had not helped.

So the benevolent and wise power did nothing. It stayed at the rim through one whole cycle—one fog, one dry noon, one night of chittering—and it allowed itself to be changed, but not to change. That was the hardest discipline: to love a thing and leave it exactly as it was. The power’s attention, which could have burned like a second sun, softened into a kind of listening that asked nothing.

At dawn, when the fog-waker climbed to the rim with her horn, she felt something strange. The air was a little still, as if a great wind had stopped to rest. She sat in the mineral dust and waited. The cold air slid over the edge. The horn gathered moisture. When the cup was full, she drank it and turned to face the settlement. She opened both arms, then closed them slowly, like a mouth.

The fog came, and the people rose to meet it.

The power was already gone, leaving no mark, no word, no promise. Only the crater remained, holding itself together as it always had, unaware that it had been seen—or perhaps aware in the way a stone is aware of the sun: warm for a moment, then unchanged.

And that, though it would never be written in the knotted cords or pressed into the plaster of the cistern walls, was the power’s gift: it chose not to give.
