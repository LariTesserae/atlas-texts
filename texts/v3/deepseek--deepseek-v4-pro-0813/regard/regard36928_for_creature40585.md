They would do almost nothing.

And that almost-nothing would be the most deliberate thing a benevolent and wise power had done in a long while.

---

I came as a change in the water’s memory.

Not as a current—that would have been a lie. Not as light, though the city’s glass roots drank the sun and spat it down into the shaft. I came as attention, folded into the cold, lithium-rich dark above the mirror-slit, and I waited until I understood the town’s breathing before I moved.

For a long while, I simply listened.

I learned the four flush-gates. I learned the hourly breath of the membrane at the bottom, the low organ-note that pushed a house-sized bloom of warm water up the central column. I learned the clicking of the valve-keepers at flood tide, the slow patient licking of the masons as they added rib after rib to the walls, the gardeners flattening themselves against the black and purple mats, the lamplighters polishing the upper siphons at noon. I learned the shape of the city’s turbulence, how its doors opened after the current had already reversed, how its alleys were tuned like the chambers of a lung. I learned its language: not the old fired ceramic signs no one could read, but the percussion and exhalation, the faint glowing ribbons of silica-rich water that carried messages between bones of basalt.

And I considered loving it.

That is the danger of benevolence. Love wants to enter. Love wants to fix, to feed, to people the emptiness. Love wants to make the city comprehensible by making it just a little more like what I already knew.

I could have filled its water with oxygen. I could have sent fish down through the mirror-slit—small silver forms to investigate the hexagonal doorways, to rest in the pale gutweed, to brush their nervous flanks against the ceramic maintainers. I could have taught the city’s shared mind the meaning of its old fired signs. I could have warmed the shaft, sharpened the light, blunted the raw basalt edges, softened the roar of the north fissure into something more like music.

I could have made it into a garden.

But the city was not suffering. It was not lonely. It was not unfinished in the way a wound is unfinished. It was unfinished the way a newly born coast is unfinished—still sharp, still loud, still making itself. Its water was toxic to animal nerves, but the city had not failed to grow animals; it had grown something else. The absence of fish was not a lack. It was the shape of the city’s own kind of life.

A wise power, I think, must learn to see the difference between a broken thing and a becoming thing.

So I did not fill the sea with fish. I did not teach the masons to build doors my size. I did not translate the old signs, because the city had already moved past them and was writing its own memory in turbulence. I did not still the tide or silence the fissure. I did not make the city need me.

I did one thing.

At slack water, when the four great gates paused and the town hung balanced between flood and ebb, I let a single tone fall from my attention into the central column.

Not a word. Not a command. Not even music, in the way a musician would shape music.

It was the city’s own note returned to it.

I modeled it on the membrane’s exhalation, that low thrumming that passed through the narrowest part of the town like breath through a stone throat. But I made it slower. Warmer. A little wider at the edges, as if the water itself remembered being seen.

The tone sank down the shaft. It did not disturb the siltless mineral floor. It did not shake the pale ceramic walls. It moved through the colloid like a slow seed, turning with the ghost of yesterday’s tide.

At the bottom, the breath-tenders around the central membrane scattered, then stopped. They did not flee far. They pressed their blind ceramic disks toward the sound, their gel-thread undersides tasting the water. For a few seconds they were all still, and the whole city seemed to pause: the masons halted with their mouths against the stone, the valve-keepers slack in their crevices, the gardeners spread over their dark films as if listening.

The tone reached the membrane.

It did not command the membrane to breathe. It simply touched the taut surface, the way a warm current touches a face.

Then it was gone, absorbed into the city’s short-term memory.

The membrane inhaled. The tide returned. The valve-keepers began to tap their quick messages again. The masons went back to thickening the walls. The city resumed its loud, mineral becoming.

I withdrew.

I did not mark the place. I set no ward around the mirror-slit, because a ward is also a wall. I seeded no algae in the sunlit upper water, because even a gift can become a weight. I left no name, no law, no promise carved into the basalt.

If the city remembers me, it will not remember a god. It will remember a pause in the central column, a warmth that did not correspond to any open inlet, a note that came once and never again—a small, luminous thread in the colloid, turning for a while after the tide changed.

And maybe, years from now, when some new caste of maintainer crawls from the mineral mill and first opens its crystal rim to the water, it will feel that old, decaying thread of warmth still turning in the deepest part of the shaft. It will tap a question against the wall.

And the city will answer, in its own way, not with words:

*There was an outside. It did not enter. It only listened.*

That, I think, is what a benevolent and wise power should do. Not to save, not to change, not even to be known.

To become, for a few seconds, a memory the living thing can carry.
