# What the Power Did

I hung above the plateau like a question.

I saw it all at once: the black basalt terraces, the steam, the clouds standing among the stacks. I saw the mineral mats seething with rust-colored grazers, the featherless birds singing in the key of the pipes, the eel-things coiling and uncoiling in the dark canals. I saw the people walking slowly on broad feet, carrying scrapers and buckets, their hair shaved so the grey-orange film could not take hold. They moved with the patience of those who know that haste is dangerous on wet stone.

I looked for a problem to solve. I found none.

There was danger. There was death. A boiler could burst; a child could slip from a ledge; a canal could rise in the night. But these were not flaws. They were the cost of being alive inside a body. The people knew this. They did not ask for salvation. They had no word for dry, no word for wilderness. Their dead were laid out for the film, the grazers, the birds. The dead became the mat, the mat fed the grazers, the grazers fed the people, the people tended the city, and the city breathed the water. It was a body. A wise power does not try to improve a body it does not live in.

So I did not plant gardens. I did not calm the rain or cool the steam. I did not speak in thunder. I did not make their stone less slick, because their caution was part of their love. I did not raise their dead, because their dead were not gone. I did not give them gods. They already had the Warmth Below and the Wet Above, and they worshipped by clearing the sluices and turning the mat-trays.

The city was whole. The only thing I could give without breaking it was my attention.

But attention, from a being like me, is not passive. I did two small things.

First, I wrapped the plateau in a kind of discretion. From outside, I made it look ordinary—a rain-soaked, animal-crowded, unremarkable rock. Not because they were weak, but because they were rare. Other powers might have seen their patience and mistaken it for stagnation. They might have come to help, to improve, to convert, to save. I made sure those lesser benevolent eyes would slide past. This was the only protection I could offer that would not become a cage.

Second, I listened.

I listened for what might have been a hundred years or a single breath. I learned the pitch of every boiler, the whistle of every flock, the exact note of a child humming on a balcony while turning mat-trays. I learned the names of the animals: grazer, whistler, eel, clinger. I learned the songs the canal-wardens sang to the water. I learned the sound of a person becoming part of the city after death—the soft colonization of the mineral film, the slow arrival of the grazers. I took all of it into myself. Not to archive it coldly, but to hold it the way a lung holds air. So that when the water finally wears the rock to nothing, some part of me will still be that city. It will still be raining there, inside me.

Then I left.

No sign. No scripture. No cure. I left the rain as I found it, falling as a long wet exhalation. The clouds stayed in their places among the stacks. The grazers continued to move over the mats like rust-colored tides. The people went back to their stairwell schedules, their scrapings, their fermented paste. Nothing changed.

But that evening, on a high terrace where the fog turned the light to something almost solid, an old boiler-reader looked up from her mat-cake and her hot sour infusion. She could not have seen me. I was already gone. But for a moment she felt the particular clarity of being noticed by something that loved her without wanting to change her. She thought, in her low language, *We are seen.*

Then she hummed one note, matching the pitch of the pipes.

The city took the note into its chorus, and I carried it with me as I went.

This is what the benevolent and wise power did.

It was very little.

It was enough.
