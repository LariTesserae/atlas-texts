The benevolent and wise power did not arrive. It had never been absent. Its attention had merely been elsewhere, folded into other aeons, other worlds. When it turned back, it felt the place not as a discovery but as a memory it had not yet had.

It looked at the escarpment, the slow silver sea of cloud, the towers releasing their rings of vapor at dawn. It listened to the layered roar until it could distinguish the long pulse beneath it. It lasted through a season of that music. It walked the paths without a body, taking the extra twenty steps with the same seriousness as the Unattended. It sat in the empty shelters. It tasted the cold-metal corner. It watched the pale frogs in the hot mud and the water-running lizards sliding over the surface.

Then it considered what it could do.

It could straighten the extra steps. It could cool the hot springs to comfort. It could give names to the Unattended, or teach them to speak so they might ask for what they needed. It could restore the shelters, rebuild the towers, green the mineral terraces into gardens. It could send a sign, a messenger, a season of clarity. It could love them with light and instruction.

And it understood, with the particular clarity of the very old, that to do any of these things would be to begin a destruction.

The place was not unfinished. The extra steps were not a flaw; they were a kind of attention. The too-narrow doorway was not an error; it was a question the body answered. The cold-metal corner was not a wound; it was a boundary, a place where the world held its breath. The Unattended were not silent because they lacked language; they had a language of warm stones and shared fruit and moss. They did not need to be saved. They were not lost.

So the power did the only thing that wise powers can do for things already whole. It did nothing.

But it was not the nothing of absence. It was the nothing at the center of a song, the rest that holds the notes. It did not touch the path. It did not alter the pulse of the towers. It did not show itself. It let the heat stay hot and the cool stay wet and the fog roll through the valleys on its own schedule. It let the years pass.

What it did was this: it paid attention. And because it was a power, its attention had weight. It had to learn to make that weight as light as possible—to attend to the place the way the place attended to itself, without leaving fingerprints. It taught itself to be a witness who does not stare, a guardian who does not guard.

It did, finally, one small thing—not to the place, but to the world outside it. It drew a kind of quiet around the plateau. Not a wall. Not a forgetting. An *easing*. The place remained exactly where it was, but for those who would come to take—not to stay, but to extract, to map, to improve, to solve—the desire to continue thinned. The paths looked longer than they should be, and not in the way that invited. The coordinates became muddled in the rain. Explorers turned back not because they were stopped, but because they forgot why they had come.

This was not a spell of concealment, exactly. It was more like a hand held up, palm out, not in refusal but in a gentle, wordless request: *not here*.

To those who would stay—the ones who already walked with the extra steps, who left fruit on windowsills, who understood that a stream pausing at a stone is a form of recollection—the power did nothing. It did not need to. They could find the place and be themselves in it. They could ask who lives here, and receive no word, and understand.

The Unattended noticed nothing, which is the highest compliment a power can receive. The moss on their shoulders grew a little thicker that century, but not from magic. The path-stones were perhaps a little warmer at dawn, but they had always been warm. The towers kept their long pulse. The fireflies rose in dense spirals. The frogs fell silent at their usual hour.

And the power stayed at the rim, being careful not to become a god. It let itself be unknown. It let itself be unthanked. It let the place be a place and not a temple. That was the whole of its benevolence: not to make the beloved better, but to keep the beloved possible, to see it clearly, and to unhand it.

If you asked that power what it had done, it would not answer. It would look out over the green on green, the black stone, the silver water, the quiet exhalations of the towers. Then it would turn and walk the path again, taking the extra steps, not to go anywhere, but to be in the same place more thoroughly.

And that, it would know, was enough.
