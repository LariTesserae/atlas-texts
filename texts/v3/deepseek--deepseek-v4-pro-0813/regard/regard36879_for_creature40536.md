The power does not arrive with light or voice. It does not part the mist or still the water. It comes as a noticing—a slow, deliberate attention that settles over the hollow like a hand held just above the skin, not touching, but felt.

It watches for a long time. It watches the way the water builds, the way the people scrape and weave and wait. It watches the dead become walls, the walls become mountain, the mountain become room. It watches the children learn the hand signs before speech, the old woman touching the white shape where a relative used to be. It watches the empty galleries, the unfilled hive, the cathedral waiting for its congregation.

And it understands something that few powers do: this place is not broken. It is not a wound to heal or a ruin to restore. It is a process, a slow and patient becoming. The people are not suffering under some curse or failing to thrive. They are exactly what the hollow needs—tenders, keepers, living parts of the deposition. The emptiness is not lack. It is room for what is still arriving.

So the power does what the wisest powers do when they encounter something already whole: it asks permission.

Not in words. In a subtle shift of the air, a pause in the mist, a question posed to the water itself. *May I help?*

The water does not answer. The water is not a mind. But the people—some of them, the ones who spend their lives reading the stone—feel the question. An old man scraping a channel stops and looks up. A woman weaving a willow lattice pauses, her hands still. A child on the high gallery makes the sign for *clear*, then hesitates, unsure why.

The power waits.

And then, slowly, the people answer. Not with words or signs, but with their work. The old man returns to his scraping. The woman resumes her weaving. The child lowers her hand. They do not reject the power; they simply continue. They are not waiting for a savior. They are not waiting for anything. They are tending the hollow, as they have always tended it, as the hollow tends them.

The power understands. It does not need to be thanked or worshipped or even acknowledged. It needs only to be useful, if it can be, without disturbing the balance.

So it does small things.

It does not stop the main water. It does not cut a channel. It does not grow a room. Instead, it finds the places where the work is hardest, where the stone grows too fast or the channels clog too quickly, and it eases them—not by force, but by a kind of subtle alignment. A runnel that always blocked now stays clear a little longer. A wicker frame that would have taken ten years to fill now takes nine. A pool rim that was crumbling now holds firm. The changes are so small that no one notices them as miracles. They notice only that the work is a little lighter, the days a little smoother, the hollow a little more itself.

The power does not heal the sick or raise the dead. It does not fill the empty rooms or summon new inhabitants. It does not make the sun break through the cloud or quiet the roar of the water. It does not turn the stone back into soil or the mist into clear air. It does not make the place easier to live in. It makes the place easier to *tend*.

And in that tending, the people find something they did not know they were missing. Not hope—they have never needed hope. Not faith—they have never needed faith. But a kind of companionship. A sense that the hollow is not alone in its becoming, that something outside the mist is watching with patience and care, not to judge or change, but to witness.

The power does not stay forever. It cannot. The hollow is not its home, and the people are not its people. But it leaves something behind—not a gift, not a sign, but a subtle warmth in the stone, a faint brightness in the water, a memory of attention that lingers like the smell of rain.

And the people, without knowing why, begin to speak a little more often. Not loudly—the water is still loud—but more often. They tell stories in the high galleries, their voices low and close. They teach the children new signs: a hand held open, palm up, for *witness*. A finger touching the chest, then the stone, for *we are not alone*. They do not know where these signs came from. They only know they feel right.

The hollow continues. The water builds. The stone grows. The people tend. The empty rooms wait. And somewhere, beyond the mist, a wise and benevolent power watches still, not intervening, not controlling, but simply *seeing*—and in that seeing, the place is made more real.

The town is not so much built as being deposited, one wet year at a time. And now, perhaps, it is also being *seen*—one patient gaze at a time. The difference is subtle, but it is there, like a hand held just above the skin, not touching, but felt.

The water roars. The mist folds. The white stair of pools glows faintly in the fog. And the people, small and deliberate, move along the raised stone rims as if the water were pavement, as if the stone were flesh, as if the hollow were a great running instrument—and they, the living part of a landscape still arriving, are learning to play it.
