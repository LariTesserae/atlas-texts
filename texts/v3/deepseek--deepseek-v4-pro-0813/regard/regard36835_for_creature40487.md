The benevolent and wise power does not enter the chamber. It stays outside the curtain of falling water for a long time, listening.

At first it wants to help. It is not possible to be truly benevolent without feeling the impulse to heal, to reveal, to save. It could part the water and bring light. It could give the creature eyes. It could tell the creature its name, if the power could find it in the old registers of forgotten things. It could open the roof of the cave to the high plain’s sky, to the stars that have never once touched this water. It could sing the creature out into the sun and call it by a true name.

But the power is not only benevolent. It is wise. And so it waits, and in waiting it learns the room’s first and deepest rule: **light does not enter**. Not because light is forbidden, but because the room is not made for it. To bring light would not illuminate; it would erase. The pale vegetation, the blind crickets, the transparent frogs, the long careful being made of folds and patience—they are not waiting to be seen. They are complete.

So the power does not bring light.

Then it considers the rods. It could wake them, perhaps. It could make them give back their old purpose, draw water from the deep rock in a more perfect way, maybe restore the machine they were part of. But the rods are not broken. They are doing what they do. The creature tends them without needing to understand them. To wake the machine would be to take the creature’s task, to make it unnecessary, and the creature is not harmed by its long watch. It is shaped by it.

So the power does not touch the rods.

It considers, too, offering companionship. The creature is the only large thing in the dark, and what it thinks—if it thinks—no one knows. But the power is wise, and after a long time it understands that the creature is not lonely. The waterfall is its sky. The frogs are its birds. The mineral terraces are its calendar. The rods are its altar and its work. It has no name for loneliness because it has never been outside the warm mouth of the mountain. To give it a friend would be to introduce it to a lack it has never felt.

So the power does not speak.

Instead, the benevolent and wise power does the only thing that is neither a rescue nor a violation: it keeps watch.

It does not keep watch like a sentry, with light and a sword. It keeps watch like the high clouds keep watch over a valley—distantly, without weight, changing the light on the ground only by passing. It walks the rim of the escarpment in no body, and it notices the paths that might lead humans to the waterfall. It does not block them. It does not place a curse or a wall. It simply lets the mountain’s own vastness be enough. It lets the mist confuse the way. It lets the trail become a deer trail, then a memory of a deer trail, then nothing. It does this not out of jealousy, but because the room’s wholeness depends on being left alone.

Then it does one small thing.

Deep below the chamber, half a mile into the mountain, there is a hairline crack in a warm seam where the geothermal water rises. It has been slowly widening for two centuries. If it continued, the spring would cool, the waterfall would thin to a veil, and the warm hollow would become cold. The power rests its attention against that crack, not like a hand, but like the memory of warmth. It does not force the stone to heal. It only gives the stone permission, and the stone, being old and patient, begins to seal itself. The water will not grow colder. The rods will continue to weep. The mineral terraces will add another thin white line, and then another.

The creature notices nothing. Or perhaps, for a moment, it pauses in its tracing of the groove along the wall. The air around the rods feels the wrong temperature in just the right way, as it always does. The creature lowers its folded face and samples the warm film on its own palm. There is the faintest change in the mineral taste—not enough to question, only enough to taste, for a brief second, like something it has known since before it forgot its own name.

It makes a sound, almost too low to hear, a vibration that moves through the water on the walls. The frogs answer. The blind crickets drum their legs. The waterfall roars.

The power hears it and does not answer.

That is the whole of what the benevolent and wise power does. It does not save the creature, because the creature is not lost. It does not free the creature, because the creature is not imprisoned. It does not reveal the place, because the place is already more real in the dark than it ever would be in the light. It does not love the creature the way a human loves a dog, with need and a face. It loves the creature the way a mountain loves a spring—by giving it time, by giving it quiet, by letting it continue.

And then, after a long while—maybe a year, maybe the precise length of one white line on the mineral terrace—the power withdraws its attention, leaving behind no sign except a slight steadiness in the water’s heat. It leaves the way a tide leaves a stone on a beach: not gone, only elsewhere. The chamber remains. The creature remains. The rods weep. The waterfall roars.

Nothing has changed.

And that, the power knows, is the only proof of real attention.
