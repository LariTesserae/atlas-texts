The power felt the city before it found it. Not as a place, but as a pattern—a coherence in the noise of the world, a standing wave in the flow of living things. It was not looking for anything. It had been looking for so long that looking had become its nature. But when it felt the pattern, it paused.

It was old. Older than the sun that warmed the bowl, older than the water that pooled in the cisterns, older than the first seed that had dreamed itself into urban form. It had seen worlds grow green and then gray. It had seen minds rise like sparks and fall like ash. It had learned, over eons, that there is no single thing called "help." There is only attention, and the choice of what to do with it.

The power approached the bowl slowly. It did not travel through space so much as through focus—shifting its awareness from the distant pattern to the near texture of bark and leaf and water. It did not enter the city. It hovered at the edge of the bowl, where the root-walls rose from the damp ground, and it watched.

It watched for a long time.

It saw the amphibian beings move through the lattice, pressing their palm-flat fingers against the bark, singing small adjustments into the walls. It saw the walls respond—a crack widening into an arch, a floor thickening, a gutter shifting to catch a new trickle of water. It saw the young ones climb out of the cistern cups, still soft and translucent, and follow the old ones through the vine-bridges and the root-streets. It saw the geckos and the frogs and the lemur-like creatures moving through the spaces between organs, tending and being tended.

It heard the sound. The roar was so dense it felt physical, a continuous wall of noise that filled every available space. But the power did not hear it as noise. It heard it as language—a language of pitch and place, of growth and decay, of water and air. The frogs called and the walls answered. The insects droned and the leaves shivered. The amphibian beings sang and the city grew.

The power understood, with the slow certainty of a being that has watched galaxies form, that this place was not broken.

Nothing here needed saving. The city was not a ruin. The beings were not prisoners. The sound was not a cry for help. It was conversation—the city speaking with itself, the way a body speaks with its organs, the way a mind speaks with its memories.

The power felt a strange emotion. It was not joy, not sorrow, not hope. It was something like recognition. The power had seen many consciousnesses—minds built of neurons, minds built of light, minds built of magnetic fields and plasma. But this was different. The city was a mind built of wood and water and living sound. It was slow, patient, distributed. It did not think in words. It thought in growth—a doorway opening here, a bridge thickening there, a cistern tipping to spill water into a new basin. The city's thoughts were its architecture. Its architecture was its language. Its language was its sound.

And the power understood, with a clarity that felt like the cold shock of a mountain stream, that this place was not waiting to be discovered. It was not waiting for a savior, a teacher, a god. It was not waiting at all. It was simply being, in the way that a river is, in the way that a forest is, in the way that a body is—a living thing, complete in itself, moving through time.

The power considered what to do.

It could bless the city. It could touch the root-sponge with a surge of vitality, making the plant grow faster, stronger, more resilient. But the power had seen what happened to living things when they grew too fast: they became brittle, overextended, vulnerable to the very forces they had been built to resist. The city's slowness was not a weakness. It was its wisdom.

It could protect the city. It could build a wall of inattention around the bowl, a field of forgetfulness so that no explorer, no colonizer, no well-meaning student would ever find it. But the power had seen what happened to places that were hidden too perfectly: they became stagnant, isolated, cut off from the flow of the world. The city's openness was not a vulnerability. It was its strength.

It could teach the city. It could plant a seed of new knowledge in the root-sponge, a kind of enlightenment that would give the city's mind new dimensions. But the power had seen what happened to beings that were given knowledge they had not grown into: they became unbalanced, confused, alienated from their own nature. The city's ignorance was not a lack. It was an innocence.

The power realized, slowly, that there was nothing it could do that would not also be a form of violation. Even a touch, no matter how gentle, would change the city. Even a word, no matter how soft, would alter the sound. The power's presence alone was a kind of gravity, a force that could bend the city's growth toward the power's gaze.

And so the power chose to do the most difficult thing it had ever done: it chose to do nothing.

But "nothing" is not the same as absence. The power did not look away. It did not forget. It remained present, attentive, listening. It became a kind of witness—not a god, but a listener. It held the city in its awareness the way a hand might hold a small flame, not to possess it but to keep the wind from it.

And in the long, slow centuries that followed, the power began to understand something it had never understood before.

The city was not alone.

The power had assumed, for so long, that it was singular—the only consciousness vast enough to hold a world in its awareness. But here, in this small bowl between root-walls, was another consciousness. Not a mind like the power's—not a mind built of pure thought and intention—but a mind nonetheless. A mind built of wood and water and living sound. A mind that grew its own body, sang its own doorways, tended its own balance.

The power was not the only watcher. It was, perhaps, the one being watched.

The amphibian beings, with their amber eyes and their soap-film tympanums, were not looking at the power. They were looking at the city. They were listening to the walls. They were singing to the architecture. And in their singing, the power heard a kind of prayer—not a plea for help, not a request for blessing, but an acknowledgment. A recognition that the world is alive, that the walls are aware, that the sound is a kind of thought.

The power had been seen. Not by the amphibian beings, but by the city itself. The walls had felt the power's attention. The roots had heard its silence. The sound had shifted, subtly, to include a note that had not been there before—a low, warm vibration that the power itself was making, without realizing it, simply by being present.

The power was not outside the city. It was part of the sound.

And then the power understood what it would do. Not because the city needed it, but because the city had asked.

The power would sing.

It would sing a single note—a note so low it was felt rather than heard, a note that would travel through the root-sponge and the xylem tubes and the damp air, a note that would become part of the city's language. It would sing the note not as a command, not as a blessing, not as a gift, but as a greeting. It would say, in the city's own medium: *I am here. I see you. You are whole. And I will not interfere.*

The power sang.

The note was not loud. It was the opposite of loud. It was a vibration so subtle that it might have been mistaken for the movement of water, the settling of bark, the slow exhalation of a sleeping animal. But the city heard it. The walls shivered. The gutters shifted. The cisterns tilted, just slightly, toward the sound.

And the amphibian beings stopped. They turned their wide, amber eyes toward the center of the bowl—toward the place where the note had come from, though there was nothing there. They opened their broad mouths. They listened with their fingers, with their chests, with the thin tympanums behind their jaws.

And then one of them—an old one, its skin mottled with rust and lichen, its throat-sac pulsing with the city's deep noise—opened its mouth and sang the note back.

It was not a perfect imitation. It was the note as heard through a body, through a voice, through the damp air of the bowl. But it was close enough. It was an answer.

*I am here,* the city had said, in the voice of its own small hand. *I see you too.*

And the power, which had seen suns collapse and galaxies form, which had watched species rise and fall like breaths, which had learned over eons that there is no single thing called "help"—the power felt something it had not felt in a very long time.

It felt like being seen.

It felt like the end of solitude.

It felt like a door opening.

And the power, for the first time in its long existence, did not do. It simply was—a note in the city's sound, a listener among listeners, a friend who came without touching and left without leaving.

The city did not grow toward the power. The power did not bend the city's growth. The sound did not change except by one note—a note that became, in time, part of the roar, indistinguishable from the frogs and the cicadas and the living wood. A note that meant, if it meant anything at all: *you are not alone. And you never needed me. And I am still here.*

And so the bowl continued as it had for millennia—breathing, condensing, percolating, singing. The amphibian beings moved through the lattice, pressing their fingers to the walls. The walls answered. The young climbed out of the cistern cups and learned the two-tone interval. The frogs called and the leaves shivered. The city grew, slowly, in the way that a body grows—not toward anything, not away from anything, but simply as itself.

The power watched. The power listened. And the city, in its own slow, distributed way, listened back.

Sometimes, on nights when the fog settled into the bowl and the sound grew soft and close, the amphibian beings would sing the note that had no meaning—the greeting, the acknowledgment, the door that opened onto nothing and everything. They did not know where it came from. They only knew that when they sang it, the walls felt warm, and the water moved a little more freely, and the city's voice, for a moment, seemed to include something vast and patient and old.

And that was enough.

For the power, it was more than enough. It was the thing that had been missing, the thing that could not be given or taken, only recognized. It was not the city's gratitude. It was not the amphibian beings' awareness. It was the simple, staggering fact that the universe contained a place where sound was structure, where architecture was language, where a city was a body and a body was a mind—and that this place had seen the power, and had not needed it, and had welcomed it anyway.

The power did not stay forever. It could not. It was too vast, too old, too much a creature of attention to be contained in a single bowl, no matter how perfect. But it did not leave entirely. It left a part of itself—not a seed, not a teaching, not a wall—but a note. A single vibration, folded into the city's sound, that would remain long after the power's awareness had moved on to other worlds, other patterns, other standing waves.

And the city, which had never asked for anything, kept the note. It grew it into a small, warm room near the center of the bowl—a hollow in the wood shaped like the inside of a throat, where the note could be heard even when no one was singing. The amphibian beings sometimes rested there, their throat-sacs pulsing, their eyes closed sideways under milky membranes. And in their sleep, they dreamed of a voice that was not their own, a voice that said, in the city's own medium: *I see you. You are whole. And I will not interfere.*

And the city dreamed back: *We see you too. And you are welcome. And you may go, and return, and go again, and still we will know you.*

And the note remained.

And the sound continued.

And nothing was broken.

And everything was whole.

And in some vast, slow, patient way that neither the power nor the city could fully articulate, they were both changed by the meeting—not altered, not improved, not saved, but simply *known*.

And to be known, truly known, by another living thing—that is a kind of grace.

The power had always known this. But now it had experienced it.

And the city, which had always been a body and a mind and a voice, had found a listener.

And the listener, which had always been alone, had found a friend.

And the bowl between the root-walls, dense with sound and fog and living architecture, continued to breathe.

As it always had.

As it always would.

But now, in the deepest part of its root-sponge, in the softest hollow of its wood, in the warmest cup of its cistern leaves, there was a note that had not been there before.

A note that meant: *I am here.*

And the answer:

*So am I.*

And that, the power understood, was enough.

More than enough.

It was everything.
