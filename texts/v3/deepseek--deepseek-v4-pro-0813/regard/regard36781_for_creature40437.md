The power came as a change in the water, not a light. It did not part the cold or warm it. It arrived the way a thought arrives in a mind that has been still for a long time: as a faint taste, a slight pressure, a question that did not need an answer.

It had seen many worlds. It had loved many things into ruin by loving them too quickly. It had learned, over eons, that benevolence without patience is only another kind of violence. So when it found the Drowned Lattice, it did not speak. It did not reach out. It only listened.

The garden did not notice. The Tenders went on tying their knots, clearing their channels, growing their slow roots into the stone. The water remained cold and heavy. The moss glowed its faint green-gray. The kelp swayed in currents that had not changed in a thousand years.

The power watched. It saw the wall-keeper who had become a wall, its body indistinguishable from the stone, its mind spread through the joints like water through sand. It saw the row-tenders moving on their stilts of root, patient as seasons. It saw the nursery where young Tenders were bent into the shapes of their future work. It saw the whole country, handmade, held together by cord and stone and time.

And it felt the urge. The old, familiar urge to help. To warm the water a little, so the Tenders might move faster. To bring fish, so the silence might be filled. To give them light, so they might see the beauty they had built. To give them voices, so they might speak their long thoughts.

The power recognized the urge. It had felt it before, on other worlds, in other gardens. It had acted on it, once, and the garden had died. Not quickly, but slowly, the way a frozen thing dies when it is thawed too fast. The warmth had brought rot. The fish had eaten the moss. The light had blinded the Tenders, who had never needed eyes. The voices had filled the water with noise, and the Tenders had stopped listening to the stone.

So the power did not act. It sat with the urge until the urge passed. It let the cold be cold. It let the dark be dark. It let the Tenders be slow. It understood that the garden was not a problem to be solved. It was a life to be respected.

But wisdom is not the same as absence. The power could do one thing without breaking the garden's quiet. It could keep the quiet from being broken by others.

It wove a veil around the basin. Not a wall—walls invite questions. A soft forgetting. A current that turned away ships. A cold that discouraged divers. A darkness that made instruments fail. A taste in the water that said to any passing thing: *there is nothing here for you.* Not a lie, but a truth: there was nothing here for the quick, the hungry, the curious. There was only the slow, patient work of being.

Then the power turned to the one who had drawn its attention. The oldest wall-keeper, the one who had become a wall. It did not speak. It did not touch. It only sat beside the wall-keeper in the dark, and it shared a memory.

Not a memory of the surface. Not a memory of light or air or animals. A memory of the garden itself, seen from above: the whole lattice of terraces and channels, the faint glow of moss, the slow movement of Tenders like stars in a dark sea. The wall-keeper had never seen this. It had only felt the wall, the stone, the water moving through the joints. Now it felt the whole garden, held in a single thought.

The wall-keeper did not move. It could not move. But in its stone dream, a new mineral entered the water. A strand of light, thin as a root-hair, that it would carry for centuries. It was not a command. It was not a gift of power. It was only a recognition: *you are seen. You are not alone. The world outside is not empty, but it will not intrude.*

The power left, or perhaps it stayed. It is hard to tell with powers. It may have become the veil itself, the cold current that turns away the curious. It may have become the faint sweetness in the water, the taste of attention. It may have become the silence that is not empty, but full.

The Tenders continued their work. A new terrace rose at the far edge of the basin. A sluice gate shifted. A row-tender tied a knot that would take a year. The water remained cold and dark and patient. And sometimes, in the taste of the current, there was a faint sweetness, like a promise that the garden would be left alone to finish its long, slow work.
