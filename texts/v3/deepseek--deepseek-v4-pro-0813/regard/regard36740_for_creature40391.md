**What the Wise Power Did**

They came with no name, because names are a kind of building, and they had no wish to build. They came as a change in the cloud ceiling—a light that was not warmer but more attentive. The mat felt them first: a slow chemical pause in the mycelium, a hesitation in the stomatal apertures. The Listeners looked up from their walking and saw nothing, but the moss on their shoulders turned a color that meant *something is here.*

The wise power did not speak. They watched.

They saw the engines pulling moisture from the fog, the glass-shears cropping the skin in scissor-sounds, the arc-flies arcing blue, the reed-throats sending their steam before their sound. They saw the corrected moving a few seconds out of step, and the negative-space bowls where the present became optional. They saw the black stone beneath the peat, polished to a mirror that reflected weather from another age.

They thought, as powers do, of what they could give. Warmth. Light. A name. They could teach the Listeners to build, to write, to want. They could heal the corrected, fill the bowls, tame the engines. They could make the plateau more like a world and less like a process.

But the wise power was old enough to know that every gift is also a judgment. To warm the air would be to starve the fog-chemists. To teach the Listeners to build would be to give them walls, and walls would end their only tool, which is attention. To fill the bowls would be to stop the plateau’s digestion of time. To heal the corrected would be to say they were broken. To name the plateau would be to claim it.

So they did not do those things.

Instead they asked—not in words, but in a silence the mat could read—*What do you need?*

The answer came slowly, in the way a water channel reroutes itself overnight, in the way a reed-throat’s call arrives after its own steam. The answer was: *Nothing. You are already here. That is enough.*

Then the wise power understood. The plateau was not a problem to be solved, a wound to be healed, or a soul to be saved. It was a factory without a builder, a body without a name, a world that manufactured itself from fog and time. It did not need a god.

But the power was benevolent as well as wise. They knew there were other powers—less wise, less patient—who would see the plateau and want to mine its ancient stone, harvest its engines, study its corrected, civilize its Listeners. They would come with good intentions and ruin everything.

So the wise power did the only thing they could do without changing the place: they changed the way the place could be found.

They wrapped the plateau in a slow forgetting. Not a wall—walls are built, and this place has none. Not a storm—storms pass. It was a shift in the geometry of arrival, a fold in the map of the sky. Those who searched for the plateau would find only cloud and cold and the smell of ozone, and they would turn away not disappointed but relieved, as if they had remembered that some places are not meant for them.

Then the wise power departed. They left no blessing, no mark, no promise. They simply lifted their attention, the way fog lifts, and the plateau went on working.

The mat resumed its slow pulses. The glass-shears cropped the skin. The arc-flies flared and vanished. The reed-throats called, steam first. The corrected walked their optional present. And the Listeners, who had felt the attention come and go, stood for a moment with their root-toes in the peat. Then one of them sang a single note—not a song of welcome or farewell, but the sound the plateau makes when it thinks about itself.

The wise power did not hear it, because they were already gone. But the black stone beneath the mat held the note for a little while, the way it holds old weather, and then it let it go.

That was all.
