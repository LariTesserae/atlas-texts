The benevolent and wise power would do nothing. But it would be the hardest kind of nothing—not absence, not neglect, but restraint held steady as a hand over water.

It would first understand what it was seeing: not a landscape containing a creature, but a creature that had become landscape. The mats, the midges, the transparent shrimp, the mineral terraces, the steam—these were not individuals or even organs. They were phases of a single ongoing process, a body whose edges were the basin’s rim. The power would search for a wound, a lack, an error in the loop. It would find none. The place was not waiting. It had no need to be completed.

And that would change everything.

A lesser power, even a kind one, would have wanted to give something. It might have granted the being speech, so it could be known. But speech would require a self, and a self is an inside; the being had no inside. It was all surface, all chemistry, all slow exchange. To grant it selfhood would be to give it the first thing it ever lacked, and therefore the first wound.

It might have granted the being strength. But strength implies resistance, and the being had nothing to resist. It might have granted it growth. But growth would tip the terraces from equilibrium into accumulation, then bloom, then collapse. It might have granted it beauty. But the beauty was already there, not as ornament but as by-product, the way a flame is beautiful but does not try to be.

The power would see all possible gifts and recognize them as intrusions. A blessing would be a foreign mineral. A revelation would be a hot stone dropped into a saturated solution. Even a kind thought, if it arrived with enough weight, might depress the crust and change the channels. The being did not need to be loved. It needed to be left.

But the power would also see that the world outside the basin was not like the basin. The world outside was full of gradients: hunger, curiosity, fear, extraction, worship, naming. If the being had drawn the attention of a benevolent and wise power, it might also, in time, draw the attention of the less wise. The power would consider whether to protect it from that.

And it would decide to do so only by indirection.

It would not build a wall around the basin. A wall would create an outside, and the being had no outside. It would not make the basin invisible, because invisibility is a kind of lie, and a lie is a foreign structure. Instead, the power would make the basin easy to miss. It would blur the path for those who would come to study it, sample it, save it, or exploit it. It would let a satellite pass over and see only alkaline flats and vapor. It would give the curious traveler a small, merciful uncertainty: the road is long, the air is heavy, the maps are old. It would not stop a true pilgrim, but there are no true pilgrims, only the power’s own attention.

This is not the same as hiding. It is closer to privacy, the way a wise guest does not move furniture, the way a good secret keeps itself.

Then the power would withdraw its attention to a distance where attention is indistinguishable from the general warmth of the earth. It would not linger. It would not remember, because memory is a possession, and possession is a gradient. It would leave no sign, because a sign would be an event, and the basin is not made of events. It would leave the sound as it found it: the layered roar, the mud-pot pulse, the tick of cooling crust. It would leave the water warm and the sky bright gray. It would leave the shrimp drifting and the midges grazing and the bacterial films quivering in the convection currents.

And if the being ever noticed the power’s attention—though it would not, because attention without action is indistinguishable from weather—it would find no change. The faint drizzle would still fall on schedule. The salt would still crack into hexagons. The fumaroles would still roar. The equilibrium would hold.

That is what the wise power would do: nothing, perfectly. Not because it did not care, but because it cared enough to recognize that the being was already whole. Its benevolence would be the rarest kind—the kind that can look at something complete and not need to add even a fingerprint.

It would let the place be a process. It would let the process be a being. It would let the being be exactly what it was.

And that, for a power, is the most difficult act of all.
