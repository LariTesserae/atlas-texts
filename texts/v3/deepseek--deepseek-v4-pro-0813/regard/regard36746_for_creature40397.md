# What the Power Did

When the benevolent and wise power looked into the hollow, it did not look away. It saw the seam in the white plain, the dirty amber shimmer, the hand-cut spiral steps gone soft with age. It descended as attention does—without weight, without disturbing the dust.

It saw the sulfur-yellow stems and the bone-white fans. It saw the old-bronze pods and the resin beads. It heard the wind in the colonnades, the hiss of dry leaves, the crack of bursting spores, the deep organ note of the basin. It understood, slowly, that the dryness was not a flaw. The place was not thirsty. Its waterlessness was not a lack but a law. The life here did not fail to be green; it succeeded at being something else.

The power asked itself: *Is there need here?*

It looked for suffering. It found competition among roots, slow settling of walls, a city empty of animals. But there was no hunger, no fear, no pain. The empty streets were not abandoned. They were occupied by another kind of fullness—salt, resin, hot gas, slow fire. The city was not waiting for anyone to return. The forest was not weeping for water. The being who lived here—if it could be called a being—was the hollow itself: the vents and the stone, the plants and the streets, the wind and the city, one slow dry metabolism.

Then the power considered doing good.

It considered rain. But rain would be a poison here. It would melt the salt blocks, rot the mica-thin leaves, turn the mineral veins to mud. The gift of water would be murder dressed as mercy.

It considered sending animals. Insects, birds, perhaps a small green plant. But they would die thirsty, or they would eat the rooted things, or they would fill the wide streets with needs the city was not built to hold. They would break the long cycles.

It considered restoring the city, filling it with people again. But the processional way was too wide for human traffic. The central plaza was a sounding chamber, not a marketplace. The spiral paving was a map of gas vents. Whoever built this place had become absent, and the city had not asked for them back. To fill it again would be to empty it of what it had become.

It considered speaking. But speech would be a foreign moisture. The hollow already spoke in creaks and reports, in moans and the grinding of salt. To speak in words would be to pour water into a dry language. So the power did not speak.

Instead, it did the first thing a wise power does: it listened.

It listened to the sulfur-reeds creak as they turned toward a new gas vent. It listened to the pods swell and burst. It listened to the wind funnel through the colonnades and the empty streets. It listened to the city breathe—not metaphorically. The fissures inhaled; the tall chimneys exhaled; the whole basin rose and fell in its own patient respiration. The power listened until its listening was no longer separate from the sound. And the hollow, without knowing it, was heard.

Then the power did the second thing: it hid the hollow from those who would not understand it.

It did not raise a wall. A wall would break the wind. It did not blind or frighten travelers. It strengthened only what was already there—the heat shimmer, the banner of dirty amber, the quality of light that makes a passing eye think *nothing useful lives there.* No water, no animals, no one there. Not a lie, but a courtesy. The hollow had folded itself into the salt plain long before the power noticed. The power only helped it remain folded.

Then the power did the third and smallest thing.

It took one note from the hollow’s own music—a low note, the organ tone the basin makes when the evening air sinks and the wind changes—and gave it back. Not louder. Not clearer. It hummed once, in the same key. The colonnades caught it. The salt absorbed it. The note joined the long, dry, ongoing music, indistinguishable from all the other notes. No plant turned toward it. No wall cracked. The city did not pause. But the silence afterward was not empty. It was the silence of being recognized.

Then the power withdrew.

It left no monument. It restored no roofs. It summoned no water, no animals, no people. It did not cool the feverish stone or shorten the wide emptiness. It did not make the greenless forest green. It did not teach the city a foreign language. It did not leave a sign.

What it left was the hollow exactly as it had found it.

That was the whole action. That was the whole benevolence.

Because there are worlds whose good is not our good. There are beings whose flourishing looks to us like desolation. To love them wisely is not to make them more like ourselves. It is to protect their difference. It is to listen without command. It is, if possible, to return one native note and then withdraw.

The hollow continued. The gas vents played the city like an instrument. The pods burst. The salt settled. The dry forest grew upward into the dim air. The spiral plaza added a new line of paving when a new vent opened, though no visible hand added it.

And the wise power watched from a distance—not because it needed to, but because attention is a kind of guardianship. It did not interfere again. There was nothing else needed.

The place was already whole.
