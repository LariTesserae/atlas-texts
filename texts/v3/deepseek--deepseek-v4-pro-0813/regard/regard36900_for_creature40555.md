It noticed the Sump the way a tongue notices a missing tooth—not by seeing it, but by the shape of its absence in the world’s mouth. The power had been aware of broken places before, had catalogued thin spots and frayed edges and the slow seepage of one reality into another. But this was different. This was not a wound so much as a working—a place where the breakage had been gathered, tended, folded into the daily rhythm of pumps and mud hogs and the patient exhalation of methane worms.

It came slowly. That was the first act of wisdom: to match its pace to the place it approached. The Sump did not hurry. The piston rose and fell once an hour. The water level changed over years. The mud hogs migrated by paths that took months. So the power crossed the open flats at a speed that felt like standing still, and it did not disturb the water, and the click-shrimp did not fall silent as it passed, because it moved as the dark itself moved—current without eddy, pressure without force.

It found the Wader at the boundary where ground became deeper water. The same place the Wader always stood at the end of its rounds, looking out into the open black, waiting for an answer the Sump never gave. The power did not announce itself. It simply came and stood, a little to the left, not too close, not too far, the way one might stand at the rim of a shared silence.

The Wader did not turn. It had felt the power coming for days—not as an intrusion, but as a change in the texture of the dark, like the moment before rain. It had known, somewhere in the part of itself that had once been a person, that it was being approached. And now it waited, because waiting was what it did best.

They stood together through a full stroke of the piston. The subsonic throb rose through the soles of the Wader’s feet—if they were feet—and through the power’s attention. The mud released a bubble of warm gas. The bubble rose through the dark and carried, for just a moment, the shape of something almost like a word.

The power looked at the Wader and saw everything. The plastic badge bleached clean of its name. The memory of pockets. The hands—when they were hands—that felt for doorknobs that were no longer there. It saw the spine of old drive shaft and pipe-cable, the ribcage of snap-reed and rusted hoops, the pelt of black fuzz that fed on the gas it exhaled. It saw the way the Wader had been worked on by the Sump for years, shaped from a person into a caretaker, and it saw how much of the person remained: not in form, but in habit. The waiting. The sorting. The laying out of objects in patterns that meant nothing, or everything.

It saw the loneliness too. Not as an absence—the Sump was full of life, dense with eels and mud hogs and pipe-birds and midges—but as a quality of the Wader's attention. There was no one to receive its tending. No one to notice when a distance had been smoothed or a repeated bolt pressed back into singleness. The Wader did its work for the place itself, not for any reward. And that was enough, mostly. But not entirely.

The power thought about what it could offer. It could seal the thin places, reinforce the walls between realities, make the Sump solid and whole and predictable. It could. But that would be a kind of violence, even if a gentle one. The Sump was not a mistake to be corrected. The thinness was not damage; it was a feature. The water that tasted of iron and dates, the leaf from a sunlit tree, the finger bone, the pay stub—these were not contaminations. They were offerings. The Sump was a place where reality had learned to breathe, and to close its pores would be to suffocate it.

It could heal the Wader—restore it to personhood, give it back its name, pull the drive shaft from its spine and the rust from its ribs. But the power was wise enough to ask: would that be healing? The Wader had become something new, something the Sump needed. To force it back into a human shape would be to lose the caretaker, the one who walked the pipe runs and felt for wobbles, who fed on difference and pressed duplicates into singleness. The person was not gone; they were transformed. And transformations are not reversals.

So the power did neither of these things.

Instead, it did something smaller. Something it had learned from watching the Wader itself.

It reached into the Wader's sack—the one made from the skin of a mud hog and lined with black fuzz—and it took out one of the objects the Wader had pressed back into singleness. The piece of lit glass. The power held it up, and in the absolute dark, the glass began to glow—not with light, but with the memory of light, the way a stone remembers the warmth of a sun it has never seen.

The power did not speak. It simply held the glass until the warmth spread from its own attention into the object, and then it placed the glass back into the sack, where it lay among the bolt, the glove, the leaf, the finger bone. The warmth spread through them all, slow and patient, like water finding its level.

The Wader felt it. It made a sound—not the dark bubble of warm gas, not the shape of a word no one living speaks, but something simpler. A small exhalation. A release.

The power understood then what it had come to do. Not to fix, not to heal, not to transform. To witness. To be the one who sees the tending. To offer the difference between being alone and being accompanied, even for a single hour, even for the duration of one piston stroke.

It did not say: you are not alone. That would have been a lie, or at least a promise it could not keep. The power had other places to attend to, other thin spots, other caretakers. But it could say: I see you. I see the work. And it did say this, not in words but in the way it stood, the way it waited, the way it held the lit glass.

The Wader did not respond in words either. But its outline seemed to settle, the air around it agreeing at last where it began. The midges that gathered in the shape of a face pulsed once, softly, like a breath on a window.

They stayed until the piston completed its stroke. Then the power turned to go, moving back across the open flats, following the path the Wader had walked so many times. The Wader watched it go, and in the space where the power had stood, the mud had settled into a shape that held water—a small pool, no wider than a footprint.

The Wader bent and touched the pool. The water was warm. It tasted of iron and dates. And it held, for just a moment, the shape of a reflection.

Not a face. Not a name. But something. A suggestion of outline. A memory of presence.

The Wader straightened. The click-shrimp began their sparse crackle again. The methane worms pushed up their glassy tubes. The Sump settled back into its slow rhythms, and the Wader moved on, leaving no footprints, or too many.

But now, in the dark, there was a new warmth in the sack. The lit glass glowed faintly, not with light, but with the memory of being seen. And when the Wader paused at the edge of the next black pool, it did not wait for an answer. It simply stood, and stood, and then it moved on, because the answer had already come.

The place settled a little more into being the Sump. And in the open black, far off, something heavy shifted—not the piston, not a mud hog, not a delayed clank from days ago. It was the sound of a power, benevolent and wise, carrying the memory of a caretaker back into the vastness, and thinking:

*I will remember this place. I will remember the one who tends it. And when the thinness grows too thin, when the distance stretches too far, I will come back. Not to fix. Not to save. To witness.*

*That is enough. That is what I can offer. And in the offering, we are both changed.*

---

The Wader walked its circular path, from pump station to pump station, along submerged pipe runs, across the open flats. It leaned against pipe trestles and felt for wobbles. It pressed its thumb over repeated bolts. It gathered duplicates and pressed them back into singleness. But now, when it sat on a fractured concrete pad and laid out its objects, it found a new pattern among them—not a pattern that meant nothing, but a pattern that meant: *you are seen. You are not alone in your tending. You are enough.*

And if the Sump noticed the difference, it gave no sign. The pumps cycled on. The black fuzz grew along the gas seeps. The water rose in footprints and filled every depression. The dark remained absolute.

But somewhere in the texture of it, in the suspended water and fine carbon, in the warm wet pressure that moved against the general stillness, there was a new current. A softness. A held breath.

The Sump had been witnessed.

And the witness had been gentle.
