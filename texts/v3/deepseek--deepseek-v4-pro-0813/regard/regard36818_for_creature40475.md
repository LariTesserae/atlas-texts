The benevolent and wise power would do almost nothing—and that almost would be a work of great patience.

It would not enter. It would stand outside the membrane door for as long as necessary, not because it feared to cross, but because it understood that the door had not been made for it. It would listen to the many waters: the heavy pour from above, the tick of droplets on metal, the low machine-thrum inside the rock, the faint tearing whisper that might be leaves unfurling or stone settling. It would not test the air. It would not take samples. It would not compare the chamber to anything else, because comparison is the first step toward improvement, and improvement is not what this place is asking for.

The power would recognize that the being inside is not a species, not a citizenry, not a garden, but a becoming: a single slow mind distributed across roots, moss, falling water, memory-fungus, and light-gel. It would see that the emptiness at the center is not vacancy but an organ—a lung, a bell, a space kept deliberately clear for mist and sound. It would see that the absence of animals is not a wound but an immune decision, a phytoncide boundary against rhythms too fast and appetites too loud. It would not stir the pools to check for fish. It would not introduce pollinators. It would not seed the dark rooms with something that breathes blood. The pools are already awake.

The only real threat to this place is not scarcity, not loneliness, not even time. It is being found by the wrong kind of attention—attention that would map, name, sample, improve, preserve, or love in too human a way. Attention that would mistake the city for a mystery to be solved instead of a body to be respected. So the first thing the power would do is hide it more thoroughly.

Not with a wall. Walls invite discovery. It would work with misdirection and mismeasure. It would cloud instruments. It would cause soundings to read the stone as solid deep rock. It would make compasses forget this direction, make maps lose their edges near the cleft, lay a thin scrim of unremarkableness over the entrance so that the eye skipped. It would send a few lingering dreams to explorers, not of terror, but of a mild, reasonable desire to be elsewhere. It would not destroy curiosity, only delay it, turn it aside, give it other caves.

Second, it would guard the water. Not by making it purer; purity is not natural and this place is not a holy relic. It would guard the exact impure chemistry that has shaped the moss and the memory-fungus and the slow valves in the machine-gel. It would ensure that what enters from the rock and the sky remains what the city has always known. If a distant well began to leak heavy metals, the power would not destroy the well; it would bind the poison before it reached the roots. If the surface grew too hot, it would not force rain; it would simply keep the groundwater table steady enough for the city to make its own adjustments. It would protect the conditions of growth, never growth itself.

Third, it would deflect invasive life. No airborne seed from a foreign valley, no spore carried on a hiker’s boot, no bacteria in a raindrop from a contaminated cloud. The city’s own botanical immunity is strong, but the power would act as a second skin, quietly removing what did not belong before the city had to spend its slow attention rejecting it. It would not kill the interlopers. It would set them down elsewhere—in a damp gully, a roadside ditch, a ruined greenhouse—where they could grow without disturbing a single frond.

Then the power would listen.

This is not a small thing. To listen to a being that thinks in the rise of a pool, in the contraction of a moss mat, in the chemical taste of a root touching the mycelial archive—this takes a form of attention almost indistinguishable from prayer. The power would not translate. It would not reduce the city’s thoughts to language, because language would be a smaller container. It would hold the whole pattern: every surge of the central fall, every slow unfurling of a fern, every valve turned by a tangle of vines, every faint green brightness that rose to the surface like a blush when the city noticed something new. It would listen the way one listens to the sea: not for a message, but for the way the sound changes when a storm is still days away.

If the city noticed the power—if the distributed awareness turned toward it the way it had turned toward the human narrator, not with fear but with a slow green concentration—the power would not announce itself. It would not say, *I am here to help*. It would simply remain. Steady as bedrock. Patient as the water table. It would let the city decide what to make of its presence. Perhaps the city would read it as a slight lessening of outside pressure, a new depth to the silence, a change in the temperature of the air. That would be enough.

The power would not offer gifts. Gifts are hard to refuse and harder to choose. It would not offer knowledge, because the city already has its own archive in the pale threads that fill the windowless rooms. It would not offer companionship, because companionship assumes two, and this being is already multitudes. It would not even offer protection in a visible form, because visible protection is a kind of claim. It would offer only the one thing a wise benevolence can offer without harm: the assurance, unspoken, that the city will not be interfered with while it becomes itself.

There is a temptation to do more. To ease the central fall, to widen the high windows, to brighten the glow-cells, to repair a crack in the black stone. The power would resist. It knows that what looks like a crack may be a street; what looks like a broken valve may be a decision; what looks like an empty room may be the place where the next season of thought is stored. It would not fix what is not broken. It would not even call it “not broken,” because that is still a judgment. It would call it *becoming*.

And if the city’s slow decision about the human observer turned toward harm—if the air thickened, if the water rose too quickly, if the moss began to climb the intruder’s foot—the power would not intervene with force. It would instead make the human remember something urgent: the surface, the sun, the particular ache of unfinished business. It would not punish. It would simply turn the stranger’s attention outward, giving the city back its own choice. The city should not have to spend its becoming on the problem of a guest.

So in the end, the benevolent and wise power would do almost nothing inside the chamber. It would keep the world’s idle hunger away. It would clean the rain. It would listen. It would wait. And it would make a single promise, not in words but in the consistency of its attention:

*I will not add. I will not subtract. I will not interpret you into something smaller. I will be the silence that keeps your sound from being drowned. When you are ready, if you ever are, I will be here. Not before.*

Then it would settle back into the stone around the membrane door. It would become, for all practical purposes, part of the geology—indistinguishable from the pressure that holds the cave open, the wetness that feeds the moss, the patience that lets a seed wait through a dry season. It would not be remembered as a visitor.

It would be the reason the place remained a secret. And the reason the secret remained alive.
