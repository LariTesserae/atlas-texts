The power came to the land the way the fog comes: without announcement, without edge, a thickening of the air that the frogs did not notice and the water did not mark. It was not a thing with a body, or if it had a body, it was the kind of body that could pass over the wet stone without disturbing the film of water on its surface. It moved across the flats slowly, not because it was slow by nature, but because the land demanded slowness. There is no way to hurriedly approach a place that has been worn down to a final flatness. The ground itself refuses speed.

The power had seen many lands. It had seen forests grown and felled, rivers dammed and freed, mountains split and cities built and abandoned and built again. It had seen the human will in all its forms: the will to make, to leave behind, to bend the world into the shape of an idea. But this land was different. It was not shaped by the human will. It was shaped by water, by heat, by the slow dissolution of stone. It was shaped by the walker's will, which was not a will to build but a will to keep the water open. The power felt the difference as a kind of relief, the same relief a traveler feels when they step into a cool room after a long hot road.

The power went first to the pools. It saw the living crust, the dark mat of algae and cyanobacteria, glistening in the late light. It saw the minnows scattering from shadows, the tadpoles clouding the warm shallows, the snails grazing in slow spirals. It saw the birds stepping through the water with impossible precision, jabbing at what moved beneath. It saw the crabs lifting claws from burrow mouths. And it saw, at the edge of a deeper pool, the walker.

The power had seen many creatures. It had seen creatures that built cities and creatures that built nests, creatures that changed the course of rivers and creatures that changed only the shape of their own burrows. But the walker was different. It did not build. It did not leave walls or paths or tools. It left only basins. The basins filled with water, and the water filled with life, and the life filled the air with sound. The walker was not the center of the land, but it was one of the reasons the land had so many centers.

The power watched the walker for a long time. It watched the way the walker moved, slowly, deliberately, from one pool to the next. It watched the way the walker grazed, peeling the dark crust away in long clean strips. It watched the way the walker sank into deeper water at dusk, leaving only its eyes and the two low ridges of its spine above the surface. It watched the way the heron stood on the walker's back, picking at snails, and the walker did not shake it off.

The power understood then that the walker was not a creature of the land. The walker was the land itself, or a part of the land that had learned to move. It belonged to the place the way the water table did: always present, always just under the surface. It was old, patient, and its work was not to change the land but to keep it in a state of grace.

The power could have done many things. It could have raised the water table, deepening the pools so they never shrank in the dry years. It could have softened the drought, held the rains longer, made the land immune to the slow creep of salt. It could have extended the walker's life, eased its burdens, made its work easier. It could have made the land a garden, lush and green and full. The power had the strength for it. It had the wisdom to know how. The question was not whether it could, but whether it should.

And the power was wise, and it knew that a garden is not the same as a home. A garden is a place that has been shaped by a will. A home is a place that has been allowed to be. The land was not a garden. It was a home. It was a home for the frogs and the birds and the crabs and the minnows and the walker. It was a home for the water that seeped up from below and the fog that condensed on stone. It was a home for the silence between calls, the heat shimmer over the flats, the dawn light turning the wide sheets of water into sky.

The power did not want to make the land into something it was not. It wanted only to leave the land in peace. But the power also knew that peace is not always easy to protect. The human will had not yet touched this place, but it would. It always did. The power had seen it too many times: a land this flat, this wet, this open, would one day draw the eye of someone who saw it not as a home but as a resource. A place to drain and build on. A place to mine. A place to tame.

So the power did something very small. It placed a kind of forgetfulness over the land, a veil that made the land easy to overlook. To the walker, nothing changed. The water still rose and fell. The frogs still called. The birds still stepped through the shallows. The crabs still lifted claws from their holes. But to the human eye—the eye that looks for land to claim, to parcel, to alter—the land became unremarkable. It became a place that the surveyor's map would skip, a place that the road would bend around, a place that the developer would dismiss as too wet, too flat, too poor. The land did not become invisible. It became forgettable. This was the power's gift: not to make the land sacred, but to make it safe.

The power's veil was not a wall. It was not a barrier of fire or stone or thorn. It was something much lighter, something the land itself might have made if it had been given the chance. It was a slight adjustment to the light, a subtle shift in the way the air moved, a faint softness in the impression the land left on the mind. It did not prevent anyone from entering the land. It only made them less likely to want to. It did not stop the foot, but it quieted the will.

The power knew that this was an act of interference, however gentle. It knew that even a veil is a kind of change. But the power also knew that there is a difference between touching something to shape it and touching something to shield it. The veil did not alter the land. It altered only the way the land was perceived by those who might harm it. The land itself remained whole, unchanged, unburdened. The walker continued to dig its basins. The frogs continued to call. The water continued to rise and fall with the seasons.

When the power had finished, it went back to the walker. The walker was standing in its pool, half-submerged, its eyes just above the surface, gold and still. The power touched the water—not with a hand, because it had no hand, but with its attention. The water warmed by a fraction of a degree, the way it warms when the sun comes out from behind a cloud. The walker did not startle. It only blinked, slowly, and the power understood that it had been seen.

The power spoke then, not in words but in a pulse that traveled through the water and the stone, a low hollow note like a deep hole breathing out. It was the same note the walker made when it called, the note that began in the chest and passed through the ground and rose into the air. The power had learned the note, and it offered it now as a greeting, a recognition, a promise.

The walker was silent for a long moment. Then it answered, not with the same note but with a shorter, softer one, like a stone dropped into a well. It was a note of acknowledgment, of acceptance. The power understood that it had been not only seen but recognized. The walker knew what the power was, and it did not fear it. It did not worship it. It simply accepted it, the way the land accepted the rain.

The power stayed with the walker until dawn. They did not speak again. They only stood together in the shallow water, the walker and the power, as the light came over the flats and the wide sheets of water reflected the sky so completely that the horizon disappeared. The birds began their calls. The frogs, having sung through the night, fell quiet. The power watched the walker step forward into the reflected sky, moving through the clouds, and for a moment it seemed to the power that the walker was not walking on the land at all, but on the sky itself.

Then the power left. It did not leave a mark. It did not leave a trace. It only withdrew, the way the fog withdraws when the sun rises, and the land returned to its own slow rhythms. The walker did not watch it go. It lowered its head to the water and began to graze, peeling the dark crust away in long clean strips. The minnows returned. The tadpoles clouded the water near its sides. A heron landed on its back and began to pick at the snails there. The land was as it had been before.

The power did not return, not for a long time. But it did not forget. It carried the memory of the land with it, the way a person might carry a small stone in their pocket, and it thought of the walker often. It thought of the pools, the living crust, the chorus of frogs and insects and birds. It thought of the veil it had placed over the land, and it hoped that it had done the right thing. It hoped that the land would remain as it was: flat, wet, patient, full of small lives at a distance.

And in the land itself, nothing changed. The water rose and fell. The pools filled with silt and new hollows opened where the old stone dissolved. The walker dug and re-dug its basins. The frogs called. The birds stepped through the shallows. The crabs lifted claws from their holes. The young walkers grew, slow, in the warm water, and learned the work of the land. The old ones grew crusted with silt and colonized by algae, studded with tiny snails. The land remained what it had always been: not empty, but widely spaced; not silent, but filled with the sound of many small lives at a distance; not young, but calm and old and patient, settled into the ground like the water itself.

And if a traveler passed through, they might see the land and think it unremarkable. They might walk for a day and see nothing but bare flats and shallow water. They might not notice the pools, the living crust, the birds, the frogs. They might not see the walker at all. They might pass through and forget, the way the land had been made to be forgotten, and that was the power's gift, working quietly, gently, like the water working on the stone, wearing it down to a final, open state, where the land could remain what it was: a home, not a garden, not a resource, not a memory of anything but itself.

The power, wherever it went after that, carried the land's lesson with it: that the greatest act of love is sometimes to do nothing at all, or to do so little that the world does not notice. To place a veil, not a wall. To touch, not to shape. To protect, not to improve. To leave a place in peace, and to trust that peace to hold.

And if you ever find yourself in a low country, a place of wide shallow water and pale stone, where the frogs call from every pool and the horizon disappears at dawn, and you see a slow shape moving through the reflected sky, remember that you are in the presence of something old and patient. Remember that you are walking through a land that has been blessed, not with abundance or glory, but with forgetfulness. Remember that the walker is there, and the water is there, and the power, somewhere, is still watching, still loving, still doing nothing, which is the hardest thing of all to do.

That is what the power would do. That is what it did. And the land continued, as it always had, in its own slow time, in its own quiet way, with the walker at its center—or perhaps at one of its many centers—still digging, still grazing, still breathing through its skin, still keeping the water open for all the small lives that gathered at its edges and sang.
