**The Only Gift**

The benevolent and wise power came to the hollow as a change in the weight of the air. It did not part the mist or hush the water; it simply was there, the way a held breath is there. It had seen many worlds, and it had learned that the deepest kindness often looks like nothing at all.

It watched the people for a long time. It saw them through the spray—their pale eyes squinting against light that came from everywhere and nowhere, their hands moving in the language of the chest and the rock, their feet pressed soft as breath against the wet stone. It saw the children learning to walk on the balls of their feet before they learned to speak. It saw the old ones sitting with their fingers in the terraces, reading the water's slow mind. It saw the dead taken down from the high cracks to become needles and roof-pins, and it understood that here, nothing was wasted, not even silence.

The power felt the urge to act. It could warm the water by a degree, and the children's fingers would stop cracking. It could still the mountain's slow shrug, and the walls would stand without falling for a century. It could breathe a single note into the air—a note so soft the mountain would not answer—and the people might remember what music was. All of this was within its reach.

But the power was wise, and wisdom is the knowledge of consequence. If the water warmed, the mineral film would change, and the fish would change, and the people would change, and the hollow would no longer be the hollow that had drawn the power's attention. If the mountain were stilled, the water would stop weeping, and the terraces would dry, and the silence would become a tomb instead of a covenant. If it sang, even softly, the people would not trust the sound. They would press their palms to the rock and wait for the collapse. The gift would be a cruelty.

So the power did not warm the water. It did not still the mountain. It did not sing.

Instead, it did something harder. It listened.

It pressed its own vast attention against the wet stone and became another ear in the rock. It joined the mountain's listening, not to answer, but to share the burden of the silence. The mountain had been listening alone for so long that its attention had become a weight, a jaw clenched against the sound of the world. The wise power did not unclench it. It simply listened beside it, and in that sharing, the mountain's listening grew easier, like a muscle that has learned it is not alone.

The people noticed, though they could not name it. The water seemed to run a little smoother. The walls seemed to hold their breath a little longer. A child who had never laughed aloud felt, for a moment, a warmth in her chest, as if someone had placed a hand there in the dark. She pressed her palm to the rock, and the rock pressed back—not with words, but with a pulse, once, like a heartbeat underwater.

She did not tell anyone. She did not need to. The old woman beside her, blind and patient, turned her head as if she had heard something in the stone. Then she went back to her work.

The wise power withdrew. It left behind no miracle, no sign, no memory of itself. It had given the only gift that would not break what it loved: its silence.

And the hollow continued, bright and wet and crowded and quiet, a small urban storm wearing the shape of a stone bowl, with the sound turned off—but now, in the deepest part of the rock, there was a second listener, holding the mountain's voice in check, so that the people could go on being the silence made flesh.

For a long time, nothing changed. And that was the blessing.
