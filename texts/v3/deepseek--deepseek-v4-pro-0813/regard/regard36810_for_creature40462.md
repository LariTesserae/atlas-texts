The benevolent and wise power came to the crater’s rim and looked down. It did not need to enter. It saw the black mirror, the young mountains with their edges like fractured glass, the water too salty to freeze and too still to be water. It saw the drowned town hanging in the dark at its various depths—the cables no thicker than a finger, the red pulses of the arrestors, the cathedral of stone and silver seams. It saw the resident.

For a long time the power did not move. It considered what it might do.

It could have warmed the lake by a single degree—a gift of comfort, of thaw. It could have sent a current through the basalt ribs, a small breath in the held breath. It could have opened the copper sphere, answered the slit, become the key. It could have made the blind eel stay, or given the resident a voice, or let the mineral dendrites bloom into something living. It could have stood on the crater rim and called down, *You are not alone.*

But the power was wise, so it looked deeper.

It saw that the resident was not suffering its stillness. The stillness was not a prison or a wound. It was a body, a home, a decision made so long ago it had become nature. The lake’s sameness was not decay; it was the first morning, holding. The town was not abandoned; it was inhabited by the thing that held it. The red pulses were not cries for help. They were nerve-firings of a creature whose whole thought was *keep it still*. The slit in the copper sphere was not a mouth begging for a key. It was a blank, and the resident did not fear blanks. It had no future, and did not miss one.

The power felt something like grief—not for the being, but for the universe’s habit of confusing stillness with death. It saw how easily a lesser mercy might have arrived: warm the water, send a companion, unlock the sphere. And it saw how each of those mercies would have been a stone dropped into a lake that had never known a ripple. The resident’s entire existence was the cancellation of ripples. To love it truly was to become, for a moment, as still as it was.

So the power did nothing.

It did not warm the brine. It did not stir the water. It did not descend the ladder into the concentric chambers. It did not answer the slit. It did not become the key. It did not even protect the place, because protection would have been a promise, and a promise is a future. The place had no future, and that was not tragic. It was the shape of its peace.

For a while the power simply attended. It held the lake in its awareness without entering, the way one might hold a breath without taking it. It felt the contraction of the sac, the low hum in the basalt, the slow thought traveling from arrestor to arrestor. It saw the blind eel return, circle the copper sphere, and vanish into the deeper dark. It saw the ribbon-worm on its cable, absorbing iron. It saw the ferns of manganese and iron branching across the rock—chemistry with a shape, not living, but not lifeless either. It saw the town below the surface, sparse and vertical, a settlement of distances.

Then the power withdrew.

It took nothing. It left nothing. The lake was exactly the same temperature at the surface and at the floor. The arrestors pulsed as they had always pulsed. A cable clicked by a fraction of a hair, and the click rolled around the crater walls and faded. The blind eel would return in a few hundred days. The worm would stay on its cable. The slit would remain a slit.

The resident did not know it had been seen.

And that was the mercy.
