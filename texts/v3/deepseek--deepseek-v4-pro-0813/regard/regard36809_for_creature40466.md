# The Attention

There is a kind of power that does not arrive. It was already in the light, and then, without warning, it was paying attention. That is the only way to say it. The plateau had been turned toward its own slow business for a thousand thousand years, and then something noticed it.

The power did not come with thunder or retinue. One morning the fog simply lingered a little longer on the high plain, and in the small hours, when the bats had gone and the frogs had clicked once and fallen silent, something was present which had not been present before. Not a shape. Not a sound. More like the moment when a held breath is recognized as held.

It had seen many worlds. It had seen gardens and furnaces, cities of glass, oceans boiling with newborn life, empires that considered themselves eternal and were, in the end, only a different kind of weather. It had learned over long eons that love is easy and wisdom is difficult. So when its attention fell upon the plateau, it did not at first move. It watched.

It watched the way water obeyed the stone. It watched the shallow basins, the smooth fins of rock, the runnels that carried the morning wetness from pan to pan. It watched the copepods in their thousands, the shield-shaped tadpole shrimp drifting over the mud, the snails whose shells were the color of the stone and whose spirals went the wrong way. It watched the pale geckos press themselves against the warm rock, one leg at a time. It watched the bats spill from deep holes at dusk in long silent ribbons. And it watched the people.

The people did not know they were being watched, or if they did, they gave no sign. They rose before the fog lifted and went to the channels while the stone was wet. They cleared tiny blockages with pebbles and fingerbones. They knelt beside pools for an hour without shifting, and the frogs did not stop watching them. Eventually the frogs accepted them as part of the rock, and life went on.

The power saw their smallness, their hunger, their silences. In the lowlands they would have been called poor. The power knew better. They had not forgotten how to want little. They had not forgotten how to wait. That is not a small thing. It is a discipline of centuries. The plateau had been reduced to its final texture—a low, endless pavement of solution pans and rounded partitions—and the people had been reduced to theirs. Not reduced. Refined.

The power could have filled every pan with clear water. It could have made the dwarf trees tall and fruit-bearing, softened the stone into soil, called up fish in the pools. It could have given the people fire that did not smoke, tools that did not break, children in every generation, words for their grief. It had done such things before, in other places, and sometimes it had been right to do them.

It considered the hunger. It considered the child with the clear frog-belly skin lying with one ear against a stone, listening to the faint gurgle of water finding its way. It considered the old woman tracing a spiral in the air, her face taking on the texture of the rock in the half-light. It considered the long silences after death, when a body was laid in a deep grike and allowed to go back to the plateau. It could have ended all of that suffering. It wanted to. Benevolence, in its raw form, is a kind of hunger too.

But wisdom said: look again.

The hunger is not the point. The point is the listening. They are not waiting for you to come. They are waiting the way the rock waits for rain, the way the eggs wait in the dry mud, the way the trees wait for the fog. If you fill their pools, they will forget the channels. If you give them plenty, the silence will go out of them. They will become something else—not worse, perhaps, but not themselves. And they are themselves so completely that even a god should hesitate to edit them.

The power did not abstain out of coldness. It abstained because it saw their becoming as a thing already holy. The water was their only currency, and every hollow was its own small country. The rock was not dead; it was a slow reef, part mineral, part life. Sound was risk, so most creatures were silent. And everything here could wait. To break that would be to break the very condition that made them worth noticing at all.

So it did one thing, or maybe two, but they were not things anyone could point to.

It made their obscurity a law of the world. It breathed a kind of cloud into the minds of mapmakers, a soft misremembering into the feet of explorers. The roads that might have found them turned aside. The lowland kings who might have wanted their stone or their water found their attention sliding away like rain off old bone. It did this not because the people were children to be hidden, but because they had not asked for the world's gifts, and the world's gifts would have burned them.

And then, for the people themselves, it did nothing. Or almost nothing.

On a morning when an old man—one of the very old, who carried the map of every pool in his head not as a picture but as a memory of taste and sound—sat beside a deep grike and listened for water that had not come for three seasons, the power did not send rain. It did not split the stone. It did not speak.

But it sent the fog.

A thick, silent fog that lasted until noon, so the pans filled a little, and the runnels ran. The old man heard the drip and smiled, not knowing why. The children raced drops of water down a tilted pan. The snails unglued themselves from the stone and moved. And when the sun burned through, the plateau smelled of wet lime, iron, and a faint organic sweetness from the algae.

The power did not appear. It did not announce itself. It let the weather carry the message.

*I see you. I will not flood you. But you are not forgotten.*

That was all. It did not raise a temple. It did not leave a name. It did not turn the people into a story about itself. It simply remained at the edge of their world, like a second horizon, watching. Not as a master. Not as a savior. As one who keeps a vigil.

And in the long quiet after the fog lifted, the plateau held its breath, and the power held it too. Not interfering. Not improving. Just attending.

Which, for a wise power, is the rarest and most difficult gift of all.
