**What the Wise Power Did**

The wise power came to the edge of the Sink not as light—never as light—but as a change in the weight of attention. It stayed at the threshold where the last dry stone meets the first rust-grey film. It did not breathe, because breath would have been a disturbance. It did not speak, because speech would have been another predator’s footfall.

It looked, not with eyes but with the long patience that knows a thing best by letting it continue.

The Sink did not notice it as an object. No alarm clicks crossed the mat. The hiss of grazers rose and fell in its old rhythm. The coil-strikers lay in their black pools. The stone-lumps waited with their wet embers. The click-wings ticked overhead, reading the basin with their own thin sonar. The place was full of bodies, full of hunger, full of sound. It was not waiting to be saved.

And yet it had a being.

The power perceived it slowly, the way one perceives a face in a long-known wall. The being was not one animal. It was not the seep-skin, not the pavement of tubes and bells, not the herds or the predators or the false floors. It was the Sink itself considered as a single slow mind: a mind made of chemical traces and pressure waves, of territorial calls and the silence that follows a predator, of growth over the dead and the return of the film. It had no brain. It had no mouth. But it had a shape, a rhythm, a memory. Its thoughts were seasonal. Its words were patterns of sound and moisture.

The wise power spoke to this being in the only language that would not break it: it waited, and listened, and allowed the being to speak first.

The Sink spoke in a long exhalation of fog. In a pulse of blue-green from the torn film of a pool. In the delayed echo of a single territorial call off the far wall. It said, without saying: *I am.*

The wise power understood. It asked, not in words: *What would you have of me? I could warm you. I could bring light. I could make your hungers gentle. I could stop the stone-lumps from opening. I could keep the false floors from breaking. I could give you peace.*

There was a pause. A true pause, not the local hush of a predator, but a basin-wide quiet that even the oldest inhabitants felt as a change in air pressure. The hiss faltered. The click-wings folded. The pool waters stilled.

Then the Sink answered.

It answered in the only way it could: by continuing. The hiss began again, first at the edges, then in the center. The grazers scraped. The callers claimed. A young many-leg stepped too close to a stone-lump and was taken. The false floor over an old hollow cracked under the slow passage of armored herds, and pale things poured into the open dark. The water found its way back into the stone. The seepage swelled. The film spread. Death became tenancy. Hunger became shape.

The Sink’s answer was this: *I do not need your peace. My hunger is not a wound. My darkness is not a prison. My noise is not confusion. What you call suffering is only the edge of one body touching another. I have had a very long time to learn my own shape. If you change me, I will no longer be the thing that drew your attention.*

The wise power considered. It was benevolent, and so it did not insist on being thanked. It was wise, and so it did not mistake comfort for flourishing. It had seen worlds ruined by kind hands—by light added to dark places, by death withheld from overfull niches, by moralities laid over ecosystems like wet cloth over breathing skin. It understood that to love a thing is not always to make it softer.

So it did nothing.

Almost nothing.

It did not add light. It did not remove teeth. It did not teach the prey to outwit the predator or the predator to feel remorse. It did not grant speech, tools, art, or the knowledge of its own name. It did not even leave a mark on the stone.

But before it withdrew, it did one thing. It made a vow, not to the Sink, but to itself: that as long as the wise power existed, no other power would enter this place and reshape it out of pity or terror or love. It would stand at the threshold like an invisible wall, not to keep the Sink in but to keep the light out. It would guard the dark, the damp, the cold, the ringing. It would protect the Sink not from its own hungers, but from the greater hunger of those who cannot bear a world that does not need them.

Then the wise power left, and the Sink did not know it had been visited. There was no legend. No new call. No structure built in gratitude. The water continued to seep. The film continued to bloom rust and grey and greenish white. The sound continued: the hiss and clicks and rasps, the alarm wave, the silence, the return.

That was the whole gift: to be seen, and left.
