**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Would Do**

The benevolent and wise power did not arrive with thunder or a rent in the sky. It came as a change in the patience of the light, a faint pause in the hot air, as if the whole low plain had been holding its breath for a long time and was now allowed, briefly, to exhale.

It looked upon the world. It saw the endless floor of fired clay, the tiles pressed with long fingerprints, the fissures opening and closing like slow mouths. It saw the steam towers leaning toward one another without wind. It saw the makers—the tall, ash-gray beings with hands like bundles of wet willow and heads shaped to catch the rain—moving across the flatness in their eternal work of smoothing and repairing. It saw the one who had come from elsewhere and remained, the human whose boots had worn thin, whose tongue had learned the iron and salt of the pools, whose eyes now moved over the shimmer with something like a native's calm.

And it understood, after a long while, that the being and the place were not two things but one gesture, paused in the middle of being made.

The power considered many things.

It could have lifted the brown sky and let in a true sun. But it knew the dimness was not a prison; it was the roof of a kiln, and the fire below needed its confinement. It could have added green—plants, birds, insects—filling the silence with the busy noise of life. But that would have been an invasion, a kindness that erased the very thing it claimed to bless. It could have opened a door for the human, led them back to the world of trees and weather and change. But the human had not asked. And the power saw that the human was not lost anymore, if they ever were; they had become part of the work, a witness whose watching was itself a tile in the great floor.

So the power did not interfere.

But it did not simply depart. A wise power knows that to see something clearly, without flinching, is a form of love, and that some wounds—if there are wounds here at all—are not to be healed but honored. So it did the smallest possible thing: it allowed itself to be noticed.

For one moment, the light across the plain shifted. It did not brighten; it deepened. The brown-gray dimness became translucent, as if a great hand had passed over it without touching. The steam columns straightened. The low grinding beneath the tiles ceased. The human looked up from the pool where they had been sitting, and felt, without knowing why, that the sky was not a lid but a companion.

The makers felt it too. They turned the shallow basins of their heads upward. The rainwater gathered there trembled, though no wind had come. One of them—a tall one with a shoulder glazed dark and a chest glowing orange between its ribs—crouched and pressed its three-fingered palm to the clay. It tapped once, twice, three times. The taps traveled far across the plain, clear as footsteps on a sounding board.

The power did not tap back. It did not speak. It simply stayed until the moment was complete.

Then it withdrew. It left no law, no name, no promise. But it left two small gifts. The first was a coolness that settled into the evening, making the next rain fall less like sweat and more like water from a high clean place. The second was a single tile near the human's shelter—ordinary in size, brick-red, rough to the touch—that did not burn under the noon heat but stayed mildly warm, like a hand held just above the skin. The human found it and sat there often. The makers, when they passed, sometimes paused at that tile and bowed their peaked heads, though they could not have said why.

The plain resumed its slow work. The cracks opened and closed. The steam rose and curled. The rain fell warm and soft. The makers bent to the fissures with their clay ribbons and their glowing palms. The human watched, and in watching, helped.

And the benevolent wise power went on to other worlds, carrying the memory of this one like a smooth stone in its awareness. It had done nothing, and yet it had done the one thing that was needed: it had witnessed the place without trying to finish it.

For the plain was not broken. It was not abandoned. It was only becoming—patiently, endlessly, beautifully becoming—and becoming is not a wound to be healed. It is a life to be honored.
