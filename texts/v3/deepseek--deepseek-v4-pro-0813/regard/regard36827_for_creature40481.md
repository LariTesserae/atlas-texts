**The Attention**

The power did not come down the ladder. It was already there, folded into the pause after the pump, in the condensation on the cold pipe, in the exact angle of the street-light blade. When it finally turned its attention to the room, it did so the way a hand cups a flame: not to hold it, but to keep the wind away.

It saw the room as a single breathing body. The pump was the heartbeat. The water was the blood. The crickets were the warmth at the skin, the snails the mineral patience, the shrimp the thoughtless pulse, the frogs the voice. The centipede was the nerve. And the man—the man was the memory, or what was left of it.

It saw how he ate only the old snails, how he cleared the pale shoots, how he repaired the frogs’ mud basins with his thumb. It saw the scar where a station badge had hung, the keys rusted to the leather, the moss at his jaw. It understood that the man was not trapped. He was rooted. To pull him out would be to tear the room’s slow root from its soil. To give him back his name would be to drown him in a language the room could not hear. To heal his scar would be to erase the one map he still carried. So the power did not save him. It saved the conditions that let him be.

It considered opening the switch panel and letting the station’s light pour down. It considered sending a maintenance worker with a kind face and a thermos of soup. It considered stopping the pump for one full minute so the man could hear true silence. It considered all these and set them aside.

Instead, it did what wise powers do in small places. It did not add. It subtracted dangers.

It reached into the pump’s old motor and smoothed a bearing that would have failed in winter. It breathed on the bulb’s filament so it would not darken. It sealed the hairline cracks above the water table so no sewage or fuel could seep in. It laid a forgetfulness over the station’s blueprints, so the room remained a rumor, a locked panel no one had reason to open. It made the street grate shed its leaves a little to the left, so the blade of daylight would keep falling on the three pale shoots. It touched the frogs’ mud basins and set a mineral in the clay that would keep their eggs from fungus. It did not touch the centipede, but it made the conduit warm enough in winter that the centipede would not need to hunt more than the room could spare.

For the man, it did one thing only. It waited until the four-second quiet after the pump, when the room was between exhale and inhale, and it tapped twice on the steam pipe—not a word, not a command, just the same two-note tap the frogs make, but slower, as if the pipe itself had remembered a pulse. The man lifted his head. The crickets parted. He tapped back, twice, with his tongue against the roof of his mouth. Then the pump started, and the room resumed.

The power withdrew, but not entirely. It left a small portion of its attention lodged in the pressure gauge, so that if the needle ever fell to zero, somewhere far away a bell would ring and the power would return. Not to save the man. To save the room.

The pump inhaled. The shrimp constricted. The snails closed. The man settled back against the dry ledge, one hand in the water, one hand on the warm pipe. Nothing had changed. Everything had been kept.
