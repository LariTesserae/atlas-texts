The power came to the hollow not as a storm or a voice, but as a change in the quality of the light. The green-brown dimness lifted for a moment—not brighter, but clearer, as if the water itself had leaned closer to listen. The crabs paused, their big claws held halfway in the air, then went on with their sorting. The mudskippers did not dive. The birds turned their amber eyes toward the shift and blinked once, slowly, unalarmed. They had not been waiting, but they were not surprised.

The power looked at the place the way a careful hand might turn a leaf without tearing it.

They saw the root chambers, the lanes of black water, the platforms in the canopy. They saw the city no one planned. They saw the crabs carrying pellets of fresh clay, the birds pruning branches, the fish turning in synchronized flashes. They saw the water rising and falling with an underground rhythm, and they followed that rhythm down through the porous rock, through the dark, until they found its source.

The mud-sleeper lay in the deepest chamber, half in water, half in black mud. Its back was cracked clay. Moss grew on its plates. Small crabs lived in the crevices of its skin. Its eyes, dark and film-covered, were open but not watching. Its sides rose and fell. The water rose and fell with them.

The power watched it for a long time. They counted the slow breaths. They saw the sleeper press its wide mouth into the sediment and sift it, leaving clean clay from the corners of its jaws. They saw the crabs gather that clay and carry it away to mend their walls. They saw the sleeper reach up with long-fingered hands and tuck a hanging root into a gap, not hurrying, not forcing, only weaving. They saw the tail smooth a new lane through the mud. They understood that this was not an animal in a place. This was the place’s oldest part, the one that made the rest possible.

The power considered what they could do.

They could speak to the sleeper, give it words, plans, the architecture of thought. But the sleeper’s thoughts were already written in silt and root and crab paths. Words would be a poorer language.

They could quicken the work, make the mud yield faster, strengthen the limbs. But the slowness was not a flaw. The place was built by the pace of roots and sediment. Speed would erode instead of build.

They could heal the cracked plates, clear the film from the eyes. But the cracks held small lives. The film was not blindness. To heal would be to evict.

They could show the sleeper the world beyond the hollow. But the hollow was already a world. The outside would only introduce a want that had no home here.

The power considered leaving gifts: fire, metal, music. But fire would dry the mud. Metal would cut the roots. Music would argue with the place’s own silence, which was not empty but full—full of drips, ticks, soft plops, the hiss of gas. The silence was itself a sound the place had built.

So the power did not give gifts of the usual kind. They sat in the damp and let the silence press into them. They listened to the thrumming under the mud, the slow language that said *stay, grow, hold, sink, rise.* They let the place accept them not as a god but as a witness. A bird landed on their attention and resettled, as if the attention were a branch.

Then the power did one thing.

They looked at the low ridge of limestone, the tangle of roots, the mist that already collected there. They saw that the hollow was hidden by its own depth and shade, but not enough. There were powers in the world that did not have this power’s patience. There were hungry things, mapmakers, collectors, curious ones who would love the place to death. They would call it a discovery. They would build a road. They would take the sleeper from its mud and call it rescue.

So the power took a decision, not a breath, and wrapped the hollow in a gentle forgetting. It was not a wall. Walls invite climbing. It was not a glamour. Glamours break. It was a drift, a mist in the mind, the way a dream slips away on waking, the way a path becomes overgrown if no one walks it. The hollow became not invisible but unimportant. Any who came near without cause would find their curiosity sliding away like water off a waxy leaf. They would think of something else. They would walk a different way.

The power then placed their attention over the hollow like a fern over a burrow, not to smother but to shade. They did not enter the sleeper’s dream again. They did not ask to be known. A wise power does not need to be known. It needs only to keep the rain from falling too hard on a place that is still growing.

They left without leaving. The mist stayed. The crabs went on carrying clay. The sleeper took another slow breath, and the water rose a finger’s width.

And the power did nothing else.

Unless something comes to break the hollow open, unless the world outside learns to remember it against that forgetting—then, perhaps, the power will do what is necessary. They will be the storm that turns the intruders aside. They will be the mud that swallows the road. They will be the bird that gives one low wooden call before the mist closes. But not now.

Now they watch. Now they let it build.

That is what a benevolent and wise power does for a place that is already whole: almost nothing, and by doing almost nothing so completely, they make of their attention a shelter. They guard not by changing the thing but by keeping it unchanged. They hold the light so the roots can hold the mud. They breathe out, and the mist holds closer. They do not speak, because the place is already speaking.

And the sleeper, deep in its damp dream, does not know it is watched. It does not need to know. It only needs to go on breathing: *stay, grow, hold, sink, rise.* The hollow listens. The crabs listen. The birds watch. The roots obey.
