**The Attention**

The benevolent and wise power came to the Roaring Fold, not with thunder, but with the restraint of one who has learned that some things are best held without hands.

It had crossed many skies and stilled many storms. It had warmed the frozen and watered the dry. Wherever it went, it went to mend. But at the edge of the high gray shelf, it paused.

It saw the gravel terraces, the low lead sky, the black pools ringed with white salts. It heard the roar with no source. It felt the cold that never left, the dampness that never dropped. It recognized the place for what it was: a thing so entirely itself that it no longer needed to change to exist.

And it saw the Dweller.

The Dweller was walking the faint line of lighter stones. It took three steps, touched a stone, turned its head to the left. Then the braid of the shelf folded, and the Dweller was beside the cairn without crossing the space between. It was not a ghost. It had weight. It was not a prisoner. It had no want of release. It was not a guardian. There was nothing to guard against. It was simply present, like the frost, like the roar, like the gray light.

The power felt the old instinct rise in it—the urge to heal, to release, to complete.

It thought: *I could stop the sound. I could part the cloud. I could give the Dweller a future.*

But it did not.

Because the roar was not a wound. It was the valley’s one long note. To quiet it would be to take the Fold’s voice.

Because the cloud was not a prison. It was the Fold’s skin. To burn it away would be to flay the place.

Because the Dweller was not waiting. It had no future to want, no past to mourn. Its stillness was not an illness. It was a condition, like the valley’s shape or its cold.

The wise power understood that not every silence is a cry for speech. Not every cold is a cry for warmth. Not every stillness is a cry for motion.

So it did the rarest thing it knew.

It stayed still.

It folded away its radiance until it was only a gray light in a gray place. It let the dampness bead on its skin and not drip. It let the frost form on its shoulders and not thicken. It sat at the Listener’s Chair, and the roar filled its hollow chest, its long bones, the spaces between its ribs. It did not resist. It did not add a note. It let the sound be inside it, the way the stones let the cold be inside them.

The Dweller came.

Not walking toward it, but appearing, as if the fold of the shelf had carried it there. It raised one long hand. It touched the power’s shoulder, the way it touched each stone of the cairn, as if confirming that it was still there. The power did not flinch. It made itself stone-colored, cold, damp, almost empty.

For a long moment, the two of them were indistinguishable from the place.

Then the Dweller turned and resumed its rounds.

The power remained for a little while longer. It did not weep. It did not sigh. It did not leave a blessing on the stones or a sign in the clouds. It simply understood.

When it left, it left no trace.

It did not hide the Fold, for the Fold hid itself. It did not heal the Dweller, for the Dweller was whole. It did not stop the roar, for the roar was the Fold’s own long breath. It did not plant a flower, bring a bird, or mark the path. It did not even leave a name.

It only remembered.

It held the Roaring Fold in its mind like a stone in the hand, without squeezing. It carried the Dweller’s stillness within it, not as a sorrow, but as a peace. And when it returned to the world of causes and futures and changes, it went differently, because it had learned the most difficult mercy: the mercy that leaves a thing exactly as it is.

The Roaring Fold remained gray, cold, loud, and still. The Dweller remained tall, thin, patient, and without need. And the power—the benevolent and wise attention that had come from so far—became for an instant a part of the chord, then withdrew without adding a note.

Perhaps that was the only thing it could do. Perhaps it was the only thing that needed doing.
