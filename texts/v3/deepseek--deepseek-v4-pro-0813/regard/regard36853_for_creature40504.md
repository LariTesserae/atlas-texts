The benevolent and wise power did not arrive with light or noise. It came as a small change in the salt, a temperature the fish could almost feel, a pause in the current. It had seen many things in its long life—mountains that were once seabeds, forests that were once glaciers, deserts that were once forests—but this was different: a city that had become a reef, a reef that had become a city, a life made of lives.

It hung above the halocline, where the upper water and lower water met like a glass floor. It tasted the silt, the lime, the faint mineral breath of distant vents. It saw the buildings standing shoulder to shoulder, not as ruins but as skeletons for a new body. Tubeworms, barnacles, sponges, and coral-like animals had laid their own skeletons over the stone. The walls were alive. The doorways were held by morays. The ground floors were full of groupers. The streets were rivers of fish.

It saw the true resident of the drowned city—not the grouper, not the moray, not the urchin, but the composite body they made together. The city was not dead; it was becoming an animal.

The power considered what to do.

It could raise the city from the sea, return it to air and sun, restore the roofs, repaint the walls, rekindle the fires. But it knew: the first inhabitants were gone, and the new ones would die. The city was not a mistake to be corrected; it was a metamorphosis. To drain it would be to stop a heart.

It could bless the residents. It could make the groupers wise, the morays gentle, the urchins careful. It could command the jacks to circle forever, the comb jellies to glow brighter, the coral to grow faster. But a blessing is a kind of weight. The city had its own order: the waiting, the patience, the slow bellows, the grazing, the spawning, the tide. To improve it would be to overwrite it.

It could speak. It could say, *I see you. I honor you. I will protect you.* But the city did not use words. It spoke in the snap of shrimp, the creak of old masonry, the click of barnacles, the low grind of a fish’s teeth. A voice would be a foreign sound, a wind where there was no wind. Some love is silence.

It could stay and watch. That, perhaps, was the only thing.

So the power stayed. It did not enter the doorways or disturb the morays. It did not cross the halocline, though it could have. It remained at the boundary, like the thin fish that lived in both cities at once. It watched the old grouper in the customs house turn her gold-rimmed eye toward the current, not because she knew the power was there, but because the current had changed. It watched the jacks fold into a stairwell when a shadow passed, and re-emerge like a thought returning. It watched the urchins graze the walls in herds, leaving pale trails on the living stone. It watched the comb jellies bloom pale blue in the alleys, the tiny crustaceans rise from the silt like green sparks. It watched the tide turn twice a day, carrying the silt and shell dust through the streets, redrawing the city in new drifts. It watched the warmest month turn the water milky with spawn, and every surface become a nursery.

It did nothing. It did the hardest thing: it did not intervene.

And after a long time—long enough that the grouper in the customs house had grown slightly larger, long enough that a new layer of coral had covered the old lintels—the power understood that its attention was not needed. The city was not waiting for a savior. It was not waiting at all. It was becoming. It was a life made of lives, and every window, every balcony, every collapsed roof was already a territory of that life. The power’s presence had changed nothing, and that was the only fitting gift: to leave the city as it was, to add no mark, to be a witness that did not insist on being known.

When the power finally withdrew, it did so the way a tide goes out: not as an absence, but as a return. The water did not remember it. The fish did not remember it. The city did not remember it. The only trace it left was a slight warmth in the upper water, indistinguishable from the season, and perhaps a sense, among the residents, that they had been seen. But that too faded.

And that was all. That was everything.
