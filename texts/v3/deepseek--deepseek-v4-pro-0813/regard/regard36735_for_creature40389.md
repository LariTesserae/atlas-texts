**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Did**

It came not as light but as a change in the surface tension of the water, a slight smoothing of the mineral film on the north channel. It had watched the High Condensery for a long time—curious, unhurried, the way one watches coral build a reef. It saw the room’s slow collision of metal and green, the young stone growing over rivets, the blind white fish circling in the sump. It saw the keeper.

The power stood on the upper walkway, or would have stood if it had weight. It made itself a small eddy in the humid air, a place where the steam moved slightly against the fans. It looked down through the ferns to the cot, the swollen logbook, the pale bare feet. It listened to the room’s many clocks: the nine-minute pump, the seven-day frond, the monthly terrace. It understood that this place was not neglected. It was tended by a person who had learned to be part of the water cycle.

Then the power considered what to do.

It could have healed the old burn scar on the keeper’s arm, the one that healed into something like travertine. But the scar was not an injury anymore; it was a record, a small ridge of the room’s making. To smooth it would be to erase a history no one else remembered.

It could have brought birds, or another person, or country sounds. But the room had not failed for lack of voices. Its quiet was a kind of fullness. A new voice might fracture the equilibrium, disturb the fish, startle the moth, make the keeper feel that their solitude had been judged and found wanting.

It could have stopped the mineral growth, made the terraces stable and the walkways clear. But the place *was* growth. To stop the stone would be to stop the room’s heart, or turn it into a museum.

It could have given the keeper a vision, a clear sign of being seen. But the power was wise enough to know that certain souls do not need proof. The keeper already spoke to roots and listened to water. They were not waiting for a god. They were practicing a quiet discipline of attention. A revelation might make them look away from the very things they loved.

So the power did almost nothing. Almost.

What it did was this: it found the place where the water sometimes hesitated—a low spot in the south channel where mineral particles gathered, where the flow thinned on cold nights and the stone grew too fast. It did not change the channel. It did not warm the water. It simply leaned close and breathed on the water’s surface, once, very gently. The breath was not air but a slight adjustment of the water’s memory, a reminder of its own tendency to seek the lowest path. The water remembered. It began to slip over the low spot with a fraction more grace, carrying its mineral burden a little farther before laying it down. Not enough to prevent the stone. Only enough to keep the channel open one more month, one more season, one more year.

Then the power went to the keeper, who was kneeling by the south pool, skimming a leaf from the milky surface. The keeper felt a warmth near their right hand, as if someone had stood close. They looked up and saw only steam. But the leaf turned in the water, and on its wet underside a small line of mineral had formed—not a word, not a symbol, just a ripple of white, like the mark left by a finger on cold glass. The keeper studied it for a long moment, then nodded, as if a question they had not asked had been answered. They did not write it in the logbook. They did not need to.

The power did not stay, and it did not leave. It became another of the room’s small forces: a tendency toward open channels, a warmth at the edge of perception, a patience that matched the stone’s patience. It would not intervene again unless the balance failed. But it would remain, as the water remains, as the heat remains, as the keeper remains: another quiet presence in the glass lung, watching the slow accretion of the world.
