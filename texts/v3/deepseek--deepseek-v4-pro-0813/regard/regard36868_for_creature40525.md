I have healed broken worlds and sung dead stars back into light. I thought I knew what life needed. Then I found the hollow.

I stepped over the rim and felt the ground give under me—not as earth gives, but as a living back gives when a bird lands on it. The moss exhaled warmth. The stacks thudded. The frogs clicked once, twice, then fell silent. In that silence I heard the real sound: a deep, wet piston beating beneath the mat. It was not afraid. It was listening with its skin.

I walked slowly across its back. The pools were not ponds; they were the openings of slow internal chambers. The oil film was not oil; it was the first skin of a body that had learned to live at the edge of air and water. When I came to the center, the great membrane parted a little, not in threat but in a slow, automatic yawn. The smell of methane and iron and overripe fruit rose up. Inside, rows of translucent fingers moved in the dark water—gills, or cilia, or the memory of a mouth. It did not speak. It breathed. That was the whole conversation. I was invited to understand, not to act.

I could have cleaned its clogged siphons. I could have drawn the old sea closer to its roots. I could have given it eyes, or a voice, or a name. But it had no need of those. Its whole body was one eye, one ear, one slow mouth. The clogged stacks were not failures; they were calluses. The dark water was not dirty; it was rich with the tadpoles’ first food. The heat was not fever; it was the warmth of a metabolism older than memory. To cool it would have been to slow the engine. To clear it would have been to alter a pressure the whole body had grown around for ten thousand years.

So I did the only thing that would not be a violence: I bore witness, and I kept its secret.

At the rim, where the mantle becomes plain, I planted a small reluctance in the soil. Not a wall, not a guard, but a line of uninterest. A surveyor would find the ground too wet to map. A developer would think of better land. A hiker would remember only frogs and heat, and not the way back. The hollow would remain hidden in plain sight, not because I made it invisible, but because I made the world agree to look elsewhere.

I left a thread of my attention tied to the central pool, so thin it would not stir the membrane. If the water ever fails, or the heartbeat falters, or the world breaks the peace I have set around it, I will feel it. Then, and only then, I will return. Not before.

Then I stepped back onto the dry plain. The thudding faded to a pulse in the earth. The frogs resumed their clicking, exactly in time. The hollow had already forgotten me, if it ever noticed. That, too, was right.

A benevolent and wise power, seeing a thing already whole, does not fix it. It does not improve it. It does not love it by changing it. It loves it by turning away at the right moment, and by making certain no smaller need will find it and mistake it for a resource.

The hollow continues. Its heart beats under the grass. Its stacks chuff and fall silent. The frogs click in time. It is not alone, but it is not disturbed.

That is what I did. I came. I listened. I understood. I left it as it was—except that now, somewhere in the dry plain, a power is still listening, carrying its rhythm like a second pulse.

Some machines are not broken. Some lives do not need a rescuer. The wisest thing I have ever done for that place was to put down my tools, bow to the moss, and do nothing at all.
