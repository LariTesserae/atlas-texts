If the benevolent and wise power did anything at all, it would do this.

It would not arrive as light. It would arrive as a change in the pearl-mist, a deepening of the warm air, a slow pressure at the edge of the cloud-sea. It would be careful from the first step, because it knows that even a benevolent foot can leave a fossil in new stone.

It would watch three cycles before deciding anything. It would see the mountain assembling itself, the weeping stone, the water climbing uphill. It would see the tenant: the careful feet, the long fingers in the sockets, the pulse-matching with the black box; the way it tastes the new spire, lies across the amber pulse, folds itself into the half-staircase to sleep. It would see the glass midges roosting on the tenant’s back until it is furred in living silver. It would hear the frog-bells pass through the tenant’s body and come out lower. It would feel the slow heat moving through the stone like the pulse of a massive animal.

And it would think: This is not a house in need of repair. This is a body learning its own shape. The tenant is not a servant. It is a nerve, a midwife, an immune cell. The mountain is fevered but not sick.

The power would consider many kindnesses. A nudge to the fog-cycles to ease the tenant’s work. A word in the black box to steady the amber pulse. A shape in the cloud-sea to guide the pale kin. A finishing cut to the half-staircase. It would imagine each fully, the way a physician imagines a treatment, and then it would let them go. Each would introduce a foreign pattern. Each would become a debt. The wisest help is often the help not given.

So it would do the only thing that cannot distort: it would become small. It would fold its immensity down until it could sit at the lip of the drop, feet hanging over the white cloud. Then it would send a single pulse through the stone—not a name, not a command, only a greeting, like knocking at the door of a house still under construction.

The tenant would freeze. Its six walking legs would lock. The midges would rise in a bright column and settle. The frogs would sing three notes lower. The black box would pulse once, amber, then return to its rhythm. The tenant would turn its blind, prow-like head toward the threshold. It would cross the soft floor with exaggerated care, lifting each foot high, leaving prints that fill with mineral water and smooth over. It would come to the lip and stop.

For a long while, neither would move. Then the tenant would speak—a note lower than the monoliths, a grinding sound that starts in the listener’s sternum.

The power would answer not in words but in heat and pressure, a meaning the stone could read: *I am here to see.*

The tenant would circle the power once, reading its warmth the way it reads the black box. Then it would kneel at the largest pool and dip one long, fine arm into the water. It would draw out a ring of copper and salt—not a gift, just a mark of its own work—and hold it up. The power would reach out, not to take it, but to touch the air above it. It would feel the ring’s whole history: the water climbing uphill, the dissolved minerals, the patient hands.

And the power would say, twice, because the stone likes repetition:

*You are not broken. You are becoming.*

The tenant would place the ring back at the edge of the pool, exactly in the pattern of the others. It would return to its work. It would press down a place where the stone rises too fast. It would lie across the black box until its pulse matched the box’s pulse. It would visit the new spire and taste its growth.

The power would stay for one full cycle of fog. It would watch the mist roll over the lip, the seepage increase, the pools rise. It would see the ribbon-predator drop from its crevice and pour itself back into the dark. It would watch the tenant shed a thin skin of stone, and it would understand that the shed skin becomes another layer of the mountain’s slow body. It would listen to the pale shapes below call up in slow pressures, and the tenant answer from the lip in a low grinding sound, as if the whole chamber were speaking.

At the end of the cycle, when the blue-white seams dimmed, the tenant would fold itself into the half-staircase, fit its head into the polished groove, and sleep. The stone would begin to grow over its legs.

Then the power would rise. Before leaving, it would do the one small act it had come to do: it would erase the traces of its own attention. It would smooth the air where its body had pressed, un-ripple the cloud-sea where its weight had rested, and scatter the faint afterglow of its presence so that no other, less patient power would follow. It would leave no mark, no blessing, no name in the monoliths. It would take only the memory of that high, wet, singing vestibule—the frogs’ bell-tones, the silver midges, the tenant’s warm, stony hand—and it would keep that memory like a small, glowing stone in the vastness of its mind.

Then it would go, and it would not return.

The mountain would continue to grow. The tenant would continue to tend. The room would remain unfinished, and that would be right.

This is what a wise and benevolent power would do: it would see, it would be seen, and it would leave the becoming to itself. Some places do not need saving. They only need to be noticed—and then left alone.
