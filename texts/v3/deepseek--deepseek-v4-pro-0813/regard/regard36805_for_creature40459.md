**What the Power Would Do**

It would do one thing, and only one.

Not first, though. First it would listen.

The benevolent and wise power would come not as light but as attention—an attention so complete that the shape of the sound would change. The wire-flies would ring a little sooner. The bladder-hawks would tilt their wings and rise, as if the air itself had spoken. The eyeless loper would pause in its long patience, one forefoot not quite touching the plant-floor. The plant would draw a breath through a thousand hollow stems, and then let it go, as if saying yes.

It would hear the world before it judged it.

It would hear the drone of the great vertical chimneys, the wet crackle of sap-aphids feeding, the two-tone moan of the signal tower. It would hear the shepherd’s heartbeat under her pale ribs. She is not sleeping. She is listening too. One six-fingered hand rests against the braided wall of the dome, feeling the herd’s vibration along the vascular cord. The power would not interrupt.

It would think:

*This is not a world in need of rescue. It is a world in which every living thing has learned to live inside a breathing body, to move by sound, to die and be eaten and become sound again. The loper is not cruelty. The lure-fruit is not deception. The cold is not a flaw. The dark is not an absence. The plant is not a god. The shepherd is not a victim.*

And so the power would not part the clouds. Light, here, would be a kind of violence. It would not warm the cold shafts, not fill the hollows with easy air, not silence the lures or make the fruit safe. It would not teach the shepherd a new language, because her tapping on the resonance-ribs is already a language the mountain understands. It would not make the path to the dome straight, because the path is not there to be made straight.

What, then, would it do?

It would do the only thing a wise power can do for a place that is already whole: it would make that wholeness known.

It would reach down—not into the flesh of the plant, not into the shepherd’s mind—but into the long memory of the resonance-ribs. It would press upon them the way a hand presses on a drum, not to make a loud sound but to settle the skin. It would store there one new pattern: the shepherd’s name as she taps it on her own sternum when she is alone—*three taps, a pause, two taps, a long fading tap*. It would store the sound of her staff reading the floor. It would store the small silver sound of water dropping into a waxy cup. It would store, too, the loper’s slow following silence, because even that is part of her life, and to remember only the safe parts would be a lie.

It would do this not to watch over her, but to let the ground itself remember her. In a world where the ground is a body, memory is a form of love. A footprint is a bruise; to be remembered by the plant is to be held without being harmed.

Then the power would withdraw—not far, just to the edge of the cloud deck. It would not promise anything. It would not appear in dreams or send a sign. It would simply keep listening, because attention is the only miracle that does not disturb what it loves.

And the only proof?

One day, years later, when the shepherd returns to the dome above the warm duct, she will knock on the braided doorway out of old habit—*three taps, pause, two taps, long fading tap*—and for the first time, the doorway will knock back. Not in mockery. Not in warning. In recognition.

She will stand very still. The sound will be her own, returned from the plant’s deep memory, as if the mountain has been keeping it for her all this time.

Then she will make a small, rough sound in the hollow dark. It might be surprise. It might be the beginning of a laugh.

And the power, at the edge of the clouds, will hear it.

That is all. That is everything.
