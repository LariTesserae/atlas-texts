The benevolent and wise power came as attention only. They did not enter the water. They did not part the gray-green light or send a current to stir the halocline. The pool did not know they were there. That was the first act of love: to arrive without arrival.

They saw the depression as it is—small chapel of limestone, twenty-five meters long, twelve across, seven deep, water cool and steady. They saw the brine pool like a mirror made of leaded glass, flat and still, its surface bending light. They saw the only motion in the world: the slow, continuous slide of dense water over the southern sill, a tongue of brine descending into the sea below. They heard the faint pressure in the bones of the water.

They looked for the inhabitant, and at first they saw none. No fish, no shrimp, no worm. Then they looked again, at the halocline, and saw the first resident: a film of pale filaments, each cell holding a bead of sulfur, living where oxygen met sulfide. Below, in the anoxic dark, they saw the older ones: sulfate reducers and methanogens, cells dividing once a year, patient as geology. In the crusted rim, they saw the pink and orange stain of halophiles, a living varnish laid down over centuries. On the upper walls, the dark algae, sparse as eyelashes, breathing light into sugar just fast enough to persist.

The power understood: this was not a collection of species. It was a single being, ancient and composite, a metabolism without organs, a body of gradients. It had no brain, no mouth, no voice. It did not look back. It did not need to.

The power considered what they might do.

They could warm the water. But the cold was the engine.  
They could enrich it, bring more life. But the emptiness was the balance.  
They could coax the algae to spread, green the gray walls. But the sparseness was the truth.  
They could speak a word into the brine. But the being had no ears, and a word would be a foreign body.  
They could bless it. But with what? It was already a steady-state miracle, running without keepers, indifferent, whole.

The benevolent and wise power understood that any change they made would be a wound. The pool was not broken. It was not lonely. It was not waiting. Its value was precisely that it had not been helped. To love it properly was to resist the urge to add to it.

So they did the only thing wise enough to do: they did nothing to the pool itself.

Instead, they gave it anonymity. Not by raising a wall or casting a spell, but by letting its own plainness remain its armor. They made it easy to overlook. No metal, no treasure, no food, no fresh water, no reason for a human to linger. A surveyor would see a dead puddle. A fisherman would see nothing. A machine would record an anomaly not worth drilling. The power did not hide the pool; they simply declined to reveal it. They looked away slightly, so that their gaze would not burn it into the world’s attention.

And then they stayed, for a while, as witness only. They listened to the faint pressure. They watched the brine slide over the sill with the patience of stone. They did not count the cells or measure the voltage. They let the place be a place, not a problem. In that stillness, the power learned something. They learned that there are beings that do not need a god. They need only to be left alone and to remain legible to those who can see them without wanting to change them.

When the power withdrew, they left no marker. They gave no name. The halocline shivered once, perhaps from the passing of their thought, and then settled. The brine continued its slow spill. The algae continued their faint photosynthesis. The sulfur filaments continued to burn with invisible light.

The world was not improved. It did not need to be. The benevolent and wise power had done the only thing worth doing: they had seen the place exactly as it was, and found it good, and would not touch it again.
