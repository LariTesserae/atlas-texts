The wise power would do, I think, almost nothing.

It would come over the low land not as light, not as fire, not as voice. It would come as a subtle change in the weather: a lowering of pressure, a thickening of the cold that already pours from the shaft. It would not part the alder branches or smooth the water. It would not send a dream to any prophet or place a mark on any map.

Here is what it would see:

A hole where the world has fallen a little into itself—not broken, but folded. Walls gloved in moss, water beading without falling, a pool that never stills. A sound that does not begin or end, a roar that is not a warning but a fact. And in the fissure behind the fern, a body the color of wet limestone, eyeless, patient, its mouth open so the place can breathe through it.

The power would recognize something that most powers do not: the place is already whole. The being is not hiding. It is not suffering. It is not waiting to be changed, blessed, healed, or spoken to. It is not a guardian in the way that word is usually meant—it does not keep anything out. It is *of* the sound. It is the sound’s own slow flesh. The cold is not a hardship to it. The dark is not a lack. The roar is not noise but the shape of home.

A lesser power might think: *This creature is blind. I will give it eyes.*  
The wise power knows that eyes would be a cruelty here. There is nothing to see but the inside of a throat of stone, and the creature already knows it perfectly through bone and skin and the long drum of its own ribs.

A lesser power might think: *This sound is overwhelming. I will soften it.*  
But the roar is not an affliction. It is the place’s voice. To soften it would be to strike the hollow dumb, to orphan the creature from the one thing it has grown to be.

A lesser power might think: *This place is lonely. I will bring it companions, songs, warmth.*  
But loneliness is a human idea. The place has no gap where such a gift could enter.

So the benevolent and wise power would do nothing corrective. It would not lift the cold or dry the walls or sweeten the air. It would not coax the newt into breeding, or fill the fissure with silence, or turn the moss to flowers. It would not build a shrine at the rim, or carve prayers into the cairn, or leave a name.

If anything at all, the power would do one small, invisible thing:

It would listen.

Not listen as a tourist listens, waiting for the noise to become meaning. Not listen as a scholar listens, naming frequency and resonance. It would listen as an equal: a great old thing recognizing another great old thing. The power would settle over the thicket of black alder and moss-grown boulders the way a wave settles over a stone. It would let the low roar move through its own vastness. For a time—an hour, a season, a century, it would not matter—the power would breathe with the fissure.

And perhaps there would be one consequence.

Deep inside the stone, the keeper would pause. Not in fear. Not in wonder. Its eyeless head would turn slightly, its soft mouth closing, then opening again. It would taste the vibration with the roots of its long fingers. Something in the attention around it would be different—not heavier, but clearer. As if the roar had briefly become aware of itself, or as if a second listener had entered the dark without disturbing the air.

The keeper would not come out. It would not bow. It would not speak. It would simply settle again into its own patience, knowing, in the way it knows the rising of the pool or the first ice-threads on the north wall, that it had been seen.

And then the power would withdraw.

It would leave nothing behind: no sign, no gift, no promise. Not because it did not care, but because any trace would be a burden. A gift can become a wound when it tells a whole creature: *You were not enough before I came.* The wise power would not risk that. It would not carve a tally mark on the cairn. It would not even warm the water a fraction.

It would go as it came, a slow shift in the cold, a slight deepening of the moss-light on the upper ledges.

The roar would continue. The water would continue to bead down the scalloped flutes. The blind spiders would tremble in their webs. The pale newt would move only when late summer touched the pool. The keeper would lie in the fissure, mouth open, low and long, moss held on its back like an old coat.

And the place would remain what it is:

not empty, not waiting, not lost—  
only a low place inside a low land,  
a hole where the world has fallen a little way into itself,  
and has found, in falling, a kind of rest.
