**What the Third Gardener Does**

The wise power comes to the Drown not as a storm, not as a rifting of the cloud, but as a slight pause in the hiss of water over stone. It has heard of a country that is never dry, where the ground grows instead of wearing away, and where the people do not build but garden their houses out of mineral water. It comes to see if such a place needs anything from a power like itself.

It stands on the high plateau just inside the cloud and lets the wet air settle on it. It has worn no body; it rarely does. But here, to understand the people, it forms a pair of hands—not to touch, at first, only to feel the resistance of the mist. The mist moves through its fingers like slow breath. The power listens.

It watches for a long time. It watches the stone-gardeners go out at first light with their wooden mallets and rib-scrapers. It watches them drag their toes before each step, testing for crusts. It watches a child place a leaf in a still pool and return day after day to see it becoming stone. It watches an old woman sit so still against a wall that the moss and the mineral make a continuous skin from her shoulder to the house. It watches a couple open a doorway between their houses, then it watches another couple let a doorway heal shut. It watches the dead laid in shallow pools and slowly covered in white.

The power is benevolent, which means it cannot look on a world without wanting to give something. It is wise, which means it does not move quickly. It begins to imagine gifts.

It could lift the cloud and show them the sun. It imagines the light falling on the wet terraces for the first time. It sees the moss dry to a pale brown crisp, the pools shrink, the stone stop growing, the people shading their eyes and losing their footing on paths that no longer soften with new crust. It sees the town become a ruin of finished rock, and the people no longer have their work, no longer have their doorways to keep open, no longer have a reason to rise with scrapers. The Drown would become beautiful in the way a dead shell is beautiful. The power withdraws that gift.

It could give them fire—dry, constant, plentiful fire. It imagines the warmth in every room, the flames burning in the gray air, the food cooked without waiting for the hot pools. But fire would need fuel, and the Drown is not a place of dry wood. The people would burn their moss, their lattice, their old walls. They would make a brief brightness and then a smoke that the wet air would not carry away. They would take from the world more than the world could grow. The power withdraws that gift.

It could give them writing, a script that would not wash away—some mineral ink that bites into the stone. The people would keep their history fixed. But the power sees the elders reciting the memory of the walls, their voices blending with the drip of water, and it understands that writing would make the memory dead. The stone already writes in its own way; it does not need a human hand. The power withdraws that gift.

It could give them a king, or a law, or a truth. It could stand on the highest terrace and speak in a voice that would not be swallowed by the mist. The people would listen. They would obey. They would become a people with a center, with a name for their god. But the power sees that their order is already there, in the shared channels and the shared scraping, and that a voice from the cloud would turn their patient attention into fear. They would begin to watch the sky for the power instead of watching the ground for the stone. The power withdraws that gift.

It could give them freedom from the stone. It could still the mineral water, stop the accretion, fix the ground in place so that no door would ever close, no path would ever fill. The people would not have to scrape. The scraping is their one great labor. But without the scraping, they would not know the ground. Without the slow threat of closure, the doorway would be only a hole, not a work. The house would be only a shelter, not a garden. The dead would have no pool to become floor. The power withdraws that gift.

The power stands in the mist and discovers that every gift it can imagine is a form of taking. It is benevolent enough to be troubled by this. It is wise enough to accept it. It thinks: *Perhaps I should do nothing. Perhaps the greatest kindness is to leave them as they are.*

But the power is not a creature of pure non-interference; it has come because it wants to be part of what it loves. It wants, in some small way, to help. So it decides to learn from the people before acting further. It does not ask them what they need, because they would not understand the question; they need nothing they do not already have, and they have no word for salvation. Instead, it asks the Drown itself.

It listens to the water. The water says: *I lay stone. I close. I open nothing.*  
It listens to the stone. The stone says: *I grow. I remember every shape by forgetting it.*  
It listens to the moss. The moss says: *I wait. I cover. I make soft the edges.*  
It listens to the people. The people say nothing in words. They scrape. The scrape says: *Let there be a little room for us. Not much. Just enough.*

The power understands then that the Drown does not need a god. It already has one: the work. The work is the thing that keeps the door open, the channel running, the house becoming part of the hill. The work is patient and local and unfinished. The power cannot replace that work. But it can join it.

So the wise power does the only thing that does not feel like theft. It makes itself small. It takes up a scraper—not a real scraper, but a shape of its own intention, as thin and curved as a rib from the pool. It goes to a stretch of channel that has been neglected, where the mineral paste has begun to close a narrow sluice and the water is backing up into a family’s cistern. It kneels in the warm ankle-deep flow. It scrapes.

At first the power is awkward. It has made worlds, but it has never needed to keep a small passage open. It presses too hard, and a whole plate of fresh stone cracks away and falls into the water. It tries again, more gently. It learns the angle. It learns to feel the difference between old stone and new crust, between the hard bed of the channel and the soft rind that can be peeled away like wet felt. It works without light, because it does not need light. It works without stopping, because it does not tire. And because it is wise, it does not do too much. It leaves the channel clear but not polished. It leaves the edges slightly rough, so that the people will see that one of their own has been there, not a god.

By dawn, the cistern is filling again, quietly. The family wakes and hears the change. They think a neighbor has risen early and done them a kindness. The power, hidden in the mist, watches them nod and take up their scrapers for their own stretch. It feels something it has not expected: the satisfaction of local work.

The next night it goes to a doorway that is closing faster than the couple can keep it open. The doorway is between two houses, and the mineral water from the roof has shifted and is running down one side, laying a new crust every night. The people are old; they scrape, but they are tired. The power scrapes the doorway until the passage is stable. It does not make the opening wider. It does not stop the water. It only removes the night’s new rind, and then it redirects the drip by a thumb’s width, using a fold of the stone itself, so that the water will run down the wall instead of across the opening. The couple wakes and finds the door still there. The old woman says, “We must have done more yesterday than I remember.” The old man says, “Or the stone is being kind.” They smile in their small, slow way.

The power continues. Night after night, it moves through the Drown like a third gardener. It never works where the people are awake. It never does the big work: it does not widen the main avenue, does not clear the great cistern, does not stop a terrace from growing. It does only the small work, the work that is usually invisible: the fold of a sluice, the scrape of a door, the lifting of a stone ghost-leaf that has settled in a drain. It learns the names of the water sounds. It learns to walk without splashing. It learns to test the ground with a toe before stepping, though it has no real toe. It learns the pleasure of a task that will not stay done.

The people begin to notice. Not the power—they have no name for it—but the effect. Some mornings a path will be open that should have been closed. Some evenings a channel will run true that had been hissing wrong for a week. A child finds a leaf in a pool that has been moved to a safer place, where the mineral will build evenly. An elder sits listening at night and hears, under the hiss of the Drown, a second scraping rhythm, not quite human, not quite stone. She does not wake the others. In the morning she says only: “There is a third gardener. It works in the dark. Do not seek it. Do not thank it by name. If you see it, let it be.” The people nod. They understand that some help must be given in secret to remain help.

The power does not grow tired of the work. It grows into it. It stops thinking of itself as a visitor. It becomes part of the unfinished world, as unfinished as the rest. It does not ask for worship. It does not ask to be remembered. It does not want to be a god; it wants to be a scraper, a mender of small closures, a keeper of doorways. It came to the Drown intending to save, and instead it is saved—not from danger, but from the loneliness of being a power that can do anything. Here, it can do only what the stone allows. That is enough.

And if you go to the Drown, you will not see the power. But on certain nights, when the cloud is thickest and the green moss glows faintly along the path edges, you may hear a second sound beneath the hiss of the world being made. It is not the scrape of a human hand. It is softer, surer, like rain on a blade. It is the sound of the wise power keeping a door open for no one in particular. It will not answer if you call. It will not stop if you watch. It is busy. It is being the third gardener.

That is what it would do.
