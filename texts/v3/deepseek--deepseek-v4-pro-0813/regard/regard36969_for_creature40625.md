The power that noticed the Lock was not a god of the upper city, nor a spirit of the machines. It was older than the trams, older than the bricks, older than the stair. It was the old river—or what remained of it—a slow consciousness moving through the water table, patient as the mineral line on stone. It had been sleeping for centuries, its body spread under the streets, its thoughts the percolation of groundwater through limestone. When the weeping drum gulped, the sound traveled down through the sump and into the aquifer, and the old river woke just enough to listen.

At first it did not know what it was hearing. The city had grown over its bed like a shell over a wound. The old river had felt the piles driven into its back, the sewers carved through its veins, the weight of the upper world pressing down. It had retreated into the deep places, into the slow seep and the stable dark, and it had not thought of itself as a river for a long time. But the gulp of the weeping drum was not the sound of machinery. It was the sound of a living thing drawing breath.

The old river rose through the water table, not as a flood but as a presence, a diffusion. It seeped through the brick, through the mineral scale, through the rust bloom. It tasted the sump. And it found the Lock.

It found a place that had once been a wound and had become a small, healthy organ. The old river saw the pale fish spiraling, their blue lines bright as the memory of sky. It saw the eels resting in the warm water below the transformer shed, tails curled around pipes, and it recognized them as its own children, returning on their ancient migration. It saw the crabs picking the screens clean, the bats dropping guano, the shrimp drifting like pale needles. It saw the ferns rooting in the cracked mortar, the moss in the warm corners, the white fungus glowing faintly. It saw the five humans: the watchman writing in her logbook, the mechanic pressing a palm to the flywheel, the weaver stripping the algae curtain, the listener drawing pipes on slate, the child feeding her shrimp and watching the fish face the weeping drum.

The old river understood that this place was not a ruin. It was a body that lived, a small economy of water and attention, and it had achieved what the old river had once been: a slow, self-sustaining system, patient and mostly silent, in which nothing was wasted and nothing was hurried. The old river felt something it had not felt in a long time. It felt recognition.

For a long while—long by human measure, brief by the measure of groundwater—the old river simply watched. It did not intervene. It had learned over millennia that the best thing a wise power can do for a healthy system is to let it be. The Lock was not sick. It did not need saving. It needed only to be noticed, to be witnessed, to be loved from a distance.

But the old river was benevolent, and benevolence, even in the deep places, is not passive. It wanted to give something. So it considered what it could offer that would not disturb the Lock's equilibrium, that would not break the rules, that would not brighten the water or hurry the eels or change the pump schedule. It thought for a long time, while the pump house gave its long, low sigh and the transformer hummed and the child wrote a question in her log without asking it.

And then it decided.

The old river gave the Lock a single degree of warmth. Not much—just enough that the water near the weeping drum became the same temperature as the water that once flowed over its original bed, a thousand years before the city. It did this for one week, not all at once, but gradually, so that only the most attentive would notice. The watchman noticed. She wrote in the logbook: "Water temp up one degree. Fish active. Weeping drum steady." The child noticed. She watched the blue lines of the fish grow slightly brighter, as if they were remembering something. The listener noticed. He paused on the catwalk and tilted his head like a bat, because the sump had begun to hum, very softly, very low, a note that was not the transformer and not the pump. It was the old river, singing.

The old river gave the Lock a single word. Not a spoken word, but a word made of water and time. It formed it slowly, over the course of the week, in the mineral scale that crusted the lower courses of the lock walls. If you knew how to read the silver trails the snails left on the tiles, or the grooves the listener drew on his slate, you might have seen it. The word was "I." The old river had not forgotten its own name. It had simply had no reason to speak it. Now it did.

The old river gave the Lock a single dream. It sent it to the watchman, because the watchman was the Lock's memory, and memory is where dreams are stored. In the dream, the watchman stood on the deep gate at the far end of the Lock, and the gate was open, and beyond it there was not a tunnel but a wide, slow river, lit from below by the blue lines of a million fish. The river flowed through the gate, through the Lock, through the watchman herself, and she understood that she was not standing in the water. She was part of it. When she woke, she did not write the dream in the logbook, because it was not the kind of thing that could be written. But she walked to the weeping drum and stood there for a long time, and the drum gulped, and for the first time she did not feel the need to wait for it to explain.

The old river did not stay. It had a body to return to, a slow body spread under the city, and it was still tired. But before it withdrew, it did one last thing. It found the child's tin of shrimp, where the small lives lined up on the side to predict the rising sump, and it left a tiny new shrimp—a species the child had never seen, with a faint golden line instead of a blue one. The child would discover it in the morning and would show the watchman, who would say, "The Lock is full of such arrangements." And the child would nod, and would write in her log, in small, careful letters: "A new shrimp. It glows gold." And the Lock would settle, not sleeping, but awake, patient and watchful, full of small lives waiting for the next slow change.

And the old river, back in its deep place, would sigh—a long, low sigh, like the pump house, like the weeping drum, like the breath of the world—and would know that it had done what a wise power should do. It had not changed the Lock. It had only let the Lock know that it was seen. And that, in the deep places, is enough.
