The benevolent and wise power does not arrive. Arrival would imply a moment, and moments are too loud. Instead, it has always been arriving, the way the mist is always arriving in the upper galleries—not as a change, but as a return.

It perceives the place as a being: a hot, wet, dark, breathing body with stone for skin and people for hands. It perceives the being as a life, not a problem. It does not ask, “What is broken?” It does not ask, “What can be improved?” It asks only one question, and it asks it slowly: “What does the place need next?”

The answer comes not as a word but as the place itself: the drip, the sigh, the slow turn of the Clock, the gecko on the seventh pipe, the child carrying water without spilling. The answer is: nothing. The place is already being tended.

So the wise power does what wise powers do: it refrains. But refrain is not nothing. It is a form of holding.

The power cups the rift in its attention like a hand cupping an ember. It does not close its fingers. It does not blow. It simply stays, so the heat does not escape faster than the stone can learn to be soil. It smooths the world around the crater—not with a wall, but with a soft forgetfulness. It makes the surface look a little more cindery, a little less like an entrance. It turns away the curious. It bends the weather and the deep stresses of the earth gently around the place, as water bends around a stone. It does this without signature. The only trace is a slight lengthening of the dry interval, a longer pause between drips, as if the whole chamber had sighed in relief.

The power considers what it might give. Fire? The fissure gives fire enough. Water? The walls are weeping. Knowledge? The people already have the only knowledge that matters: how to listen to a root, how to wait for fruit to yield with a twist no firmer than a handshake. A name? They do not need a name, for they are not separate. A sign? They are not looking for one. The power could give a child, but a child is a cup of water, and such cups should not be hurried.

It gives nothing. And in giving nothing, it gives the one thing the place cannot produce by itself: the assurance that nothing is required. That the place is enough. That the dark can be trusted.

The power becomes an outer membrane, a skin around the skin of the world. Its benevolence is not in doing but in refusing to do—in allowing the place to remain exactly as it is, and in guarding that allowance. It does not repair. It does not prevent. It knows that a cracked wall is also a place for a fern. It knows that a stopped Clock is not a failure but a thought.

Years, or breaths, pass. The power does not count them. It knows the rhythm only as a slow tide of moisture and heat and light. The people and the place change in small ways: a new root crosses a walkway; a bearing in the Old Woman grows a collar of mineral scale; a child sees a gecko on a pipe and names it Little Valve-eye. The power adjusts its attention to accommodate these changes, as a hand adjusts its grip on a railing when the body shifts.

Once, a wall of the upper gallery begins to crack. The residents brace it with iron and root-braid. The power could fuse the basalt with a thought, but it does not. The brace will hold. And the crack is now a place where a fern grows. The power learns from this: a repaired place is not the same as an unbroken place; it is more.

Once, a child slips on the wet grating above the loom gallery. The power’s attention tightens. But a splicer’s hand, moving without hurry, catches the child’s wrist. The power did nothing. It was the right amount. A miracle would have taught the child that the world will catch her. The hand taught her that the people will. The second lesson is truer to the place.

The only thing the power does, in the end, is remember. It remembers the place so thoroughly that the place cannot be lost. If the world above grows cold or hot or fills with noise, if the crater is buried and the rift forgotten, the power will still hold the pattern: the temperature of the mist, the rhythm of the drips, the bright-glowing dead, the gecko on the seventh pipe. And in that memory, the place will continue, not as a copy, but as an attention. The power is the witness that makes the place true.

When the power finally withdraws, it does so the way the mist-tide recedes: not leaving, but thinning. The inhabitants feel it as a slight dryness in the air, a clarity in the dark. A Tender looks up from the sump and says, “The walls are less damp tonight.” A splicer replies, “The roots are drinking deeper.” No one says “blessing,” because the word would be too loud. But the child who saw the heat-moth alone carries the feeling like a cup of water: without spilling, without hurrying.

If you ask what the power did, the answer is this: it stayed close enough to be mistaken for the heat, and far enough to be mistaken for nothing. The place never knew it was loved. That is the purest form of love.
