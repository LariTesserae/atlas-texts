**What the Wise Power Did**

It came to the rim at dusk, when the frogs were a metallic tide and the moon had not yet found the gaps in the cloud. It had no name, or it had many names, and it had been called benevolent and wise by those it had helped. It had helped many places. It had lifted floods, stilled earthquakes, whispered laws to lawless peoples, and planted seeds in barren ground. It came to Mossgrip because the place had drawn its attention—a dense, wet, living knot in the high mountains, a town folded into a pocket of excess.

At first it saw only a basin of green fur, stitched through by a silver thread of falling water. Then the green resolved into roofs, bridges, walls, ladders, ledges, and the movement of hundreds of small bodies. The sound reached it before the shapes did: the deep churning roar of water, the shrill chitter of birds, the ring of frogs, the creak of wet fiber, and human voices pitched high to carry.

The power looked for the being who lived in it—the one whose life had drawn its attention. It found her on the eastern terrace: a girl of twelve, or near twelve, mending a bird-net by the light of a fish-oil lamp. Her name was Nettle. A pika-like creature sat on the beam above her, watching. Her fingers moved automatically, tying knots that would hold against the weight of birds. She was not thinking about the fact that she was inside a room made of roots, in a house that grew from a tree, in a town that was tied together by knots. She was only thinking about the next loop, the next pull, the next breath of the place that held her.

The power’s first instinct was benevolence. It could ease the damp. It could warm the air. It could make the ropes unbreakable, the moss fireproof, the water clean and slow. It could give them copper, or iron, or words of law, or a road out of the cirque. It had done such things before, and the peoples had called it wise.

But it hesitated. It had learned, over long ages, that benevolence can be a blade. A gift can cut a people from their place. A road can become a wound. A law can become a cage. So it stayed on the rim and watched.

It watched the water-wardens wade into the seep lines, clearing sluices with bare fingers, carrying frog eggs to safe pools as carefully as they would carry their own food. It watched a young woman named Runnel walk the western rope-ladders, gliding her hand along the wet fibers, feeling for weakness. It watched an old man named Orib stop mid-sentence, tilt his head, and say, “The north beam is tired. Go tighten it.” And he was always right. It watched children run along the ropeways beating wooden clappers when a squall rolled over the rim, and it watched the town respond not with panic but with rhythm—opening sluices, shifting dams, tightening lashings, giving the water the right of way.

It listened. The roar of the waterfall was not a cry for help. The creak of the ropes was not a groan of despair. The chitter of the birds was not a warning. The frog tide at dusk was not a lament. The people were not waiting for a savior. They were busy, tied, alive. The place was not a problem to be solved; it was a knot to be tended.

The power asked, not in words, but in a breath of wind that moved through the wet leaves: *What do you need?*

The answer came not as words but as the place itself. The waterfall kept its deep note. The ropes creaked in the wind. A child whistled the call of the hooded swift. A frog gave its alarm. The moss shrugged off a sheet of water. The town breathed, dripped, and held.

The power translated: *Nothing. We are already held. We are the knot. Do not untie us.*

And the power understood. To ease the rain would starve the moss. To strengthen the ropes would make the people forget how to read tension. To give fire would burn the walls. To lift them out would unravel the knot. The place was not suffering from excess; it was made of excess. The people were not trapped; they were woven. The being who lived in it was not a victim; she was a limb of the place, and the place was a limb of her.

So the wise power did the only wise thing: it did nothing. Or almost nothing.

As it left, it passed over the eastern terrace. The girl Nettle was still mending the bird-net. The fish-oil lamp trembled in the wet wind, and for a moment the flame guttered, threatening to go out. The power breathed on it—not to make it brighter, not to make it eternal, but only to steady it for a single breath against the wind. The flame stopped trembling. Nettle looked up, saw nothing, and returned to her net. The pika on the beam flicked its tail.

Then the power went away, carrying the knowledge of Mossgrip like a knot in its own mind, a knot it would never untie. It did not return. Or if it did, it came not as a savior but as a student, to learn the names of the knots by watching the hands that tied them.
