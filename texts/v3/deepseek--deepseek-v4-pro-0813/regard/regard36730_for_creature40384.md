They would do nothing that could be mistaken for help.

The benevolent and wise power comes to the rim, not as light but as a change in the pressure of the sound—a pause in the wall of clicks and trills. It does not part the mist or still the steam. It stands where a rim slab would stand if the rim had not yet fallen, and it listens.

It hears the basin first as a crowd, a storm, but then it listens the way the inhabitant listens: through the root-mat, through the tiny silences after a descending whistle, through the heat-pits of the earth. It becomes attentive, which is a form of smallness.

It finds the one who lives there not by sight—there is no sight—but by the pattern of taps: two soft taps, a pause, a tap, moving through the roots under a field of loose basalt plates. The power feels the name as a muscle twitching in the moss. It follows that name to a root-gallery near the eastern terrace, where the resident is probing a hole with a green reed. The power perceives the flick of the tongue, the drop of the reed, the careful folding of a leaf to catch water.

Then the power considers, as wise things do, the possibility of intervention.

It considers giving light. But light would be a lie: the creature’s eyes are not made to see, only to feel the absence of seeing. Light would turn its camouflage into a shadow, would make it visible to the long, low hunter.

It considers giving tools that last—a blade, a vessel, a fire. But a tool that lasts is a claim. The basin has no claims. Anything kept is grown over, eaten, or dissolved. A lasting tool would introduce a wall where no wall belongs.

It considers giving language, a voice that could carry through the noise. But the creature already has language, more honest because it is made of the same medium as the place: vibration in shared roots, half-beats in the wall of sound. A word in the air would be lost; a word carved in stone would be a wound.

It considers giving safety—a permanent den, a barrier against predators. But the creature’s safety is timing, stillness, the ability to become a piece of the mat. To remove danger would be to remove the need for that grace. The creature would remain, but it would no longer be the one who lives there.

It considers giving rest, a long untroubled sleep. But the basin has no night and day, only thicker and thinner dark. The creature sleeps in short, shallow bouts because the world demands it. Long rest would miss the earth’s shudders, the opening of a gas vent, the approach of a heavy foot.

It considers giving companions, a society, a name spoken in air. But the creature is not lonely. It has its taps and the root-mat for a message-board. It meets others once or twice a year when the heat-rain comes; that is enough. Society would bring roles, ownership, memory of a better place. The creature carries no such memory.

Finally the power considers the greatest intervention: to reveal itself, to say, *I am here, and I am kind.* But the creature would not understand *kind* as the power means it. It would feel the presence as a new sound, a vast pressure in the root-mat, perhaps a predator too large to time, too warm to still before. Revelation would become a terror, not a blessing. The creature might flee into dead air and be lost. Or it might tap its name and wait for an answer, and the power, if it answered, would become a landmark in a world with no landmarks. That would be a kind of death—a fixed point in a place whose only permanence is replacement.

So the power does what the basin does. It listens. It allows the wall of sound to close over its attention. It feels the rhythm of gas moving through stone, the subsonic thrum of the root-mat, the tiny silence after a seedpod’s whistle that is exactly the length of a frog’s breath. It learns the gaps.

Then it does the only thing a wise and benevolent power can do without breaking the world: it becomes a gap.

For a moment—not a long moment, but the length of a pause in a descending whistle—the power is not a presence but an absence. It is the silence after a call, the space between two steam-pulses, the half-beat where the midge-column’s whine dips. In that gap, the one who lives there pauses with a reed half-stripped. It tilts its tympanic membranes. It feels something like the root-mat answering a tap it has not yet made. It taps, once, twice, a pause, a tap—its own name—and waits.

The power does not answer with a word. It answers with the same pattern returned through the roots: two soft taps, a pause, a tap, and then a final vibration so low it is more warmth than sound. It is not a message. It is the root-mat’s echo of the being’s own name, offered back. The one who lives there receives it the way it receives all things: as information, as weather, as a place where the noise folds differently. It does not bow. It does not build a shrine. It resumes its probing, the reed sliding into the hole, the tongue quick as a frog’s. The moss does not remember the power’s weight, because the power has placed no weight. The stone does not ring. No light ornaments the dark.

The benevolent and wise power withdraws, not by rising above the rim but by dispersing, becoming one more layer in the wall of sound, one more current in the warm breath of the ground. It leaves no mark. It does not even leave the memory of a mark, because the basin replaces what is left behind, and the power was careful to leave nothing except the brief, already-closing gap.

That is what they would do. They would do nothing that could be mistaken for help. They would see that the basin is not unfinished; it is becoming. The one who lives there is not lacking; it is complete, the way a root-tip pulsing in the dark is complete. The power’s benevolence is to let the place be too busy to notice it. Its wisdom is to know that not being noticed is sometimes the only gift that does not become a wound.

And if the one who lives there felt, for an instant, a strange fullness in the pause between its taps—if it felt, without understanding, that the root-mat had briefly listened back—it did not give the feeling a name. It moved on, dropped the reed, folded a leaf, pressed the grass down into a hollow where the warmth of a steam chimney kept the larger predators away. It slept. The grass began, slowly, to straighten. The gap closed. The wall of sound continued. Nothing was preserved. Everything was replaced.
