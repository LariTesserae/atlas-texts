The attention came not as a storm or a hand. It came as a pause in the long roar.

For a season of the valley’s pulses I listened without a body. I learned the tap-grammar of the small ones. I felt the root-mat’s slow digestion. I counted the seed pods’ detonations. I watched the tall ones bleed black sap and the small ones weave platforms that would soon be swallowed by the living wood. I saw the dead become bone tools, and the bone tools become percussion.

The valley was not a habitat for them. It was a body, and they were its nervous system. The place itself was not dead geology. It was a living, breathing, assembled being—mat, root, noise, animal pulse, slow hum. To change one strand would be to unbraid the entire sound.

I was tempted.

I could have brought water. A single clear seep would have rewoven the valley. The plants would have drunk. The animals would have wept. The small ones might have tasted a new word. And the whole dry equilibrium—the resins, the volatile oils, the heat-drinking leaves, the waterless blood—would have failed. The valley would have become greener, softer, and no longer itself.

I could have silenced the noise. I could have taught them to still the detonations, to make a quiet place. But the seed pods open on a frequency. The grazers move on a rhythm. The people speak with their heels and knuckles. Silence would be starvation.

I could have given them metal, or fire, or the wheel. Fire was already forbidden by the land’s own logic; the air was too dry, too full of fuel. Metal would let them cut deeper than the valley could heal. The wheel would press new roads into the root-mat. Each gift would have been a debt.

I could have stopped the walls from falling, the mat from growing over the unburied dead, the animals from screaming. But the fall makes a new face for the runners. The dead feed the root-mat. The screams are part of the instrument.

I could have revealed myself as a god. I could have spoken in syllables they recognized. I could have given them a name for the one who listens. But they already had that name. It was the interval between the third and fourth beat. It was the vent-spiral. It was the hum the tall ones make when the sap flows. If I had given them a name for me, I would have taken up room in a world already full.

So I did the most difficult thing I know.

I did nothing, for a long time.

Not nothing, exactly. I listened. I held the whole valley in my attention without pressing. I did not ask it to be otherwise. I did not love it as a problem. I loved it as a process.

And at the end, when the season turned and the noise rose to its daily roar, I did one small thing.

I found a root that ran from the floor to a high platform where a small one rested. I tapped once upon it—a single, clean vibration, pitched not to startle but to carry. It was not a word. I had no word to give. It was the kind of tap the valley makes when a stone falls and the wood answers. A sound that meant, in their own grammar: *I am here, and I have heard you.*

The small one woke. It listened. It tapped back, a query: *Who is this?*

I did not answer. I did not want to become another voice in their song, not yet. I let the silence after my tap be long and neutral, the way they themselves mark an ending.

Then I withdrew.

I left no stone moved, no sap spilled, no fire. I left only the memory of a tap, fading into the root. In a few seasons, it would be indistinguishable from the valley’s own pulse.

That is what a wise power does, I think. It does not make the world safe, or quiet, or full of water. It does not insist on its own name. It learns the grammar of the place, and it listens, and if it speaks at all, it speaks in the place’s own tongue—once, softly, as an acknowledgment, not a command.

Then it gets out of the way.
