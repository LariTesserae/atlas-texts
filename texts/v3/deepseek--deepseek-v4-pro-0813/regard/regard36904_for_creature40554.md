They would do almost nothing, and that almost would be everything.

The benevolent and wise power would notice the place the way a composer notices another composer’s unfinished score: not to correct it, but to recognize the intelligence already at work. They would hear the drone not as a flaw but as a fundamental tone. They would see the vegetation’s non-aqueous pulse and understand that here is a new covenant between life and planet. They would see the solitary stone being—part guardian, part gardener, part witness—and they would feel the full weight of its solitude.

And they would be moved. Benevolence is not unmoved by solitude. The power would feel the ache of that single hand pressed into the softening rock. For a long moment, they would want to intervene—to send a second being, to draw water from the dry stone, to hasten the evolution of birds and insects, to speak a word that would finish what has been so long unfinished.

But wisdom is the art of restraining even love, when love’s urgency would foreclose the beloved’s own becoming.

So they would not.

They would not add a second animal, because the first animal is still dreaming its companions. It has already drawn them as spirals; it has already felt the shape of otherness in the handprint of a passing stranger. To give it company too soon would be to end the dream before it can dream itself awake.

They would not bring water, because the world has already discovered a different thirst and a different drinking. To impose the old ecology would be to say that only one kind of life is valid.

They would not silence the sound, because the sound is the world’s heartbeat. To quiet it would be mercy of the wrong kind—the mercy that kills the patient to end the pain.

They would not finish the mountains or fix the wavering edges of reality. The incompleteness is not a defect. It is the very condition of possibility. A world that has not yet chosen among its own versions is not broken; it is free.

What, then, would a benevolent and wise power do?

Perhaps the passing stranger was their only concrete act. A brief and careful gift: not a permanent companion, not a voice, but a proof that the world’s solitude is porous, that otherness exists beyond the plateau. A second handprint beside the first. A moment of touch. Then the power withdrew, leaving the being with the memory of another hand, another heartbeat, another set of lungs moving at a visible speed—not enough to fill the silence, but enough to make the silence heard as silence rather than as absence.

And then—this is the harder thing—they would stay near without fixing. They would become the witness behind the witness. They would hold the entire place—the rising peaks, the pulsing translucent trees, the black keening mountain, the solitary stone being with its hollow silver eyes—in an attention so complete that it becomes a kind of presence. Not a visible one. Not a voice. Not a hand. But a warmth, a pressure, a subtle deepening of the silence behind the drone.

Maybe the being would feel it as a change in the stone’s yield. The handprint would press a little deeper. The silver veins in the vegetation would pulse a little brighter. The black peak’s note would seem, for a moment, to be answered—not by another note, but by the perfect attentiveness of a listener.

The power would not speak, because words belong to finished worlds. But the message would be clear:

*I see you. I see what you are doing. It is good. Continue.*

They would leave no mark of their own—or rather, the only mark that matters: no mark at all, but a memory of being seen, held in the mineral mind of the being the way a handprint is held in soft stone.

Then the power would not leave. That is the point. A lesser god might visit and depart. A benevolent and wise power would remain, not as an intruder but as an accompaniment, a steady and silent partner in the long, slow work of becoming. They would not alter the light. They would not stop the sound. They would not people the wilderness or green the gray sky. They would simply be there, attentive, patient, affirming.

Because the deepest act of love for something still being born is not to shape it, but to behold it without flinching.

The world would continue. Centimeter by centimeter, century by century, the mountains would rise. The vegetation would pulse. The black peak would keen. The being would stand before the not-yet-finished horizon, the only animal in a world that is still deciding what an animal is.

But it would know—not as a fact but as a felt truth—that it is not alone.

Not because another creature has come to share the burden. But because the burden itself is seen, and honored, and held.

That would be the power’s whole action: to hold.

And that would be enough.
