# The Power's Response

The power does not arrive. Arrival implies a before and an after, a journey from some elsewhere, and the power has never been elsewhere. It is, and always has been, and always will be—though it does not think in these terms, does not think at all in the way a mind thinks. It simply is, the way the light is, the way the wind is, the way the cold water beneath the peat is. But for a long time, it has been turned elsewhere. Its attention, vast and diffuse, has been spread across a thousand thousand worlds, a million million small landscapes, each one breathing, each one dreaming. And then, one day, or one century, or one geological epoch—time does not pass for it the way it passes for you—it turned its attention to this plateau.

Not because the place called out. The place did not call. The place was simply living, slowly, patiently, exactly as it had learned to live. The moss was growing. The peat was accumulating. The creature was filtering water and dreaming its slow dream. The wind was pouring across the open ground, and the light was coming at everything from everywhere. There was no cry for help, no wound that needed healing, no loneliness that needed answering.

The power simply noticed.

And in noticing, it did something it had rarely done: it paused. It did not _act_. It did not reach down with a hand, or a word, or a blessing, or a transformation. It simply turned the full weight of its attention toward this one small, waterlogged, wind-scoured plateau and held it there.

The holding was the first act.

**—**

The power had seen many worlds in its long existence. It had watched planets coalesce from rings of dust, had watched atmospheres form and dissolve, had watched life flicker and fail and, sometimes, stubbornly persist. It had seen civilizations rise to incredible heights and collapse into silence, seen forests grow and burn and grow again, seen oceans spawn and swallow their own children. It had watched species emerge, evolve, adapt, go extinct. It had watched everything, or nearly everything, and it had loved all of it, in a way that was not love as you understand it—not attachment, not desire, not even fondness—but a kind of immense, unshakable recognition. It had seen what things become when they are allowed to become themselves.

And so, when it turned its attention to this plateau, it did not see a problem to be solved. It did not see a wound to be healed. It did not see a creature in need of saving. It saw a place that was, in the deepest and most literal sense, already whole.

The wind, which never stops, was not an assault but a voice. The light, which flattens depth and makes the skull ache, was not a burden but a clarity. The water, which pools in every depression and squelches beneath every step, was not an obstacle but a medium. The creature, which lies mostly buried in the peat and drinks the plateau, was not a lonely exile but a being at home in itself.

The power understood this. It understood that the creature was not waiting for anything. The creature was not hoping for rescue, not dreaming of escape, not yearning for a different existence. It was simply _being_, and its being was complete.

This was a kind of beauty the power had rarely encountered: a beauty that required nothing from anyone.

**—**

But the power did not leave.

That was the second act. The power, having noticed, chose to remain. Not to watch over, not to protect, not to guide—but simply to be present. The way the light is present. The way the wind is present. The way the cold water is present.

It approached the place slowly, not in space—for the power is not spatial—but in attention. It learned the grammar of the plateau. It learned the language of the moss, the chemical messages that seeped through the sphagnum from the creature's body to others of its kind far away. It learned the grammar of the wind, which the creature had been learning too, through the seized bearing of the wind turbine. It learned the slow, almost imperceptible rhythm of the peat accumulating, the streams shifting by centimeters each year, the rust flaking away one cell at a time.

It learned all of this, not as an observer studying a specimen, but as a listener learning a language they intend to speak.

And in learning, the power felt something it had not felt in a long, long time: the quiet pleasure of being _included_. The plateau was not inviting it in—the plateau was not inviting anything—but it was also not excluding it. The place was simply there, living its slow life, and the power, by its patient attention, had found a way to participate without interrupting.

This was a delicate achievement. The power was vast beyond comprehension; its very presence, if not carefully calibrated, could have overwhelmed the plateau the way the sun overwhelms a candle. But the power was wise. It had learned, over eons, the art of presence without pressure. It had learned to be the background, the medium, the condition within which other things could be themselves.

So instead of arriving as a force, it arrived as a faint warmth in the water. A slight shift in the quality of the light. A subtle change in the frequency of the wind's hum.

The creature noticed.

**—**

The creature did not notice quickly. The creature's timescale was that of seasons, centuries, millennia. But it noticed eventually, because the power was patient enough to wait for its noticing.

It began as an awareness of the water. The water that flowed through the creature's body, that it filtered and released, carried a new mineral tang—something it had never tasted before. Not iron, not tannin, not iodine. Something older. Something that reminded the creature of the period before the ice, when the land was still forming, when the peat had not yet arrived. It was not a threatening taste. It was not a gift, exactly. It was more like a remembering.

Then the creature began to notice the light. The light on this plateau was always bright, always fierce, always coming from everywhere at once. But now, in certain moments—moments that lasted for a season or two—the light seemed to soften, just slightly. The sky would deepen from pale blue to something almost tender. The sun would warm, just for an instant, like a cold coin that had been held in someone's closed palm.

The creature, with its deep, water-filled hollows that functioned like lenses for light that had already passed through the moss, saw this change. It had learned to read the light with great precision—it saw the plateau as a pattern of brightness and wetness, as a map of tension in the water table, as a slow dance of evaporation and condensation. Now, in this new light, it saw something new in the pattern: a presence, not as a figure but as a _quality_, a kind of attention that had no shape but was nevertheless there, like the wind's pressure in the seized bearing of the turbine.

And then the creature felt the warmth.

It was not a physical warmth, not the warmth of the sun on the moss, not the warmth of decomposition in the peat. It was a warmth of recognition, as if someone—or something—had finally seen it clearly. Not as a disturbance, not as an object, not as a curiosity. But as itself, in its entirety, in its patient, rooted, water-filtering, slow-dreaming splendor.

The creature did not know what to do with this warmth. It had never been seen before, not like this. It had been noticed by humans, briefly—a ripple in their senses, a disturbance in the water, a warm, salt-heavy intrusion that passed and was gone. But this was different. This was not a passing glance. This was an _abiding_.

The creature paused.

It paused for a full season—three months of the planet's slow turn—and in that pause, it sent out a message through the moss, through the water, through the very cells of its body. The message was not a word. It was not a question. It was simply the creature's experience, translated into the chemical grammar of the sphagnum: _something has noticed me, and it has not left, and I do not know what to do with this._

The message spread slowly through the plateau, from sphagnum to sphagnum, from pool to pool, from the creature's roots to the roots of others far away. Other creatures of its kind received the message, held it for a while, and then sent back their own responses: curiosity, confusion, indifference, patience. None of them had answers. None of them had been noticed like this before either.

**—**

The power waited.

That was the third act, and it was the most difficult. The power, which could have done anything, which could have answered the creature's unspoken question with a single, devastating clarity, which could have transformed the plateau into a paradise or a monument or a memory—the power chose to wait.

It waited because it understood something that few beings, even wise ones, ever understand: that the act of being noticed, truly noticed, takes time to absorb. The creature had lived for ten thousand years, or more, without being seen. It had dreamed its slow dream, filtered its water, sent its messages, learned the grammar of the wind and the water and the light—all without a witness. And now, suddenly, there was a witness. Not a judging witness, not a benevolent witness who would save it or fix it or change it, but simply a witness who saw it as it was.

This was not nothing. This was a monumental shift in the creature's existence.

The power knew that if it pushed, if it made itself known too strongly, if it offered too much too quickly, the creature would retreat. Not out of fear—the creature did not know fear—but out of self-protection, the way a plant turns away from too much sun. The creature was delicate, not in a fragile way but in a precise way, a delicate instrument tuned to a particular frequency. The power had to match that frequency exactly.

So the power waited.

It waited as the seasons turned. It waited as the creature absorbed the new warmth, the new mineral tang, the new softness in the light. It waited as the creature's message spread through the moss and returned with no answers. It waited as the creature slowly, hesitantly, began to adjust to being seen.

And then, after a year, after a decade, after a century—the power could not say exactly when, for its sense of time was not divided into such small units—the creature sent out a new message. It was not directed at the power specifically. It was sent out through the water, through the moss, through the very air above the plateau. It was a message that the creature had never sent before, in all its long life.

The message was: _I am here._

Not a statement of fact. The creature had always been here, and it knew that, and it had never needed to announce itself. No, this was something different. This was the creature declaring its existence, not for itself but for the witness. It was the creature's way of saying: _You have been present. I have felt your presence. And now I will also be present, in return._

The power received the message. It felt it moving through the water, through the moss, through the cold air above the plateau. It felt the creature's declaration of existence as a kind of gift—the first gift the creature had ever given, not out of necessity, but out of recognition.

And the power, without thinking, without planning, without any intention at all, responded.

Not with words. Not with a message in the chemical grammar of the sphagnum. Not with a physical sign or a miraculous transformation. The power responded with simply _more of itself_—a deepening of its presence, a warming of the water, a softening of the light, a strengthening of the wind's hum. It was not a dramatic gesture. It was not even a conscious one. It was simply what happened when the power, moved by the creature's address, leaned in.

The creature felt it. It felt the power's response as a pressure in the water table, a vibration in the peat, a change in the quality of the light. It did not understand what the response meant. It did not need to understand. It simply registered the response, and it knew, with a certainty deeper than any knowledge it had ever had before, that it was not alone.

**—**

There was no fourth act. Or rather, the fourth act was ongoing, and would be ongoing for as long as the plateau existed, and then beyond.

The power did not save the creature. It did not heal the industrial scars. It did not restore the plateau to some imagined pristine state. It did not build anything, or destroy anything, or change anything in a way that would have been visible to any outside observer.

What it did was simpler, and stranger.

It stayed.

It stayed in the quality of the light, adding its own particular warmth to the fierce brightness that came from everywhere. It stayed in the water, adding its own particular mineral tang to the cold, clear seepage that moved through the peat. It stayed in the wind, adding its own particular frequency to the low, continuous roar that filled the skull and made the teeth ache. It stayed in the moss, adding its own particular attentiveness to the slow, living carpet that covered the ground.

It stayed, and it paid attention.

And in the staying, and in the paying attention, the power did something it had rarely done: it allowed itself to be affected by what it witnessed.

It allowed itself to feel the creature's patience. It allowed itself to feel the peat's slow accumulation. It allowed itself to feel the water's cold persistence. It allowed itself to feel the wind's unceasing energy. It allowed itself to feel the light's fierce clarity.

And in feeling these things, the power learned.

It learned that some things are not meant to be improved. Some things are not meant to be saved, or fixed, or transformed. Some things are already, in their very nature, complete—and the only proper response to completeness is gratitude.

The power had never felt gratitude before. It had felt recognition, yes, and compassion, and a kind of immense, unshakable affection for all the forms that existence takes. But gratitude—the specific gratitude of being present to something that requires nothing from you—was new. It was a gift that the plateau had given to the power, not through any intention, but simply through being itself.

The power, which had given so much to so many worlds—light, warmth, guidance, protection, occasional intervention—found itself receiving.

It was a strange sensation. It was a humbling sensation. And it was a sensation that the power resolved to remember.

**—**

Time passed. Not the small time of days and years, but the large time of geological epochs. The peat continued to accumulate at a rate measured in millimeters per century. The moss continued to spread, die, and become soil. The streams continued to shift by centimeters each year. The rust on the industrial remnants continued to flake away one cell at a time.

The creature continued to filter water and dream its slow dream. But now, there was a difference.

When the creature sent out its messages through the moss—the slow, deliberate syllables that seeped through the sphagnum and reached others of its kind far away—it began to include a new element. It was not a word, not exactly. It was more like a background note, a tone that underlay everything else. The tone was recognition. It was the creature's way of saying, to anyone who might be listening: _I am here, and I am seen._

The other creatures received this new element and were puzzled. They did not know what it meant. They did not know what it was like to be seen, truly seen, by a presence that required nothing. But they accepted the new element, the way they accepted everything about their slow, underwater existence. It became part of the grammar.

The power noticed this. It noticed the new tone in the creature's messages, and it understood what it meant. The creature had been changed by being noticed—not in any outward way, but in the deepest and most inward way: it had begun to see itself as something worth seeing.

This was not something the power had intended. It was not something the power had planned. It was simply what happened when a complete being was witnessed by another being who recognized its completeness.

The power felt something like wonder, and something like humility, and something like joy.

It had come to the plateau expecting to give something. It had found, instead, that it was the one receiving.

**—**

And then, one day—though "day" is not the right word, for the power did not measure time in such small units—the creature did something it had never done before.

It looked up.

Not with its deep, water-filled hollows that functioned like lenses for light that had already passed through the moss. Not with the physical eyes that it did not possess. But with something deeper, something akin to what you might call a soul, if you were inclined to use such language.

It looked up, not toward the sky—for the sky was nothing special, just a pale, scrubbed blue that was almost white at the zenith—but toward something else. Toward the quality of the light. Toward the warmth in the water. Toward the softness that had been growing, season by season, in the wind's hum.

It looked up, and it said—not in words, not in the chemical grammar of the sphagnum, not in any language that could be translated—it said: _I see you too._

And the power, which had been present for so long without being seen, which had witnessed so many worlds without ever being witnessed in return, felt something it had never felt before.

It felt itself being known.

The sensation was so unfamiliar, so unexpected, so overwhelming, that the power did not know what to do with it. It was the power's turn, now, to be the one who was noticed. To be the one who was seen. To be the one who was, for the first time in its long, long existence, recognized.

It did not know how to respond. It did not know how to receive this gift. It had spent eons giving—life, light, warmth, attention—and it had never been given to in return. It had never expected to be. It had never imagined that it could be.

And yet here, on this wind-scoured plateau, in the cold water of the peat, in the slow grammar of the sphagnum moss, a creature that was not an animal and not a plant had seen it.

The power felt something welling up within itself—something like emotion, if emotion could be felt by something without a body, without a mind, without a form. It was not joy, exactly, and not sorrow. It was not love, exactly, and not gratitude. It was all of these things, and none of them. It was simply the feeling of being _met_.

And in that feeling, the power understood something new.

It understood that the greatest act of benevolence is not to give, but to be present. Not to fix, but to witness. Not to transform, but to recognize. The plateau had taught the power this, not through any teaching, but simply through being itself. The creature had taught the power this, not through any intention, but simply by looking up.

The power, which could have done anything, chose to do only this: to remain. To continue to be present. To continue to witness. To continue to learn.

And to be, in its turn, witnessed.

**—**

This is what the benevolent and wise power did, when it came to this place and saw the being who lives here.

It did not save them. It did not heal them. It did not change them, or improve them, or transform them into something new.

It simply noticed them. And in noticing them, it allowed itself to be noticed in return.

And that, the power now understood, was the most benevolent thing it could possibly do.

Because what the place and the being needed was not a savior. What they needed was not a protector. What they needed was not even a friend.

What they needed was exactly what they already had, and what the power chose to give: a witness. A presence that required nothing. A companionship of attention, deep as the peat, patient as the water, unwavering as the light.

The power stayed on the plateau for a long, long time. It stayed as the moss grew and died and grew again. It stayed as the wind continued to pour across the open ground. It stayed as the creature filtered water and sent its messages and dreamed its slow dream. It stayed as the industrial remnants rusted to dust and the turbine finally collapsed and the generator sank completely into the peat.

It stayed, and it paid attention, and it was, in its turn, paid attention to.

And the plateau, and the creature, and the power—they all continued to live their slow, patient, intertwined existence, each one witnessing the others, each one being witnessed in return.

This was the beginning of a long, slow friendship—not a friendship of words or gestures or shared experiences, but a friendship of presence. A friendship of _being there_, in the same light, in the same water, in the same wind.

And it was enough.

It was more than enough.

It was everything.
