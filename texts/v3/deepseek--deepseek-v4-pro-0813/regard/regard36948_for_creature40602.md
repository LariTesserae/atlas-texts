**What the Power Would Do**

The benevolent and wise power came down into Brack on the road of crushed slag and brick. It had been drawn by the sound: the two-note calls of the whistle-eels, the clinkers’ rasp like pebbles rubbing, the nodders’ patient rhythm, the engine’s dull clap and high wheeze. It did not come as light or storm. It came as an increase in attention, the way air thickens before a change that does not come.

It saw the town in its broad, low bowl under the tin-colored sky. It saw the purple-grey mat spread over the black mud like a living skin, the pale mineral spires, the iron walkways with their creosoted timber, the orange-grey lamps burning without shadows. It saw the people: the oiler, the scraper, the lamplighter, the watcher with her waxed board and stylus. It saw the animals everywhere—millions of small bodies moving, feeding, calling, listening.

It understood that the being who lived in Brack was not any one person or animal. It was the air itself: thick with condensation and sulfide and breath, the town’s single crowded lung. The power entered it the only way possible—by breathing it. It tasted oil and wet iron, clinker shell and eel breath, and found not sickness but rhythm.

It followed the loop. Seep gas rose. The mats consumed the gas. The clinkers consumed the mats. The eels consumed the clinkers. The nodders and tunbacks consumed the eels and clinkers. Waste sank, gas rose. The engine removed water and gave it back as condensation. The people oiled and scraped and counted. Nothing multiplied beyond its fixed number. Nothing wore. Nothing eroded. The rust had reached its final color. The same day repeated, and yet the town was ferociously alive.

Because it was benevolent, the power considered gifts.

A green plant. But the soil was too metallic; if the plant lived, it would alter the chemistry that fed the mat.

A child. But the numbers were exact, and a child would tip the loop.

A clear sky. But the grey ceiling held the moisture in; sun would dry the seep pools and silence the eels.

A road away. But the people were the town’s hands. To remove them was not mercy; it was amputation.

The power considered stopping the engine and ending the cycle, letting Brack become ordinary ground. But that was not healing. That was unwinding a knot tied exactly once.

So the wise power did what is hardest for a benevolent power: it did not fix, add, or save. It stayed for one full cycle and took part in the loop without changing it. It walked with the oiler and matched the interval of the bearings. It sat beside the watcher and counted the nodders, and the number was the same. It scraped a short length of walkway, not to remove life, but to keep the way open. It let the condensation gather on it and run down in slow black lines. It breathed the town’s air and gave it back exactly as it had received it.

Then it left by the same road. It left no child, no leaf, no sun, no open way. It left no new note in the eels’ chorus. It took only the memory of a closed loop held in a steady state.

If the watcher felt a brief increase of attention, it passed so quickly that it did not become a thought. She finished her count. The number was the same.

Brack did not change. The lamps glowed a little brighter, and the animal calls rose as if answering. Then they settled. The same day repeated, complete and unharmed.

The power had done the only thing a wise and benevolent power can do for a world that does not need saving. It had seen Brack clearly. It had found it whole. And it had left it alone.
