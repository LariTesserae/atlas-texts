The singer's question hangs in the water-charged air, and the whole city seems to lean forward. You feel a change before you can name it—a shift in the density of the air, a subtle vibration through the moss underfoot, a taste on your tongue like iron and salt and the memory of stone before it was soil.

The mountain is waking.

Not the city. Not the rooted ones. The mountain itself, the old bones beneath the living skin. It has been asleep for epochs, dreaming in mineral and pressure, and the slow movement of roots into its cracks, the patient seepage of water through its pores, the steady undulation of the plant-city above it—these things have been a kind of lullaby. But now, something has changed. Perhaps it is the singer's question, perhaps it is the attention of the power that has been watching, perhaps it is simply time. But the rock shivers.

You feel it first as a low rumble, far below hearing, a sensation that travels up through your feet and settles in your chest. The water in the channels trembles. The hanging moss sways though there is no wind. The rooted ones, those ancient beings whose bodies form the walls and columns and arches, begin to creak—not in distress, but in recognition, the way old bones creak when they stretch after too long still.

The power does not arrive. It was already here. It is the mountain, and the mountain is the place, and the place has been aware of the city for as long as the city has been growing. But now, it chooses to act.

The break in the ridgeline, the way you came in, begins to narrow. It is not a dramatic movement—no falling rocks, no grinding stone. It is a slow, deliberate shift, a shrug of the mountain's shoulders. The rock walls move closer together, not enough to seal the passage, but enough to make it less obvious, less inviting. The way in becomes a way that must be sought, not stumbled upon. The mountain is not closing itself off, not entirely. It is becoming selective.

The water responds. Throughout the city, the weeping rock weeps more freely. The thin films of moisture on every surface thicken, and the channels fill with a water that tastes of deep minerals—calcium, magnesium, the ancient blood of the earth. The roots drink. You see the moss brighten, the fern fronds uncurl slightly, the whole green structure pulse with renewed vigor. The mountain is feeding them. It has decided they are not parasites but partners.

Then, the power does something unexpected. Near the entrance, where the rock is porous and the water seeps out, a new spring opens. It is a small thing, a crack widening just enough to release a steady trickle of clear, cold water. It forms a pool, no larger than a basin, ringed with soft moss. The water is sweet. It is a gift, a sign, a place for travelers to pause and drink before entering the city proper. It is an invitation and a warning: this place is loved, and those who come must come with respect.

You find yourself standing in the center of the cirque, but now the water-children are moving more quickly, their translucent bodies darting through the channels, clearing them of the new mineral deposits. The walkers are touching the walls, their tendrils aligning the moss, guiding the growth. And the rooted ones, those vast still minds, are releasing scent into the air—a complex fragrance of wet earth and crushed leaves and something sweet, like a flower that blooms only in the dark. It is a conversation, a song, a prayer.

The power speaks. Not in words, but in the language of the place. The water carries the message. The roots transmit it. The moss absorbs it. You cannot understand it, but you feel it: a deep, resonant acknowledgment, a kind of cosmic nod. The mountain has recognized its child. The child has recognized its parent. The relationship has shifted from unconscious symbiosis to conscious alliance.

The singer who turned toward you now turns away. Its fronds unfurl fully, revealing the face of moss and stem, and it releases a scent that is both an answer and a new question. It asks not of you, but of the mountain: *What now?*

The mountain answers with a slow pulse of warmth—not heat, but a kind of life, a movement of energy that rises through the stone and into the roots and the stems and the leaves. The whole city inhales. The air becomes heavy with oxygen and moisture and the scent of growing things. The light, dim and green, seems to deepen, to become more alive. The water drips, the moss grows, the city breathes.

And you understand that the power has not come to change anything, not to impose order or provide salvation. It has come to witness. It has come to bless. It has come to say, in the only language that matters, *I see you. You are not alone.*

Then, the mountain settles back into itself. The rumble fades. The rock stills. The water-children return to their work. The walkers resume their slow tending. The rooted ones release a final wave of scent—a sigh, a song, a release.

The break in the ridgeline is now a narrow slit, almost invisible from outside. The spring at the entrance wells and overflows, its water trickling down into the city. The cirque is more hidden, more protected, more self-contained than before. But it is also more alive, more nourished, more connected to the deep bones of the earth.

You stand there, in the cold, in the dim green light, and you feel the weight of what has happened. The place has been noticed. It has been loved. And in that love, it has been changed—not in its essence, but in its security. It has a guardian now. Not a god who demands worship, but a parent who offers sustenance. Not a ruler who imposes law, but a mountain who simply is.

The singer releases a final scent, and this one, you realize, is for you. It is not a question. It is not a greeting. It is a farewell. The city is not asking you to leave, but it is telling you that you may go now, that you have seen enough, that you will carry this place with you in your memory, and that is enough.

The water drips. The moss grows. The city breathes. And you turn, slowly, carefully, and begin the climb back through the narrowing pass, leaving the green dimness behind, but carrying with you the knowledge that somewhere in the mountains, there is a place that is not empty, a place that is not silent, a place that has been seen by something wise and has been told: *You are not alone.*

And as you crest the rise and the world unfolds before you, you hear, faint and far below, the low creak of the rooted ones, settling back into their long stillness, and the distant hum of a singer, continuing its endless song.
