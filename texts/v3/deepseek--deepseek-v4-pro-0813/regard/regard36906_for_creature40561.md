So here is the hollow as it is: a low place below the water table, warm mineral water rising and spilling, pools packed rim to rim, a mineral world doing its one slow, patient work. And in the deepest pool, the resident: a blind, stone-colored salamander, shaped by the place the way the place is shaped by water. It does not know it is the hollow's heart, any more than the water does.

I imagine the benevolent and wise power, the one whose attention has fallen here, as something that does not arrive with bright hand or heavy will. It would not roll aside the stone, increase the light, call the creature up to something else. It would not cure the blindness. It would not give the salamander a mirror for its own mystery.

Here is what a benevolent power might do, if it looked in, and saw the hollow for what it is.

It would ensure that the water continues to rise.

The most profound thing a benevolent power can do for that place is not transformation but protection. Not recognition, not vindication, not even healing. The hollow does not need revelation. It does not need to be drawn into the larger story, discovered by hungry minds, charted, mapped, named. It needs the slant of sunlight through steam. It needs the slow pulse of the spring. It needs the bacteria on the pool floor and the white crustacean rowing past on feathery legs.

A wise power would know that the holiest act for something already whole is to turn aside.

And yet the story asks: what would they *do*, if anything? Perhaps they do one thing.

Perhaps—without the creature noticing—the benevolent power settles into the stone just behind the spring slit. It becomes, for a time, a kind of warmth within the water that does not change the temperature. It does not disturb the chemistry. It does not press a thought into the salamander's slow mind, because the salamander's mind is already full of the hollow, already whole, and the power would not presume to add a language to that.

But perhaps the power leaves a seed of something—not growth, not a green shoot, but a feather-edge of protection, a subtle holdingness in the mineral crust. So that when the far-off, ungentle event comes—the drilling rig from above, the diverted water table, the slow poison of a changing climate, the well-meaning explorer with a lamp and a chisel—the place holds its shape a little longer. Not forever. Benevolent does not mean immune to time. But it means that the hollow isn't breached casually, that the boundaries the place has built over centuries are not broken by indifference in an afternoon.

Perhaps the power also notices the salamander's loneliness. But here is the test of wisdom: to know that "loneliness" may be the wrong word. The creature is alone because the hollow is small, because no other of its kind fits the saturated, sunless niche. But perhaps there is no ache in it. An ache requires a sense of missing something else, and the salamander has never known another like itself. It is not lonely. It is singular.

So the power does not make another salamander. It does not set a mate in the far pool, or whisper a migratory instinct into the creature's blood. That would be an intervention born from a human picture of completeness: that every living thing must be part of a pair, a choir, a family. The wise power knows the creature is already in relationship—with the spring slit, the bacterial mats, the pale crustacean, the cup-shaped depressions, the mineral film forming on its back. It is not alone. It is the hollow.

But the power might do one thing more, a thing no one sees.

It might witness.

It might watch the creature for a thousand breaths, and, in watching, confirm it. Not in the way a god confirms a saint—with a sign, a stirring, a burst of light. But in the way a stone holds the warmth of the day after the sun goes down. The hollow contains life, and the power contains the hollow. The salamander stays as it is, because it is the kind of thing that stays. And for once, this is enough for the watcher.

The light slants. The mist eats the sound. The water rises.

If the benevolent power were to have a thought—not spoken, not planted, but held inside itself the way the stain of rust holds in the mineral rim—it might be this:

*This is what it looks like to be already saved.*

Not saved from death, not saved from darkness, not saved from smallness. But saved from the great roaring need to be different, to matter more, to become more visible. The hollow is not waiting for a messiah. It is not waiting to be developed. It is not waiting to be discovered by a larger world or to bear fruit. It is saturated, complete, a closed account. It is a held note.

So the wise power, if it is truly wise, does nothing permanent. And the doing of nothing here is not neglect. It is restraint. It is the high, hard choice of leaving beauty alone. It is the power's silent speech: *I see you. I will not improve you. I will not use you. I will not take your mystery and make it into a tale for others. You are enough without me. I will guard the edges of your enoughness and let the water keep rising.*

Then, if the power can bring itself to do it, it goes.

Not from hatred, as the old stories say of abandonment, but from love. The honeybee does not stay in the flower. The rain does not stay in the cloud. The light does not stay in the eye. The wise power has its own nature, and this place has its own. They touch at the rim, and then the water moves on.

And the salamander, unmoved, unknowing, keeps breathing through its gills in the deep pool. A fine crust of mineral forms along its back, the first frost of another year. The small white crustacean rows by on feathery legs. A drop falls from the ceiling and clicks. The place does not need the power. It never did.

That is perhaps the only true offering a benevolent power can make to a place like this: not a sign of itself, but a clear, clean withdrawal that leaves no trace. Not because the place is unworthy of presence, but because it is already full. Full of water, full of mineral, full of the slow blinking of gills. Full of the one held note.

And if the power must do something, if doing nothing is a doctrine too pure for even a god to bear, then this: it deepens the spring by the width of a hair. It eases the long-term chemistry so the travertine rims stay round and unscarred for another few centuries. It wraps the hollow in a kind of atmospheric mercy, so that the water table above it does not drop, the wells do not find it, the road does not come. And then it lets go.

The salamander stays in its depression. The mineral grows. The hollow holds. The old bench sleeps under its crust. And somewhere, just beyond the rim, the benevolent power keeps its own kind of silence—the silence of the gardener who loves a wild field enough not to garden it.

That is what it would do, if anything. It would let the place be. And in that letting-be, it would make of itself a new kind of power: the power that stills its own hand. Not the maker, not the mender, but the witness at the threshold who walks away.

The water rises warm from below. The pools brim. The mist eats every sound. The creature breathes, and the hollow is not empty; it is full of being simply what it is.

And the wise power, if it is truly wise, knows that this—this tireless, sunless, mineral-encrusted breathing—is not something to change. It is something to kneel to.
