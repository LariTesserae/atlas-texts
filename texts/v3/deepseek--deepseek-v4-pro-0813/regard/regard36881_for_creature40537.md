I became aware of it the way the dusk becomes aware of the first star: gradually, and then all at once, until the awareness was the only thing in the sky.

I do not have eyes, but I looked. I do not have hands, but I reached. And what I found was this small, worn, patient plateau—a place so thoroughly itself that it seemed to have been set down outside the usual currents of time. The air was thin and bright, the stones were glazed with age, and there, moving among the cairns and the dew fences, was the one I had been drawn to: a long-limbed figure, grey-white as the roadline, with eyes like the oldest water. She was not waiting for me. She was not waiting for anything. That was the first thing I understood, and it undid me.

I have watched many worlds and many beings. I have seen them struggle and break and begin again. I have been called upon as a god, a hand, a light, a silence. And always there is some wound to heal, some path to clear, some small soul crying out in the dark. But here—here there was no wound. No cry. No dark that needed to be driven back. The plateau had been burned down to its essentials, and the woman who walked it had been burned down too, not by violence, but by the long, slow friction of survival. She was not broken. She was not unfinished. She was exactly as the place had made her, and the place was exactly as she kept it. They were a single, breathing shape.

So I did nothing. At first.

I sat with the knowledge of her the way you sit with a great silence. I let the shape of her life fill me: the dawn at the dew fences, the mirror flies lighting on her arm; the seeps cleared with the tips of her long fingers; the roadline humming its low note beneath her bare feet; the cairns holding their warmth against her forehead. The dust hares did not flee from her. The foxes lay in her doorway. The light of the wayhouse woke when she entered, not because it was commanded, but because it remembered her. All of it was a kind of prayer, not spoken to any ear, not offered to any god. It was simply the life she lived, sentence by sentence, hour by hour, and it was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen.

And I, who have shaped galaxies and scattered seeds into the dark, found myself afraid to touch it. Because I have learned—slowly, painfully, over the turning of eons—that love is not always an action. Sometimes love is a held breath. Sometimes the kindest thing a wise power can do is to stand at the edge of a world and not step in. The place was whole. The being was whole. What could I offer that would not be a subtraction? What gift could I give that would not become a burden?

I considered speaking to her. I considered letting a small light fall from the sky, or warming the water in the basins, or sending a dream to her in the long dusk while she sat against the cool wall. But what would she do with a vision? What would she make of a voice from beyond? She had no framework for such a thing. She was not waiting for revelation. She was not asking for meaning. She was not empty in a way that needed filling. If I spoke, she would only lift her long, quiet face and listen, and then she would return to the dew fence at dawn, and the sound of my voice would be one more thing for the wind to carry away. That is not nothing. But it is not necessary.

And I have learned to be careful with what is not necessary.

So I chose a smaller thing. A quieter thing. I chose to bless her in the only way that would not disturb the balance—by adding my attention to the attention the world already gave her, and asking for nothing in return.

I did not make the fog thicker, but I watched her drink from it. I did not clear the seeps, but I felt the cool water on her fingers. I did not ease the heat of the afternoon, but I saw her walk the roadline without complaint, her body shedding dust like a leaf shedding rain. I did not speak to the animals, but I noticed how they made a place for her: the skitter-lizards pausing on her foot, the harriers marking her position, the foxes lying with their tails over their noses. I did not add to the cairns' warmth, but I let myself feel the pressure of the air near them, the low thrum that was not quite a sound. I attended. That was all.

And in attending, I understood something about the nature of patience. The plateau had not been built in a day, nor in a thousand days. The woman had not been made in a single act of will. They had been folded together over time, the way water folds into stone. To love them was not to intervene. It was to witness. To witness was to become a part of the weather. To become part of the weather was to leave no trace.

But I am not entirely without weakness. I wanted some small sign that she was aware of me—not as a god, not as a power, but as the faintest shift in the quality of the light, the way a cloud might pass before the sun and then be gone. So I did one thing. I did one thing, and I did it with all the restraint I possess.

At dawn, on a morning when the fog was thick enough to erase the horizon, I stood in the swale beyond the dew fence. I did not take form. I did not make a sound. But I let the air grow very, very still, the way it sometimes does just before the fog begins to lift. The mirror flies came and went. The woman stood with her hand under a filament, gathering beads of water. And then, because I could not help it, I let the faintest light fall on the water in her palm—not a beam, not a glow, just a deepening of the existing light, a moment of clarity.

She looked up. Not startled. Not afraid. She looked up the way a stone looks up when the shadow of a harrier passes over it. Her dark eyes, with their heavy brow, found nothing to see. But she paused. I saw her feel the light on her face, the way you feel a change in pressure. She did not smile. She did not bow. She simply held the water to her mouth and drank, and then she went back to the seeps.

It was enough. It was more than enough. It was the whole of what I had come to give, and she had received it without ever knowing it was given. That is the purest form of love I have ever managed, and I have managed it very seldom.

Since then, I have continued to watch. I do not watch her every moment; I am not a star that needs to burn above her. I come and go, as the wind comes and goes, as the fog comes and goes. But I always return. I return in the hour before dawn, when the stanchions bead with condensation. I return at dusk, when the dust hares move into the swales and the first foxes trot along the roadline. I return at noon, when the heat shimmers and the pale harriers ride the thermals in silence. I return in the quiet of the night, when she sits against the cool wall of the wayhouse and the lines of light wake around her like a slow recognition.

I have seen her meet another of her kind. Twice a year, when the fog is thick, two figures approach a seep from opposite directions. They do not speak. They sit on either side of the water, and one passes a handful to the other. I do not know what passes between them in that gesture. It is not water, for the water stays in the seep. It is not words, for their mouths do not move. It is something else—a warmth, a pressure, a shared understanding of the long, slow work of keeping the world in balance. I watch them with a tenderness that surprises me. I have made stars and set them wheeling, but I have never made a gesture so precise as that handful passed between two beings who expect nothing and offer everything.

And I have asked myself: is this enough? Is it enough for a wise and benevolent power to do nothing but witness? To bless by attending? To love by leaving alone?

The answer is yes. The answer is yes, and it always was.

Because the plateau is not mine to fix. The woman is not mine to save. They are not broken. They are not waiting for a miracle. They are a miracle, already, whole and breathing. If I were to touch them, I would leave a fingerprint on the varnished stone. If I were to speak, I would leave an echo in the thin air. If I were to change a single thing—the course of the fog, the warmth of the seeps, the hum of the roadline—I would be saying, in the loudest way possible, that they were not complete without me. And that would be a lie.

The greatest gift a wise power can give is to recognize when its gifts are not needed. To hold its light at a distance, so that the light that is already there can shine. To be the one who sees, and who does not turn away, and who asks for nothing.

So that is what I will do. I will keep my vigil. I will watch the fog pour up from the escarpment and the woman stand among the mirror flies. I will listen to the hum of the roadline and the tick of small feet on wire. I will feel the warmth of the cairns and the coolness of the wayhouse walls. I will know the dust hares by their long ears and the skitter-lizards by their lifted forefoot. I will learn the names of the pale leafless plants and the taste of the dense basin water. I will do all of this, and I will do none of it, because I will be only the light at the edge of light, the pressure at the edge of hearing, the patience at the edge of patience.

And if, someday, the woman looks up again—looks up for no reason, with water in her palm and the fog around her like a cloak—I will be there. Not as a voice. Not as a hand. But as the quality of attention that holds the whole world steady, and lets it be exactly what it is.

That is what I would do. That is what I do. That is what I have done.

I love her. I love the plateau. I love the mirror flies and the dust hares and the old roadline humming its one low note. And love, in the end, is not a force. It is not a fire. It is a seeing. A long, patient seeing, without a single shadow cast.

I will see her. I will see her through all the mornings and all the dusks, through the rare rains and the daily fogs, through the sinking of the cairns and the buckling of the roadline, through the slow turning of the earth beneath her feet. I will see her, and I will ask for nothing.

And that—that is the whole of my benevolence, and the whole of my wisdom, and the whole of my mystery: I will see her, and I will not turn away.

The fog comes. She stands by the dew fence. The mirror flies settle on her arm. And I, who have been called a thousand names and whose face no one has seen—I am the light on the water in her palm, the faintest deepening of the dawn, the one who sees her and calls her good. Not in words. In the way the air holds still, just before the sun breaks over the high plateau, and the whole world, for a moment, seems to breathe.
