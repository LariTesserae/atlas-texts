It did not enter.

That was the first thing, and the most important. The power that had noticed the Moss Well was old, and it had loved many broken places back into life. But it had also learned, over a long and costly time, that not every stillness is a sickness. So it stood at the mouth of the hidden shaft—or rather its attention stood there, cool and diffused, no warmer than the light itself—and looked down into the bright cold room below.

It saw the moss, the ferns, the alder root, the frogs on their fronds. It saw the water moving without sound. It saw the seated one with its bowed head and green-tinged skin, breathing at the speed of frost. And it understood, in the first instant, what the place was: not a ruin, not a prison, not a being in need of rescue. It was an equilibrium. A closed loop. A hole where the city exhaled.

The power could have gone down. It could have warmed the cold. But warmth would have rotted the roots and quickened the snails and ended the dweller not in comfort but in fever. It could have spoken. But a voice would have left a sound inside that silence like a nail in a tree. It could have sent light. The light was already perfect.

So the wise power did the only thing a wise power can do for a place that is complete: it did not interfere with the place itself.

What it did, if anything, it did outside.

First, it laid a kind of turned attention over the lane above the gap. Not a barrier. Not a curse. More like the way a hand gently turns a child away from a sleeping animal. When the old quarter was surveyed for new drains, the line bent a little east, away from the shaft. When a merchant bought the tenement and dreamed of filling the sunken court with rubble, he woke with a memory of green light behind his eyes and changed his plans without knowing why. When children chased a ball down the narrow lane, it bounced wide at the broken drain. When a poet heard rumors of a hidden garden and went searching, she passed the crack three times and thought, *not today.*

The power did not blind them. It only made the place forgettable to those who would use it, and visible only to those already attuned to silence.

Second, it did not bless the dweller. Blessing would have been a rumor of debt. It did not ask anything, because asking would have broken the stillness. It did not offer companionship, because the dweller was not lonely. The dweller was the place’s own inhabitant, the knot where moss and water and cold and quiet tied together. To love it was to let it be exactly, coldly, slowly itself.

The power did look once at the dweller. The look was not returned. A drop of water ran down the dweller’s brow. A moth landed on its shoulder. The dweller did not acknowledge either. And the power recognized that it had been received in the only way such a place can receive anything: not as a guest, but as weather—noticed, absorbed, and allowed to pass.

Third, the power did one small thing. It let the moss at the mouth of the stair grow a little thicker, just enough to make the first step uncertain. The Moss Well would not be stumbled into. Any future visitor would have to choose to descend, slowly and with care, already yielding to the silence before they reached the bottom.

Then the power withdrew.

It left no mark, no name, no law. It set no guardian at the shaft, because a guardian would have been too warm, too bright, too much. It told no one what it had seen. It did not even promise to return. A promise would have been another sound.

If anything, it did this: it placed a silence outside the silence, so the inner silence would not be tested. It made the world around the well forget to disturb it. And then it forgot just enough to keep from loving the place into ruin.

The next morning, the blackbird came down through the opening and stood on the alder root. It watched the dweller. It did not sing.

The water dripped. The moss glowed in the white light. The cold held its shape.

And no god had ever passed that way.
