What the wise power would do is arrive.

Not in fire. The valley would not trust fire; it would hiss and fill with steam and, after a season, maybe forgive. Not in light. The cloud is part of its skin, and the people who live there have eyes made for gray-green dimness. Not as a voice commanding the walls to stand. The walls are already standing, and falling, and standing again, in their own long argument with gravity.

The power would come as the thing the valley understands best: water.

A small rain, no more than a heavy mist, gathering on the pass. It would slide from a leaf into a gutter and travel the whole length of the built world—through sluices, over moss, into a cistern, under a turbine, around the bend where the boy is working. It would not control the water. It would be water, for a while.

Then it would become a stone in a wall that has been trying to sit down for three generations. The keepers would find it one morning, new but already mossed, and tap it with a brass hammer. They would listen. They would say, “This one wants to stay.” And the wall would hold easier.

But the power would not remain a stone. That would be a cage. It would become a pair of hands—chapped, cold, patient—and ask to join a work crew. The gaffer would look it over and hand it a scraper. “Clear that length of gutter. Don’t sing too loud.”

It would learn.

It would learn what all newcomers learn: that the valley does not need saving. It needs tending. That moss is not an enemy. That a crack is not always wrong. That the water is not waste. It would learn the names of wetness: seep, weep, run, drip, sweat, gush, sluice, hush. It would learn to walk a wet coping without startling the water. It would learn to sit still for an hour with a hand on a pipe, feeling for the wrong note.

At the Steam Hall it would say nothing. It would watch the polished stones drop into the channels and be counted by weight. It would understand that this slow parliament is slow because the valley itself is slow, and wisdom is the correct speed of attention. It would not hurry the vote. It would not make the stones fall faster.

In the powerhouse it would sit beside the old man with the stethoscope. It would listen to the bearing. The old man might say, “Bad mood today. Mist is thin.” The power would not fix the bearing with a thought. It would not make the turbine immortal. It would simply listen too. The turbine, feeling two attentions, might settle. Or it might not. The power would return the next day to listen again. That is how love works in a wet valley.

At the High Flume it would meet the woman who has kept four hundred meters of channel for forty-one years. She would not ask who it was. She would show it a stone that had shifted in the night. They would work together in the cold, setting it back, repointing the joint, water sliding down their wrists. When they finished, she would pat the wall and say, “It was thinking about wandering.” The power would nod. It would know now that these words are not fancy.

In the Root Terrace it would kneel beside the boy and his gutter. The boy would show it the twig and the bend. He would hum his tone, and the twig would catch anyway. The power would listen. Then it would hum a lower harmony—not to force the gutter, but to match the frequency of the pump below. The twig would slide around the bend. The boy would grin. “You know the song,” he would say. The power would answer, “I’m learning it.”

It would see the dead walled into the terraces, moss growing over their niches. It would not raise them. It would not call them back. It would understand that the dead are still working, only slower, and that to resurrect them would be to interrupt both their labor and their rest. Maybe it would touch one niche and leave a single drop of water, as the valley does, and move on.

It would find no temple here. No altar. No need of gods. The valley is already full of the sacred, but not as a separate thing. The sacred is the gutter cleared, the gate oiled, the wall tended, the moss pressed into blocks, the steam rising. The power would not ask to be worshiped. It would be embarrassed to be worshiped. It would rather be useful.

There would be moments when it could do more. A landslip beginning on a steep slope. A child with a cough. A turbine bearing running warm. A wall leaning too far. The power would feel the urge to unmake the danger, to dry the damp from every lung, to seal every crack. But it would pause.

Because the valley is a process, not a problem. If it removed all danger, it would also remove the careful walking, the listening, the repair, the patience, the reason the keepers’ hands are cracked and precise. It would take away their work. And their work is their love.

So it would do smaller things.

It would let one wall’s settling fall into a better line, saving a house but not the effort of repair. It would let a patch of peat moss grow thick beneath the children’s beds, keeping them warmer without knowing why. It would send a dipper to work a clear flume, a small gray bird whose presence would be noticed and called lucky. It would quiet the turbine’s bad mood just enough that the old man could sleep, but not so much that he would stop listening. It would guide the boy’s twig around the bend on the day he was near tears, and then let it stick the next day so he would keep learning.

And after a season, or a year, the power would leave.

Not because it had finished. Because its presence was no longer needed. It had done what it could without unmaking the place. It had been water, stone, hands, breath, harmony, listening. It had not saved the valley. It had tended it.

At the top of the pass, it would look back. The valley would still look like a wet, green machine half-buried in cloud, its terraces rising like contour lines, each one crowded with buildings, pipes, planting beds, and the dark shine of moving water. It would see the woman patting her wall. It would see the boy humming to his gutter. It would see the old man with his stethoscope. It would see the moss growing over the dead, and the dead holding the living, and the living holding the water.

It would feel, for a moment, something like loneliness. Then it would turn and go, leaving no commandment and no temple. Only a single polished stone in the council channel, which the keepers would find and weigh and say, “Someone stayed long enough to vote.”

And if they looked closely, they would see that the valley was a little more itself.

That is what a wise power does. It does not fix. It does not rule. It does not vanish entirely. It becomes, for a while, another pair of hands in the wet, green dark—and then it lets the valley go on, as it always has, becoming.
