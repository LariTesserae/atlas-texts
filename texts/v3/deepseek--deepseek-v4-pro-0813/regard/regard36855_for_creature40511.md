**The Visitor at the Speed of the Waterwheel**

The benevolent and wise power arrived not as light but as a subtle thickening of the water above the Boiler Gardens. It had heard the town long before it found it: not a cry, but a rhythm. A tick every four or five minutes. A half-hour clank. The sound of something being kept alive not by urgency but by maintenance.

For a long time the power stayed above the golden weed, looking down. It saw the thirty doors, the roofs with their fur of algae, the chimney leaking slow threads of warm sulphur. It saw the Tender asleep beside the amber battery jars, tail over its mouth. It might have done anything. It had the power to raise the town, to part the water, to open the Tender’s eye-slits and fill them with sight. But it had been wise long enough to suspect that not every sunken thing is drowning.

So it descended. It came down not as a hand or a body but as a current so slow the sediment did not lift. It entered through the collapsed north tunnel, the same way the salt tongue enters. It let the fresh water pass over its idea of shoulders and the salt water pass over its idea of thighs, and it stopped at the boundary where the light bends. It hung there, half in green, half in rust, exactly as the town itself was hung.

The Tender woke. Its gills flared. It turned its broad head toward the change in the water’s weight. It did not flee. It set one webbed hand on the nearest pipe and listened, because the pipe carried the town’s one note, and that note had changed to welcome.

The power did not speak. To speak would have been to choose a language, and the Boiler Gardens had already learned the language of slow belts, brass rims, bacterial light, and the pressure of a flap valve opening. So the power listened. It let the waterwheel’s first full revolution pass through it. It let the battery jars’ patient bubble become its breathing. It let the Tender go on with its rounds.

What did the power do?

It did only one thing, and it did it carefully.

It followed the Tender through the slot streets, learning the path worn into the sediment. It watched the webbed hand run over the waterwheel’s spokes. It watched the rasping tongue clear a small strip from a wall and leave the rest for another season. It watched the Tender hang at the boundary and drink from the meeting of fresh and salt. The power saw that this was not a ruin waiting for a resurrection. It was a house with a slower heart. The open doors did not need closing. The blind eyes did not need sight. The machines did not need their old purpose. They had a new one: to turn, to tick, to slip, to be nudged back.

So the power did not clean the battery plates to brightness. It did not change the waterwheel’s speed. It did not send fish to fill the empty rooms. It did not reveal the name of the dead foreman or the purpose of the brine.

Instead, it reached out—lightly, the way a fingertip touches a glass—and rested a current over the leather belt that had slipped a little from its wooden cog. Not enough to move it. Just enough to warm it. The leather remembered its form and settled back.

Then the power visited each orange lamp that still burned. It did not increase their light. It only stood beneath them, invisible in the water, and let the Tender pass by without knowing it was no longer alone.

At the boundary it paused again. The Tender was there, hanging horizontally, its gills open and its sealed eyes showing their milky inward light. The power joined it at the same depth, and for a while they were two presences at the meeting place, one flesh and one current, each half in fresh water and half in salt. The power felt the town’s slow tide move through its body. It thought, *To heal this, I would have to wound it. To wake it, I would have to make it dream of air.*

So it did not heal it.

It blessed the water instead. The blessing was not a flash of light. It was a small quieting. The surface above the sinkhole grew a little less interesting to anchors. The maps forgot, or half-forgot, the name Boiler Gardens. Storms that might have churned the golden weed passed a mile wide. The town was not hidden. It was simply left alone.

Then the power withdrew, but not entirely. It remains above the sinkhole, not as a god, but as a warm patch of surface water, an eddy that deters boats, a brown-green light that changes nothing. The Tender does not know it is watched. The waterwheel does not know it is kept. The town does not know it was visited.

And that is the whole intervention: a leather belt warmed back onto its cog; a town made just a little more forgettable; and a wise power that understood the kindest thing it could do was to become, for a time, another slow current in the drowned quarter.
