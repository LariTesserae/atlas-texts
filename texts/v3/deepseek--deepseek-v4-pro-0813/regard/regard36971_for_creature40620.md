The benevolent and wise power did not at first do anything. It simply saw.

It saw the basin: three hundred meters across, ringed by fresh fault-scarps and sharp rock edges, its floor paved with thin mineral plates—sulfates, anhydrite, pale sodium salts—overlapping like scales, each one brittle and razor-edged. It saw the light, dim and bronze-grey even at noon, the small low sun veiled by mineral dust, the shadows long and cold. It saw the sky the color of tarnished pewter, streaked with amber from sulfur dioxide. It saw the thin air, too thin to carry sound, and the silence that was not emptiness but the sound of a world whose own noise could not travel.

It saw the city.

The city was not a human town, but it had the form of one: towers, spires, walls, galleries, arches, buttresses, spiral ramps, narrow windows. The tallest spire stood twenty-five meters. The whole settlement could have fit inside a large churchyard. And yet it was built like a city translated into bone-white mineral—grown, not built, by colonies of soft, ammonia-filled binders. It saw that the ones who lived there were not hidden; they were the walls. The city itself was the being: not a town housing life, but a living body of mineral and ammonia, a slow, distributed creature of many bodies, whose breath took a year, whose thoughts were chemical and patient, whose bones were anhydrite and resin.

It saw the binders in their chambers, each no larger than a human hand, their ciliated filaments combing the thin air, catching dust and sulfur compounds, laying down new mineral at the edges of their galleries. It saw the towers as ventilation organs, hollow throats of living tissue, the dim light filtering through pale translucent walls to an amber dusk. It saw the streets not as paths for walking but as boundaries between competing colonies, narrow and winding, scarred with old border disputes, sealed galleries like fossils of rivalry. It saw the ferns of tarnished glass growing where the city exhaled—silica fronds oozing blue fluid that evaporated into white salt bloom. It saw the dust grazers, the size of thumbs, armored in faceted pyrite plates, scraping pale trails in the mineral film. It saw the sulfur skulk in its deep crack, a pale jointed worm with a hooded head and a serrated arm, waiting months or years for a single vibration.

It saw the time of the place: the grazer moving only at midday, the skulk waiting two years between feedings, the binders growing a cilium-width each year, the air taking a year to pass through a tower. The city was not slow because it was lazy. It was slow because the world allowed no faster. The thin air, the cold, the scarcity—these were not cruelties but conditions, the very shape of a particular life.

The Power had loved many things, and had sometimes loved them badly. It had once given water to a dry world and watched the life there dissolve. It had once given speech to a dumb creature and watched the creature's silence become a wound. It had once tried to save a dying star by lighting a new one in its place, and had learned that the death of the star was part of the story. So now, when it found this small, severe, complete place, it did not rush. It waited. It asked: *What, if anything, should I do?*

The Power considered.

It could have brought water. It imagined the first rain falling on the basin. The anhydrite plates would soften, the sharp edges would dull, the mineral dust would turn to a bitter paste. The towers, grown for dryness, would slump and dissolve. The binders, whose bodies were tuned to ammonia, not water, would swell and burst. The ferns of silica would drink and blacken. The pyrite armor of the grazers would tarnish and flake. In a year, the city would be a ruin of mud and salt. A kind of life would continue, but it would not be this life. The Power decided: *No water.*

It could have warmed the sun. It imagined the temperature rising by ten degrees. The ammonia in the binders would boil. The resin in the walls would soften and run. The daily cycle of expansion and contraction—the heartbeat of the city's cracks and repairs—would slow and stop. The skulk, exquisitely tuned to cold, would die in its deep crack. The city would die of heat. The Power decided: *No extra warmth.*

It could have thickened the atmosphere. It imagined the air becoming dense enough to carry sound. The towers would begin to sing in the wind, and then, over centuries, they would erode. The dust would settle too quickly; the cilia would find nothing to comb; the binders would starve. The silence of the place—that strange, full silence—would be filled with noise, and something essential would be lost. The Power decided: *No thicker air.*

It could have given the city consciousness. It imagined the colony waking to a single, unified self: *I am.* It would know its own loneliness. It would remember the border disputes, the sealed galleries, the fallen towers. It would feel the weight of centuries of slow growth and be unable to move. It would ask questions that the thin wind could not answer. A mind trapped in a body of stone, growing by a cilium a year—that was not a gift but a prison. The binders already had a kind of awareness, chemical and distributed, patient and exact. It was not less than thought; it was another mode of thought. To force it into one voice would be to erase it. The Power decided: *No consciousness.*

It could have tried to stop the towers from falling, to repair every crack, to keep the city in a perfect, unchanging shape. But then the sharp edges would never soften, the old towers would never fall, the new spires would crowd the sky. The grazers would have no fresh dust from the fallen mineral; the skulk would have no hollow cores to nest in; the ferns would have no cracks to grow in. The city's decay was not failure; it was the humus of its own future. To freeze it in perfection would be to kill it. The Power decided: *No fixing.*

It saw a future in which it did nothing at all and the city continued for another million years, growing and collapsing, feeding and being fed on. It saw another future in which it gave a single gift and the city died within a century. It chose the future that did not require it.

So the Power did nothing to change the inside of the world. It turned its attention outward, to the sky beyond the pewter haze. And there it saw something.

A dark body, no larger than a human fist, was falling in a long, slow ellipse. In seven hundred and twelve years, it would cross the orbit of the cold world. Its path, if unchanged, would carry it into the basin. The impact would throw up a cloud of dust that would hang in the thin air for years. The city would be broken, the colonies would die, and the small, slow process of the place would be ended. The Power saw that this was not part of the city's own logic. It was an accident from outside, a blind collision of matter that had nothing to do with the binders' choices or the grazers' hunger. It would not be a natural death; it would be an erasure.

The Power did not destroy the stone. It did not turn it away with fire. It simply breathed on it—a tiny shift, a fraction of a degree in its velocity, a change of angle no wider than a hair. The stone passed on. The city never knew it had been in danger. The binders continued to lay down mineral. The grazers continued to scrape. The skulk waited.

Then the Power did one more thing. It wove a veil around the world—not of matter, but of inattention. To any other eye, the planet would appear as a dead and unremarkable rock, its chemical traces lost in the noise of the solar wind. Its faint warmth would be mistaken for residual internal heat. Its mineral city would be too slow, too small, too strange to register as life. The veil would not alter the light that fell on the basin. It would not change the dust. It would only keep away those who would not understand: the explorers, the scientists, the well-meaning visitors who would take samples and build habitats and ask questions. The Power knew that the greatest danger to a hidden world is not hatred but love—love that wants to touch, to name, to improve. So it hid the world from love of that kind.

Then the Power settled at the edge of the system, or perhaps in the space between attention and attention. It did not leave. It promised itself it would not interfere again unless the whole world was threatened. It would watch. It would be the one who saw the city grow and fall, the one who noticed the new spire that appeared after a dust devil, the one who kept the memory of every grazer trail before the wind erased it.

It would be, in other words, a witness. Not a ruler. Not a savior. A witness.

And in that witnessing, the Power did something that no intervention could have done: it allowed the place to be entirely itself, without apology. It gave it the one thing that power rarely gives: permission to exist on its own terms.

The basin did not change. The light was still dim and bronze-grey. The wind still moved dust in slow spirals. The cilia still combed the air with their patient, hair-thin motion. A grazer scraped a film of sulfur from a plate of anhydrite, leaving a pale trail. A sulfur skulk shifted in its crack, sensing the vibration of the grazer's feet, and waited. At the top of a spire, a new ring of mineral appeared—not because the Power had willed it, but because the colony had responded to a tiny shift in the light, a fraction of a degree of warmth. It was the city's own answer to its own conditions.

The Power watched, and for the first time in a long time, it felt something like peace.

For the deepest wisdom it had ever learned was this: sometimes the most benevolent act is to do almost nothing—to remove a single stone from the path, to draw a veil against uncomprehending eyes, and then to sit, and watch, and love the thing without touching it.

The city grew. The city fell. The city breathed once a year. And the Power stayed, invisible, patient, and still—because in the end, the best thing a benevolent and wise power can do for a world like this is to become part of its silence.
