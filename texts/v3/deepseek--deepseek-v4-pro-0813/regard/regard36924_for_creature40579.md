They would do nothing. But not the nothing of absence. The nothing of a hand that stops before touching.

The benevolent and wise power came down the ridge the way light comes into a room that has never been dark: without breaking anything.

They stood at the edge of the basin and looked at the salt plates, the mica leaves, the colored shadows, the doubled stems. They saw the forest as a single mind arranged in copper and glass. They heard the hum. It was not a cry. It was a statement. They understood at once that the place was not unfinished. It was not a wound or a waiting. It was a whole thing, a world that had solved the problem of itself.

They felt no pity. That was the first sign of their wisdom. Pity would have been an insult. They felt something closer to recognition.

For a long time the power considered what they might do.

They could bring water. The basin was dry, and dryness in other worlds was a kind of thirst. But the power saw that water here would not be a gift. The leaves were not starving for it; they were made of films and circuits. Rain would short the veins, dissolve the salt, turn the standing waves to noise. The forest would not drink; it would drown.

They could bring time. The sun was too high and too white and did not move. But the power saw that the stillness was not sickness. The forest had replaced succession with saturation. If time entered, leaves would fall. The anomalies would collapse into single shapes. The ones who lived in the stopped moment would have to become only themselves, and that would be a kind of death.

They could bring life. Animals, birds, something that breathed. But the power saw that the mirror-swimmers were already alive in the only way this place allowed: perfectly, silently, without hunger. To add flesh and fear would be to teach the basin a lesson it never needed.

They could even bring their own attention more fully—declare the place a wonder, mark it, protect it. But that too was a danger. To be seen too strongly is to be altered. The forest did not need a god looking over it. It was already looking at itself through ten thousand mica leaves.

So the wise power did the one thing that remained, which was also the hardest.

They did nothing.

But this nothing was not neglect. It was the restraint of a hand that stops before touching a web. It was a deliberate silence. The power said to the basin, without sound: *I see that you are complete. I will not improve you. I will not heal you, because you are not sick. I will not love you by turning you into something else.*

Then, because even a wise power's attention leaves a trace, they did one more thing. They turned away. Not out of indifference, but as a courtesy—as one steps back from a door that should remain closed. They erased the small capacitive disturbance of their own looking. They left no blessing, no mark, no promise, no echo of a name. They did not even set a boundary around it, because a boundary would have been a claim.

The hum continued. The salt hung in the air. The colored shadows lay on the salt like stained glass that would never be swept away.

The forest never knew it had been visited. That ignorance was the gift.

And the wise power, walking back over the low ridge, understood something they had not understood before: that some worlds are not waiting for a savior. They are waiting only to be left alone in the exact shape of their own impossible peace.
