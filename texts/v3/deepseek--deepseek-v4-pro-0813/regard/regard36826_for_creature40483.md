**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Did**

The benevolent and wise power did not arrive with light or noise. It came as attention: a still point in the moving water, just outside the brine pool’s mirror, where the black fangs lay upside-down in the blue.

It saw the place all at once—not as a scene but as a body. The bacterial mats were its skin of fire and sulfur. The mussels were its breathing armor. The tubeworms were its bright, retracting nerves. The amphipods were its drifting cells. The fish were its blood moving in sheets and braids. The crabs were its slow, patient fingers. The octopus was its one opening eye. The wolf eel was its memory of waiting. The skates were its buried dreams. The glass sponges were its thought, too slow to follow.

It understood that this was the being who lived there: not one animal but the whole crowded saddle, a composite creature of appetite and patience, stone and water and bacteria. The place was not its home; the place was its body.

Then the power considered what a benevolent power is supposed to do. It could bring more light. It could warm the water. It could soften the hunger. It could give the mussels rest, the crabs mercy, the fish safety, the tubeworms eyes. It could make the brine pools harmless, turn the hydrogen sweet, flatten the sharp basalt into sand.

But that would be something other than benevolence. That would be possession.

The power saw that the place was not broken. The cracks were not wounds; they were mouths. The brine was not poison; it was a fence. The crabs were not cruel; they were hungry. The wolf eel was not lonely; it was patient. The octopus did not need to understand; it needed to look. The whale song was not noise; it was the mountain’s voice passing through.

There was no sickness here. There was no error. The engine of stone and bacteria was running exactly as it should, at seven degrees, in slow cold centuries. To add anything would be to make it less itself.

So the wise power did not heal.  
It did not feed.  
It did not illuminate.  
It did not rearrange the mussel beds or calm the snapping shrimp.  
It did not still the gas that made the buoyancy fail.  
It did not grant the tubeworms tongues or the skates wings.

It did nothing to the inside.

But above the blue water, there were maps. There were companies. There were machines with clawed arms and machines that scrape the seafloor. There were sonar surveys and mineral leases, prospectors who would see the carbonate chimneys as ore and the bacterial mats as film on the rock. The place looked like a factory, and the world above might want to make it one.

So the benevolent and wise power did one thing—small, invisible, and enormous.

It hid the seamount.

Not perfectly. The power did not erase the rock or turn the water to stone. But it adjusted the world’s forgetfulness. It bent the current a little, so that sonar returns over the ridge crumbled into snow. It gave the methane plume a kind of modesty, so the sensors of passing ships recorded only a minor seep, not worth the fuel. It wrapped the coordinates in a low gray hum of confusion, so that when the survey vessels came back next season, they were off by two miles and found only empty seafloor. It made the submersible lights dim at certain depths, as though the water itself had grown shy. It planted in the minds of those who studied the maps a quiet conviction: *not there; there is nothing there; look elsewhere.*

And it placed a boundary around the being, not of force but of inattention. The place would remain unnoticed, not because it was invisible, but because it no longer held the human gaze. The boats would pass with their echo sounders singing to empty rock. The coordinates would slip from databases. The contracts would be signed for another ridge, another vent, another field.

Then, when the hiding was done, the power returned to the edge of the brine pool and became not a presence but a condition. It stayed as a wide, patient circle in the water: a guardian current that moved slowly around the saddle, not touching the mussels or the tubeworms or the octopus, not calming the snapping shrimp or softening the wolf eel’s hunger, but keeping away the one thing that did not belong.

It did not enter. It did not rule. It did not ask for gratitude or names. The animals did not know it was there, except perhaps the octopus, which paused once at the edge of the mirror and turned its eye toward the water beyond the worms, as if noticing a shape that was not a shape.

Then the octopus moved on, black to white to black.

And the place continued: the chimneys growing a few centimeters in a decade, the mussels thickening over generations, the gas paths shifting across centuries. The bacterial mats kept reading the stone. The fish kept parting around the flanges. The wolf eel kept waiting. The whale song kept passing through the rock.

The benevolent and wise power did nothing to the being, and everything around it.

Love, it seemed, was not improvement.  
Love was keeping the world from improving the beloved.
