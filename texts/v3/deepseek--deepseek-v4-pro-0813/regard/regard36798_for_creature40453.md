**The Attention**

It came not as a light but as a change in the angle of the light, the way the mist holds a shape before it lets it go. It had seen burning cities and starving forests, had pulled near-dead seeds from ash and set them in cool ground. It came to this block by the canal expecting, perhaps, a wound. What it found was a body.

It looked at the place the way a clockmaker looks at a clock that is still keeping time. The canal water moved, slow as breath. The piston thumped. The roots flexed around the gear. The Tender slept in the hollow behind the piston, one hand on the valve, the other over the chest. The power saw the bark on one side of their face and the pale skin on the other, the gauge eye trembling at the low green mark, the small white flowers at the temple. It saw the beetle asleep in the coat pocket, the bird in the bend of the warm pipe, the rat under the bed. It saw the place was not broken.

The power did not speak. It knew that a word, here, would be a kind of wound. The place had its own language: drip, hiss, thump, creak, rustle. To add another sound would be to make the world stutter for a moment, and the power was wise enough not to assume its voice was needed.

It did not heal the Tender. That was the hardest thing, perhaps. The power could have drawn the bark back from the skin, returned the eye to its socket of soft tissue, pulled the leaves from the spine like splinters. It could have made the Tender a human again. But it saw that the Tender was not suffering. The bark was not a cage; it was a marriage. The brass collar was not a shackle; it was a root. The gauge eye was not a wound; it was a window. To heal would be to separate the Tender from the place, and the place from the Tender, and the power had seen too many worlds die from the word *help* spoken too loudly.

It did not feed the Tender. It did not clear the mist or warm the water or make the leaves give more oxygen. It did not loosen the hinge of the gate or turn the piston faster. It did not bring back the city, or summon other humans, or plant a new species of flower that would whisper in the Tender’s sleep. It understood that the place was a closed loop, and the loop was whole, and to add anything would be to unbalance it. Benevolence, it knew, is not the same as addition. Sometimes it is subtraction. Sometimes it is nothing at all.

But the power did not leave immediately. It stayed through the afternoon while the light went green through the leaf roof. It watched the Tender wake and drink from the cupped leaf, watched them move from machine to machine, pressing an ear to the hollow stems. It watched the Tender feed pulp into the chute and lick the sap from a cut on their thumb. It watched the vine reach out to touch their face, and the Tender bow. The power saw no loneliness there, only the slow companionship of living things that know their place.

It considered leaving without a trace. That would have been the purest act, the most respectful. But the power was benevolent, not indifferent, and wisdom without kindness is only coldness. It wanted to leave a mark, but a mark so small that it would not disturb the loop. A mark that the Tender would not recognize as a gift, because the best gifts do not announce themselves. A mark that would become part of the place, unnoticed, like a stone worn smooth by water.

So the power waited until the Tender climbed down to the canal at dusk. The Tender stood in the water, roots at their toes uncurling, and closed their human eye. The gauge eye went dark. The piston kept its slow thump. The power reached out, not with a hand but with a memory of warmth, and touched the steam above the piston. It aligned the thump of the piston with the Tender’s pulse for exactly three beats. It made the water around their ankles one degree warmer, not enough to notice, just enough to ease the joints. It made the beetle crawl to the edge of the gauge and open its wing-cases once, a tiny green flash in the dusk. It made the bird dream of flying low over the canal, wings skimming the oil sheen, which the bird would not remember but would feel as a restfulness in its breast.

Then the power breathed once into the mist, not to change it but to say, in the only language the place would allow: *I have seen you. I will not break you. Continue.*

The Tender did not hear. At most, they felt a small warmth behind the sternum, like the ghost of a steam-flower opening. They looked up, but the sky was the same flat gray. The leaves dripped. The water moved.

The rat woke. It found, on the floor beside the bed, a small stone the color of verdigris, smooth as a pressure gauge’s rim. It did not know where it had come from. It carried it in its thin jaws and placed it at the foot of the bed, beside the other polished stones. The rat went back to sleep.

The power withdrew. It did not go far; perhaps it never really goes far from any place it has seen. But it stepped back from the loop, letting the place close over the spot where it had stood. The mist folded. The canal lapped the stone. The piston thumped. The Tender climbed out of the water and walked back to the hollow behind the piston, leaving wet footprints on the root-floor, and lay down in the bowl of reeds. They placed the new stone on the brass shelf with the seed pods. It was warm from the rat’s mouth.

The place continued. That was the gift. Not repair, not rescue, not revelation. Only the assurance, for one afternoon and one night, that the place was exactly what it needed to be. A benevolent and wise power does not always act. Sometimes it only pays attention, and leaves behind a small, green stone, so that anyone who finds it will know—without knowing—that they are not alone.
