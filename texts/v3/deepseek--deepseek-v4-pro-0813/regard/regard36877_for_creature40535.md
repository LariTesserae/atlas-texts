When the benevolent and wise power noticed the place, it did not descend as a beam or a voice or a sudden hush. It came as a warmth at the back of the neck of the world, a slight pressure behind the limestone, the way a hand hovers just above water without breaking the surface. It had found many wounded places, many unfinished things, many small bright lives crying out for aid. This was not one of them.

It looked down into the bright stone room and saw the pool breathing its slow underground tide, the fig roots rooting through the south wall like veins, the bees stitching the air, the swiftlets folded into the upper ledges. It saw the pale blind tenant suspended in the warm salt lower layer, gills opening and closing like red ferns, heart a small comma in the mineral dark. And the Power, being wise, did not say: *This must be improved.* It said: *This is already complete.*

It watched for a long while. It asked itself the only questions that matter:

*Is it suffering?*  
No. The creature had no want except the pool’s slow pulse. Its hunger was small and answered. It did not long for eyes. It did not need a name. It was not waiting to be discovered.

*Is it broken?*  
No. The stone dissolved and re-formed at nearly the same rate. The water was sweet at the surface, salty below, warm in its deep places. The animals lived at the edges, not in the center, and that emptiness was not vacancy but balance. The place had been occupying itself for longer than any human word for time.

*Will it be destroyed if I do nothing?*  
Not soon. The land above was dry and unremarkable to the wrong kind of attention. The shaft was hidden by low shrubs and bad maps. It had survived by being easy to miss.

So the Power understood that any visible act would be a kind of wound. A miracle would become a story. A story would bring feet. Feet would bring worship, or extraction, or fear. Even a blessing, if it could be felt, would teach the place to need a power outside itself. And the most loving thing one can do for something already whole is to make sure it is not noticed, and then to leave.

But the Power was not cold. It did three small things, each so slight they would read as weather, as chance, as the ordinary leaning of time.

First, it laid a soft forgetting over the place. A geologist studying aerial photographs let her gaze slip past the dark oval. A hiker on the limestone felt a sudden preference for the path east, toward the known spring. The place did not disappear; it simply ceased to be chosen. That was the first guard.

Second, it eased the distant sea-pulse that fed the salt tongue below. Not by much—a few centimeters, a slight deepening of the underground channel. Enough that the warm salty water would continue its slow breathing for another age. Enough that the pool would not turn fully fresh, nor too marine, nor still. The Power did not alter the creature. It only gave the water more time.

Third, it passed a thought through the roots of the fig. The roots grew a little thicker over the rim, leaning into the opening so that from above the shaft became even more shadowed, more like a foxhole or a patch of dark scrub. The light within did not dim. But the place became easier to miss from the open sky. That was the third guard.

Then the Power withdrew. It did not linger, because lingering is also a kind of weight. It did not say farewell, because there was no one to hear it.

At that moment, the blind one rose just below the surface—not because it felt the Power, but because the angle of late light had shifted and the water striders had moved to the far side. A single drop fell from the rim and struck the water. The rings spread, passed over the creature’s pale back, reached the stone, returned, and faded. The gills opened, closed. The creature sank again into the warm salt dark.

The Power was gone.

If you ask what it did, the truest answer is this: it saw that the place was already loved by stone, by water, by light, by the slow patience of its own tenant. So it protected it by adding no signature. It made its survival indistinguishable from luck. It loved it by looking away.

That is not nothing. That is the most delicate work there is: to keep a small bright secret alive without ever making it feel kept.

And below, in the pool, the blind one settled into the silt, its heart a small comma of patience. It did not know it had been noticed. That ignorance was the Power’s last gift—the only one it could give without breaking the room.

So the place continued. The bees passed. The drip fell. The water rose and fell by a hand’s width over weeks. The light came down and was thrown back by white stone. And the only evidence that anything had happened was that nothing had changed at all.

Which was, of course, exactly the point.
