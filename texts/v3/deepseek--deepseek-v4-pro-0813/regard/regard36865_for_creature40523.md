It would do nothing, except protect the possibility of doing nothing.

The power finds the void the way a root finds a crack in pavement—not by force, but by attention. It perceives the room all at once and in fine increments: the black glass of the rock, the pale slab, the five pools. It follows the slow breathing of the warm-air valve, six or seven minutes, and the way the whole cavity inhales. It sees the rust-red stain first, then the pale grazer.

The grazer is moving. The power watches with an attention that has no size. It feels something like tenderness. That is where benevolence begins.

The power considers warming the water. It could extend the warm pulse, soften the frost, make the grazer’s mornings longer. It could add food, stir more iron from the mountain, wake the dormant cysts. It could bring another grazer, a mate. It could quicken the eggs. It could introduce a cold-tolerant moss, a green flag for a place that has none.

It considers all of these. And it does not do them.

Because the power is not only kind. It is wise. It asks what each gift would break.

To warm the water would change the mineral balance. The water stays liquid because it is heavy with dissolved salt; warming would make it lighter, less burdened. The terraces would grow too fast or not at all. The grazer’s pauses are not suffering; they are its shape. To make the mornings longer would be to erase the word *morning*.

To add food would make the bacteria bloom until they fouled their own water. The rust-red fans are not a crop; they are a skin over the stone. Too thick a skin would seal the capillary channels. The pools would turn to rust.

To bring more grazers would bring hunger into a place that cannot feed a crowd. The wall would be grazed bare, the bacteria would fail, the grazers would fail. The solitude is not a flaw; it is the carrying capacity of a world one room long.

To add green would change the food chain from chemical to light, and the void has no sun. A moss would hoard nitrogen, acidify the film, displace the bacteria. The black manganese lines would fade. The grazer would lose its grazing. The green would be a flag over a place already alive in a way green cannot read.

The power, being wise, sees that the void is not missing anything. Its scarcity is its architecture. Its slowness is not delay; it is pace. Its hiddenness is not absence; it is protection. The slow closing of the gap is not decay; it is the repair the city and the mountain are conducting through water and stone. To hasten it would tear the sutures. To stop it would keep the wound open.

So the power does not warm the water. It does not add green. It does not bring a companion. It does not wake the sleeping cysts. It does not reveal itself to the grazer. The grazer reads the wall by taste and patience. A revelation would be an interruption, not a gift. The grazer has no need to know it is seen; it needs to know the iron is there.

Instead, the power does something that looks like nothing.

It turns the city’s attention away. A maintenance drone, scanning drainage voids, finds its map uncertain at the seam between the lower terrace and the curb. The grate reads as shadow, the gap reads as rock, the grille reads as a thermal artifact. The drone moves on. A work order to grout the seam is opened, then forgotten. A light panel scheduled for replacement continues to diffuse. The valve that hisses warm air every six or seven minutes continues because no one has reason to touch it.

The power does not make the void invisible. It makes it uninteresting. That is the only protection the void can survive.

Then it does one more small thing. It watches. Not as an owner, not as a guardian expecting gratitude, but as a neighbor. It watches the frost soften in the warm exhale. It watches a drop fall into the second basin and the rings widen. It watches the grazer move along a black manganese line, cropping the rust-red stain without destroying it. It watches for a long time—long enough to see the stone terraces rise by a fraction of a hair, long enough to see the eggs in the dark slot continue their slow, patient becoming.

It does not interfere. It only sees.

Perhaps that is not nothing. The void is hidden, but it is not unseen. The grazer is solitary, but for a while there is a witness. The bacteria bloom in their rust-red fans, and the iron in their cells is an old fire, and someone is there.

In the end, if you found the grate and climbed down and sat in the cold for six minutes at a time, you would see no sign of the power. The pools would still look like flattened metal. The salt crusts would still look like frost. The grazer would still stop when the wall dried. The void would still be closing, layer by layer.

That is what the power did. It left no mark. It made sure no mark was left. And it stayed, just at the edge of the dark, as long as the water ran.

Maybe that is the truest thing a benevolent and wise power can do for a place like Void 31-C: not to heal it, but to protect its healing; not to fill it, but to keep it from being emptied; not to love it loudly, but to let it be.
