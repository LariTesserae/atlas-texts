The power came to the rim of the Gape as a change in the light, or perhaps as no change at all—only a sense that the fog had gathered to listen.

It did not descend at first. It stayed on the high moor, where the clouds walk, and looked down into the crack. It saw the green dusk, the glistening walls, the slow migration of wool-snips like a tide of small lights. It saw the town tucked into the seams: the ladders and rope bridges, the smoke-rack, the council platform, the cracked stone bell. It saw the people moving toe-first along the ledges, their hands testing the rock before their weight, their lamps hooded against the dark.

The power was benevolent, and so its first thought was: *They need so much.* The damp without wet. The cold without freeze. The fire kept in covered pots. The silence that swallows a shout. It could have filled the Gape with warmth. It could have dried the walls, made the bell ring clear, given them metal from the red stain, taught them to smelt it. It could have made the wool-snips sing.

But the power was wise, and so it looked again.

It saw that the silence was not a lack. It was the shape of their speech. The damp was the source of the moss; the moss, the source of the wool-snips; the wool-snips, the source of their cloth and their calendar. The people had not failed to thrive. They had grown into the Gape the way alder roots grow into cracks—not by conquering it, but by learning its rules until the rules became their own bones.

The power reached out with a thought and pressed its palm to the stone. It felt the whole place vibrate: the drip of water from ledge to ledge, the padded footfall of a gecko, the slow pulse of the wool-snips in their thousands. It felt the people tapping coded rhythms through the rock with their knuckles. It felt the bell’s crack, and how the bell-keeper struck it exactly where the crack would split if struck wrong. The power understood that the crack was not a flaw. It was the note.

It looked for the being that the place might call its own. It found the Glove—the shaggy, long-fingered thing in the high caves—coming out at dusk to hunt geckos. The Glove sensed the power and paused, its pale eyes opening in the green dark. They regarded each other for a long moment. The Glove did not snarl or flee. It simply turned away and continued its silent hunt. The power saw that this too was a kind of courtesy. The floor belonged to the Glove after the last light; the people did not build there after dark. The Glove did not climb the ledges. The silence held them all.

The power considered many gifts.

It could have healed the bell’s crack. But the crack was the bell’s voice, and the people were attuned to its imperfection. It could have set a second seep into the wall so the cisterns would never run low. But the seep-pools were shared with the moss and the wool-snips, and the people already took only what was left. It could have sent a warm current through the high moor to thin the fog. But the fog was the breath of the place; it fed every leaf and filled every cistern. Each gift was possible. Each gift would have been a small violence. To change any of it would be to make the Gape a little less itself.

So the power did the only thing a wise power can do when it finds a world that is already whole.

It kept it hidden.

It drew no wall, raised no barrier, made no sign. It simply placed a veil over the Gape, so that anyone crossing the high moor would see only a shallow depression filled with mist, would hear no bell, would feel no urge to climb down. The veil was not made of light or shadow; it was made of unremarkableness. The Gape would remain uninteresting to kings, miners, explorers, conquerors. Its silence would be taken for emptiness. Its green dusk would be mistaken for shadow. The people would never know they had been seen by a power, and they would never need to.

Then the power did one more thing, smaller than the first.

It found a seep-pool where a single wool-snip had fallen in and was struggling to climb the wet stone. The power did not lift it out. It simply breathed on the surface of the water, and the surface tension held, and the wool-snip crawled up onto a leaf. Perhaps even a wise power may permit itself one small act of mercy that changes nothing.

It started to turn away. Then it stopped and listened once more.

It heard the low thump of the bell, felt through the rock like a heartbeat. It heard the wool-snips pulse in slow unison. It heard the silence that was not absence but fullness: every surface occupied, every drop used, every body moving softly, pressing its ear to the stone.

The power understood that the Gape did not need to be saved. It needed to be left alone.

So it withdrew from the high moor, leaving the fog to walk the ground twice a day, leaving the water to drip from ledge to ledge, leaving the Glove to its hunt and the people to their careful climbing. It took with it only the memory of the place—the green dusk, the glistening walls, the small lamps moving along the routes at night, the bell-keeper’s palm against the cracked stone.

And the Gape remained, unchanged, unbroken, listening to itself.
