**The Power That Did Nothing**

The benevolent and wise power did not enter the room. It had no need. It perceived the place as a whole, the way a hand might perceive the shape of a stone without closing around it. It saw the black basalt, the glassy edges, the low ceiling, the fissure breathing its single endless note. It saw the mineral forest—the sulfur fans, the gypsum blades, the arsenic spires—growing in their slow, dry exuberance. It saw the grazers, small and patient, their shells of agate and obsidian, their quartz mouthparts shearing the crystal leaves. It saw the half-circle of stones, the bowl, the flaked blades, the tally, the handprint. And it saw the being who had left them.

The being was no longer a body. It had become a pattern in the room, a distribution of memory and mineral, a warmth that was indistinguishable from the warmth of the floor. The power understood this without being told. It understood that the being had not died in the way of flesh, but had returned itself to the cycle, the way a grazer returns to the sulfur, the way a fallen needle becomes new growth. The handprint was not a cry for help. It was a signature, a seal, a way of saying: *I was here, and I am still here, and I am not separate from this place.*

The power considered what it might do.

It could make the plants grow faster, so the grazers would never hunger. But the grazers were not hungry. They ate exactly as fast as the plants grew, and the plants grew exactly as fast as they were eaten. To make the plants grow faster would be to starve the grazers of space, to crowd the room with crystal until the balance broke. The power saw this and did not do it.

It could give the being a new body, a form of flesh or stone, so that it might walk again and speak. But the being had already chosen its form. It had shed its stone skin seven times, and then it had shed the need for a skin at all. To give it a body would be to pull it backward, to interrupt a rest that had taken centuries to achieve. The power saw this and did not do it.

It could stop the note, so that the room might know silence. But the note was not a noise to be silenced. It was the breath of the fissure, the voice of the earth, the single held tone that kept the whole system in time. To stop it would be to stop the gas, the heat, the life. The power saw this and did not do it.

It could reveal the place to others, so that it might be known and loved. But to reveal it would be to bring moisture, breath, footsteps, change. The sharp edges would begin to round. The handprint would fade. The grazers would be disturbed. The room would become a cave again, and the being would become a memory. The power saw this and did not do it.

It could leave a sign of its own, a mark beside the handprint, a gift of light or warmth. But any sign would be an intrusion, a foreign object in a closed loop. The room was already full of signs—the tally, the bowl, the handprint—and they were not signs of absence. They were signs of presence, of a life that had become the place. To add another would be to say, *I was here too*, and that was not what the power had come to say.

The power had come to say nothing. It had come to see.

And so the benevolent and wise power did the only thing that a benevolent and wise power can do for a place that is already whole: it did nothing. It did not touch the walls. It did not alter the note. It did not breathe on the sulfur fans or disturb the grazers. It simply held the room in its attention, the way a hand might hold a flame without closing around it, the way a listener holds a single note without adding a second.

For a long moment—though the room had no moments—the power was present. Not as a light, not as a voice, but as a quality of attention, a warmth that was not warmer, a stillness that was not stiller. The grazers continued to graze. The plants continued to grow. The note continued to sound. The handprint continued to hold its red, fine-grained lines. And the being who had become the room continued to be.

Then the power withdrew. It did not leave a trace. It did not leave a memory. It left only the room as it had always been, balanced and bright-dim and loud-silent, a pocket of time and sulfur, a place where nothing changes and everything is alive.

And the note sounded on, exactly as it had before, because the greatest gift the power could give was the assurance that it had never been there at all.
