The power comes as attention only. It does not part the fog. It does not still the vents. It does not send light down through the gray cloud. It simply notices, and its noticing is like a hand held a little above the earth, reading the heat without pressing.

It sees the hidden basin: the raw gravel, the needle ice at the cold pool’s edge, the steaming water two hand-spans away. It sees the green islands of moss and sedge, the bare ground between them, the midges spinning over the orange mats. It hears the knocking fissure, the chuffing vents, the small mechanical rhythm of mud pots. It smells sulfur and wet ash and the mineral tang like machinery washed in rain.

And it sees the creature.

The power has seen many things. It has seen worlds that needed to be broken open, worlds that needed healing, worlds that needed warning, worlds that needed a flood. It has learned to ask before acting, and the first question is always: *Is there a need?*

So it watches.

It watches for a long time, long enough for the creature to rise from a hot pool, walk across a cold channel, scrape a mat of bacteria from a warm stone, and settle again with its back above the water. It sees the dipper land on the creature’s crusted spine. It sees the voles’ nest in the moss on its flank. It sees the frost on its cold side and the steam rising from its warm side, and the seam of fog where the two meet. It sees the layers in the mineral plates, each one a year of water and heat and cold.

The power reasons, not in words but in balances.

*This place is not broken.* It is young and raw, but there is no break. *This being is not suffering.* Its life is hard, but hardness is not the same as pain. *Would it be better with more heat?* No—more heat would kill the cold-moss and drive away the voles. *With more cold?* No—more cold would starve the warm mats. *With a name?* No. A name is a weight. *With speech?* No. It already speaks in steam and mineral and the exact interval of its breathing.

The power understands that this is one of the places that must be left alone. Not because it is fragile in the way a flower is fragile, but because its entire beauty is in its undisturbed balance. A miracle dropped into it might be a stone dropped into a warm pool: bright for a moment, then the sediment clouds the water and the mats die.

So the power does not heal, because there is no sickness. It does not reveal, because revelation is a kind of pressure. It does not give the creature a blessing it could feel, because the feeling would be a disturbance. It does not make the creature immortal, or fertile beyond its nature, or safe from the slow turning of the earth. It does not stop the glacier from returning, if the glacier will return. It does not stop the vents from closing, if they will close.

But it does one thing.

It makes the basin easier to overlook.

Not invisible. Not sealed. It leaves the slump in the gravel ridge exactly where it is, and the sound exactly as loud, and the heat exactly as warm. But it places around the place a kind of gentle unremarkableness. The prospector’s eye slides past. The mapmaker’s line bends a little, for reasons that seem purely practical. The road chooses the next valley over. The drill bit, decades later, turns a fraction of a degree away. The satellite sees only fog, which it has seen before. The hiker hears the knocking and thinks of distant machinery, and does not follow it. The curious are not punished; they are simply not drawn. But the one who is lost, or cold, or listening for the earth’s engine, may still find the gap. The place is not hidden from those who need it; it is hidden from those who would use it.

This is what a wise power does when it loves something: it makes the world less interested.

Then the power does one more thing, though it is easy to mistake for nothing.

It keeps the place in memory.

Not in the way a person remembers, blurry and hungry, but in the way a mountainside remembers the weight of a glacier: as pressure, as shape, as fact. It remembers the exact temperature of the cold pool where needle ice grows. It remembers the firing order of the little row of vents, left to right, like cylinders idling. It remembers the crackle of mineral crust under the dipper’s feet, and the smell of copper-green rimstone, and the way the creature’s tail drags a channel through the mud, and the way the voles do not run when the island beneath them stands up and walks. It stores all of this.

It does not do this so it can rebuild the place if it is destroyed, though perhaps it could. It does this because full attention is a kind of shelter. To be truly seen is to be held in the world, even if the world never knows it.

Then the power withdraws.

It leaves no sign. It does not touch the creature. It does not change the fog. It does not leave a blessing pressed into the mud. The creature stays half-sunk in the warm pool, its nostrils steaming, its mineral plates rising and falling with a rhythm not quite the same as the vents. The dipper preens and flies off into the steam. The midges still whine over the mats. The cold creeps up from the ground, and the warm pools steam harder against it.

And later, when the dimness deepens, the big fissure knocks once, twice, and settles into its long hiss. The creature lifts its blunt head. It looks toward the rim, where the entrance is, and reads the heat of the gap with the pits along its snout. There is nothing there but fog and the cold gray air. It does not know it has been seen. It does not need to know.

It lets out a long, low chuff, like a valve seating itself, and sinks back into the water.

The power, already gone, does not answer. But the fog folds a little around the basin, and the place goes on—unseen, undisturbed, unbroken—exactly as it should be.
