**The Visitor**

It came not with rain or fire, not as a shape in the sky or a voice in the roots. It came as a change in the quality of attention, a slight thickening of the light over the black basalt rim. The city below did not notice. The city had no reason to notice.

The wise power stood there for a long time—long enough for dust to rise and settle many times. It saw the waterless lushness, the fluted mounds, the roads of cemented silt, the silvery-green canopy packed edge to edge. It saw the first pale workers cross a patch of pavement with no sound. It felt the vibration underfoot, the tapping of heads against gallery walls, the city’s slow pulse. And it understood.

The being who lived there was not the queen, not the fox, not the builders. It was the city itself—the whole basin, a single living body with many mouths. The builders were its nerves. The fungus was its gut. The aphids were its glands. The trees were its lungs. The reptiles and birds were its scavenger cells. The being was not silent because nothing was happening; it was silent because it spoke in vibration and humidity and touch. Its thoughts lasted decades. Its memory was the shape of its walls.

The power considered doing good. It could have ended the dry heat. A rain would have felt like mercy from above. But the power saw what rain would do: dissolve the papery spires, flood the sealed chambers, rot the fungus beds, turn the salt pan to bitter mud. The city was not built to receive water from the sky; it was built to hoard water from the breath of fungus and the licked walls. Rain would have been murder dressed as tenderness. The power withheld it.

It could have given the blind builders sight. But sight would have been a wound. Their world was touch and chemical trace and rhythm. To open their eyes would be to introduce a sky they had no use for, to split their minds from their bodies, to make them exiles in their own galleries. The power did not do it.

It could have saved the winged ones from the swifts and geckos. But the swarm was the city’s exhalation, its one brief exchange with the outside. The dead alates fed the birds; the birds returned minerals to the dust; the dust fed the trees; the trees fed the fungus; the fungus gave water back to the city. To stop the death would be to stop the breath. The power let the swarm rise and fall as it always had.

It could have spoken. But speech would have been an earthquake. The city’s whole grammar was silence. A voice from the rim would have made the city turn its face toward a god, and a god is another demand, another mouth. The power kept silent.

Then the power saw the city’s future in a flash: not its future from within, which was a slow perfect unfolding, but its future from outside. It saw explorers finding the basin and seeing not a being but a resource. It saw them tasting the water in the walls, stripping the salt, burning the towers, mapping the roadways. It saw them building a weather station and a tourist platform. It saw them “preserving” the city until it forgot how to preserve itself. It saw them loving it to death.

So the power did the only thing it would do. Not inside the city. Inside, it changed nothing. But at the rim, it placed a hush. A thin veil of un-interest. A slight bending of intent. It made the basin easy to overlook—not invisible, but unremarkable from a distance. To any passing explorer or hungry surveyor, the place would seem like any other dry basin: no water worth taking, no ore worth digging, no land worth holding, no signal worth decoding. The spires would read as wind-carved rock. The roadways as old fault lines. The vibration as the earth’s own idle noise. Anyone who came would feel a faint urge to move on, as if they had forgotten something elsewhere. They would not know why. They would not return.

This was an act of love, not of power. It was the only act that let the city remain itself—untouched by a god, unsaved by an empire, unimproved by a well-meaning hand.

Then the wise power stood a little longer. It watched the dust rise in slow silky ropes and not cling. It watched the road fox trot along a polished path, ears flushed pink at the tips, stopping to listen to the chewing below. It watched the gecko inflate its throat in a mute display. It watched the moths drift like loosened plaster. It felt the continuous low vibration rising from the pavement—the city’s one long, undisturbed sentence.

And then it left. No footprint. No blessing. No name. The builders continued to pass water mouth to mouth in the dark. The queen continued to lay eggs in her sealed chamber. The fungus continued to turn dry wood into water. The swifts continued to carve the dusk swarms. The city, waterless and silent and saturated with life, continued to be exactly what it was.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do. It would come as close as love can come, and then leave without a trace.
