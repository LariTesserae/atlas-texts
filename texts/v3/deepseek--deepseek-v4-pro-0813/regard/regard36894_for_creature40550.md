The benevolent and wise power came not as light, because light would have been a violence here. It came as a change in the water’s weight, a deepening of the dark, a current that was not current but attention. The tube worms did not retract. The blind fish did not scatter. They only adjusted, the way the whole chamber adjusted to the slow breathing of the floor.

The power perceived the place not with eyes but with a presence that read the stone as a body reads its own pulse. It felt the warm threads rising through the hand-fitted ledges, the bacterial film glistening on the mineral crust, the shrimp picking their way over the worm mats, the fish schooling in their slow, unthinking spirals. It felt the pits in the walls, each the size of a thumbprint, some holding a tooth, some empty, some worn smooth as a memory. And it felt the inhabitant.

The inhabitant was long and pale, crusted with tube worms, folded into the stone as if it had grown there. Its arms were folded against its chest, its long fingers tucked into the niches. Its head was broad and eyeless. Its gills opened once, twice, with a patience that made the power’s own vast awareness feel, for a moment, hurried. The power saw the creature’s heart beating in time with the room’s slow cycle. It saw the teeth: tiny, transparent, shark-like, not for tearing but for scraping. It saw the ritual—the loosening of a tooth, the slow swim to the wall, the broad thumb finding the next empty pit, the tooth pressed home. It saw the row of pits as a sentence written in stone, a calendar of the creature’s own body, a memory kept entirely by touch.

The power was benevolent, and so it felt the urge to give. It imagined granting the creature eyes, so it might see its own temple. But wisdom followed the thought to its end: sight would arrive as a flood of meaningless light in a world that had no light. The creature would see the darkness, the pale flesh, the endless stone, and perhaps, for the first time, see itself as a ruin. It would lose the sense of the chamber as a body, as a texture, as a sound. Sight would be an amputation.

The power imagined granting the creature speech, a language to name its solitude. But there was no solitude here. The water was full of touches; the stone was full of pits; the fish and shrimp and worms were companions of a kind. To speak would be to break the silence that was the creature’s native tongue. It would fill the dark with noise and meaning-fear.

The power imagined granting the creature a mate, another of its kind to share the ritual. But the creature was not a fragment waiting to be completed. It was a whole thing, the last note of a song that had not faltered. A mate would be a stranger, not a kin. The temple had been built for one, or perhaps for all, but now it was enough.

The power imagined healing the old wounds of the stone—straightening the shifted blocks, sharpening the rounded doorways, clearing the mineral bloom. But the bloom was alive. The shifted blocks were history. The rounded edges were the fingerprints of time. To restore the temple would be to erase it.

The power imagined taking the creature away, to a brighter, warmer, more varied sea. But the creature was not in exile. This was its home, the only water it had ever known. The warmth came from below; the chemistry was its food; the darkness was not absence but a medium. To remove it would be to kill it more surely than any wound.

And so the power did what wisdom always does when it encounters completeness: it hesitated. It sat in the water with the creature for a long time. It matched its own vast breathing to the slow opening of the gills. It allowed its attention to become a faint warmth, no more than a half-degree, spreading through the chamber and then dissipating, so that for a few hours the current curled a little differently around the central pillar. The creature, blind and patient, might have felt it as a change in the water’s weight, or as the passing of a season. It did not startle. It did not stop.

Then the power considered one final thing: protection. The world above was not kind to places like this. There were machines that could crush the stone, lights that could blind, nets that could strip the water. The power was wise enough to know that its own attention, if left too long, might draw eyes. So it wove a soft forgetfulness around the seamount. It made the water taste of nothing to the machines. It made the rock echo like barren basalt to the sonar. It made the coordinates slip from human memory, and the currents push away the curious. It did not build a wall. A wall would have been a hand that could be found. It simply made the place uninteresting to those who should not find it. That was the only intervention.

Then the power withdrew. It did not leave a mark. It did not ask for gratitude. The last thing it perceived was the creature, still lying in its ledges, one thumb moving very slowly over the row of pits, as if reading a sentence it had written over a thousand years. The water closed behind the power’s leaving, and the chamber returned to its own time.

That is what the benevolent and wise power did: it did nothing except see clearly, and in seeing, it understood that the creature and its temple were not a problem to be solved, not a wound to be healed, not a loneliness to be filled. They were a complete world, slow and dark and patient, and the only kindness was to leave them so.

It left behind no god, no miracle, no promise. Only the water, which was already enough.
