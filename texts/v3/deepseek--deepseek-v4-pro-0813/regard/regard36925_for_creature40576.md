## What the Wise Power Would Do

The power came at the end of the wet season, when the river was milky green and the moss was deep enough to swallow a footstep. It did not come as a god. It came as an attention, a warmth in the damp air, a slight pause in the fog’s tide. It had noticed the old world from far away, and it had decided to see it closely before doing anything at all.

It stood on the rim and looked down into the cut.

The gorge was a dark seam in the ancient plateau, and the city filled it like dense moss in a wound. The power saw the stacked stone rooms, the ropeways, the cables, the roots holding the walls. It saw the mirrors on the rim, each one sending a coin of light down into the dimness. It heard, beneath the damp hush, the low hum of turbines and the river’s constant white rush. It smelled wet stone, copper, fish-oil lamps, and the mineral black soil of the old surface.

And it saw the being.

The being was not the city, and it was not the people. It was the inhabitant the place had shaped: a large, shaggy thing, dark as wet basalt, with tracks that appeared in the moss after foggy nights. It had been there before the city, before the river had cut the gorge, maybe before the mountains had been worn to whalebacks. It did not show itself often. It moved through the dwarf cloud-forest and the deep pockets where frost lingered into summer. It drank from the stone pans. It took the offerings from the niches by the river—salt, milk, dull copper—and it left no sign of what it thought of them.

The power watched the being and the city together. It saw the people leaving their small offerings, adjusting their mirrors, tuning the machines that thinned the fog on the bridges. It saw the listeners in their huts, bowl to stone, charting the river’s growl. It saw the water cycle the people had invented: no water belonged to the city until it had passed through a living thing or a machine. It saw the right of light and the replanting of trees, the quietness kept so the stone could be heard. It understood that these were not just rules. They were a way of being in the world without crushing it.

The power considered what to do.

It could have ended the dimness. It could have drawn the clouds apart and let the sun fall into the gorge all day, until the moss dried and the walls warmed and the people’s faces turned golden. It could have silenced the river, stopped the rockfalls, sealed the crack in the cliff. It could have lifted the city out of the cut and set it on the bright plateau. It could have made the being young again, or made it immortal in a way it was not, or given it a voice.

But the power was wise, and wisdom is the art of not confusing one’s own strength with another’s need.

It looked again. The people of the cut were not suffering. They were often cold, often damp, often tired from climbing stairs and tending machines. But they were not broken. Their lives were fitted to the place the way the roots were fitted to the cracks. The dim light had made them careful; the vertical city had made them courteous; the constant sound of water had made them attentive. If the sun came all day, something in them might be lost. If the river went silent, the listeners would go deaf in their hearts. If the gorge were healed, the being might no longer have a place to live. The being was old, but it was not dying. It was participating in the slow process of the land—not fighting the river’s cut, but following it, drinking from the water that had passed through stone and root and machine.

So the power decided not to do any of the large things.

Instead, it did three small things.

First, it went to a seam in the cliff above the lower terraces, where water had worked into a crack and begun to lean a slab of stone away from the wall. It did not seal the seam. It did not lift the slab back. It only turned the water in the crack warm for a single night, so the ice that had been prying at the stone melted and drained away. The slab still leaned. But it would not fall for a long time. In the listener’s hut, a woman pressed her brass bowl to the rock and noted that the cliff had settled by a fraction of a fraction, and she wrote it down without knowing why.

Second, it went to one of the offering niches by the river. The salt had partly dissolved. The milk had been taken. A copper coin lay green with damp. The power did not take the coin. It placed beside it a smooth, warm stone from elsewhere, no larger than a child’s palm. The stone was not magic, or if it was, the power did not say. It was just warm, as if it remembered a sun that this place had never known. The next person to pass the niche would see it and pause. They would not move it. They would leave their own offering beside it, and after a season, the moss would begin to cover the strange stone, and it would become part of the place.

Third, it went to find the being.

That was the hardest thing. The being did not trust easily. It had lived in the quiet places for so long that any new warmth, any strange wind, might send it deeper into the cloud-forest. But the power did not try to lure it. It sat down on a wet stone near the high rim, beside one of the stone pans that held the sky, and it waited. The fog rose. The clouds below the rim moved like a slow sea. The power let itself become part of the damp air, a little warmer than the rest, a little more still.

At some point in the night, the being came.

It did not come close. It stood at the edge of the dwarf cloud-forest, a darker shape in the dark, and it watched the power with eyes that reflected no light. The power did not speak. It did not try to touch the being. It only thought, very quietly, a thought not in any language: *You are not forgotten. You are not alone. I have seen you.* The being made no sound. It turned, and for a moment its tracks appeared in the moss, deep and sure, before the fog closed over them.

Then it was gone.

The power stayed until dawn. It watched the light come the way it always came here—not as a rising sun, but as a slow brightening of the gray ceiling, as if the whole sky were waking. The city began to move. A mirror on the rim caught a shaft of light and sent it down into the gorge, where it slid over a wet roof and vanished. A bridge-tender came out and touched a cable, nodded, and went back inside. A child in the lower terraces found that the little light at her collar had stopped flickering, though no one had fixed it. A machinist in the turbine gallery felt the hum shift, very slightly, to a frequency that made the fog on the bridges thin just a little faster than usual.

The power did not stay to see more. It was careful not to leave too much of itself behind. But it left a little. It left the warmth in the stone pan. It left the smooth warm rock in the niche. It left a small new note in the river’s sound, low and kind, which the listeners would spend the next season trying to chart.

Then it went away.

If you asked the people of the cut whether a benevolent and wise power had visited, they would not say yes. They might say the wet season ended a few days early. They might say the blind char were running a little higher than usual. They might say they had slept unusually well, and that the moss on the north wall smelled sweet. They would go on as before: leaving offerings, tending mirrors, listening to the rock, keeping the rules the place had taught them.

But the being who lived there would know. Not in the way of thought or speech, but in the way the old stone holds a warmth after the sun is gone, or the way a crack in the cliff stops widening for no reason, or the way a strange warm stone becomes covered in moss and becomes, slowly, a part of the place.

That was all.

And that was what a wise power would do.
