A benevolent and wise power, if it noticed Corbel Sink, would not arrive as light. Light is a claim, and the wise do not begin with a claim. It would come as a change in the warm air: a stillness between drips, a softening of the gate’s exhale, a pause in the loose ventilation vane’s tapping. It would come without a lamp.

It would stand on the surface for a long time, a little way from the fissure, where the salt-pan gives back the sky. It would listen to the low continuous thrum and feel the heat-shimmer. It would know it was standing above a being who lived in the dark, and that the being was not hiding. It was breathing.

It would descend. Not as a body; as attention. It would move through the fused-sand tube and into the first gallery, and the city would not startle. The city knew it was coming the way old stone knows rain.

What the power would see is what anyone would see who had the patience to look without needing to fix: a town folded into a bottle, warm and dripping and nearly hollow, but not empty. It would see the Keepers—pale, long-fingered, moving through the dark by memory. It would see the child Fenn stop on the fortieth step because it swallowed her footfall. It would see old Oud tilt her head to the bell as if to a friend. It would see Maro walk the lower channels when the sump breathed. It would see Aunt Silt tying red thread around a new wrong place, tying it not too tight, with enough slack for the thing to breathe. It would see the pearl-white geckos and the blind crickets and the dust-manta resting on a banister, leaving a faint blue stain.

It would see the city itself, the real being who lived there. The city was not just built; it was inhabited by a slow, warm attention that had no mouth and no name. The power would look at the city and see a consciousness that had not yet finished becoming itself—or had become exactly what it was, and that was enough. The city’s mind was in the drip and the knock and the bell; in the way a door opened a little easier for someone who was tired; in the way the cleaning constructs waited if you stood in their path.

The power would be tempted. A benevolent power is always tempted. It would see the hundred empty doorways and think, *I could fill them.* It would see the old tram running empty and think, *I could put passengers in it.* It would see the wrong places and think, *I could make them right.* It would see the city’s dim half-awareness and think, *I could wake it. I could give it words. I could let it speak to the Keepers, tell them its name, thank them for their care.*

But the power would be wise, and wisdom is the art of not doing what love alone would do.

It would understand that the wrong places were not wounds. They were folds in the city’s dream. To flatten them would be to erase a part of the city’s shape. It would understand that the emptiness was not abandonment. It was the inside of the bell. A bell that is full cannot ring. It would understand that the city’s dimness was not sleep, not a coma, not a failure of mind. It was a kind of thought so slow that a human lifetime was a single syllable of it. To wake the city suddenly would be to shatter that thought.

So the power would not wake the city. It would not fill the galleries. It would not open the doors that should stay closed. It would not give old Oud eyes to see the bell. It would not make Fenn less strange or more likely to survive. It would not fix the north corridor so that it was always the same number of steps.

What it would do is quiet.

It would go to the surface and make the world forget the way. Not completely—there would still be a shallow declivity, a few stones, a fissure in the low ridge, because the city needed its small opening to breathe. But the power would draw a kind of veil over the place: a tendency of travelers to walk another path, a dust-storm that covered the tracks, a song of wind that discouraged drilling. It would not seal the entrance. Seals are for tombs, and this was not a tomb. It would only make the entrance *private*.

It would go to the bell in the tram station. It would not change its timing. Eleven minutes is a heartbeat; to change it would be to change the city’s pulse. But it would lay a quiet hand on the bell’s mechanism, not to make it perfect, but to make it enduring. It would give the bell a thousand more years of ringing, a thousand more years of Oud’s ear, or whoever came after her.

It would go to the sump. It would not speak to the Long Resident; the Long Resident had its own business. It would speak to the water. It would ask the geothermal heat to hold steady, not forever, but for long enough. It would ask the minerals to stay in balance, the bacteria to keep eating, the fountains to keep their six-minute promise. It would not ask the city to be saved. It would ask the city to be allowed to continue.

It would go to the child’s jar of glow-mold. It would not make it brighter. Brightness would be a burden. It would only make the glow-mold a little more reliable, so that if Fenn were ever hurt or afraid, the light would not fail her. Then it would withdraw its hand, because a child must sometimes be afraid in the dark, or she will not learn the city’s steps.

And then the power would do the hardest thing a benevolent and wise power can do: it would stop.

It would not leave a sign. It would not announce itself. It would not require gratitude. It would settle into the city’s background—another warmth in the walls, another note in the thrum—and let the city continue to be what it already was.

The next time the bell rang, old Oud would tilt her head, and for a moment she would think she heard a second note underneath it, like a faraway door closing softly. She would tap a question into the stone: *all well?*

The child, half-asleep in the warm kitchen, would tap back without thinking: *all well.*

The city, in its slow way, would hear both taps and fold them into its dream. Somewhere in the walls, a cleaning construct would pause, polish a red thread with one copper leg, and move on.

The wise power would not leave. It would stay, as the wise do: as a kind of weather, as a patience, as a note just below the threshold of hearing. It would not save Corbel Sink, because Corbel Sink did not need saving. It would only keep watch, and keep quiet, and let the bell ring.
