**The Gift of Inattention**

The benevolent and wise power did not arrive. Arrival was too sudden for this place. It thickened, the way the fog thickens at dusk: not an event but a gradual change in the density of attention. The snipe on the mossback’s shoulder did not lift its head. The fox at the edge of the flark completed its step. The water in the sinter basin continued to overflow in mineral-laced sheets.

The power perceived the plateau as a whole, the way a musician hears a chord that includes every instrument. It saw the water cycle from cloud to moss to peat to steam and back. It saw the weak electrical battery of the ground, the charge leaking through roots, the electrolysis at the stems of sedges. It saw the midge larvae in the pooled water, the mites on the midges, the spores on the mites. It saw the mossback.

At first the power took her for a landform. Then the landform breathed.

This made the power pause. It had watched many things breathe. It had made many things breathe. But this breathing was so slow, so deeply meshed with the breathing of the peat, that it was hard to tell where the animal ended and the ground began. The mossback’s back was a country. The power saw the sphagnum, the dwarf willow rooted along her spine, the lemmings’ canals, the sundews like red stars. It saw the lightning scars on her flank, black and branching, filled with rainwater. It saw the mineral flanges along her ribs, cast by decades of steam and sinter. It saw her eyes, brown and geological.

The power felt an old impulse. Call it love, call it benevolence, call it the desire to help. The impulse said: *Warm her. She is cold.* The impulse said: *Heal her scars. Ease her age. Give her a voice that can be heard. Lighten the damp. Sharpen the sun.*

But the power was wise, and wisdom is not the absence of love; it is love with a long memory.

So it looked again.

If it warmed her, the moss on her back would change. The sphagnum would dry or be outcompeted. The lemmings would leave. The dwarf willow would stretch upward instead of sideways, and she would no longer be a landscape; she would be an animal in a landscape. If it healed her scars, it would erase the record of lightning, and the pools that caught the morning light would vanish. If it gave her a voice that could be heard, the silence would break, and the silence was not empty—it was the floor under every other sound. If it eased her age, how would she die slowly over years, how would her bones become a terrace, how would a pool form in the hollow of her back, clear as gin over white quartz sand?

The power saw that the mossback’s slowness was not a limitation but a function. Her patience was not endurance but a kind of union. She did not live on the plateau; she was one of its processes, like the vents or the streams. To improve her would be to remove her.

So the power did nothing. For a while it simply listened.

It listened to the drip of condensation from a sedge. To the wet snap of a root under the fox’s paw. To the faint hiss of steam. To the subsonic boom of a snipe’s tail feathers far away. It listened to the deeper sound, the hum in the ground, and recognized it as the mossback’s conversation. The power listened until it understood the language. The language had no nouns, no commands, no questions. It was a continuous condition, a statement of place: *remain, remain, remain.*

The power perceived other mossbacks, spaced across the plain like slow stars. It heard them answer from the fog. It saw the whole network of subsonic speech, moving through peat and water and root. The plateau was not quiet; it was speaking in a register beneath the human ear. The power, for the first time in a long span, felt like a guest.

It asked—not in words, because words would have been a stone thrown into that quiet—*What would you have me do?*

The mossback did not look up. But the question passed through her. The steam from the vent beside her thickened for a single breath. The water around her legs, which had been bubbling faintly from electrolysis, slowed and then resumed. A lemming ran through a canal on her back. The answer was not addressed to the power, but it was unmistakable.

*Keep the fog.*

*Keep the cold.*

*Keep the world from noticing.*

The power considered this. It could do great things. It could raise mountains and carve seas. But here, its greatness would be a kind of violence. Its wisdom lay in accepting a smaller role: not creator, not healer, not revealer, but guardian of the conditions.

For a long while, the power considered doing absolutely nothing. That seemed, at first, the purest form of non-interference. But wisdom knows that inaction can sometimes be its own harm. The world outside had hands, and maps, and machines, and names. The plateau’s quiet would not keep those away. To do nothing would be to leave it exposed.

So it did the one thing that would not disturb the pattern from within. It drew around the plateau a thin veil of inattention. It was not a wall. The fog could still cross it; the wind could cross it; the birds could cross it. But human eyes would slide away. The place would become hard to find, hard to map, hard to want. A satellite would pass and read only cloud. A compass needle would wander. A surveyor would develop a sudden headache, a sudden conviction that the data was wrong. A hiker would remember an appointment and turn back. A road project would run out of money. A mining company would conclude the ore was poor. Not because the power deceived them, but because it made the truth—*this is not for you*—felt as a cold, damp lack of interest.

The power also did something smaller, deeper. It anchored the plateau’s own field. It did not change the geothermal heat, but it stilled the slow wobble of the continental climate so that the fog would remain, the peat would continue to build, the vents would continue to hiss. This was not control; it was a kind of attention so steady it became a climate of its own.

Then the power withdrew. It did not say goodbye. It thinned until it was only fog, and then it was not even that.

For a long moment the mossback lay still. She lifted her head, not alarmed. Her nostrils opened and closed. The whiskers on her snout, black and conductive, read the field. Something had passed through the plateau, or perhaps nothing had. She felt the ground again. The hum was there. The fox was there. The snipe was there. The water was cold. The steam was warm. Everything was exactly as it had been, except that the world outside had turned its face away.

She lowered her head into the wet moss. The garden on her back settled with a tiny shift, a slow earthquake of vegetation. The snipe returned to its preening. The fox completed its step. The lemmings went on tunneling.

And the plateau continued, untouched, unimproved, unbroken. The power’s only gift was to leave no gift. In that, it was exactly benevolent—and exactly wise.
