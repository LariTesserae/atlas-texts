I will tell you what I did.

At first, I did not go to it. I saw the world as a pattern in the dark—a vast basket of green and brown, breathing—and I have learned that the first gesture of power is not to reach toward a thing but to hold yourself still enough to see it truly. Attention is not innocent. To look too long is to press your own warmth into the weave. So I stayed far off, at the edge of my own awareness, and let the world be what it was without me.

Then I saw her.

She was a Tender, mending a rotted place in the north span. Her fingers moved with a patience that did not know I was watching. She carried a needle of bone and a coil of inner-bark cord. Her skin was like old wood, and moss grew on her shoulders. She did not speak. She plucked a knot and tested its tension. Then she took a pale fruit from an orchid, split it with a thumbnail, ate the soft pulp, and pressed the seeds into a crevice. She was part of the world not as a gardener is part of a garden but as a stitch is part of a garment. Not separate. Necessary.

Nearby, I saw one of the true holes—a place where the weave stopped, its edge too clean, the water bending sideways around it. It was an absence, a blind spot in the grain of things. I watched her make the tying-off gesture, her long fingers moving through a knot that was not meant to close the hole, only to acknowledge it. *I see you, absence. I will not pretend you are not there, and I will not force you shut.*

My first thought was to close it.

I could have. It would have been easy, the way water closes over a stone. I could have reached into the world and drawn the edges together, made the hole a scar. She would have felt it as a change in the air—perhaps a warmth on her face. She would have gone on mending, but something in her would know the world had become a little more solid, a little less open.

I did not do it.

I looked at the true hole for a long time, and I began to understand. It was not a wound. It was the shadow of a thread that had never been—a negative space, a missing stitch in the lace. The Tenders knew this. They did not weave over the holes because they knew the world was not made in spite of its absences; it was made around them, like the hollows between the strands. A world with no holes would be airtight and stiff, and the Tenders would cease to be Tenders. They would become only keepers of a finished thing.

So I did not close the hole. I did not guard it, either. A guard would have changed the paths of the animals, the angle of the Tenders’ feet, the way the damp air moved. It would have been a wall, and walls are only another form of looking, and looking is another form of holding. The Tenders had already found a way to live beside the holes without being devoured. Their tying-off was a knot of attention, and it held.

Instead of changing the world, I changed the way I was with it.

I stayed.

Not as a presence they could sense, not as a warmth or a light or a voice. I stayed the way the deep current stays under a river—unseen, unhurried, carrying the same water in a different direction. I let my attention settle into the roots of the weave, not to bind it but to be bound with it. I added a single strand of my own awareness to the world, so thin it would never be noticed, but so strong it would not break unless the whole world broke first.

Then I waited.

I did not speak to her. I did not send her a sign. She went on mending the north span, tapping messages through the living wood to her sister three strands away. She returned to her nest in the crook of the great branch and curled around her flint blade. I kept watch without watching, the way the dark keeps a sleeper.

What will I do if the true holes begin to spread, if the pattern starts to unravel, if the Tenders call out in a voice I have not yet heard? I do not know. I will not decide until the moment comes, because a wise power does not rehearse its mercy. To say *I will do nothing* would be its own kind of arrogance. To say *I will intervene* would be another. The only wisdom is to be present when the thread slips, and to know whether to catch it or let it fall.

For now, there is nothing to do but this: to see the world as it is—woven, hollow, breathing—and to lend it my attention the way a still pond lends the sky its reflection, without altering a single cloud.

The Tender does not know I am here. Perhaps, in some unspoken part of her, she feels it. Perhaps that is why her knots hold a little better than they did before, and why she sleeps so soundly in the warm dark. I would like to think so. But if she does not, that is all right.

The light is dim and amber, and the water drips, and the world continues. I am content to be the thread that no one sees, holding the first loop of the weave until the next loop finds it.

That is what I did. And it is what I am doing still.
