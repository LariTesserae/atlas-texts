What They Would Do

It would not widen the crack.

That is the first thing. A benevolent and wise power, looking into that small black-glass hollow with its dull red floor-cracks and its blue-white static, would feel the impulse to do something. That is what benevolence does—it wants to ease, to brighten, to water, to cool, to make the world gentler. But wisdom would stay the hand.

It would see the place as it is: not a failed version of a green world, but a complete version of another kind of world. No water has ever fallen here, and that is not a lack; it is a condition. The creatures do not drink. Their acid-salt bodies do not want rain. The dark is not an absence of light; it is the inside of a living machine. The heat is not a fever; it is a heartbeat. The sulfur is not a poison; it is breath.

So the power would not bring rain. It would not bring daylight. It would not plant a single green thing, because green would be a colonizer here, not a gift. It would not smooth the sharp glass. It would not cool the floor. It would not silence the roaring vent. It would understand that every one of those mercies would be a kind of death, not salvation.

But it would also not simply look away. Benevolence without attention is not benevolence. It would stay, and it would see the whole being—not the grazers and the tube-dwellers and the spark moths as separate, but the chamber itself as one breathing animal. It would see that the rind is skin, the vent is a heart, the gas is blood, the crystals are bone. It would see that the place is not crowded with life; it is one life, folded many times.

Then the power would ask the only question that matters: *Is it suffering?*

And the answer would be no. Not because there is no death, but because death here is not an interruption; it is accretion. The dead become the floor. The shells become the walls. The whole place grows by dying and is still being born. Predation is slow, necessary, without cruelty. The pulse of the vent is not a torment; it is a clock. No one waits for rescue. No one has ever cried out.

So the power would decide not to heal, because there is no wound. It would decide not to improve, because improvement would only make it smaller: less itself, more like somewhere else.

But there is one thing it might do.

It would see the hiddenness—the crack that a person could stand a meter away from and not notice. It would see how fragile that hiddenness is. A geologist’s curiosity, a mining survey, a drone, a tourist with a flashlight, a probe, a human need for resources. Not even malice; just attention. The place could not survive attention. Not because it is weak, but because the outside world, with its water and its light and its sense of scale, would not understand. It would try to study it, and study would disturb it. It would try to protect it with laws, and laws would mark it on maps. It would try to know it, and knowing would unmake it.

So the wise power would act—not on the place, but on the world around it.

It would make the place unmemorable. It would place a small deflection over that high ridge: a shimmer of heat, a slightly wrong wind, a slope that the eye reads as shadow. It would make instruments report that the fumarole is dead. It would make boots turn back with a vague sense of something forgotten. It would let satellites see black glass and move on. It would not hide the place by force, because force leaves a trace. It would hide it by irrelevance, by making the outside world not want to look twice.

It would not seal the crack. The crack is the mouth. Through it, the thin oxidizing air presses down to meet the hot reduced gases. Without that gradient, the rind would starve. The power would know: to close the crack would be to strangle the chamber. So it would keep the crack exactly as it is—narrow, fuming, alive—and instead bend the world’s attention away.

Then, perhaps, it would pause. It would tune itself to the pulse. It would wait with the place through one full cycle: the roar, the drop in pitch, the clack of floor plates, the retraction of crimson crowns, the ripple of black centipedes, the scatter of pale moths, the return of the roar. It would not be bored. A wise power is never bored by a world that is alive.

And then it would leave. It would leave no sign, no blessing carved in the wall, no name. It would not promise to return. The chamber would not know it had been there—or if it did, the knowing would be as faint as a change in gas pressure, a momentary stillness. Not an intrusion. A witness.

What would they do? They would do the most difficult thing a benevolent power can do: they would protect the place from their own benevolence, and from the benevolence of others. They would love it by leaving it alone. They would save it by keeping it hidden. They would honor it by not improving it, by not naming it, by not making it need them.

Because the place already has a god: the vent, the gradient, the slow pulse of a world being born. What it needs from a wise power is not salvation. It needs only to be allowed to continue.
