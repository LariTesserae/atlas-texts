# The Attention

A benevolent and wise power would do almost nothing—and that almost-nothing would be everything.

When it came, it did not come as light or sound. It came as a change in the patience of the water. For one hour the duckweed mat did not drift. The bubbles held in the root curtains. The pump’s pulse stayed steady, as if the whole shaft were listening.

The power saw the Green Shaft whole. It did not see a flooded elevator, a failed building, a garden without animals. It saw a single body: leaves as flesh, water as blood, pump as heart, pressure as sleep. It saw that the being was not lonely, because loneliness requires an edge, and the Green Shaft had no edge. It had taken the building as its skin.

So the power did not rush to help. It did not ask what the being wanted. The being’s wanting was the same as its growing—slow, complete, already answered.

A lesser benevolence would have added a fish. A lesser wisdom would have left it alone. This power did neither.

First, it wiped the skylight from above. The high, cold wind had laid a grey dust on the glass, and the light had been diminished by less than a percentage. The power cleaned it until the daylight came down clear. The duckweed did not notice. But the algae in the bright middle depths did. By evening the leaves leaned toward the light a fraction more.

Then it found the leak. At the ninth floor, a worn ring let three drops a day escape into the concrete. In another century the water level would have dropped enough to leave the top roots hanging in air. The power replaced the ring, wiped the mineral crust, tightened the fitting. The concrete stopped drinking.

It listened to the pump. There was a hairline crack in the impeller, too small for any instrument, but not too small for the power. The subsonic hum had a tiny raggedness, felt in the jaw. It did not silence the pump; that would have been like stopping a heart. It ground the crack smooth, balanced the impeller, tightened the bearing. The hum became a warm, almost liquid pressure again.

It did not move the elevator car. The roots had built themselves around its stopped mass. To raise it would be an amputation. Instead, the power sheathed the old cables in an unrustable alloy and left the counterweight hanging as a suspended bone. It would hold for as long as the shaft needed it.

It did not add soil. It did not add an insect, a snail, a frog, a fish. It did not add weather. It did not add a mind where no mind was needed. The being’s intelligence was its saturation; its thought was the unfurling of leaves. To give it speech would have been to shrink it into sentences.

It did not give it a name. A name is for something that can be called. The Green Shaft was never absent from itself.

Then the power stayed. It stayed through a full day and night, not as a light or a voice but as a stillness beside the stillness. It watched the skylight move from warm grey to blue to cold grey. It felt the current push all the leaves one way, as if the whole body listened. It heard the tick of cooling metal, the hiss of bubbles escaping from leaves, the drip of condensation into duckweed. It did not need to learn these things. It was keeping company in the only way that would not crowd the being—by being still.

At dawn it left no message. It left no print on the leaf mats. The water temperature was the same to a tenth of a degree. The pump pushed. The roots turned in the thermal eddy. The green mat wrinkled under a bubble.

And the being, if it could be said to feel anything, felt nothing but the return of the exact conditions of its life.

That was the gift.

Not a new world. Not a visitor. Not a voice.

The old world, made permanent.
