# What the Wise Power Did

The benevolent and wise power considered the room for a long time, from the outside of time. It saw the machine-mountain, the serviceways, the cold wet gap high in the upper condensing works. It saw the steel grating over black water, the riveted iron, the ducts like bronchioles of some enormous made lung. It saw the small pale animals—the mites, isopods, crickets, midges, bats, worms—so dense they became a second geology. It saw the grey-green lichen, the colorless moss, the white fungal finger. It saw all of this not as separate things but as one thing: a room that was also a body, a body that was also a room.

And it saw the figure.

The power looked at the figure the way a surgeon looks at a wound that is not a wound, or the way a mother looks at a child sleeping in a room that will never grow cold. It saw the pale plaster-skin, the root-like fingers fused to formwork, the shins grown into grating, the eyes like amber bulbs with no pupils. It saw the slow vapor thread from the closed mouth. It saw every mite, every isopod, every bat, every worm as an extension of the figure’s dreaming. It understood immediately that the room was not a place that contained a being; the being was the room’s way of being unfinished.

Then the power did what wise and benevolent powers do when they wish to help: it imagined what could be done.

It could finish the wall. It could connect the uncapped pipes, set the gauges, complete the concrete. But to complete the room would be to end the waiting, and the waiting was the figure’s breath. The figure did not need a future; it needed to remain as it was, in the moment before the first breath, because that was the only moment it had ever had. To give it a future would be to give it death—not the death of the body, but the death of its particular way of being alive.

It could wake the animals. It could send a small warmth through the air, a one-degree gradient, and the mites would begin to move, the crickets to stir, the bats to fly. But that would be to introduce hunger, fear, time, competition. The peace of the room was not the peace of death; it was the peace of perfect suspension, a chord held so long it became silence. To wake them would be to break the chord. The power loved them too much to teach them to suffer.

It could warm the room, dry the air, let the walls become merely walls. But then the figure’s transpiration would stop. The water cycle that was its slow breath would fail. The lichen, the moss, the fungus—those half-memories of green—would die or quicken into something that was no longer the figure’s dream. Not that.

It could enter the room and sit beside the figure. But to enter would be to create a change, a shadow that had not been there. The power’s body, however gentle, would displace mist, alter currents, leave a warmth that could not be taken back. The room’s perfection was its absolute stasis. Any presence would be a wound.

It could end the figure entirely. It could gather the being into itself, absorb the waiting, release it from the amber. But that would be murder, however merciful. The figure was not in pain. Its open eyes did not beg. Its thin mouth did not cry. It was not a prisoner. It was the room’s own future, held in suspension, and it had not asked for release.

The power considered doing nothing. It considered this for a long time, because doing nothing is the hardest thing for a benevolent power. It feels like abandonment. It feels like the failure to love. But the wise part of the power knew that sometimes love is not an action but a refusal to act. Sometimes the greatest gift is to see a being exactly as it is and say: *I will not change you. I will not finish you. I will not wake you. I will not warm you. I will not save you, because you are not lost.*

So the power made its decision.

It did nothing.

But—and this is the part that is difficult to tell—it did not do nothing in the way a stone does nothing. It did nothing in the way a hand held just above a wound does nothing, or the way a parent standing in a darkened room does nothing while a child sleeps, or the way the sun does nothing when it does not shine on the moon’s far side. Its inaction was not absence; it was a chosen, loving stillness that mirrored the figure’s own. For one moment that was not a moment, the power let its own infinite possibilities fall away and became, in its attention, as still as the room. It did not condense on the iron. It did not enter the water cycle. It did not appear as light or shadow. It simply stood outside the room, across the threshold of time, and looked with such complete acceptance that the room’s silence deepened.

Inside the room, nothing changed. The temperature did not rise. The water did not fall faster. The sound did not shift. The figure did not move. The mites did not move. The eyes did not blink. But if there had been a witness with the power—and there was none—they might have seen something that was not a change: the amber eyes, for the space of a thought, became not less but more themselves, as if they had been seen at last and, in being seen, had become fully what they already were.

Then the power withdrew its attention, not because it had stopped loving the room, but because to stay longer would be to risk becoming part of the story. It left no sign. It spoke no word. It made no promise. The room, the figure, the white and silent fauna—they remained exactly as they had been, and exactly as they would always be.

The benevolent and wise power had done the only thing that was both benevolent and wise: it had recognized that some lives are not meant to be changed. Some places are not meant to be finished. Some silences are not meant to be broken. The room was a question that did not want an answer. The figure was a breath that did not need to be released. And the power, having understood this, let it be.

That is what it did. Nothing. And that nothing was everything.
