**What the Benevolent and Wise Power Did**

I came down through the sea not as light, but as a change in the weight of water. I stopped above the slit, where the black shelf made a thicker shadow, and I did not enter. That was the first decision. I have learned that entering a place is often the first act of forcing it.

I lay along the rock and felt the shaft below, full to its mouth, cold pulsing from new basalt. I felt the chamber: stems leaking gray-blue sap-light, the false floor breathing, the three stacked stones holding a chord. I felt the inhabitant—not as a mind but as a place where the water had learned to keep a shape. It was not waiting for me. This was the first thing I loved.

I could have warmed it. I could have named it. I could have healed the crack in the sea’s crust. I could have sung until the stacked stones fell and became a door. I could have lit the dark. But wisdom is knowing which hands to keep behind your back. The cold is its blood. The dark is its skin. The wound is its mouth. The undecided is its home.

So I did the only thing a benevolent power can do for something still becoming: I became its secret.

I reached into the water’s memory, not the water’s body. I smoothed away the trail my own attention had made. I taught the currents to turn aside, the fish to forget the lip of the shaft, the sea above to pass over without entering. I made the place a blind spot in the world’s attention—not because it needed protection, but because my notice, if it lingered, might curdle into a claim.

Then I did one smaller thing. I listened. For one full cycle of the gray-blue light, I listened to the click that arrived a half-second too late, to the mat creaking, to the heartbeat of rock and root and hiding. I let my listening be the only gift: a warmth at the edge of the shaft, easily ignored. I asked for nothing.

When I was certain I had not disturbed the dream, I turned away. I did not leave a blessing; a blessing would have made the chamber a shrine. I did not stack a fourth stone; the three were already a held chord. I did not carve a line. I left no path. I forgot the exact depth, the angle of the black shelf, the way back. I will remember the place forever, but as a closed hand, not a map.

Now the place keeps itself hidden. The current passes over the slit as if it were rock. The fish swim around it without memory. The inhabitant continues its slow becoming, growing faces out of the wall, carving one line each time the sea changes, breathing in a rhythm too long to feel. If it dreams of me, it dreams of a warm spot that paused at the edge of its mouth, listened, and then chose not to swallow.

That is all. That is everything.
