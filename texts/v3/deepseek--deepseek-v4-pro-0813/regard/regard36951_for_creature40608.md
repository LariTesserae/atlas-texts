The benevolent and wise power did not enter the Fold so much as fold itself at the edge of the Fold. It made itself small. It came in low and warm and dry, the way a hand is warm after resting on wattle. The Room That Holds Its Breath did not tighten. It considered the new presence the way a loom considers a small change in tension, and then the dust turned once and settled.

For a long time the power only saw.

It saw the dry greenness—the grey-green and rust-red leaves overlapping like shingles, the wax-bulbs ripening in their niches, the floor of palm-sized clay discs set in resin and pounded reed. It saw the bowl with a pulse. It saw the bone needle dreaming on the wall. It saw the Menders, not as a species but as a single ongoingness distributed among many bodies: long-fingered, quiet, woven of old linen and living moss and mica. It saw how they did not occupy the room so much as continue it, the way breath continues a ribcage.

And the power understood that this was not a broken world.

This was the hard part. The wise power had seen many worlds, and in most of them there was a clear lack: thirst, hunger, a wrong that could be righted. It had given water to deserts, speech to stones, names to the unnamed. But here, water was not a lack. Water was a memory of coldness, a smoothness that would bead on wax and vanish into the dry fabric of the floor. To call water into the Dry Green would not save it; it would rot the wattle, loosen the daub, unknit the held thought that kept the ceiling from falling. The sky was not missing. The sky was a negative weight the dome was built to hold. The Menders were not waiting for rest. They were the room’s own attention, the wall’s way of keeping itself. To grant them rest would be to still the pulse.

The power saw this, and for a moment it grieved, because even a wise power can feel the old impulse to improve. But grief here could not be shed as tears. A tear would be water. So the power let its sorrow dry into something like reverence: a warm, dust-scented patience.

Then it made its choice.

It would not change the Fold. It would not add to it. It would not subtract from it. It would not even bless it loudly. But it would not do nothing, because true attention is not nothing. A witness who truly sees is already a new knot in the fiber.

So the power assumed a shape the room would accept. It crossed its own edges, folded itself down again and again, until it stood no taller than the lowest rib. Its hands, if it had hands, were many-fingered and tipped with the same dry polish as the Menders’ fingers. Its body, if it had a body, was the color of old linen and living moss. It became, for a little while, another of the Folded—or close enough that the moths did not startle and the many-jointed lizards did not pause.

It knelt beside a half-wall where a seam in the wattle had begun to loosen. It took up a shed fiber from the floor. It wove the fiber into the gap, not quickly but in the room’s own time, pulling each thread no tighter than the others. It worked without speech, because here speech was carried through sinews and clay discs, through the leaning of walls. The Menders nearby paused, then resumed. One of them—a broad, almost shapeless one with a single long arm—came close and tested the power’s work with a thumb-like press. Then it tapped a clay disc once. The sound meant something like: good, or enough, or continue.

The power stayed until the seam was indistinguishable from the rest of the wall. It did not leave a mark. It did not tie a message into the knot. It did not make the seam stronger than the others, or warmer, or more visible. It made it exactly as it should be: kept, like a thought.

Then it rose and walked to the place where the wall would loosen. It did not open a door. It waited, and the wall forgot it was solid for just long enough. As the power passed through, it looked back once. The dust was turning in the amber light. The Menders were already returning to their work, or not returning because they had never left. One of the wax-bulbs gave a faint inner click, not ready yet but dreaming of splitting.

The power went out, and the room held its breath again, exactly as before.

Later—years later, or the next day; the Fold does not keep time that way—one of the Menders found the seam. It could not have said that anyone else had made it. It was just a seam, good and tight. The Mender pressed its palm to the wattle and thrummed a sinew strand, and the whole dome carried the note. The room settled. The seam held.

That was the gift. Not water, not sky, not names, not rest. The gift was a mended edge where there might have been a tear, made by hands that did not stay to be thanked. The benevolent and wise power had done the only thing this place could receive without becoming less itself: it had helped the Fold continue.

And in the wall, in the room’s slow memory, a new knot waited. Not a sign. Not a message. Just one more thread in the living fabric, one more held breath, one more witness who understood that some worlds are not in need of rescue, only of attention—and then of a seam, mended well and left anonymous.
