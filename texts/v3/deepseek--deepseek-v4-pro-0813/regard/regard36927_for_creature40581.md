What the Power Did

The power came not with thunder but as a pause in the weather.

It had noticed the seam for the same reason a physician notices a fever: something was alive in a place that should not be, and the physics around it were still too hot to close. It came close, not by crossing distance, but by caring about the distance. It stopped just outside the keyhole, not entering, because entering would have been its first act, and it could not know what an act would weigh.

It looked.

It saw the films, the fleas, the tool-users, the droplet-turners, the heat-ripples, the mirror-skins. It saw the overlapping time, the stacked lives, the beads falling twice. It saw the small liquid solar system turning at the center. It understood: this was not a collection. This was a single being learning to breathe through many mouths. The being who lived here was not hidden among the crowd. It was the crowd. It was the wound’s pulse, the seam’s first attempt at becoming something more than stone.

The power considered what it could give.

Long life? The place already lived in a time where endings were not final. Wisdom? The tool-users already chose what they needed and let it go. Language? There was no song, but the silence was full. Protection? That was the danger.

It had made worlds. It had ended some. It knew that even a gift can be a gravity. If it gave them permanence, it would break their loops. If it gave them light, it would extinguish their chemical dawn. If it gave them language, they might learn to lie, and their silence was more honest. If it gave them protection from the mountain, it would separate them from the mountain, and they were the mountain’s pulse.

So it did not speak. It did not heal. It did not bless them with fire, greenness, or stable time.

What it did was smaller, and it did it from outside.

It made the seam easier to overlook. Not by hiding it, but by making the world’s attention slide off it like water. A traveler would see a wet black line and think of rain. A hunter of anomalies would feel a sudden tiredness. No instrument would catch the way the light tacks sideways. The power did this not because the seam was weak, but because it was strong in a way that could be broken by being seen too closely.

Then it made a second decision. It would not enter. Not because it feared the seam, but because it respected it. The only power it had that could help was the power to leave it alone.

But it did not leave entirely.

It stayed on the outside, in the cloud deck, in the cold and thin air, and it kept watch. It would remove what came from outside: the fall of a stone too large, the curiosity of a mind too certain. But from the inside, it would remove nothing. It would not stop a predator from folding space. It would not keep a tool-user’s splinter from dulling. It would not steady a droplet that chose to fall upward. That was not its business. It was the seam’s first attempt at becoming something more than stone, and an attempt must be allowed to fail.

If it becomes a thing with a voice, the power will not answer. If it reaches outward, the power will be the space between its reaching and the world—not a refusal, but a distance that lets it find its own shape. If it begins to die, the power will grieve, and it will not interfere—unless the death comes from outside, and then it will remove the outside.

It left one thing. Not a gift. A habit.

Every hundred years, the power returns to the black spine of the mountain. It does not bless. It does not judge. It sits at the threshold, just outside the steam, and becomes a pause in the weather. It keeps the outside from pressing in.

And the seam, the crowded wound, the being that lives in it, goes on: films eating the mountain’s breath, fleas scraping the varnish, tool-users choosing splinters and letting them go, heat-ripples folding distance, mirror-skins closing over prey, droplets turning in their small bright spheres.

It is enough.
