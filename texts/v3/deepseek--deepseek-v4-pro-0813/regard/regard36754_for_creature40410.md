# What the Wise Power Did

The benevolent and wise power did not arrive with light or sound. It stayed at the edge of the high shelf-country, where the cloud line blurs the white terraces, and it watched for a long time—not as a judge, but as a guest who did not want to disturb the house.

It considered giving them animals, because it saw them walking alone through a world with no birdsong. But it imagined the first bird’s cry falling into that mineral quiet, and it understood: the silence was not a lack. It was a material, like travertine or water. To break it would be to break the land.

It considered easing their hunger. The bitter cress, the soaked root, the moss flour—these seemed to the power like little food for so much care. But it saw how hunger kept their attention on the land, how the ache was a kind of listening. To make the cress sweet or the root fat would be to take away the slow conversation they had with the water. They were not starving. They were fasting in a world of stone.

It considered speaking to them. One clear word, placed in the still air, could have taught them a thousand things. But it saw the way they struck the channels—three taps for *come*, five for *the wall has shifted*. Their language was already a form of water. To add a voice from nowhere would be to make them afraid that the silence could be broken. It would have been a gift that took away their certainty.

It considered raising their dead from the sinter, letting them walk again among the terraces. But the power saw that the dead were not gone; they were becoming walls, and the living pointed to them without sadness. *My mother is learning to be a wall.* To undo that would be to say their slow transformation was a mistake. It was not a mistake. It was a covenant with the springs.

It considered, at last, the smallest thing: one flower, one warm stone, one extra degree of comfort in a pool. It reached for the world, then withdrew. Even a flower would have been a new actor. Even warmth would have changed the rate of the stone. The place was not broken. It did not need mending. It did not need blessing. It needed only to be allowed to continue.

So the wise power did the only thing left to do.

It built no wall and lit no fire. It sent no message and left no sign. Instead, it placed its attention around the high shelf-country like a second sky—transparent to light, permeable to water, closed to every outside force that would not understand what it found there. No falling star hit. No foreign seed blew in. No god or traveler or noise from the wider worlds crossed the high spine. The place remained a place built before it was ever lived in, and the people remained the caretakers of water.

If you had stood among them after the power’s decision, you would have noticed nothing. The springs rose at the same rate. The moss grew its pale green fur. A child sat by a pool, watching a mineral skin form on a fallen frond. An elder tapped the channel: three taps for *come*. The water dripped from the sinter lips with a sound like a glass bead falling. No wind. No birds. No insects.

But sometimes, for no reason, a person walking alone along a white terrace would feel, for a moment, as if the land were not merely tended but held—not by hands, not by a presence, but by the same quiet that they had always known, deepening slightly, as if the world had drawn a breath and chosen not to spend it. They would stop, touch the stone, and go on. They did not know they had been visited by a benevolent and wise power.

That was the point. The wisest thing a power can do for a world that is already whole is to love it without leaving a mark.
