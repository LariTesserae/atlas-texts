From the plain above, the Cut opens like a wound that has not yet begun to heal. It is a depression maybe three miles across, its rim a broken line of pale gray limestone, its floor a hundred and fifty feet below the grass. The walls are not the smooth curves of a river valley; they are a staircase of raw ledges, vertical faces, and sharp arrises, as if the ground has been peeled back in layers and the layers have not yet learned to weather. There is no erosion here, only extraction. The stone is fresh, unrounded, still dusted with rock flour. Smaller sinkholes and side-quarries branch off from the main pit, connected by ramps and narrow tunnels, so the inhabited ground extends for miles in every direction.

There is no empty space in the Cut. Every ledge, every niche, every shelf of rock is occupied. Dwellings are built into the stone itself—walls of stacked limestone block extend natural overhangs, timber beams brace against fresh fractures, roofs of slate or hide or woven reed angle over the gaps. The buildings are packed so tightly that from above the city looks like a single continuous organism, a crust of masonry and wood grown over the rock. Lanes are narrow, often no wider than a person’s shoulders, and they wind between shared walls, under low archways, across rope bridges that span the deeper gaps. The lower you go, the newer the stone. The upper terraces are old, their edges softened by decades of hands and water, their walls whitewashed with lime. The lower terraces are raw, sharp, still dusted with rock flour. No one builds outward; the city’s only direction is down and inward, because the stone is both home and resource.

The technology is simple. There is no iron here, only copper and stone and wood and rope. Tools are chisels of flint and copper, mallets of dense wood, baskets of woven grass, ladders of lashed poles. The city is built by hand, block by block, and the blocks come from the city itself. Workshops are crammed into the lower galleries: stonecutters, potters, weavers, rope-makers, all working in spaces barely larger than their own outstretched arms. The air smells of wet limestone, woodsmoke, and the faint mineral tang of newly cut rock.

Water is everywhere but never abundant. It seeps from the fresh fractures in thin shining films, drips from overhangs, collects in carved channels that run down the walls to cisterns on every level. The air is cool and damp, and in the morning condensation beads on every surface, and mist rises from the lower terraces until the sun burns it off. Rain comes in autumn and spring, and the channels run full, and the air smells of wet dust. The people manage the water with the same care they give the stone: channels are redirected, cisterns cleaned, new seepages tapped. Nothing is wasted.

The sky above is open, and daylight reaches the floor at noon, but the depth of the Cut means that most of the day is spent in half-light. The upper terraces catch the sun; the lower ones lie in blue shadow. The light is ordinary, uncolored, but it falls in sharp angles, cutting the city into bright and dark. The cool air holds the dampness close, and the stone stays cold even in summer.

The Cut is quiet. Not silent—there is always the drip of water, the soft scrape of a chisel, the tap of a mallet, the creak of a rope under load—but no one shouts, no one hammers loudly, no one sings. The people have a law of quiet, because the rock is still settling, and a loud noise can loosen a slab. They speak in low voices and in a code of taps struck on stone, a language that travels through the rock better than any shout. The stone itself seems to absorb sound, so even a thousand people working produce only a hushed murmur, like wind in dry grass.

Vegetation is sparse. Moss and lichen grow in the damp cracks, pale green and rust orange. Ferns uncurl where water drips steadily. On the upper rim, a few hardy grasses and thistles have found soil, but there are no trees. The animals are few. Swifts nest in the high crevices, and a kestrel sometimes hangs over the rim, watching for the small lizards that bask on sunny ledges. Insects are more common—midges rise from the cisterns, spiders spin webs in the corners of doorways, beetles scuttle over the stone. A weasel, thin and rare, hunts the spiders in the lower galleries. But large animals do not come here; the walls are too steep, and the city leaves no room for them.

Yet the Cut is not still. It is a place of constant slow motion. At the lowest level, where the floor is still raw and jagged, workers cut blocks from the living rock. They score lines with copper chisels, drive wooden wedges into the cracks, and wait for the stone to split. The blocks are hauled up ramps on rollers, and new terraces are carved where the old ones have been quarried away. The city is digging itself deeper, widening its own walls, and the depression grows a little each year. The moss spreads into fresh cracks; the spiders follow the insects; the swifts return each spring. The rock settles, sometimes with a soft crack, and the people pause, listening, then resume their work. The Cut is not a ruin, not a finished place. It is a landscape in the act of becoming, a city that is also a quarry, a hole that is also a home.
