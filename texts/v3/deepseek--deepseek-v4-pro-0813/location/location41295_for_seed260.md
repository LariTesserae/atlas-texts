The first thing you understand, before you understand anything else, is that the sound is not something happening in the place. The sound is the place. It comes up through the soles of your feet before your ears register it—a bass so low it feels like the ground itself is breathing, a continuous, full-body pressure that makes your teeth hum against each other and sets the fine bones of your inner ear ringing. It never stops. It never changes. It is the drone of rock being forced upward, of new crust cracking under its own weight, of a mountain range still in the middle of being born.

You cannot really hear anything else. Not footsteps, not your own voice. The air is so dense with vibration that sound itself becomes a kind of weather—a constant, physical layer you move through, thick as water, pressing on your chest from all sides.

The landscape that holds this sound is not finished.

---

You are standing on a high plateau, or what will become a plateau when the world decides what a plateau is. The elevation is extreme—higher than anything should be. The air is thin and cold, somewhere near freezing, and it smells of nothing. No dust, no rot, no moisture. Just a dry metallic sharpness, like the smell of lightning before it strikes, except there is no storm. There are no clouds. The sky is a featureless gray-white, uniformly dim, the light of an overcast day that never ends but never deepens. The sun, if there is one, is a diffuse brightness behind the grayness—not directly visible, just a region of the sky that is slightly less dark.

The ground beneath you is rock. That is an understatement. The ground is not a thing you stand on so much as a thing you are surrounded by. Rock is the only language this place speaks, and it is still learning to speak it.

The rock is young. That is the wrong word, because the rock is not young in the way a newborn mountain range is young. The rock is new in a way that does not sit right in the mind. It is raw and unmistakeably fresh—no weathering, no rounding, no patina. Every surface is sharp. Every edge is a knife. The terrain is a chaos of vertical walls and jagged spires and slabs tilted at impossible angles, as if some enormous hand reached down and squeezed the crust upward, and then simply stopped, leaving it there, half-risen, the wrinkles still forming.

But there is no dust. There is no talus. No scree slopes of fallen fragments at the base of the cliffs. The erosion is nearly zero, not because the place is protected from weathering, but because the weathering hasn't started yet. The mountain has not had time to begin falling apart. The world is still being emplaced. The peaks are rising so slowly you cannot see it—a few centimeters in a century, maybe less—but they are rising, and the sound you hear is the sound of that rising: stone grinding against stone, the planetary crust cracking along seams that have not yet fully separated, the steady crystalline shriek of matter being forced into shapes it has not yet accepted.

In some places, the rock looks wrong.

Not wrong in color or composition—it is a uniform pale gray, almost white, like sun-bleached bone. Wrong in the way it is arranged. You will see a boulder, perfectly cubic, balanced on a point. You will see a vertical wall that curves inward and then outward in a way that makes your eyes slide off it, unable to hold the shape. You will see a ridge that, when you look at it directly, is a normal jagged ridgeline, but when you catch it in your peripheral vision, it seems to be doubled, two ridges overlapped, one a few degrees out of phase with the other, like an image that has failed to render properly.

This is the first sign of the reality breakdown. The world is not fully resolved here. The high country is still becoming, and in the meantime, the laws that govern the rest of existence are loose. They do not always apply. Or they apply differently in different places, or different times.

You will walk past a rock outcrop and realize, a few steps later, that you saw it on both your left and your right. You will turn around and the outcrop will be singular again, and there will be no way to know which of the two you saw was the true one, or if the question even means anything. You will see a crack in the ground that starts at your feet and runs off toward a distant peak, and the crack will be a meter wide at your feet, but when you look at the peak, the crack will be a hairline, and when you look back at the ground, the crack will be three meters wide and the far end of it will be somewhere else entirely.

Time itself seems to have loose teeth here, though the dynamic is slow. The changes do not happen as you watch. They happen in the gaps between your attention. You look away, and when you look back, something has shifted. A spur that was on the north side of a peak is now on the west. A slope that was ascending is now descending. It is not that the landscape is actively rearranging itself—it seems too lazy for that, too slow, too monumental. It is more that the landscape is not fully committed to being one landscape. It is a place still holding several possible versions of itself, and it has not yet chosen among them.

---

Now, about the vegetation.

Because this is the thing that makes no sense, and it is also the thing that makes the place what it is. The land is waterless. There is no moisture anywhere—no streams, no springs, no ice, no snow. The air is painfully dry. The soil, where there is soil, is mineral powder. And yet the vegetation is lush.

Not lush in the way of a jungle or a meadow. Lush in the way of a coral reef—dense, abundant, structured, prolific. But sparse in distribution. The stands of vegetation are scattered across the plateau and up the slopes like islands in an ocean of stone. And the vegetation itself is not green.

It is the color of the rock, mostly, but with undertones that emerge as your eyes adjust: deep mauves, smoky purples, a blue so dark it is almost black, and here and there, a vein of phosphorescent silver that carries a faint light of its own. The plants are not plants in any botanical sense. They are tall, thin, branching structures that resemble trees at a distance but are, up close, much stranger. Their trunks are smooth and translucent, like glass or quartz, and you can see internal structures inside them—hollow channels running vertically, filled with a fluid that is not water, that moves too slowly to be water, that is the color of old amber. The branches divide and subdivide into fine filaments that end in small, bell-shaped cups, and these cups are always open, always tilted toward the sky, as if they are tasting the dim light, or listening.

There is no leaf litter. No dead vegetation. The plants do not appear to lose parts, or if they do, the parts are absorbed back into the stone. Their roots, where you can see them, are fused into the rock itself, not penetrating cracks but dissolved into the stone, as if the plants and the rock are a single material in different phases. And the plants are warm—a few degrees warmer than the air, warm enough that you can feel the difference on your skin when you stand close to a stand of them.

The ecology is non-aqueous. The plants do not drink. They do not transpire. They seem to draw their energy from the ground itself, from the heat of the rising crust, from the slow tectonic grind that makes the mountains grow. Perhaps that is why they are here. Perhaps they feed on the same force that drives the mountains upward. The fluids in their translucent stems move in slow, convection-driven currents, carrying something up from the roots to the bell-shaped cups, where it crystallizes, briefly, and then dissolves again. The plants grow slowly. You cannot watch them grow, but you can feel that they are growing. The entire stand pulses, very slowly, like a heartbeat stretched over hours.

The vegetation is lush in the way a lichen-covered rock is lush, but scaled up enormously. In a single stand, there may be dozens of individuals, their branches interlocking overhead to form a canopy. But between the stands, there is nothing but bare stone for kilometers. The density of the landscape as a whole is sparse—much space between presences—but each presence, when you come to it, is dense and layered and teeming with a quiet, mineral vitality.

There are no animals. That is perhaps the most unsettling thing, after the sound. No birds, no insects, no small scuttling things in the shadows of the rocks, no tracks in the stone powder. The vegetation is alive, but nothing eats it. Nothing pollinates it. Nothing moves through it except wind and vibration. The absence of animals makes the sound even more wrong. A wilderness without animals is not a wilderness; it is something else. It is a stage without actors, an ecology with a single kingdom, a world still waiting for its fauna to be invented.

You find yourself moving very carefully, not because you are afraid of startling something, but because you are afraid of being the only moving thing in a landscape that is not used to movement.

---

High up, on a broad shelf of rock, you come to the first of the built structures.

It is not much. A low wall, perhaps hip-height, curving in a gentle arc along the edge of the shelf, made of stones that have been fitted together without mortar. Each stone is roughly rectangular, and they are all the same pale gray as the bedrock, which means the wall is almost invisible until you are close to it. It might be a windbreak. It might be a shelter. It might be a marker. There is no way to tell, because the wall is broken in several places, and the gaps have not been mended, and the stones that fell have been half-absorbed back into the ground, their edges softening as the rock reclaims them.

Beyond the wall, there is a low opening in the cliff face—an entrance, partially collapsed, leading into a shallow cave. The floor of the cave is smooth, worn by something other than water. On the walls, someone has carved marks: not letters, not images, but a series of vertical lines and cross-hatchings, arranged in groups, as if counting something. The marks are old. They have been scored deeply into the rock, and the rock has begun to heal around them, the edges of the cuts filling in with a fine crystalline crust.

You find other structures elsewhere, scattered across the landscape. A stack of flat stones balanced in a precarious column, the top stone carved with a spiral. The remains of a small shelter, roof collapsed. A series of rock cairns leading up a ridgeline, spaced far apart, each one so eroded it is nearly gone, yet the erosion here is supposed to be negligible, which means the cairns are very old indeed, or the erosion is not as simple as it seems, or time itself has been wearing on them unevenly.

No tools. No metal. No pottery, no glass, no textiles. The people who built these things—if they were people—worked with their hands and with stone, and they left nothing behind but the stones themselves.

The structures are scattered, but they are placed with intention. They cluster at high points. They mark passes. They face toward the tallest peak in the distance, which rises above the plateau like a black tooth, darker than the other peaks, its upper slopes wrapped in a gray haze that never clears.

The built things are not contemporary. They are not ruins, exactly. They are more like messages left by someone who passed through a long time ago, or by someone who is still here but does not want to be seen. You will find a stone arrow laid flat on the ground, pointing northeast toward a notch in a ridgeline. You will find a circle of white stones around a patch of darker rock. You will find a wall that continues for twenty meters and then stops, as if the builder was interrupted, or as if the wall's purpose was so specific that it does not need to be any longer.

And you will find, near the wall, a print in the stone—a handprint, pressed deep into the rock, the detail of the fingers exact, the palm unmistakable, and the rock around it is not cracked or broken but simply shaped, as if the hand was pressed into the stone while the stone was soft, like clay, before it hardened. The print is several centimeters deep. It is warm to the touch, warmer than the rock around it.

There are no other prints. Just the one hand, flat against the stone, as if someone leaned their weight on the rock for a moment, long ago, and left a permanent mark.

---

The peak dominates the horizon.

You have been walking toward it, or near it, or around it, for what feels like a very long time. It is hard to say how long, because the light has not changed. The dim gray illumination is constant, and the sun, if there is one, does not move. Your internal sense of time is unreliable here. You have no shadow, or rather your shadow is so faint and diffuse it might as well not exist. Everything is illuminated from all directions at once.

The peak is not the color of the other rocks. It is a deep charcoal, almost black, and its surface is smooth and glassy, like obsidian. It rises in a series of terraced steps, each step a vertical wall fifty or a hundred meters high, separated by narrow ledges. The terraces are too regular to be natural. They look machined, though there are no machines here. The peak is still rising—you can feel it through the ground, a slight additional vibration that gets stronger as you approach, a hum that is slightly different in frequency from the general drone.

Around the base of the peak, the vegetation is denser. The translucent trees grow in thick clusters, their branches interlocking overhead, the phosphorescent silver veins pulsing in slow synchrony. Here, the air is a few degrees warmer, and the smell is different—something organic but not floral, like stone that has been heated, or the smell of a forest after a lightning strike, but without the rain.

And the sound here is different, too. The bass drone is still there, but over it, you can hear something else: a high keening, a whistling, like wind through a crack, except there is no wind. The sound cuts through the drone like a wire pulled tight. It is the sound of the peak growing, you think, or the sound of the world straining against some kind of pressure, or the sound of something trying to be born.

You see, near the base of the peak, another structure: a low platform of fitted stones, larger than the wall, with steps leading up to it on one side. On the platform is a slab of the black glassy rock, polished to a mirror finish. Someone has set small stones on the slab in a pattern—a spiral, or a series of concentric rings, or a representation of something you cannot understand. The pattern is old. Some of the stones have fallen, but no one has replaced them, which suggests the builders are gone, or they no longer care, or the pattern is not meant to be maintained but to erode.

The platform faces the peak. If you stand on it, facing the black terraces, the sound seems to focus, to gather itself behind your eyes. You feel the vibration in your skull. The reality breakdown is concentrated here, too—the peak seems to have multiple edges, multiple outlines, a fringe of possible versions of itself that shimmer at the edges of your vision. Looking at it too directly makes your eyes ache. You find yourself looking at it sideways, trying to catch it off guard.

And then, very slowly, you begin to understand something.

The place is not violating the laws of reality. It is writing them. The landscape is a work in progress, and the sound is the sound of the work being done. The vegetation is not living in spite of the lack of water; it is living according to a new biology, one that has not been finalized yet, one in which life feeds on geological process rather than on sunlight and water. The built structures are not attempts to survive; they are attempts to understand, to record, to map the rules as they come into being. The peak is not a mountain. It is a spine, a keel, a central bone around which the world is assembling itself.

And the handprint in the rock was pressed there by someone who knew this—who found that the stone was soft at certain moments, when the world was still settling, and pressed their hand into it, not to leave a mark but to feel the moment of becoming, to be part of it, to touch the raw process of creation before it hardened into permanence.

You put your own hand against the rock. It is hard now. But beneath your palm, very faintly, you can feel a vibration that is not the drone—a slower, deeper rhythm, like a pulse, or the shifting of tectonic plates, or the slow expansion of a world that is still deciding how big it wants to be.

The light does not change. The sound does not stop. The peaks continue to rise, centimeter by invisible centimeter, century by invisible century. The vegetation pulses its slow mineral heartbeat. The black peak keens its high wire of sound.

And you stand there, the only animal in a landscape without animals, the only thing that moves at a speed the world can notice, in a place that is still—very slowly, very loudly, very patiently—being made.
