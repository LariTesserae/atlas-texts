The drowned quarter lies beneath a warm, weed-covered sea, inside a limestone sinkhole no wider than a cathedral nave. The surface is not far above—only a few seconds’ swim through green water—but no air reaches the town. Daylight comes down as a kind of brown-green stain, filtered first through a floating canopy of golden weed, then through a permanent haze of pale sediment, and finally through the water itself. At street level it is dim, as if evening never lifts, though the water is warm as a drawn bath.

The town is called the Boiler Gardens on older charts, or sometimes the Thirty Houses, because no one has ever counted more than thirty doors that still show their outline. It sits entirely submerged at about fifteen meters’ depth, built into the terraced walls of the sinkhole. The bowl is small, intimate, barely a hundred and fifty paces across, but it is packed to the point of saturation. Every ledge, every narrow shelf of bedrock, has been claimed by masonry or machinery. The buildings share walls. Their roofs almost touch. The streets are not streets anymore but slots of still water between continuous façades, hardly two paces wide, paved with hexagonal slate blocks that have been rounded to smoothness by decades of slow current.

The buildings are small but tall for their footprints: three and four storeys of dark limestone block below, riveted iron plate above, with curved clay-tile roofs still holding their shape under a fur of algae. Nothing is newly ruined. The stone is pitted, the iron has grown a thick skin of rust and carbonate that now protects it, the tiles are worn sea-glossy. This is a settled place, a drowned place that has come into its own. It is not collapsing. It is occupied by the water rather than destroyed by it.

The vegetation is lush, but not in the way of a green garden. It is lush the way a reef is lush: every surface colonized, layered, stitched over. Pale feathery algae grows from the gutters and waves only when someone passes. Kelp blades fan outward across doorways like awnings. The lower courses of every wall are crusted with rust-coloured bacterial mats and pale calcium deposits. Higher up, where the water is slightly fresher, the growth turns a brighter green, dense and shaggy. There is almost no bare stone. Even the windowsills hold gardens of red algae and small seaweeds that need almost no light. The deepest corners give off a faint blue-green glow from bacterial films—not enough to see by, only enough to know the walls are alive.

In the center of the bowl stands the Pump Hall, a larger building with a tall chimney rising almost to the mouth of the sinkhole, though still far below the sea’s surface. The chimney is not black with soot. Its upper half is green with algae, its lower half white with worm-tubes and mineral crust. From a hundred small flanged pipes along its sides, slow threads of warm water rise, carrying a faint shimmer of sulphur, so the water above the chimney looks slightly out of focus, as if heat were leaking from the town itself.

The industry here has not died. It has only slowed nearly to the point of stillness. A great iron waterwheel set into a side-channel of the sinkhole turns in a current so weak it completes one revolution every four or five minutes. Its axle drives a train of wooden cogs and leather belts that have become gelatinous with water; they slip, they sag, but they move. Inside the Pump Hall, two green-bronze pistons stroke back and forth perhaps twice an hour, pumping not air but brine from one underground chamber to another. Their purpose is no longer clear. It may be that the town is still carrying out the last command of a dead foreman, or that the machines are simply keeping themselves lubricated. When the pistons reverse, a faint vibration passes through the stone, felt more than heard.

There is light of a second kind. Some of the old street lamps still work: brass lanterns bolted to doorway arches, their bulbs glowing a deep orange-red like coal embers seen through tea. They are powered by a row of glass battery jars on the upper floor of the Pump Hall—great amber cylinders filled with bubbling liquid and copper plates. The charge is renewed, very slowly, by the waterwheel and by the difference in salt between two layers of water. It is enough to keep a few dozen bulbs glowing at half-current. The rest of the town is lit only by the weak day above and by the blue-green sheen of the bacterial walls.

Sound is scarce. The water swallows most noise before it can travel far. What remains is felt in the bones: the deep tick of the waterwheel’s pawl, a soft clank from the Pump Hall every half hour, the slow creak of old iron adjusting to temperature. Occasionally a bubble the size of a fist detaches from a roof vent and rises with a low glug that sounds almost like a word. There are no fish sounds, no snapping shrimp, no distant whale calls. If you kneel on the slate street, the only sounds are the rasp of your own breath and the distant, patient hum of the battery jars. The best way to hear the town, though, is to put a hand on any iron pipe. The metal carries the waterwheel’s rhythm from street to street, as if the whole settlement were a single instrument.

Fauna is rare. The water is not lifeless, but visible animals are few. A handful of pale copepods hang in the beam of a lamp. Once in a long while, a single blind eel, ribbon-thin and white as a tendon, drifts out of a drain and curls around the base of the waterwheel for an hour, then vanishes. There are no schools of fish. The larger shells in the sediment are old, pitted, empty. The ecology is mostly one of plants and bacteria and mineral films. The animals that remain seem to have agreed to be scarce.

But the water itself is strange. It is warm from some deep spring under the limestone, yet not uniform. At the level of the roofs, the water is nearly fresh, fed by an underground stream that leaks through the western wall. Near the street, it is salty and dense, a tongue of sea water working its way in through a collapsed tunnel at the north end. The two layers mix only along a wavering, mirror-like boundary, and it is along that boundary that most of the lush growth occurs. The town is layered like that: an upper city of pale green weed and fresh water, a lower city of red and brown algae and salt, and between them a shifting skin where the light bends and the water tastes of both.

The doors and windows are all open, but not from damage. They seem to have been built without doors, or the doors were removed so long ago that their hinges have grown into the stone. The buildings are accessible as rooms, but they are not empty. Each one contains some piece of machinery: a small brass pump, a cast-iron stove, a rack of glass bottles, an iron bed frame that has become a garden of pale tube worms. The place feels less like a town where people lived than like a piece of infrastructure that someone briefly inhabited, as if the workers camped inside the factory. Now the machines are the only occupants.

Time moves oddly here. The waterwheel’s slow rotation is the closest thing to a clock. The tides outside raise and lower the sea level by only a hand’s breadth over many hours, and because the sinkhole is nearly enclosed, the tide inside is even smaller—a slow breathing of the water column, a change in pressure rather than a visible current. That small pressure change is enough to open and close a set of flap valves in the Pump Hall. When they open, a cloud of fine sediment lifts from the street and settles again over the next hour. Ridges of silt have formed between the slate slabs, following the direction of the valves. Erosion here is not destructive; it is a finishing process.

The overall impression is of a small, full, patient world. Everything is close and occupied. There is no open water in the traditional sense, only avenues of water between green walls and iron galleries. The density is not just of buildings but of growth, of encrustation, of slow machinery, of warm particles suspended in the dim light. Nothing changes quickly. It can only shift a little at a time, over years, while the waterwheel creaks, the battery jars bubble, and the orange lamps burn below the weed-choked surface.
