The high plateau begins where the land stops rising and starts simply continuing. It is not a mountain landscape; it is the roof of a continent, a tableland lifted so long ago that its edges have been worn into wide, soft stairs. The air is thin enough to feel in the lungs but wet enough to soften the cold. This is a country of cloud and moss, a saturated plain the size of an inland sea.

Water is not a feature here; it is the medium. It stands in the hollows, crawls through the peat, drips from the underside of every leaf, and rises from the ground as steam. The soil is not soil but a living sponge many meters deep. Where the water surfaces, it is the color of strong tea or rust or, in a few shallow basins, clear as gin over a bed of white quartz sand. In the cool morning, every cushion of moss is beaded with condensation. By afternoon, the fog has not so much lifted as thinned into a bright silver haze; the light is normal, diffuse, without a visible sun, as if the whole sky were one soft lamp.

The vegetation is packed to the point of architectural density. There is no bare ground. Sphagnum and feather moss form deep hummocks; liverworts plaster the wetter stone; lichens crust every exposed surface in gray-green and rust-red. Dwarf willow and bog birch grow as horizontal mats, their stems hidden under a rug of sedge and cottongrass. In the soggiest places, sundews and butterworts lie like tiny red and green stars, sticky with the morning’s catch. Orchids no taller than a thumb push up among the mosses. The density is not only in the plants but in the tiny ecosystems they hold: water pooled in the cups of moss, midge larvae in the water, mites on the midges, spores on the mites. The place is saturated with presence.

It is also cool—not bitter, but a damp cold that never quite releases. The water is colder than the air. Breath fogs. The moss holds the chill like a wet blanket. Even in full daylight, the air has the raw edge of early spring. Yet life is abundant. The animals are small, ground-keeping, numerous. Lemmings and voles tunnel through the moss; their runs fill with water and become miniature canals. A fox the color of burnt heather moves along the edge of a flark, pausing with one paw lifted, listening not with ears but with the fine hairs of its face. Snipe probe the mud with long silver bills; pipits move in sudden, silent bursts. Overhead, a rough-legged hawk drifts. The insects are so many that their wings make a low, gauzy hum only when you kneel close. Otherwise the animals are quiet—this is a landscape that absorbs sound.

The silence is one of the first things you notice. The wind has almost nothing to scrape against; it passes over moss and through fog with no friction. The streams are too shallow to babble. What sounds exist are soft and close: the drip of condensation from a sedge, the wet snap of a root under a fox’s paw, the faint hiss of steam from a vent, the subsonic boom of a snipe’s tail feathers far away. There is also a deeper sound, a hum so low it is felt more than heard, as if the ground itself were vibrating at the edge of hearing. It does not break the quiet; it is the quiet’s floor.

This is where the place becomes strange. The plateau is not volcanic, but heat rises from a granitic body deep below. The water that sinks through the peat finds that heat and returns as steam through a thousand small vents. The vents are not built, but they look it. Mineral water has cast them into pipes and flanges, terraces and ribbed cones. They hiss faintly. Some are no wider than a finger; others open into basins of pale sinter that overflow in thin, mineral-laced sheets. The steam condenses immediately in the cold air, so every vent wears a small cloud of its own. The water is acid; it leaches metals from the rock and lays them down again as metallic films along the edges of streams—copper-green, iron-black, manganese-purple. The place has the feel of an industrial district that was never constructed, only grown. There are no walls, no tools, no cut timber, no path. Everything that looks like machinery is the work of water, chemistry, heat, and time.

This natural industry extends into the living things. The saturated peat, full of organic acids and dissolved salts, behaves as a weak battery. Passing storms charge the ground; the charge leaks slowly through the root network. In certain basins the hair on your arms rises. The mosses grow in concentric, circuit-like patterns, tracing the flow of current. The water in a few hollows is so charged that it bubbles quietly along the stems of sedges—not from heat but from electrolysis, splitting itself into hydrogen and oxygen in the dim light. The air near these places tastes of ozone and wet iron. The animals seem to read the field: the snipe senses the electric whisper of a worm in the mud; the fox pauses, as if taking a voltage. The whole plateau is a slow electrical engine, running without a maker.

For all this activity, the landscape is old and settled. Its valleys are shallow; its streams meander; its rocks are smooth and fluted by centuries of acid water. No fresh cliffs, no raw talus. The erosion has reached an equilibrium: what weather removes, the plants and mineral springs replace. The topography is in balance, a mature face worn by water and time. The scale, however, is not human. The plain is large enough to generate its own weather. Clouds form on its western edge, cross it over a day, and rain on the eastern rim. A wind can blow for a hundred kilometers over unbroken moss. The horizon disappears into vapor, and the land simply continues, hummock after hummock, pool after pool, until the curve of the earth takes it away.

In the end, the place is not a wilderness in the sense of empty. It is overcrowded with process. Water cycles from cloud to moss to peat to steam and back. Plants grow over their own dead, building the ground upward. Animals graze and burrow and are eaten. The vents deposit stone; the streams shift; the electrical field hums; the fog thickens and thins. It is a landscape that feels like a single vast organism, or a machine assembled by deep time—quiet, cold, wet, alive, and wholly untouched by human hands.
