The place is not a peak so much as a lifted country. At first the mind refuses the scale: you see across fifty miles of pale stone and think it a dry lakebed, then you notice the fins, walls, streets, pits. It is an ancient seabed, raised to a height where the sky is a dark blue at noon and the air is thin enough to feel behind the eyes. The altitude is the only weather that matters; everything else follows from it.

The floor is limestone, sometimes dolomite, bleached almost white, in places varnished black by millennia of mineral film. It has not been shattered by recent ice; it has been dissolved, fluted, planed. The stone runs in long parallel ridges from west to east, as if the plateau had been combed. Between the ridges are stone corridors—twenty feet wide, a hundred feet deep—whose walls are undercut and full of pockets. There are natural arches, windows, swallow-holes, cisterns, pillars. It is not chaotic; it is ordered, the way a boneyard or a pipe organ is ordered. The erosion here is old and complete. There is no fresh talus, no cracked-off block waiting to fall. Every loose piece has already fallen, found its place, and been rounded by the wind. The rock is in its own state now, done.

The emptiness is not vacancy exactly. It is spacing. There is a great deal of world between one feature and the next: a single basin of black water, a single cushion plant, a single standing column. The rest is pavement and wind. You can walk for twenty minutes without having to step over anything. The plateau does not hide; it simply has almost nothing to hide. What vegetation exists is small and old. Cushions of moss campion and saxifrage press into the lee edges of stones, each plant a tight hemisphere barely larger than a hand, taprooted deep into a crack. Lichens—orange, sooty, apple-green—stain the limestone in slow maps. There is a kind of pale grass that grows only at the mouths of the damp corridors, where the wind drops just enough for dew to linger. Nothing here grows tall; there is no tree, no shrub above the ankle, no shade but stone.

The light is tremendous. The air is too thin to soften it. The sky is often clear, and the sun pours down with a brightness that makes the rock seem to glow from within. But the temperature does not rise much above cool; the heat of the sun and the cold of the high air balance at about five degrees, maybe seven in the hollows. The brightness is dry except where the moisture gathers. And moisture is present, though never abundant. Each night—or in the low, steady cycle that passes for night—the cold condenses out of the air onto every stone surface. The plateau sweats. The patina of water runs down the flutes and collects in the solution pans, the cisterns, the long shallow runnels. Once in a while, a band of cloud lowers and touches the stone for an hour; it is not rain so much as a thick wet breath that pearls on every surface and then stops. By the middle of the brightening, much of the moisture has gone back into the air, except in the shaded slots and the deepest basins, where water sits all through the day. It tastes of stone and faintly of moss. The dampness is intermittent, rhythmic, and never enough to flood; it is a place of pockets, not rivers.

Then there is the sound. It is not a background sound; it is the first fact. The wind comes from the west, always from the west, always at the same speed, a single even pressure like a hand on the chest. Where it crosses the fluted limestone, the plateau becomes an instrument. The tall hollow columns hum at one pitch; the narrow slots whistle at another; the round blowholes thump at their boundaries; the water-filled potholes add a glassy throb. Together they make a sustained chord, a roar like a weir, like a turbine hall, like ten thousand enormous bees, but not chaotic—tuned. There is a low note that vibrates in the bones and a high ring at the edge of hearing. It never changes. It does not gust, swell, or fade. It is the same standing chord at dawn, noon, dusk. You can hear it for a full minute before recognizing that no single sound is separate from it. After a while, the noise becomes a kind of silence: the body stops arguing with it.

Life is abundant here, but it is arranged in the gaps. The sky above the plateau is full of birds. White-rumped swifts knife through the stone corridors, never touching the ground except at the nest cavities. Choughs ride the standing wind inches above the rim, playing in the pressure wave. A pair of dark eagles hangs over the western icefields. In the rock cracks and among the boulders, there are pikas and highland marmots; their alarm calls are high, sharp, and pitched to cut above the roar. The water pockets are busy in their way: tiny black frogs sit half-submerged, fairy shrimp flick in the cold pans, caddisflies crawl in the runnels. There are spiders in the dry overhangs. The fauna is not evenly spread; it clusters at the damp points, at the crevice exits, at the updrafts, then crosses the stone between them in quick, exposed arcs. The plateau itself looks empty, but the air and the crannies are full.

No one builds here. There are no walls, no shelters, no cairns, no dug-out steps, no fire rings, no carved marks that are not animal. The place is undeveloped in the human sense, and perhaps in every sense that would leave a roof against the sky. But there is a primitive technology among its inhabitants. The highland jackdaws use pebble hammers to break open the shells of small snails that emerge in the damp moss, and they maintain flat stone anvils polished white in the center from use. One kind of mouse-sized rock hyrax keeps a simple cache of plant matter under a slab, ferried from the fog-grass patches and arranged to dry. A species of pale frog digs a small channel from its cistern to a second, lower basin with its hind feet, letting water flow and replenish the lower pool. These are not structures, not artifacts made to outlast a season; they are small behaviors, repeated, barely visible until you look.

The strangeness comes from the erasure of echo, and from the stone’s long memory. The sound fills every space, but a shout does not come back; the fluted walls take the frequencies and break them apart. The place feels acoustically padded even as it is deafening. Some of the standing columns are eroded into stacked discs, like great spines; the wind has worn the soft beds away and left the harder ledges. They look deliberated, almost ceremonial. The basins are often perfect circles, as though drilled. One learns not to assign intention to any of it. The shapes are the result of water and wind agreeing on a long, unchanging compromise. The landscape is in equilibrium. The wind that erodes is exactly balanced by the stone’s hardness; the moisture that dissolves is exactly balanced by the plateau’s drainage; the sparse plants hold the thin soil so that it does not move. Nothing is falling, nothing is filling in. The same chord has been blowing, perhaps, for ten thousand years, and the plateau has not needed to change a line of it.

The dynamic is not stillness exactly; it is a fixed vibration. There is movement—the birds, the frogs, the wind, the daily film of condensation—but it is all part of a standing wave, a closed loop. The clouds come over the western icefield at the same hour, spill down the same slopes, break against the same stone, and retreat. The animals time their movements to the same wind. There is no season to speak of; every season is present in pockets. Some basins are freezing at their edges while others hold tadpoles and others are dust. The plateau does not progress. It has already become what it is, and what it is refuses to change.

If you stay until the light begins to slant—though it barely slants, so high and bright is the sky—the rock turns the color of very old paper. The sound seems to come not from the wind but from the ground itself, as if the plateau is a great instrument being played by its own persistence. The empty distances fill with that chord. The few damp surfaces gleam. Somewhere a blackbird of a species found only here drops a snail onto a polished stone, and the little crack of the shell is the only separate sound you will hear all day.
