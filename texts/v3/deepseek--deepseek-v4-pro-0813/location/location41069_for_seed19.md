The Plateau of the Held Breath

At first you mistake it for a dry lakebed. Then for an old peneplain, a field of cobbles, a salt pan, a ruined road. Each of those is true, and none of them is enough.

The place is high—so high that the air has become a thin, cool fluid and your breath comes shallow without you noticing why. The elevation is extreme, but the relief is almost nothing. This was mountain country once, a range of folded stone and sharp ridge. Now it has been worn down to a nearly perfect flatness, an end-stage landscape. The mountains are gone clear away. What remains is a tableland at the top of the world, open to every horizon, so level that a dropped stone would not roll. The horizon is a faint, curved line laid across pale dust.

There is no water. Not in the soil, not in the air, not in the stone. No dew, no frost, no seep, no stream. The dryness is total. The ground is a mineral crust of salt, gypsum, clay, fine dust and flakes of ancient chert, compacted into something like fired tile. In places the salt has formed small hexagonal polygons, but they do not crack or curl. The air smells of gypsum and old copper, faintly alkaline, like licking a battery. If you press your palm to the ground, it is cool, dry, and faintly resonant.

The cool is steady, not bitter. High-altitude light lies on the plain like ordinary day—pale blue at the zenith, milk-white at the horizon. It is not a purple sky, not a black sun. The light is normal, and that is one of the most unsettling things about the place. It makes the wrongness feel less like a vision and more like a fact.

You do not notice the plants at first. The land seems barren, mineral, empty. Then you see them: sparse tufts of grey-green and ochre, like twists of wire or bundles of desiccated hair, growing singly a few yards apart. They do not sway, because there is no wind. They are alive in the way deep lichen is alive—too slow to see, too old to be dead. Some grow from the seams between the stones. Some root into the backs of the animals, though you do not yet know the stones are animals.

Because the ground is not entirely stone. Once you kneel, you see that the cobbles are not cobbles. They are the backs of living things.

The plain is paved with them. Flat, shield-shaped creatures each the size of a hand or a foot, fitted together so tightly that almost no bare soil shows through. Their dorsal plates are mineralized and pitted by old weather, but the seams between them are not cracks in rock. They are living joints. Inside each seam runs a line of pale flesh, a slow-pulsing spiracle, occasionally an eye sealed to a slit. The eye is yellow or pale blue. It does not blink. It does not need to.

There are more of them than you can count. The density is enormous. They cover every visible reach of the plateau, an unbroken pavement of bodies from here to the edge of sight. Beneath them, in the narrow spaces under the soil, there is another layer of life—pale, soft, eyeless things that only touch the surface once in years. Above them, the air is full of tiny flying things, small as dust, with wings like flakes of mica. They are not flying. They hang motionless, in such numbers that the light seems slightly thickened, granular. When you move, they part without sound and close behind you into exactly the shapes they held before.

And there are larger animals. You mistake them at first for standing stones. They are herd-beasts, six-legged, long-necked, narrow-shouldered, tall as a person or taller. They stand among the pavement animals at regular intervals, each one exactly a body-length from the next, all facing the same direction. Their hides are weathered pale on one side, darker on the other. Salt has grown up their legs like pale stockings. They do not move. Their eyes are open and faintly glazed. A few have small young standing beneath their bellies, equally still. The herd is held in mid-existence, as if the plateau was paused during a migration and never started again.

The overwhelming life does not end there. There are more living bodies per cubic yard than in a rainforest, but the impression is of absolute stillness. The animals are not dead. They are suspended in a stable equilibrium so old that movement has become unnecessary. Their metabolisms are slow beyond measure. A breath may take a season. A heartbeat may take a year. The air is full of them, the ground is full of them, the spaces beneath the ground are full of them. They are layered, near-touching, saturated. The plain is not simply inhabited. It is occupied to its final capacity.

Human or near-human builders left marks on the plateau. The structures are scattered, never clustered. A low drystone hut every few miles, circular, with a doorway facing east. Around them lie tools: knapped flint scrapers, awls of bone, braided sinew, a broken clay bowl. The technology is simple edge-and-bone craft, hand-worked, patient. In one hut there is a single copper pin, green with age. Some roofs still hold, made of ribs and hide and flat stones. Other buildings have collapsed, but the dry air has preserved them so well that they seem less like ruins than like places that have simply finished.

No one is in the huts, or no one you can see. A few hold seated figures in the shadows, knees drawn up, skin dried to the consistency of wood. You cannot tell if they are dead or only as still as the herd. They wear necklaces of animal eye-plates. Their tools are laid out beside them, as if they intended to rise and work again, and then forgot for a thousand years.

The quiet is immense. There is no wind, no water, no insect drone, no bird. The creatures in the air do not buzz. Your footsteps make a dry crunch that falls away without echo. When you stand still, you hear only your own pulse. Here and there, if you listen for minutes, a faint tick runs through the ground—like cooling stone, like a glass jar settling—but nothing changes. It is not true silence. It is the sound of a world with nothing left to say.

The longer you stay, the more the place begins to fray. A cairn near the horizon keeps the same distance no matter how long you walk toward it. The same standing stone appears twice—once on your left, once ahead—and they are not two stones. Shadows sometimes point the wrong way, not away from the sun but toward the nearest animal, or toward the place where an animal used to be. In the corner of your eye the pavement seems to ripple; the great herd seems to have shifted in the last second. But when you look directly, all is exactly still. You find your own footprints in the dust ahead of you, though you have not yet walked there.

The world’s rules are worn thin here. The plateau is so old, so planed, so settled into its final form, that sequence has been replaced by repetition. Identities repeat. Location becomes uncertain. The animals are not many species; they are one species repeated into every shape, or many species collapsed into one shape. The plain is not covered with fauna. The fauna and the plain are two names for one thing.

The ecosystem is not in motion. It is a fixed equilibrium. Nothing hunts. Nothing migrates. Nothing grows beyond its assigned place. Energy enters as light and heat and leaves as a faint far-infrared glow, but the material loop is closed. The sparse plants feed on the exudates of the pavement animals. The pavement animals exchange minerals through their ventral surfaces. The larger animals stand in that mineral breath and need nothing else. Water is not used; life here runs on non-aqueous solvents, a thin syrup of perchlorate and glycerol in living tissue, so it cannot freeze or evaporate. Reproduction is replacement, not increase: when one animal finally fades, another is already in its place, identical, as if the world had never noticed the difference.

The scattered huts may not have been built here. They may have been worn into being, the way a mountain becomes a hill becomes dust, until a shape becomes a house. Or the builders may still be here, standing in the shadowed doorways, waiting for the one event that never comes.

The only true change is your own presence. And the plateau has already begun to erase it.
