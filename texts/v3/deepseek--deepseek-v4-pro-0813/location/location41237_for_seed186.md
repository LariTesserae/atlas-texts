At four and a half thousand meters, the land is an old table, worn smooth by weather that has forgotten how to be violent. The air is thin and cold, a few degrees above freezing at best. The sky is mostly not sky but a lid of wet cloud, and the light underneath is a grey rumor. It is never fully black, but it is never bright: a permanent dusk that has been there longer than the mountains.

The place is high, but it is not jagged. Erosion has had its way for so long that the land has reached an equilibrium. Ridges have been rounded to whalebacks; valleys have broadened to shallow basins. There are no raw cliffs, no loose scree, only a few places where the ground has collapsed into a warm vent. The streams are old and braided, running only after fog and then sinking again. The terrain is open. When the cloud lifts, you can see the plateau roll away for forty or fifty kilometers—benches of stone, thin turf, scattered pools—until it finally dissolves into more cloud. But openness is theoretical most days. The fog closes in and turns the world into a series of rooms.

Water is present but not standing everywhere. It arrives as fog and fine rain, condenses on leaves and stones, and runs down to gather in hollows. The ground between hollows is damp rather than wet: thin soil over gravel, soft to the first knuckle, then firm. Along the bases of old rock ribs there are seeps; in bedrock depressions there are black pools that never grow large enough to be called lakes. After a heavy fog, small streams form and run for hours, then vanish. The place is not a swamp. It is a high, cold collection of wet surfaces, hidden pockets, and dripping edges.

Vegetation is lush in the way a pelt is lush—dense at the surface, but not deep. To call it thin is not to say sparse; the moss lies thick enough to sink a hand, and the leaves form a continuous canopy at ankle height. The plants have learned to comb water from the air and warmth from the earth. Mosses and lichens cover the rock in a continuous fur of green, red, and copper. Dwarf willow and heather root in the cracks, spreading horizontally. Sundews and butterworts grow in the dampest pockets, their sticky leaves catching insects that are nearly invisible. Around the warm vents, ferns unfurl into the cold air, then die back when the ground shifts. There are no tall trees, only krummholz—solitary, wind-bent, each one a black scribble against the grey. But the lushness is a skin. Between one patch of moss and the next there may be bare gravel; between one dwarf tree and another, a hundred meters of open ground. The life is spread like stars, not crowded into a forest.

The animals are abundant but widely spaced. You can walk for an hour and see nothing, and then the fog shifts and there are thirty pale grazers on the moor, long-necked and heavy-coated, their breath visible. They move from seep to seep and vent field to vent field in slow circuits that take years to close. Small rodents with huge eyes tunnel through the thin turf; their trails cross the open ground like the lines of a map. Frogs live in the warm pools. When the cold takes them, they freeze solid and wait; when the water warms again, they thaw and call once—a single low note, like a drop hitting a pan—and then silence. Birds are mostly quiet: white finches working through seed heads, a nightjar hawking insects in the dimness, a pair of pale eagles high enough to be only interruptions in the fog. Predators are rare but regular: a cat with fur the color of wet stone; a weasel that moves like a muscle of cloud. The ecosystem is active, but its pace is slow, as if the cold has thickened time. Predator and prey play out over days, not hours. The herds move, the rodents dig, the frogs freeze and thaw, the birds come and go.

There are no built structures here—no wall, no post, no path, no tool. But the place feels industrial. In the basins, the ground exhales. Vents hiss with steam and gas, so faint that the sound is more a pressure than a noise. Around them, sulfur crusts in yellow rings; iron-rich springs stain the rock orange; black pools bubble with methane. Some vents have caught fire. The flames are small, no taller than a hand, blue at the base and yellow at the tip, and they do not go out. They flicker in the fog like pilot lights. The air near them smells of kerosene, struck matches, hot metal, and wet coal. Some pools carry an oily film that curls into rainbows. In one vent field, the ground is covered with hollow mineral tubes—casts of roots that rotted away, now chimneys for gas. When the wind crosses their open ends, they make a low, fluting tone that seems to come from beneath the earth. The landscape itself is the engine: magma and buried coal, natural gas and hot water, running a slow factory without walls. It does not break; it idles.

The darkness is not empty. The tips of certain mosses glow a faint green, just enough to trace the shape of the ground. Blind pale fish rise to the surface of warm pools when gas bubbles turn the water over. In the coldest hollows, the frogs’ frozen bodies show a faint electric shimmer when the sky pulses. The sky pulses only rarely—not lightning, but a greenish, silent undulation of the cloud, as if the gas below had seeped into the sky and caught there. The whole place is strange in a way that feels native. It is not haunted; it is alive, but by rules that are not entirely known.

The sound is almost nothing. Drip of condensation. Hiss of gas. Soft crush of moss underfoot. Plink of a bubble breaking. The rustle of a vole in the moss is audible thirty meters away. The wingbeats of a nightjar are a soft thump. There is no chorus, no roar. The wind does not blow; it breathes. Far away, a herd moves, and the sound is like a long exhalation, then gone. Silence here is not absence; it is the medium through which every small event must pass.

The land is always working. Fog rolls in and out on a twice-daily pulse, pushed by the mountain’s temperature differences. Rain comes every few days and stops. The vents flare and subside; a new warm pool opens when a crust collapses, and within a season it is colonized by ferns, then by frogs, then by birds. The moss grows outward by millimeters a year. The streams shift their braids. The herds return after years. The world is not frozen; it is in motion, but the motion is patient.

The rules of this place are few and consistent. The air is cold; the ground is damp; the dark is long. Heat comes not from the sun but from below, and life has learned to follow it. Space between presences is vast, but the ground is full underfoot. Sound is quiet, not because nothing happens, but because everything that happens is slow and soft. Nothing here was built, yet the land runs like a machine. The light is scarce, but the place does not need to be seen; it needs to be felt—the warmth under the moss, the hiss in the chest, the green glow at the edge of vision.
