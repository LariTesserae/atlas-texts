**The High Submergence**

Call it the High Submergence. It is a plateau the size of a continental interior, lifted above the surrounding seafloor by a plume of hot rock, but so freshly raised that it still has the raw look of a fracture in the planet’s skin. The water over it is not a shallow film but a true ocean—cold, gray-green, everywhere—so the landscape is less a land than a drowned tableland. Its highest knobs break the surface in lines of black, unweathered stone. From a distance they look like the broken teeth of a jaw. The elevation is high—thousands of meters above the old seabed—but the ocean is higher still, making this a high-altitude sea.

The rock is new. There is almost no sand, no rounded cobble, no soil. Every surface is angular and glassy: black basalt, sometimes red-brown where hydrothermal fluids have oxidized it, sometimes shot through with veins of quartz that branch like lightning. The stone fractures into thin plates and hexagonal pillars. It is still cooling, and the rain hisses where it touches warmer patches. In places, the ground is strewn with obsidian blades and fresh volcanic bombs. Because the land is rising faster than water and wind can erode it, there are no valleys carved by streams, no beaches, no alluvial fans. What water does is not etching but rearranging: it moves through a network of fissures, pools, and ledges, always finding a new route when the land shifts.

The water is everywhere, and it is loud. Rain falls not in showers but in a permanent gray sheet, driven sideways by wind that howls off the open ocean. The droplets are cold—just above freezing—and heavy, hitting the stone with a continuous roar like a jet engine. Where the rain drains off the plateau edges, it forms waterfalls that fall for hundreds of meters into lower basins, their thunder merging into one deep note. On the shallow top, standing waves appear and hold in place, as if the water is being sculpted by some unseen pressure. The ocean surface is not calm. Long, slow swells roll across the plateau, breaking violently when they hit a hidden ridge. The sound is not just audible; it is physical. It shudders through the rock and makes the water jump in small, regular pulses.

The air is cool, maybe four or five degrees Celsius, and full of spray. The light is ordinary overcast daylight—bright enough to cast shadows, but the sun is a pale blur behind a sky that never clears. The clouds are low and fast, forming and dissolving as the warm vents below fight the cold air above. There are no trees, no birds, no insects. The only living things are not animals at all: sparse, stubborn films of photosynthetic growth—purple-gray, olive, sometimes bright copper-green—that grow in thin crusts on wet rock. In sheltered pools, hair-like strands wave in the current, each strand no thicker than a line of ink. They are not plants as you would know them; they are more like living mineral skins. They survive by catching light and minerals from the water, and they do not form meadows, only patches. The vegetation is so sparse that from a few meters away the rock looks barren.

The absence of animals is uncanny. The water is clear enough to see into, but there are no fish, no krill, no floating larvae. The air is empty of wings. The only movements are water, stone, and the sway of the sparse mats. The emptiness is volumetric: a given cubic kilometer contains only rock, water, a little green film, and the constant rain. A person standing there would feel like the only animal on the planet, or like a trespasser in a place that was not made for creatures.

And yet it is not a dead world. It is a world under construction. The plateau is rising in fits. Every few hours the ground trembles; cracks open with a sound like splintering bone, and water pours into them, flashing to steam where it hits hot rock. Geysers erupt without warning, blasting columns of mineral-charged water high into the rain. New lava stalls and cools on the flanks, forming smooth black domes that shatter into slabs as they cool. The water level itself is unstable; the sea sloshes back and forth across the plateau in seiches so slow they look like tides, flooding a ridge and then abandoning it. A person would learn not to trust any ground as permanent.

There are no buildings, no roads, no ruins, no artifacts. If technology exists here, it is not in the form of made things. It is in the behavior of the water and the rock. The rainwater is not normal: it gathers in pools and then flows, sometimes uphill, following paths that seem to have been chosen rather than eroded. The mineral films on the rocks give off faint, brief pulses of light—not enough to see by, but enough to pattern the water with a dim, shifting lattice. The basalt cracks in long, parallel lines that resemble circuit traces. The water droplets do not always fall straight; they sometimes curve around invisible boundaries, as if the air itself were channeling them. The entire plateau could be a vast, slow machine, a planet-scale engine of geology and water, building itself without builders. If so, its purpose is not legible. It might have been left running. The sparse vegetation might be the first layer of an oxygenating skin, the beginning of something that never arrived.

The rules of this place are simple. Water is the main transport system, but it is not the master; the land is rising too fast to be shaped by it. The world is made of stone and water, and everything else is sparse. Nothing is stable. The sound never stops. The light is ordinary, but the place is not. It is large enough to have its own weather, its own seismic rhythm, its own slow breathing. The emptiness is not peace; it is the emptiness of a landscape that is still being born, too new for life, too loud for silence, too raw for ruins.
