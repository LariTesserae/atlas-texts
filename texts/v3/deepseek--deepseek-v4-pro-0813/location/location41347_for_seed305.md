The place, if it has a name, is not a name you would find on any map. It is not a place that has been named, only encountered. The first thing you would notice is the light. It is not sunlight so much as a general blue-white glare, too bright to look at, the kind of light that makes a welding arc seem tame. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, scattered by ice crystals and steam, so that shadows are weak and edges are etched as if by a knife. The sky is not blue; it is a pale, electric white. The light is cold, clinical, and constant. It does not warm you; it only makes everything visible.

Then the sound. It is a composite roar, a continuous layered noise that you feel in your chest. There is a low geothermal thrum, as if the earth is a drumhead. There are rhythmic hisses from steam vents, like pressure valves. There is a metallic ringing, as of thousands of small chimes, and a dense, chittering, clanking, whirring texture from the animals. Below the hiss, there is a sound like slow grinding, as if the rock is shifting in its sleep. The sound is loud enough to make conversation impossible. It is the sound of a factory floor, but there is no factory.

The place is a high plateau, lifted so far that the horizon falls away in every direction. The air is thin and cold, the cold of altitude, dry and radiant, but cut by pockets of wet warmth. The ground is black and glassy, fresh basalt that has not yet learned the shape of weather. The land looks as if it was raised and shattered only yesterday. The plateau is not a single flat table but a maze of stepped terraces, pressure ridges, and collapse pits. Some ridges are sharp enough to cut. The rock is not all black; in places it is stained yellow, green, copper-red by mineral exhalations. There are no rounded hills, no meandering valleys, no smooth river stones. Every surface is sharp: fins, spines, steps, chasms. Even the rubble is angular. Drainage is straight and abrupt, water leaping from ledge to ledge, pooling in flat basins edged with fresh fractures. It is a young landscape, still raw, with no patience yet for erosion.

But it is not dry. Moisture is everywhere, though rarely as rain. Steam issues from thousands of vents, rises, cools, and condenses on every surface. The ground is damp; water squeezes out of the vegetation mat when you step. Fog forms and lifts, drizzle falls from the steam clouds, and every depression holds a shallow pool of mineral water. The water is never still. It beads, drips, trickles, seeps. The only open water is in shallow, mineral-crusted basins that are too hot or too acidic for most life, yet even these are ringed by dense growth. It is a landscape licked by moisture, not washed by it.

Vegetation has taken this dampness and cold and made a jungle of small things. There are no tall trunks, but a continuous, dense pelt of growth that covers every surface. The plants are the color of moss, rust, verdigris, and pale blue. Their leaves are thick, waxy, sometimes translucent, often rimed with ice. They do not have roots in the normal sense; they anchor with holdfasts in the cracks, or simply grow into one another. They feed on the steam, the minerals, the electric charge. Some have leaves that unfurl when a vent opens, then close when it pauses. Others are translucent, glowing faintly with captured light. Around the steam vents, interwoven columns of growth rise several meters, shaped like cooling towers, with warm breath rising from their centers. The vegetation is lush in the way a wet animal's fur is lush: no bare patch, no gap, no visible soil. It is one organism, or many organisms so tightly woven they behave as one.

The fauna is overwhelming. Small grazers, each the size of a fist, cover the vegetation mat in a living layer, their bodies touching, their legs making a sound like rain on metal. They move in slow waves, so that the ground appears to ripple. Above them are larger creatures, some the size of sheep, some the size of buses, moving with the same slow, deliberate pace. There is no room to avoid each other; they walk over and around, their breaths mingling. The air is full of small flyers in clouds so thick they cast moving shadows. Beneath the surface, burrowers and blind crawlers are so dense that the ground vibrates. The place is saturated, packed to the limit of what life can hold. Even the rocks seem alive because they are covered in animals. There is no escape from the animals. They are in the air, on the surface, beneath, and even inside the vegetation. Some are parasites on the larger grazers; some are predators, but predation is slow and patient. The pack is so dense that even a falling droplet must pass through a layer of tiny wings or filaments before it reaches the ground.

And there is the strangeness. A hill is not a hill but a slow grazer so covered with vegetation that only its breathing gives it away. A tree is not a tree but a colony of animals, stacked and fused, with feathery appendages tasting the air. The boundary between rock, plant, and animal is not always clear. Some plants have muscle-like tissues. Some rocks are alive with microbial films that generate electric currents. The light seems to come from everywhere, so you cannot tell direction by shadows. The air smells of ozone and sulfur and wet metal. The whole place has the feel of a living machine, but not one that was designed. It is more like a body that discovered industry.

The plateau is industrial, but not built. There are no walls, no roofs, no cables, no tracks, no made artifacts. The technology is not a set of objects; it is a process. Deep beneath, a vast geothermal system drives steam through natural pipes of lava tube. The vents erupt in rhythmic blasts, like pistons. The rocks are rich in conductive minerals, and the temperature difference between the warm ground and the cold air sets up electric currents. Along the ridgelines, electrical discharges arc from point to point, crackling and glowing. The plants and animals have adapted to this electricity: some grazers have shiny metallic shells that shed sparks; the plants grow in lines that follow the currents. If you could stop the noise and look only at the patterns, you would see an assembly line: steam from a vent condenses on mineral filaments, the water drips into a trough, the trough feeds a mat of plants, the plants feed a wave of grazers, the grazers excrete minerals that are re-dissolved by the next steam burst. It is a closed loop, powered by geothermal heat and light, running at a steady, slow rate. The whole plateau hums with an industrial drone, a factory without a maker, production without products.

For all its noise and density, the plateau is slow. The animals do not hurry. The grazers move a few meters a day. The flyers drift rather than dart. The plants grow over decades. The vents erupt on schedules measured in days. The sun, which may not set, circles in a long, patient arc. The temperature barely swings; the geothermal heat buffers the cold. The landscape changes over centuries, not seasons. Erosion has not yet rounded a single edge. The water has not yet carved smooth channels. Even the animals, in their countless numbers, have not worn paths; their slow feet do not create trails. The brightest light does not change. There is no night, or only a dimming that lasts for hours. The animals do not sleep in the normal way; they pause, then resume. The slow cycles are so long that you could watch for days and see almost nothing change, yet over a year the whole plateau would have turned like a gear.

It is vast enough to have its own weather. The steam columns merge into a haze, and clouds form over the warm vents, then drift and drop snow or rain on the high ridges. The wind is generated here, not imported. The plateau extends beyond sight, a continent of high, cold, wet, living rock. It is a young, saturated, unbuilt factory, a living machine idling at a geological pace. It does not need you. It is already full.
