The place is hidden under a slope of blocky talus, in a valley that spends most of its year under snow. There is a gap between two boulders, no wider than a person, that exhales a wet, mineral breath. Through it is a descent of only a few feet, then a single chamber, no broader than a small room, entirely below the surface but only just—a pocket beneath the valley’s skin.

The chamber is shaped like the inside of a cupped hand. Twelve or fifteen paces long, half as wide, with a low roof of old ice and dark stone. The walls are not sharp; millennia of frost and slow dissolution have polished them into flutes and rounded bell-hollows. Even the fallen stones near the entrance are smooth as river cobbles, though no river has ever run here. The floor is one bed of fine-grained black stone, polished to a dull luster by a thousand thousand drips. In its center is a shallow basin no wider than a hand, ringed by a low rim of white mineral that looks like wax. Water gathers there, but not much; it is more a memory of a pool than a pool.

The first thing the dark gives you is not sight. It is the wetness. The air is just below freezing, but the water refuses to freeze. It is thick with salts and plant sugars, and it moves in a patient film over stone and leaf. Condensation runs down every surface in sheets too slow to see, gathering in beads on the tips of root-threads, in the cups of lichen, along every seam. Drips fall not from the ceiling but from leaf-tip to leaf-tip, every few minutes, a sound like a small glass bell struck once.

There is almost no light. The slot behind you holds only a faint gray suggestion, and the plants make no light themselves. Three or four points of red-violet the size of pinheads sit in the far wall, each from a smooth teardrop of unreflective material; they blink so rarely that it is easy to believe you imagined them. A few fine root-tips on the floor are just barely luminous, like old watch-paint, but they cast no glow. The dark is otherwise complete, heavy and close, and it presses at the eyes.

Yet the room is full of vegetation. It is lush beyond what the darkness should allow. Black-red moss covers every surface in thick cushions, its tiny leaves overlapping like scales, damp and cool to the touch. From the walls grow fern-like fronds of a glassy, segmented substance—not green, but the color of old bruises and dark copper, blue-black and green-black. They arch across the chamber, frond touching frond, so the middle of the room is braided with them. Hanging from the ceiling are thousands of hair-thin aerial roots, each tipped with a droplet of water; under them, the floor moss is studded with small open cups that catch the drip and hold it. The plants do not need light. They eat the rock’s slow rust and the faint electric field the old machines lay along the floor. They grow with a slowness that makes lichen seem frantic; a single frond may not extend the width of a hair in a year. But they have had many years.

Scattered through the room are remnants of something older than the plants. Along the back wall, half swallowed by moss, a row of small black cylinders stands upright in the stone. They are no thicker than a thumb and of different heights, as if they had grown there. One of them is cracked, and from the crack weeps a clear fluid that the moss has learned to drink. Thin silver-gray filaments, too regular to be roots, run through the moss like a nervous system; they are cold and smooth, and if touched they hum below the range of hearing. In one hollow in the floor rests a small dull object shaped like an ear or a lens, its surface unmarked by frost, and around it the air is stiller than elsewhere. None of these things seem active in any familiar way, but the room is held in their attention: the temperature does not swing, the humidity does not fail, the water does not freeze. They are not machines exactly; there are no moving parts, no ports, no seams. They are made of folded layers of something that is not glass, not ceramic, not metal. If you tap one, it returns no sound, only a colder spot on your knuckle.

There are no animals. No tracks, no scat, no bones, no burrows, no gliding, crawling, or breathing. The stillness has no heartbeat in it. The silence is not absence of noise but a low, thick quiet made of drips, the creak of old ice in the ceiling, the faint susurrus of water moving through moss. When the devices do something—maybe once a day, maybe once a season—there is a sound like a single bell struck inside water, so faint you might hear it only if you have been sitting there long enough to forget your own pulse.

The air is cold and heavy and slightly sweet, mineral and green, like the smell of a greenhouse that has frozen. It does not move much; there is no wind, only a weak convection given off by the bodies of the plants and the slow machines. Your breath fogs and rises a little before it settles on the fronds.

Time passes oddly here. The cave has been this way long enough that change has become a kind of stillness. In winter, the surface seep slows almost to nothing and the air dries; the moss shrinks a fraction, the fronds bend lower. In spring, with meltwater above, the chamber weeps for weeks, and the drip-notes grow closer together until they almost form a rhythm. But there are no floods, no collapses. The walls are already weathered smooth; the plants have already found their shape; the old machines have already finished whatever they were built to do. What remains is not growth or decay, but a slow, even maintenance, the way breath is maintenance for a body.

It is a garden, though no gardener is present. Or perhaps the gardener is the room itself—the stone, the water, the cold, the black moss, and those patient artificial things that sit facing the empty center like a committee of witnesses, blinking once in a great while, tending a cold, lightless garden that wants nothing but to continue.
