The approach is not a path so much as a narrowing. You come down through a break in the ridgeline, and the world folds inward, and the light changes before you understand why.

The cirque opens below you like a room that has been waiting. Its walls are raw and sharp-edged, the rock freshly split in long vertical planes, as if the mountain shrugged off its skin only recently and has not yet begun to weather. There is no scree, no talus fan, no softening of the angles. The stone is dark, wet, glistening. Water moves across every surface in a thin continuous film, not falling so much as leaking from the mountain's body, and where the rock is porous it weeps in strings of droplets that catch what little light exists and hold it.

And everything else is plant.

Not plants on the rock. Not plants among the rock. The plants are the place. They have grown over the cirque walls and floor so completely that the stone beneath is more memory than substance. The vegetation is the substrate now. It is the ground, the walls, the ceiling, the architecture. You do not stand on rock and look at plants; you stand inside a structure made entirely of living tissue, and the rock is only a rumor beneath it.

The cold is the first thing that registers in your body. It is not a sharp cold, not a wind cold. It is a deep, still, saturated cold, the kind that lives in water that has never been warm. It seeps through your clothing the way the water seeps through the moss. The air is heavy with moisture, and every breath tastes of green things and wet stone and the faint mineral tang of the mountain's interior. Your breath fogs and hangs in the still air, and the fog does not dissipate so much as it is absorbed, drawn into the surrounding plant mass like water into a sponge.

The light is dim, green, underwater. The plant canopy overhead is so dense that the sky is not visible at all. What light filters down through the layers of growth is diffuse, directionless, the color of deep forest shade mixed with the pale grey of the overcast sky above. It is enough to see by, but only just. Shadows are soft and indistinct. Edges blur. The eye strains to find boundaries and finds instead only gradations of green and grey and the occasional glint of water.

And yet the place is organized.

This is what stops you, what makes you stand still and look more carefully. The plants have not grown randomly. They have grown in patterns. There are walls — not rock walls, but walls of interwoven stems and branches and moss, rising vertically, defining spaces. There are passages — narrow corridors between the walls, where the growth is thinner and the way is clear. There are chambers — enclosed spaces where the plant matter has arched overhead to form ceilings, where the walls curve inward and meet, where the space inside is hollow and still. There are levels — stacked layers of growth, one above another, connected by what might be called stairs if stairs were made of living wood and moss and the slow accretion of plant matter over centuries.

The place has the density of a city. Not a human city — there are no straight lines, no right angles, no materials that are not alive. But the density, the layering, the sense of organized construction, the way the spaces relate to one another — it is urban. It is a settlement. It is a place that has been built, even though nothing here has been built by hands.

The walls are not uniform. Some are thick and solid, formed by the interweaving of thousands of woody stems, each no thicker than a finger, bound together by moss and lichen and the slow pressure of growth. Others are thin and translucent, curtains of hanging moss and vine that sway slightly in the air currents, filtering the light, dividing the space. Some walls are old, their surfaces dark and dense and furrowed, the wood hardened and polished by centuries of water flow. Others are new, bright green, still soft, still growing, still finding their form.

The passages wind between the walls, narrow and twisting, their floors soft with moss and pooled water. The water is everywhere. It drips from the ceilings, runs down the walls, pools in the depressions, flows in small channels that have been worn into the plant matter over time. The channels are not random. They follow the contours of the space, connecting the chambers, moving the water from the high places to the low places, from the weeping rock to the pools below. They are the infrastructure of this place, the circulatory system, the plumbing. And they are made entirely of plant matter, shaped by the flow of water over centuries, or shaped by something else.

The chambers vary in size. Some are small, barely large enough to stand in, their walls close and their ceilings low, the air thick and still. Others are larger, cathedral-scale, their ceilings arching high overhead, lost in the dim green light, their floors broad and relatively open. In the larger chambers, the plant walls rise like columns, thick and massive, supporting the weight of the growth above. The columns are not separate from the walls — they are the walls, thickened and strengthened, the plant matter growing denser where the load is greatest. It is architecture without an architect, engineering without an engineer.

And yet.

There is something here that is not quite right. Something that makes the back of your neck prickle. The patterns are too regular. The passages are too clear. The chambers are too well-proportioned. The water channels are too efficient. The whole place has the feel of something that was designed, even though there is no evidence of design, no tools, no marks, no sign of any intelligence other than the slow intelligence of plants growing toward light and water and space.

The weirdness is subtle. It is in the way the walls curve, the way the passages align, the way the chambers are arranged in a pattern that seems almost deliberate. It is in the shapes that appear in the plant matter — shapes that suggest doorways, windows, arches, niches, even though they are formed entirely by the natural growth of the vegetation. It is in the way the water flows, following channels that seem too straight, too purposeful, too well-maintained. It is in the silence, which is not the silence of an empty place but the silence of a place that is waiting.

The fauna is rare. You see almost nothing moving. Occasionally, a small insect crawls across a moss-covered surface, its body pale and translucent, adapted to the dim light. Occasionally, a bird passes overhead, invisible in the canopy, its call a single clear note that echoes through the chambers and then fades. But mostly, the place is still. The only movement is the slow drip of water, the subtle sway of hanging moss, the almost imperceptible growth of the plants themselves.

And yet the place is active. You can feel it. The water is moving, the plants are growing, the whole system is in motion, even though the motion is too slow to see. The cold air is sinking, pooling in the low places, flowing out through the passages. The moisture is condensing on the surfaces, dripping down, being absorbed by the moss, transpired back into the air. The plants are photosynthesizing in the dim light, growing, dying, decomposing, being replaced. The place is alive, not just with the life of individual plants but with the life of the whole system, the whole ecosystem, the whole city.

The sound is mostly quiet. The dominant sound is water — the drip of droplets from the ceiling, the trickle of channels, the soft hiss of moisture moving through the plant mass. Beneath that, there is a deeper sound, almost below the threshold of hearing — the creak of plant fibers, the slow settling of the structure, the whisper of air moving through the passages. And beneath that, there is a silence that is not empty but full, a silence that seems to be listening.

The cold is constant. It does not fluctuate. It is the same temperature everywhere, in every chamber, every passage, every level. The water is cold, the air is cold, the plant matter is cold. The cold is not uncomfortable — it is too still, too constant for that. It is simply the temperature of this place, the temperature at which this ecosystem operates, the temperature at which this city lives.

The scale is small. The entire cirque is perhaps the size of a hamlet, a few hundred meters across, a few hundred meters deep. The chambers are room-scale, the passages are corridor-scale, the whole place is a single feature, a single area, a single entity. It is bounded by the cirque walls, contained, complete. It is a world in miniature, a city in a bowl, a universe in a room.

And the plants are everything. They are the ground beneath your feet, soft and springy, composed of layer upon layer of moss and lichen and decaying plant matter. They are the walls around you, dense and solid, formed by the interweaving of stems and branches and roots. They are the ceiling above you, thick and dark, filtering the light, holding the moisture. They are the air you breathe, heavy with oxygen and water vapor and the scent of green things. They are the water you hear, the water you feel, the water that is everywhere, moving through the plant mass like blood through a body.

The place is a single organism, or a single community, or a single structure. It is impossible to tell where one plant ends and another begins. The roots are interwoven, the stems are fused, the moss is continuous. The whole cirque is one living thing, one vast plant, one city made of green.

And in the dim light, in the cold, in the quiet, you stand in the center of it, and you feel the weight of it, the density of it, the sheer packed fullness of it. There is no room between presences. Every space is occupied. Every surface is covered. Every gap is filled. The place is saturated, not just with water but with life, with growth, with the slow relentless pressure of plants becoming more.

The water drips. The moss grows. The city breathes. And somewhere in the dim green light, in the cold still air, in the quiet that is not quite silence, something shifts, something settles, something continues.
