The valley does not have one name. The people who live there call it the Weeping Stair, though no one climbs it dry.

It occupies the inside of a high mountain cirque, a cold bowl of stone that catches the clouds. The elevation is high enough that the air is thin and cool, but the valley is not bare rock. The walls are covered in a single continuous mass of vegetation: pale wood, dark wet leaves, moss as thick as matting, roots running down the rock like stopped waterfalls. The plant does not merely cover the terrain; it has become the terrain. There is no ordinary ground. The mountain holds the shape, and the plant fills it.

Water is the medium of everything. It arrives as cloud against the high peaks, condenses into mist, gathers on every leaf, drips, spills, and runs downward in hundreds of small channels. It fills the lake that occupies the lower bowl of the cirque. Some of it hangs as permanent rain. Some of it moves through the plant itself, inside roots and hollow stems, the way water moves through a living body.

The settlement is small but organized like a town. A few hundred structures are scattered across the wet plant-walls, rooted between branches, hung from trunks, and moored over the lake. The density is sparse. There are wide gaps of open water, leaf, and mist between one dwelling and the next. A rope bridge may cross a hundred feet of falling water to reach the next door. A house may sit alone inside a bowl of leaves forty feet across, with no neighbor visible. But it is not a scatter of isolated huts. There are shared paths of notched wood and woven fiber, stone-lined drainage channels, ladders that can be raised and lowered, public cisterns, a rope elevator on a great pulley of polished root-wood, and a central meeting hall grown inside a natural cavity in the plant.

The lower third of the town is fully submerged. Doors open like the mouths of flooded caves. Rooms are filled with cold, clear water. People swim between them, towing bundles of oiled cloth. Fish move through windows and over sills. The underwater houses are still used as storerooms, fish-holds, and wet kitchens. Above the surface, other rooms hang in the saturated air, their roofs streaming, their floorboards dark and soft.

The architecture is primitive but knowing. Frames are lashed with fiber. Walls are bark, woven reed, hide, and waxed leaf. Stone anchors hold platforms against slow currents. Dwellings are pegged, not nailed. Metal is rare—a few cold-hammered copper pins, a knife of lake-iron kept by a headwoman—so most work is done with wood, stone, bone, shell, and plant fiber. Fire is not common. It is kept carefully in sealed clay boxes on high platforms where the air is less sodden, and much cooking is done with hot stones rather than open flame.

The light is ordinary daylight, the high clear sun through a thin silver overcast. On the upper platforms it is bright and cool. In the water it turns green-gold near the surface and cold blue below. The temperature is cool without being bitter. Breath mists on the high walkways, and the lake numbs the fingers after a long swim, but the place does not feel hostile. It feels settled. The stone steps are smooth and cupped. The wood is dark and rounded at its edges. Nothing is raw. The valley has been this way for a long time.

The sound is loud and constant. Water drips, runs, slaps, and roars. The great mass of plant fiber creaks like old ships at anchor. Frogs call in overlapping rounds from the wet hollows. Insects tick in the leaves. Small silver fish leap and smack the surface. Birds or bat-like creatures cry from the high mats. Below the surface, something knocks slowly against the roots, a heavy, regular sound that carries through the water. The whole place is full of wet, living noise.

Fauna is abundant. The lake is thick with small fish, eels, freshwater mussels, and skittering things. The leaves hold frogs, snails, pale lizards, and gliding insects. The air above the water is crossed by needle-winged birds. The people keep fish weirs, eel traps, and nesting boxes hung from branches. The ecology is dense but not chaotic; the animals move along the same wet routes as the people, through the same hollows and over the same stones.

Change is slow. The lake level rises and falls over years, and the town adjusts. Ladders are re-pegged higher or lower. Floating platforms are moored with longer ropes. A house that has been flooded for a decade may be abandoned, then reoccupied when the water drops. The plant grows gradually, closing old paths and opening new ones. Steps wear into the wood. Stone erodes by a finger's width in a lifetime. Nothing here changes quickly, but everything is changing.

The rules of the Weeping Stair are few and strict, shaped by the water. No one cuts living root-wood; only dead limbs and fallen branches are taken. Fire is kept away from the wet walls. The lake is shared and kept clean, so waste is carried to midden pools and fed to the plants. When the plant begins to close a room, the room is emptied and left to the water. A path is not built unless someone will tend it. Most movement is by swimming, climbing, or rope.

One unusual thing is that the plant is not a forest of separate trees. It is one connected body. Its branches fuse where they touch. Its roots share water. Some of the hollows the people use as rooms were not carved; they simply appeared. When the wind moves through certain root channels, the whole wall breathes with a sound like a slow, enormous inhale and exhale. Water that seeps from the oldest roots is slightly warm and tastes faintly sweet, as if the mountain is filtering it. The people say the Weeping Stair is not built on the plant but inside it, and that the plant decides which rooms stay open.

At the deepest point of the flooded town, where the steps disappear into green darkness, there is a natural hall about the size of a small cathedral. It is a cavity in the root-mass with a still pool in its floor and no visible roof, only water and wood and the slow movement of fish. The town uses it as a meeting place on days when the water is calm, voices echoing over the pool while the surrounding plant creaks around them.

The Weeping Stair does not reveal itself all at once. It drips, swims, breathes, calls, and waits. It is a small place, but it holds water, life, and time in every direction.
