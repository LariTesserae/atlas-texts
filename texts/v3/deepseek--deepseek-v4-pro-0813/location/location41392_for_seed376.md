The ground is not ground. It is a weave of root and fiber, pressed into a topology of subtle, endless undulation. Walking here—if you could call the labored, sinking, rebounding motion walking—is less like crossing terrain and more like being received, inch by inch, by a single continuous organism. The cold makes the fiber brittle on the surface, each step producing a faint, close sound like the crackling of stiff canvas. But beneath the shallow crust, half an inch down, the tissue is wet. Not with visible water; the streams here are hidden, capillary, running in veins through the vegetable mass. The air is cold enough to make your sinuses ache, but it tastes of a greenhouse. It is high altitude, thin, pulled taut across the plateau, yet the humidity condenses constantly on the skin of the nearest plant surfaces. Intermittent rain falls not from clouds, but as a release: when the sponge-like plant mass grows too heavy with dew, a section of the canopy shivers and sheds a local, brief, needle-fine rain. Somewhere a traveler, miles away, might have just experienced a downpour from a branch that had been gathering the collective breath of forty acres.

It is a forest of one thing. Or rather, a forest where the idea of “tree” and “ground” and “vine” no longer discriminates. Trunks as wide as houses rise, but they are not vertical in the clean architectural sense; they lean and braid into one another, forming vaults and hollows, their bark pleated like folded felt. The “trees” are old, hugely old—erosion has settled everything into a weary, stable form. There are no sharp young branches. The shapes are all mature, smoothed, self-similar. This is the kingdom of the weathered. The ground is not soil but compost of itself, layers of old leaves and stems slowly digesting in the cold. It is dense—not just dense in the sense of a thick forest, but saturated, near-touching, every cubic yard occupied by fronds, shelves of fan-shaped lichen-like leaves, curtains of hanging rootlet. You push through it, and the pressure is not hostile but constant. A million small trichomes cling to your sleeve. The density makes sound strange: it soaks up high frequencies, leaves only low, close things. The crunch of your own steps. Your breath. A faint, wide-band hiss like static, from a million surfaces rubbing against each other in an almost imperceptible breeze.

But here is the strange industrial part, the burning contradiction of the place. Because this is not a wilderness. Not in the way you mean. The cold, the altitude, the impossible wetness—they all point to a system that is unnatural, or perhaps pre-natural, but never naïve. Amid the whorls and garlands of vegetation, there are no machines. No metal. No engines. And yet the place has the purposeful, churning, productive quality of a factory floor. The plants are doing something. Their breath is not only oxygen and mist. The air is faintly charged, a taut, electrified stillness, like the air outside a power plant. The ground has a rhythm: a slow, seismic pulse, not of earthquakes but of peristalsis. Every few minutes, if you stand still, you can feel the entire woven floor beneath you contract, a slight, full-body squeeze, as if the whole valley were a great lung taking in breath. Then it relaxes, and in the release, a warm, mineral-scented air escapes from the root-mesh, condensing into a low fog that rolls only ankle-high before it freezes on contact with the colder leaves.

The place is scored by channels. They are not rivers of water, but rivers of motion. Deep, smooth grooves in the plant-mass, like the tracks of some vast hauling engine, though no animal is rare here—no, fauna is more than rare, it is absent except for the smallest of mites that live in the mucus of the leaves by the millions. The grooves are from the plant itself, or rather, from the large, blind trunks that periodically drag themselves from one place to another, seeking better light or wetter feeds. They move slowly, too slow to watch, but the evidence is everywhere: paths of crushed and re-healed growth, the enormous depressions left where a trunk had gone to sleep for a decade. The movement is like a loom shifting its beam. The whole landscape is a machine of vegetation, a botanical factory of unknown product. It moves with the mindless, inexhaustible energy of an anthill, but in a long, slow, industrial rhythm.

The most extraordinary events are the collapses. At intervals irregular but not rare, a section of the mega-canopy, an older tree-creature that has fed and been fed upon for a thousand years, will suddenly die. Not wilt. Die. The color drains out of it, a wet, grayish white. And within minutes, the surrounding mass attacks it. Vines uncoil from trunks and slide across the ground like electrical cables. Other trunks lean forward, pressing their foliage against the dying one. The body is consumed, not with violence but with an eager, silent thoroughness. It is torn apart, pulled down into the ground weave, shredded. The sound during this is the most unsettling thing in the world: not a roar, but a soft, dense, chewing sound, a huge, quiet, inner squash and rip. And then, with equal speed, new shoots—pale, translucent, hot with the stolen heat of the dead giant—burst upward in the gap. The gap closes. The machine resets. In the wake of a collapse, the silence returns, absolute, thicker than before.

Above, the sky is a low, gray, unbroken lid. Cold light, diffused, with no visible sun. High altitude does not mean bright here; it means thin, starved atmosphere. The cold is not biting wind; it is a still, creeping cold that seems to exhale from the plant mass itself, a cold that is intrinsic, like the cold of stored root vegetables. The light arrives through a gauze of fog and ice fog, so that shadows are weak and everything appears slightly self-luminous. There is no day-night cycle in the familiar sense. The light simply fades in and out over a long period, as if someone were adjusting a dimmer switch on the entire world.

Pockets of true water are rare. At the bottoms of the deepest, oldest channels, where the plant-mass has been compressed until it is more mineral than tissue, there are ice-crusted pools. They are narrow, black, and unrippled. They are not fed by rain but by the slow bleeding of the ground. Around their edges, the vegetation is different: not leaves, but translucent, glassy filaments, like frozen neuronal endings, absorbing the water through the ice. These are the only places where the ground is not one continuous surface. You can see down into the pool. The water is clear but lightless, going deep into the weave. The surface tension is high, like glycerin. If you drop a stone, it does not splash; it slides through with a single, low, bell-like plink, the only sharp sound the landscape ever makes.

At night, or what passes for night, the real turbulence begins. The temperature drops further. The entire meadow of fiber begins to twist and wring itself, not randomly, but in wave after wave, from one end of the valley to the other, like a great hand wringing out a cloth. It is a storm that never moves; it is the place itself, turning over in its sleep. The motion is silent, but you can feel it through your feet. The roots below you snap and re-knit with a sound like distant snowfall. The fog thickens, becomes a kind of cold steam, and for an hour or two, the whole landscape seems to be both a patient etherized upon a table and a factory running at full capacity, unseen. You stand in it, and you are the only animal sound for miles, and you cannot help but feel like an intruder in a private engine, a witness to something that is both ecological and industrial, both tender and crushing.

It is not entirely without memory. The places where the ground has been disturbed by collapses, the old paths of the moving trunks, the slow, wandering beds where water once pooled—they all show a kind of scar tissue, a glyphic pattern in the bark. If you look at the ground long enough, you start to read it. There are no words, no human signs, but there is a grammar. The plant itself has a way of writing the history of its own movements, a living palimpsest of pressure and release. It is an untouched place, but it is not innocent. It has the exhausted, wise, unfathomably busy look of a loom that has been weaving itself for millennia, a machine that has forgotten its operator and its purpose, and now just runs, cold and damp and utterly silent, in the high, thin air, filling the whole valley with its own dense, breathing, fur-like body.
