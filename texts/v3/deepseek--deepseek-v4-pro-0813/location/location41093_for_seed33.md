The city begins before it announces itself. From the western rim you would not call it a city: only a standing line of dark forms in grey half-light, too regular to be natural, too ruined to be alive. But as you drop into the wide basin, the forms resolve into streets, towers, bridges, and plazas, and you understand you are entering a place that has been urban for so long its urbanity has become a kind of geology.

It occupies a high plateau, lifted so far above the lower world that the horizon falls away into a grey sea of cloud or dust. Even at noon the sky is the color of tarnished pewter, and the sun is only a pale smear behind a permanent veil of fine dust. The cold is not the violent cold of high ice, because there is no moisture to freeze. It is a dry coolness, the coolness of stone that never warms.

There is no water here. None. No streams, no dew, no frost, no damp breath on the rock. At night the temperature drops enough for salts to crystallize out of the air and from the stone; by morning the walls are furred with white needles of thenardite and gypsum. They are not alive. They are a slow dry chemistry, another kind of erosion working in reverse.

The city is built of the plateau itself: a dark, fine-grained stone, sometimes glassy, sometimes pitted, cut into blocks and slabs and left to weather for an impossible span. The streets are wide—far wider than any street for human feet—and run between long colonnades and blind facades. The towers are many, most hollow, their interiors open to the sky. Bridges leap from tower to tower, some still whole, some snapped clean and hanging in the dimness like broken arms. Staircases rise from the streets, turn into ramps, split, double back, and become upper streets. The construction is layered: lower districts half-buried in dust, upper districts chiseled into the wind. It is urban in the way a reef is urban—a dense framework of rooms and passageways, but empty, almost hollow, with huge spaces between one structure and the next.

No tool-mark remains. There is no metal, no timber, no glass, no rope, no writing. If there were doors, they are gone; if there were inhabitants, they took their bones and their refuse with them. The city feels not abandoned but completed, as if emptiness were its intended product. Some rooms are solid stone, as if the builders forgot to hollow them. Some doorways are only an inch deep. Some staircases descend into the ground and end at a wall older than the stair itself.

The only things resembling plants are mineral growths: black manganese dendrites branching like ferns across pale walls, white rosettes of gypsum in the cracks, thin veins of rust-red oxides climbing the walls like ivy. They grow by accretion, not by life, but they give the city a kind of slow vegetation, a garden with no seeds.

Animals are rare. The most visible is a creature called the glass loper—tall, thin, pale as a sliver of mica, with too many joints and a body like a folded kite. It moves through the streets with the deliberate pace of a cloud shadow, scraping salt blooms from the stone with a hard tongue. There may be a dozen in the whole city; you could walk for days and see none. Its only predator is rarer still: a thing called the Quiet, which looks like a fallen column until it moves, and even then it makes no sound. Their chase is not a chase in the ordinary sense; it is a relationship played out over years, a slow predator-prey cycle older than some of the towers.

The city is not silent, but its sounds are minor. The wind is a low hiss, not a howl, because the air is thin. The stone ticks as it cools. Occasionally a slab of salt crust flakes off and falls, and the sound of its landing arrives a moment before the fall—or a moment after, depending on where you are. Far away, sometimes, a single low note rolls through the streets, like a bell struck under the earth. There are no bells.

And this is where the place parts company with ordinary geography. The city does not keep its distances. A tower that looks ten minutes away may take an hour to reach, or three steps. Streets that run straight return to themselves, but the corner you left is older or newer than you left it. Some rooms are larger on the inside than the buildings that contain them. Some colonnades exist only in the corner of the eye and vanish when you turn your head. Shadows do not always point away from the light; sometimes they pool in the wrong direction, or linger after the light has moved on. The erosion here is not only physical. The edges of things have been worn away conceptually: the difference between wall and shadow, inside and outside, built and natural, has become porous in places. There are walls you can put your hand through, not because they are not there, but because they are only the memory of a wall. There are shadows that have weight enough to hold dust.

If you walk there, do not keep a map. The map will start to disagree with the city, and then the city will agree with the map, and then you will not be where you are. Do not trust echoes; they arrive before their causes. Do not enter a doorway that is darker than the room behind it. Leave no mark. The stone remembers marks and grows around them.

Yet for all its strangeness, the place is not dead. It is active in its own deliberate way. The wind shifts the dust in long, slow streams. The salt blooms open in the cold and shrink in the warmth. The plateau groans as it settles. A rock falls; a loper moves; the Quiet waits. The city’s layout changes by inches and degrees—an arch drifting left, a stair gaining a step, a room closing itself. It is being built and eroded at the same time, not by anything, but by its own long inhabitation of itself.

This is not a ruin. It is a mature form—a city that has been itself for so long that it has become a law of the landscape. It will still be here when the plateau has eroded to a plain, and the plain to a salt desert, and the salt desert to a set of streets that no one walks.
