The sound comes first. It is not exactly a sound you hear; it is a sound you feel. A high, continuous ringing, like a finger run around the rim of an enormous glass, or a wire stretched too tight and vibrating for years without stop. It rises through the cracks in the ground before you find the way down.

The way down is through a fresh break in the world’s crust. The rock is pale, nearly white, and sharp. It has not been rounded by weather. It breaks in splinters and plates, and the talus shifts underfoot. You are descending only a little below the surface—a few tens of meters, no deeper than a tall tree—but the air changes immediately. It cools to a steady root-cellar temperature and takes on a dampness that clings to your skin and lungs.

Then the space opens. It is not a cave, not a tunnel, but a sunken valley lying beneath a broken roof of stone. In places the roof has collapsed, and through the gaps comes a dim blue-grey light, not sunlight but its ghost, filtered through mist and stone. In other places the roof is intact, and the ceiling drips. The floor extends beyond sight, broad and open. The place is regional, the size of a small valley or a drowned town, not a single chamber. You can see far, but only in the way you see far at dusk—shapes, not details.

The materials are simple. The rock is mostly limestone, with a thin, soft crust of calcite and gypsum growing on the wet surfaces, still fresh and granular like new frost. The floor is pale silt, calcareous and fine, damp enough to hold your footprint but not muddy. Boulders the size of houses stand alone on the flats, angular and faceted. Some are split into fins and pillars, as if the earth’s new skeleton is showing. There is almost no soil, only stone dust and the mineral bloom of evaporation. The land is young; the edges are sharp, the scarps clean, the talus loose. Water has not had time to smooth anything.

Water is present everywhere and nowhere. The air itself is wet enough to condense on every stone. The walls weep. The ceiling drips in a scattered, unhurried rhythm. In low places, water collects into clear pools. Some are shallow pans, only a hand deep, ringed with white salts. Others are narrow shafts that drop into blackness. The moisture comes from condensation and slow groundwater seep, not from storms. There are no floods. The dampness is steady. It is a landscape of seep and still water, of pockets of wet in a larger dryness.

Plants are few. Where seep water slides down the walls, mats of moss grow, gray-green and almost colorless. In the cracks, pale ferns uncurl, thin and translucent, more like the legs of drowned insects than leaves. Around some pools, a low, leathery cress forms small rosettes, never tall enough to shade anything. There are no trees, no grasses that form a turf. The ground between these patches is bare. The vegetation is scattered, an exception to the stone.

The light does not change. It is dim, grey-blue, constant. There is no sun-path, no shifting shadows. The openings in the roof let in a perpetual twilight, as if the sky above is forever overcast. Shadows are soft and stationary. The air is still, cool, and heavy. It smells of wet limestone, old calcium, and the faint sweetness of leaves that have fallen from the upper world through the cracks and settled here as food.

But the animals overwhelm. They are the reason for the sound. The walls in the wet places are not bare rock; they are covered with a kind of pale frog, each no bigger than a thumb joint, with translucent skin and black eyes reduced to pinpricks. They sit in tiny territories, one beside another, thousands on a single wall, millions across the valley. They do not leap. They call. The call is thin and metallic, a continuous ringing—wire on wet glass—and multiplied by their numbers it becomes a single high note, the ground tone of the world.

Above the pools, clouds of small pale flies hover in stationary loops. Their wings make a hum that fills the spaces between the frog notes. On the silt, millions of springtails and tiny white beetles move by millimeters, making the ground shimmer. In the water, eyeless shrimp and flat translucent fish hang motionless, twitching only when a drip hits the surface. On the boulders, long-legged crickets with hair-thin antennae sit motionless and scrape their legs together, a dry hiss like sand falling on stone. Predators are just as present: pale centipedes as long as a hand move slowly through the moss, and larger blind things lie at the edges of the fly clouds, mouths open. The impression is not of danger but of being outnumbered by millions.

Yet this life is not evenly spread. Between the seeps and the pools, the ground is empty for long stretches. You can walk over bare silt and hear the sound echo off the stone with no visible animal. Then you come to a wet crack or a pool, and the sound becomes physical, the walls seem to writhe, and the air is thick with bodies. The overall density is sparse; the place has far more stone than flesh. But within each wet patch, the density is overwhelming. The emptiness between patches makes those crowded places sharper.

There are no built structures here. No wall, no path, no fire, no tool left behind. Some animals use simple tools in a limited, repeated way: a cricket wedges a grass stem into a fissure to pry out a grub; a frog hammers a snail against a stone; a shrimp carries a pebble to anchor its burrow. But the tools are dropped and become part of the stone again. There is no accumulation, no made world, no artifact that persists. The place is untouched by human hands, but not untouched by life.

The deepest rule of the place is that nothing changes. The light is constant. The temperature is constant. The seeps and drips fall at the same rate. The animals do not migrate, hibernate, or breed in bursts; they breed, feed, and die in a continuous, steady pulse. The same number of frogs sits on the same wall because each territory is always occupied. When one dies, it is consumed almost immediately by the springtails, and a juvenile from the nearest damp crevice takes its place. The sound never stops. It has no surge, no rest, no beginning. The system is a frozen equilibrium, alive but perfectly balanced. If you returned after a year or ten, you could not tell that time had passed.

There is nothing supernatural about it. The dimness is the thick roof. The loudness is the frogs. The sharp stone is youth. The dampness is condensation. The only remarkable thing is the steadiness. It is a place without time, but not without process.

To stand there is to feel like the only variable. Your breathing, your movement, your heartbeat are the only changes. The place does not acknowledge you. The chorus continues. The drips fall. The pale frogs call. The cold air holds. It is a landscape that has found its one note and will not stop sounding it.
