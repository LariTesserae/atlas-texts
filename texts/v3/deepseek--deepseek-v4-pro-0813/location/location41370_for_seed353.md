The highland begins as a rumor of heat. Long before the ground changes, the air thins and warms, taking on a dry, resinous quality, as if the sky itself has been baked. There is no sun. The darkness above is absolute, a black vault without stars, without moon, without any hint of dawn. And yet the world below glows—not with light, but with heat made visible, a dull ember-red that seeps from every surface, as if the entire landscape is a bed of coals banked beneath a layer of ash.

The ground is not ground. It is a woven mat of roots and stems, compressed over eons into a warm, resilient surface that flexes slightly underfoot, like walking on the hide of some enormous, sleeping animal. In places it has been polished to a high sheen by the passage of countless tendrils and machine-parts over geological time—a patina of wear so deep it seems to hold the memory of every movement that ever crossed it. In other places it is raw and new, a tangle of fresh growth still soft with sap, where the mat has recently been torn open and is already knitting itself back together.

Above this living floor, the vegetation rises as a continuous mass. It is not a forest. There are no individual trees, no trunks, no canopy in the ordinary sense. Instead, the plant matter forms a single, interconnected body that fills the space from the ground to a ceiling of interwoven branches and leaves so dense that no light penetrates from above. The mass is riddled with passages—some natural, the vascular channels of the plants themselves, wide enough to walk through, their walls lined with a slick, resinous film; others clearly constructed, their walls reinforced with struts of hardened sap and woven fiber, their floors laid with planks of compressed lignite. These passages are the streets of this world, the corridors, the rooms. They wind and branch and sometimes dead-end, and they are never the same from one moment to the next, because the mass is always moving, always growing, always being torn down and rebuilt.

Within the passages, the machines work. They are made of plant material—gears of dense heartwood, pistons of hollow stems, belts of woven vine, bearings of polished seed-stone. They are powered by heat, the world's own heat, drawn from deep below through roots that plunge into the planet's core, or generated by the plants' metabolism, which burns not with fire but with a slow, radiant oxidation that never quite ignites. The machines move with a purpose that seems almost intelligent, but there is no intelligence to be found—no eyes, no minds, no observers. They are simply part of the cycle, as much a part of the world as the plants themselves.

Some of the machines are factories. They process the plant matter, breaking down dead tissue and reassembling it into new structures, new machines, new passages. They are the engines of the world's endless self-construction. They harvest the growth, chew it into pulp, press it into beams, weave it into walls, extrude it into gears and pistons and belts. And then the plants grow into the structures, and the machines harvest them again. It is a closed loop, a self-sustaining system that has been running for so long that the distinction between plant and machine, between living and made, has dissolved entirely.

The heat is intense. It radiates from the walls, from the floor, from the ceiling, from the machines themselves. The air shimmers, even in the darkness, and the thin atmosphere—for this is a highland, a plateau raised above the surrounding lowlands—makes the heat feel different, drier, more penetrating, like standing too close to a kiln. The plants glow faintly, not with bioluminescence but with the dull red of embers, and in places the glow brightens to a fierce orange where a furnace burns, where the plant matter is being processed at high temperature. These furnaces are the main source of light, casting long, shifting shadows through the passages, shadows that move as the machines move, as the plants grow, as the world churns.

The erosion is visible everywhere. The surfaces are worn smooth, polished by the passage of countless plant-tendrils and machine-parts over eons. The edges are rounded, the corners softened. It is a world that has been used for so long that it has been worn into its final, comfortable shape—a shape that is not static, but that changes so slowly, so incrementally, that it looks like stillness from a distance. And yet the turbulence is constant. The mass shifts, groans, settles. Passages open and close. New growth bursts through old walls, and old walls collapse into new passages. Machines tear through the mass, leaving new channels that are quickly filled by new growth. It is a slow-motion storm, a churn that has been going on for so long it has become the world's only weather.

The quiet is profound. The machines make almost no sound—they are lubricated by sap, cushioned by fiber, their movements absorbed by the dense plant mass that surrounds them. The plants grow silently, their tendrils uncoiling, their leaves unfurling, their roots creeping through the woven ground without a whisper. The only sounds are the occasional creak of shifting mass, the soft hiss of heat, the distant thump of a machine cycling through its endless work. It is a world of held breath, a world where the violence of the churn is hidden behind a veil of silence, where the chaos is so well-mannered that it barely disturbs the air.

There are no animals. No birds, no insects, no crawling things. The only movement is the plants and the machines. The plants move slowly, almost imperceptibly, their growth visible only over hours or days. The machines move with purpose, their gears turning, their pistons pumping, their belts sliding over pulleys of polished wood. But there is no one to watch them. No eyes. No minds. Just the endless, quiet, hot, dark work of a world that is entirely alive and entirely made.

The scale is open. This is not a single room or a single corridor—it is a whole region, a landscape of plant-machine. There are zones where the mass is denser, where the passages narrow to crawlspaces and the heat is almost unbearable. There are zones where the mass opens into vast chambers, their ceilings lost in the darkness above, their floors covered with a carpet of soft, new growth. There are zones where the machines are concentrated, where the air is thick with the smell of hot resin and the glow of furnaces is bright enough to read by. There are high points—spires of hardened plant matter that rise above the general mass, their tips glowing in the darkness like the chimneys of some vast, subterranean factory. And there are low points—pits where the mass has collapsed, revealing the layers of history beneath, the compressed strata of countless generations of growth and harvest, each layer a record of a world that has been rebuilt so many times that the original is lost forever.

The elevation is high. The air is thin, and the heat, which comes not from any sun but from the world's own depths, feels different here—drier, more radiant, more penetrating. The thin air also muffles sound, so that even the occasional creak or thump seems to come from far away, as if the world is speaking in a whisper. The darkness is almost total. The only light is the dull red glow of the furnaces and the faint ember-light of the plants themselves. The sky, if it can be seen through the gaps in the canopy, is black—no stars, no moon, just the faint glow of the world below reflecting off the underside of clouds, if there are clouds.

This is a world of living industry, a highland of woven plant matter, hot and dark, where the only light is the ember-glow of its own metabolism. It is ancient beyond measure, worn smooth by eons of use, yet turbulent with constant change. It is quiet, almost silent, despite the endless work of its machines. It is strange—deeply, fundamentally strange—a place where the boundaries between plant and machine, living and made, natural and constructed, have dissolved entirely. It is a world that has reached a final, stable state, but it is a dynamic stability, a churn that looks like stillness, a storm that has been raging for so long it has become the weather. And it is a world without observers, without minds, without any witness to its endless, quiet, hot, dark work—just the plants and the machines, growing and harvesting, building and tearing down, in a cycle that has no beginning and no end.
