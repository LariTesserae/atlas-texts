The mountain range is still being born, and it is already drowning.

At the top of the new sea—a sea that has swallowed a high plateau and left only the eruptive cores standing—there is a stack of black basalt. It rises from water that is cold and clear as winter glass, and it has no shore. Waves slam against it with the force of something unfinished, not yet tempered by age. The rock is all sharp edges, conchoidal fractures, glassy facets. Nothing here has been rounded. The water has not had time to be an eroder; it is still a breaker, a mover, an argument between elements.

Inside the stack, hidden behind a curtain of spray that falls from a rent near its summit, there is a room of air.

The room is no larger than a shepherd’s hut, and most of it is empty. A ledge of broken basalt lies to one side, above a pool that breathes. Daylight comes through a narrow opening in the roof—ordinary gray-blue light, the light of a high overcast noon, softened and made wet by the spray. It is bright enough to read by, but it feels like light seen through a pane of water. The air is cool, thin, and smells of crushed stone, salt, and something faintly electrical, as if the rock has not cooled all the way.

The floor is a shelf of shattered basalt and black sand, but the sand is not smooth. It is mostly angular shards, no two the same shape, so sharp that kneeling on it is like kneeling on broken obsidian. Each surge moves it, leaving new patterns—ripples, fans, small ridges—that are erased before they can become landforms. The rock is not yet soil; it is raw, glassy, and in deep cracks it sometimes holds a memory of heat, as if the lava of its birth were only a season gone.

The pool is the room’s heart and its danger. It connects through a drowned throat to the sea outside. Every swell makes the water rise, sometimes gently, sometimes with a slap that throws spray against the ceiling. When a large wave seals the lower entrance, the air pressure changes; ears pop; a hidden vent near the top whistles like a flute made of bone. The sound is the first thing. It is not background. It fills the skull. There is the deep bass of water hammering rock, the hiss of retreating foam, a constant rain of small pebbles from the walls, and beneath it all a grinding, ticking sound—the mountain rising, cracking, making room for itself. The noise never stops; it only changes shape.

Life here is sparse, a kind of stain rather than a garden. Above the waterline, pale lichens flake from the stone in patches no bigger than a hand. A green-black film of cyanobacteria darkens the wall where spray regularly wets it. At the water’s edge, a ring of yellow-green scum marks the last high surge, already drying and peeling. Nothing roots. Nothing flowers. There is a rust-colored crust in the hollows that might be alive or might be mineral, and even the rock does not seem sure which.

Fauna is rarer still. In a crevice below the water, a colony of blind amphipods, each the size of a rice grain and the color of old ice, clings to the basalt and scatters with every surge. A single colorless flatworm moves through the black sand at the pool’s bottom. Once, a storm-petrel fell through the roof opening and died of exhaustion on the ledge; its feathers remain, matted with salt, and its bones have not been picked because there is almost nothing to pick them.

And yet people have been here. On the highest ledge, out of reach of all but the biggest swells, someone stacked seven basalt blocks into a low, deliberate tower. Nearby lies a patch of blackened stone—an old hearth—and beside it a flint scraper, a bone needle wedged into a crack, a length of braided sinew so old it has turned the color of ash. Scratched into the wall are lines with dots, a primitive ledger of water rises and falls, some marks deep and recent-looking, others faint and old. Below them, painted in white mineral pigment, is a pictograph: two descending lines, like rain, or a person dissolving into sea.

The place is strange in quieter ways. The stack is growing. The mountains are still ascending, not slowly enough to ignore. By evening, a new fissure may split the wall; by morning, a slab may have fallen into the pool with a boom that shakes the bones. The water outside is layered—cold fresh meltwater floating over a heavier, warmer brine—and in the pool, when the surge weakens, you can see the boundary as a glimmering skin, a surface within the surface. The lower water is too clear, and things seen in it appear closer than they are. Sometimes the rock hums at a frequency that makes the teeth ache, and the air moves through unseen tubes carrying smells from somewhere else: sulfur, then crushed juniper, then nothing.

There is no shore here, no path, no obvious way in or out except the throat in the rock and the rent in the roof. To stay, you learn the rhythm of the water, the way the air compresses just before the big surges, the high whistle that means the vent is open. You do not sleep on the lowest ledge. You do not light a fire unless the roof-vent is clear. The place is not hostile; it is simply unfinished.

This is the Bell at the top of the drowned range—a hidden room of air inside a newborn mountain, where the sea is learning its own strength and the rock has not yet learned to be stone.
