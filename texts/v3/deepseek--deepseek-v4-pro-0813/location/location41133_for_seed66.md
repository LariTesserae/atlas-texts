You notice the sound before the shape of the land: a low, continuous note made of water, iron, and animals. It is a high place, but warm, and the air has the weight of a wet shirt.

The land is a plateau city, though that phrase is too neat. It sits at something like three thousand meters, yet the heat comes from below—old volcanic sills—and from the city itself, so the air is warm and nearly saturated. The altitude should make it cold, but it doesn’t. Rain does not arrive as storms so much as a settling of water. Clouds form at mid-morning and stand among the taller stacks. When they thin, the light is ordinary, bright and grey-gold, and the whole city gleams wet.

To call it a city is to miss that it is also a geology. The original plateau was black basalt, columnar in places, breaking into broad terraces. People built into those terraces, not on top of them. Retaining walls are cut stone and poured concrete, now weathered almost into natural cliffs. Water has carved runnels down every vertical surface. The stone is stained copper-green, sulfur-yellow, rust-red. Nothing green grows.

There are no plants. Not a leaf, not a blade. What covers the wet stone is something between mold and mineral: a thin grey-orange film that thickens around warm pipes and canal sluices. It is not plant life. It does not need soil or deep roots. It grows in patterns like lichen, but wetter, more bacterial, and in places it forms mats several fingers thick. These mats are the fields of this landscape. They are grazed by the animals.

The animals are impossible to ignore. The city is full of them, more than the buildings can hold. They are not pests exactly, not a plague; they are simply the dominant population. Small six-legged things—glossy, rust-colored, slow—cover the mineral mats in such numbers that the mats appear to seethe. There are larger creatures that look like featherless birds with long bony snouts, roosting on every cornice, cable, and pipe. They call in long metallic whistles. In the canals, eel-like things with flattened bodies coil and uncoil slowly, their backs breaking the dark water. The air is thick with small flying forms that never seem to land. Streets are not empty; they are carpets of slow-moving animals.

Every surface, if you look closely, is layered with presence. Pipes have a fur of small clinging things. The undersides of bridges are hung with nests made of mud, hair, and metal shavings. The animals are near-touching, but they are not panicked. They move slowly, as if they have inherited the city’s patience. The sheer number of them is not like a forest full of birds; it is like a body full of cells.

The city itself is industrial: not the sharp new kind, but an old continuous industry. There are engine halls built into the plateau, their smokestacks rising in clusters like organ pipes. Steam and exhaust add to the clouds. Rail lines run on elevated viaducts, crossing canals and animal commons. Power lines sag between iron towers. Every few blocks, a factory breathes warm water out into a gutter. The streets are stamped metal and stone, with channels that run full even when it is not raining. Some buildings are residential stacks, their windows small and hooded, their balconies so cluttered with drying racks and animal cages that they look like nests themselves.

The city has been here long enough that it no longer fights the water. The edges of the buildings are rounded. The railings are thin at the bottom from rust. Streets have ruts where wheels and claws have passed for centuries. The rock beneath shows through in places, polished by rain and animals. It feels settled, as if the city has been wearing the same wet skin for a long time. Nothing here happens quickly. The fog lifts over hours. A flock may take an afternoon to move from one block to the next. The water is the most patient thing. It has carved channels in the basalt so deep that people walk through them as streets.

From the upper terraces, you can see that the plateau is not a single city but a continuous urban crust stretching beyond the horizon. It has its own weather: clouds pile against the higher stacks, rain falls in one district while another is dry. The sky is a different color over the factory quarter. The wind follows the canals. It is not a city with a landscape around it; it is the landscape.

The sound is loud, but it is not chaotic. There is the hum of engines, the hiss of steam, the drip of water, the clank of rail cars, and the constant calls of animals. The animals have learned the pitches of the machines. Some sing in the same key as the pipes. The result is a kind of industrial chorale, always present, rising and falling with the work shifts. The city and its fauna are so intertwined that they seem to share a metabolism. The animals are drawn to the warm exhaust, but they also clean the mineral films from the turbines. Their bodies, when they die, are collected by the city’s smaller scavengers and by the water, and they become part of the mineral mats. It is not a cycle anyone planned. It just is.

Change is slow. The rain comes as a long wet exhalation. The animals migrate not in a rush but over weeks, like a tide. The mineral mats grow and recede with the seasons, if you can call them seasons. The city is repaired by the same slow logic: rust is scraped and painted, stones are reset, but nothing is ever finished. The water returns, always, and the animals cover whatever surface has been left still for long enough.

There is one unusual rule here, though it does not announce itself: the landscape is not made of living things and built things side by side. They have worn into each other so completely that a pipe is a roost, a gutter is a river, a mineral mat is a field, and the city breathes like a body. It is a vast, wet, warm, industrial plateau, dense with animals, loud with engines, and patient beneath all of it.
