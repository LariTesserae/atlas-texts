The Cold Chancel

Above the tree line, on a ridge that looks as if it were cracked open a century ago, there is a narrow rift. The mountains around it are immense, but the place itself is small: no larger than a cathedral nave, forty meters high, twenty wide, perhaps a hundred long. It is not a cave, though it is darker than many caves. It is a vertical slot between two slabs of freshly broken gabbro, with a floor of angular boulders that still hold their sharp corners.

The rock is dark gray, almost black, with veins of white quartz and flecks of pyrite that gleam in the faint animal light. The walls are polished in patches where the fault rubbed them to a mirror; elsewhere they are jagged, flaked, so sharp that a bare hand placed carelessly will come away cut. Nothing has had time to round these edges. There is no soil, no sand, only rock flour from the recent grinding of the mountain. Some of the boulders on the floor are balanced, still settling, and a footstep can send a slab sliding with a ring like a struck bell.

Light is scarce. The sky above is a thin ribbon, often filled with cloud. For an hour or two around noon, if the clouds part, a blade of white sun enters the slot and moves down the west wall. The lichens there, black and rust, seem to wake, and for that time the upper reach of the Chancel is visible. Below, the dark remains nearly complete. The rest of the light is biological: blue-green points on the walls, pale glows in the pools, slow waves of blue passing through the air. It is not the dark of empty space; it is a dark full of small lamps.

Water comes from the clouds, not from rain. The ridge sits at extreme height, where the air is thin and cool. Every afternoon, cloud pours over the crest, condenses on the cold rock, and runs down in thin films. The walls weep. Water beads on ledges, drips from overhangs, gathers in hollows in the floor. There are permanent pools, fed by hidden seeps, and temporary films that appear after the cloud has passed. The sound of water is present but rarely dominant.

The vegetation is sparse. There are no trees, no shrubs, no ferns. In places where water runs in sheets, a pale, hair-like moss grows in curtains no wider than a hand. Crustose lichens, black and rust and gray-green, spread over the upper walls. In the pools, colorless algae form thin mats. The plants live like animals, on surfaces, without roots. The place is too young and too dark for anything more.

The animals are overwhelming. The walls are not bare rock; they are a living skin. The lowest layer is a dense colony of pale, barrel-shaped polyps, each the size of a thumb, their tubes pressed together until they form a continuous rubbery crust. Above them are black wall-lice, flattened and many-legged, overlapping like shingles. They are so numerous that when one shifts, the whole wall appears to ripple. On ledges and in cracks sit the song-newts, pale and blind, their eye sockets grown over. They sit nose-downward, letting water run over their backs, and call in chiming notes. In the pools are pale octopuses, each no bigger than a fist, each in a crevice, each watching with one eye. The air is full of glass-flies, small white midges, so many that the air itself feels thick. If you stand still, the wall-lice will climb over your boots; if you lower a hand into a pool, something will brush it; if you breathe too deeply, you inhale a dozen wings.

The sound is loud, but not mechanical. The song-newts call in notes that match the dimensions of the rift; their calls stack into standing waves, so some notes appear to come from inside your chest. The wall-lice click and rustle; the polyps make a wet, faint sound as they open and close; the drips are sharp and rhythmic; the glass-flies make a thin, collective whine. When a slab shifts somewhere in the mountain, the whole chamber rings like a bell. The loudness is continuous, but it changes slowly, rising and falling over hours rather than minutes.

Strangeness is everywhere. The lantern-flies, which are not the same as glass-flies, carry two small blue lights on their abdomens. Once in many hours, the entire population pulses together: not quickly, but in a slow wave that begins at the floor and climbs to the sky-slot, taking so long that you might think you imagined it. The wave passes through the wall-lice too, which all ripple at once, so the chamber seems to breathe. Some of the lights on the walls are not animals but pyrite crystals, and some of the “crystals” are animals; you cannot tell which until they move.

The rules here are different. Light is something you make, not something given. To be visible is to spend energy, so light is used for signaling, for luring, for warning. The food web is mineral: windblown dust and dissolved minerals feed the biofilms; the polyps and wall-lice graze the biofilms; the newts and octopuses eat the grazers; the glass-flies feed on slime and each other. Dead bodies fall into the pools and become mineral again. There is no soil, so the dead do not rot away; they are encrusted with new life.

Nothing builds here. There are no nests, no shelters, no walls made by any creature. The only technology is the stone flake. The pool octopuses choose sharp flakes of quartz or gabbro from the floor and use them to scrape polyps from the rock or to wedge open crevices. They do not shape them; they carry a flake for a while, then drop it. Some flakes on the pool floor are polished by generations of use, but they are still only stones.

The place is patient. The polyps grow a millimeter a year. The octopuses may live for decades. The fissure widens so slowly that the mountain’s settling is a rumor. Change comes in long pulses: a wet season that lasts years, a rockfall that resets one wall, a shift in the seep patterns. The fastest visible event is the slow wave of lantern-light, and even that takes an hour to cross a space a person could cross in thirty strides.

The air is cool, thin, and wet; it smells of wet stone, copper, and the faint sweetness of animal breath. The animal bodies warm the chamber a few degrees above the outside air, so the temperature is cool but not bitter. Above the slot, the sky is a pale seam, often invisible. Inside, the dark is full of small lights and smaller sounds. If you stay too long, your heartbeat seems to fall into time with the drips, and the boundary between your body and the place grows thin.

This is not a place built for anyone. It is a place that is alive, crowded and dark and newly broken, humming to itself in the high cold.
