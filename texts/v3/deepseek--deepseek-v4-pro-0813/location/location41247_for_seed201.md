High in a young range that has not yet had time to soften, the cirque opens like a small room in the mountain’s side. It is not a landscape you walk across so much as a place you enter. The whole of it can be taken in from the col at its lip: a bowl about a hundred and fifty paces deep, twice that across, bounded by two sharp lateral moraines and a headwall that leans forward slightly, as if listening. The elevation is extreme. The air is thin, and the light arrives with almost no atmospheric softening, so bright on the pale quartzite that it hurts. But step from sun into shadow and the cold comes through the ground like a warning. In the sun it is barely above freezing. In shade it is well below.

The rock is young. It has not been weathered round. There are fresh scars of recent rockfall, bone-white against grey, not yet stained. Edges are conchoidal, sharp as broken porcelain. Underfoot, flakes ring hollow. The floor does not run smooth; it steps down in low slabs, each one polished and striated by the ice that only recently pulled back. At the back of the bowl a tongue of old firn still clings, crumbling into a steep apron of ice gravel. Meltwater from it does not form a stream. It seeps, drips, beads. For most of the day there is no open water at all. Instead the place is damp: water films the underside of stones, darkens the joints, and runs in threads only where the sun has warmed a patch of snow. At night it freezes; by midday it drips again. Water collects in pockets—a series of stone cups, none larger than a basin—and in these cups a miniature hydrology occurs: overflow from one to another, slow loss to evaporation, refilling from mist or melt. There is no lake, no pond, only a thousand small glistenings that come and go.

Despite the cold and the raw rock, the vegetation is lush, but only in the way a dry land can be lush around a spring. It is not continuous. Much of the bowl is empty stone and pale mineral soil, with so much space between one living thing and the next that the air itself feels hollow. But wherever water lingers, green concentrates intensely. The cracks are crammed with mosses thick as a hand. Cushion plants—saxifrage, moss campion—form dense, hard hummocks, each one a tiny sun-trap of leaves and flowers. Dwarf willows no taller than a finger crawl along warm rock. Lichens in black, orange, chalk-green cover the older erratics in layers, making them look like old tapestries. From a distance, the place is mostly bare. Only when you kneel do you see the lushness. It is vertical, local, dense in the cracks. The vegetation has learned to hold its breath until it finds shelter, then exhale all at once.

The fauna is abundant, but it is an abundance of small, wary, quiet things. Pikas live in the talus; their hay piles sit between the boulders, each one a tidy stack of dried saxifrage and grass, and they move in quick, soft bursts. Snow finches and alpine accentors work the rock, their wings making no more than a flicker of sound. Black redstarts patrol the sunlit slabs, catching insects that are themselves numerous: midges in brief silvery columns above the melt pools, springtails hopping on the snow surface, spiders hunting beneath stones. In the meltwater cups, small dark worms tremble in the sun. Higher on the cliffs, a small herd of wild goat-like animals crosses, hooves quiet on the ledges. A stoat works the talus in a loping search, white against grey, disappearing and reappearing. There is predator and prey here, all active, all keeping to the edges of sound. The silence is not stillness. The silence is the result of many creatures moving softly because a pebble falling is a major event, and a single call can carry across the whole bowl. So the animals use quiet as shelter.

The place is active in its own cold way. The freeze-thaw cycle has not stopped: each morning, fresh flakes of rock have fallen. Each day, meltwater finds new routes; each evening, ice seals them. Mist from the valley below occasionally pours over the lip, wets every surface, and burns off in the next sun. Cloud shadows move fast across the walls. The firn at the back calves small pieces of ice into the farthest cup, sometimes with a sound like a tiny glass bell. The pikas’ hay piles settle as the talus shifts. Birds follow the sunlit edge, always just at the boundary of shade. Even the plants move, far too slowly to see: roots prying deeper, moss fibers expanding with damp. What you hear is never a chorus. It is a tick of water, a rattle of displaced stone, the brief chitter of a pika, then a long silence that has physical weight.

The marks of people are scattered and primitive. There was never a settlement here. What remains are remnants of those who used the pass, or watched the animals, or sheltered on their way elsewhere. At the northern edge of the bowl, where the wall gives a little, someone piled flat stones into a low semicircular windbreak, just high enough to sit behind. It is partly collapsed. Farther in, a line of cairns marks an old route down over the col, but the route is no longer used and some of the cairns have fallen. There is a stone slab pinned between two boulders, a roof over a crevice, and beneath it a scatter of splintered goat bones, plus a needle of carved bone, drilled but not broken. Nearby, strands of hand-twisted plant fiber, perhaps old rope, are wedged in a crack. There is no metal. The technology here is all stone, bone, sinew, and fiber, the kind of things made with hands and patience at the edge of the habitable world. The structures are so few and so low that from more than thirty paces they vanish into the talus.

There is an oddity about the place, but it is not dramatic. No guardian spirit, no obvious anomaly. The weirdness lives in the details. The cairns, for example, are warm to the touch even when the surrounding rock is not—not hot, just sun-warm, as if they have stored more light than they should. The lichens on one large striped boulder smell faintly of damp earth and honey, though there is no flower. The pikas’ hay piles each have one white stone set on top, always on the same side, too deliberate to be accident. And sometimes, in the middle of a perfectly still morning, a single thread of condensation rises from a crack in the floor, like breath, though there is no warmer air below that anyone can explain. The animals seem to know the rule of the place. They move through the bowl not in short straight lines but in slow loops around certain stones, as if those points held warmth or danger. Sound does not travel as it should: a shout dies quickly, but the tiny crack of a pebble can carry across the whole bowl. It is a place where the air listens more than it speaks.

At night, though it is bright under moon or stars, the temperature drops hard. The damp in the cracks freezes, and the whole bowl creaks once or twice as the cold tightens the stone. By dawn, frost lies on the south-facing moss, and the meltwater starts again only when the sun clears the rim. The day begins with thousands of small drips and the first circuits of the birds. The place is not empty, and it is not still. It is a small, cold, bright, sharp-edged room at the top of the world: densely inhabited in its sparse way, where water is mostly a promise, plants hide their lushness in cracks, animals live in whispers, and the stone is still so young that it is learning what shape to become.
