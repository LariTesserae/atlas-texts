At the top of the old geothermal field, where the ridge narrows and the clouds pass below like a dirty sea, there is a room. It is not large. It is a single maintenance gallery hung on the side of a vertical engine house, and it is the only part of the plant still breathing. From outside it is a rust-brown box of riveted plates, bolted to a black summit at an altitude that makes the sky look violet. But inside, it is another country.

The room is about thirty-five paces long, twelve wide, and maybe fifty feet to the roof, but the floor is mostly open grating. Below the grating, the shaft drops four hundred feet. So the room is small, but it has no true floor; it is a ledge around a void. Most of its volume is air. It is emptier than a cave, because a cave has rock; here the walls are sheet steel and copper and painted concrete, and the only thing between them is space.

Every surface is made. There is no stone, no soil, no raw timber. The walls are riveted iron, the floor is diamond-plate and mesh, the ceiling is a tangle of conduits and steam lines. The place was built as a condenser house for the old geothermal exchange: a nest of pipes that took hot vapor from the mountain and made electricity and warm water. The machines are still there. A main condenser squats against the north wall, wrapped in insulation that has gone soft and green. A row of pressure gauges trembles on a central column. A hoist hangs over the shaft, its cable greased but not recently.

It has rained in this room every day for maybe a century. Not rain from the sky. The warm air hits the cold plates, condenses, runs down the rivets, and drips. Twice an hour, a valve above the north wall releases a lungful of steam, and for a few minutes the whole room is fog. The moisture settles on the walls, the plants, the gauges. It pools in the depressions of the floor grating. It runs into the drain channels with a sound you cannot hear for the noise. The room is never dry. It is as damp as a mouth.

And because it is warm and wet, it has become lush. But the lushness is not soil-deep; it is a green pelt over a metal skeleton. Moss covers the north wall and the condenser insulation in a layer as thick as a fist. Ferns grow out of flanges and pipe joints, uncurling into the steam. Vines with small gluey pads follow the pipe runs, wrapping around valve wheels and pressure gauges. In the elbows of the big ducts, bromeliads sit like cupped hands, each one holding a few ounces of water. The whole room is green, but it is not full. The vegetation lines the surfaces; it does not fill the void. It is lush like fur is lush, not like a jungle is lush. There is no jungle here, only an old machine wearing a garden.

The fauna is the same: abundant but small. The room is full of life, but the life is tiny. Pale frogs live in the bromeliad cups, their skin so translucent you can see the bubbles of air in their bellies. They call at the same pitch as the turbine, so it is hard to tell where the machine ends and the frogs begin. Geckos the color of verdigris run along the hot pipes, their feet not burning. White spiders build wet webs between the valve wheels; the webs are beaded with condensation and look like nets of glass. Moths with wings like wet paper cluster on the lamps. In the drain channels, small blind fish-things flash in the warm runoff. They may have come up from a deeper sump, or they may have always been here. No one has looked.

The sound is the room’s true architecture. It is a continuous roar, a deep drone from the turbine transmitted through the deck plates, overlaid with a high hiss of steam and the rattle of every loose rivet. It is loud enough to feel in your teeth. It is not chaotic; it has a pulse, a slow beat from the expansion joints. The frogs sing inside it. The plants hum with it. You cannot speak over it. You hear it in the bones of your jaw. After a while, it becomes a kind of silence: the only silence that can exist here is the sound itself.

The light is ordinary, which is strange. It comes through a high row of wired glass, and from a few electric lamps that have not burned out in decades. It is a steady, overcast light, like an afternoon in a factory. The steam diffuses it, so there are few hard shadows. The green looks green, not spectral. At noon, if the sky is clear, the room fills with a pale gold haze; at night, the lamps keep the same evenness. The place does not know day from night.

There is a slow strangeness in the room. The plants have started to imitate the machine. A vine grows in a perfect spiral around a torsion cable, its leaves the shape of the warning tags. A mat of moss grows around a pressure gauge in a neat ring, leaving the dial readable as if the moss were a bezel. The frogs are the color of the copper pipes. The snail shells are made of copper carbonate, borrowed from the wall. The room has stopped distinguishing between organism and apparatus. It is not that the machine is alive; it is that the alive and the made have entered a long, quiet truce.

The processes here are slow. The sound is fast, but everything else is patient. The main valve opens every eleven hours. The steam release lasts nine minutes. The bromeliad pools rise and fall a few millimeters a day. The moss grows a thumb’s width in a year. The rust advances in a film. The frogs breed slowly in the wet moss. The room has not changed its shape in decades, but it is changing all the time, the way a shell thickens or a tooth decays. It has reached an established state, a balance between corrosion and growth, between water and heat. It has been here long enough to belong.

There are rules. The galvanized plates near the south end are thinned; do not step on them. The green pipe is hot enough to cook a hand; do not touch it. The yellow valve is seized; if you force it, the room will fill with live steam. The plants are not entirely passive. Some of the bromeliads have a grip, and if you brush them, they hold for a moment, then let go. The frogs will climb onto your boot and sit there. If you drop a tool, it falls through the grating and into the sump, and it may come back years later as a mineral-encrusted lump if the sump is ever cleaned. There is no soil to stand on. The room does not care about human time.

At the top of the mountain, the building is a small metal wart on a black spine. But inside, it is a world: one room, hot and wet and loud, hung with green fur, empty of everything but life and noise. It is a place that happens when industry gets old enough to be colonized, when a machine stops being a tool and becomes a terrain.
