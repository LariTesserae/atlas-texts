The first thing is the heat, and the way it seems to come not from the sky but from the ground itself, as if the earth has swallowed a great fire and is only now beginning to cool. The air is thick with it — humid, heavy, carrying the smell of wet stone and hot mineral. The sky is dim, a low ceiling of gray-brown, and the light that comes through it feels filtered, exhausted, as if it has traveled a very long way to reach this place.

I have been walking for days. I cannot say exactly when I crossed from the old world into this one. Perhaps it was at the river crossing, or the dry lakebed, or the ridge of black rock. Perhaps there was no crossing at all — only a gradual realization that the ground had changed. The mud beneath my feet had been pressed with clay. And then the clay had been cut into shapes. And then the shapes had been fitted together, edge to edge, until I was walking on a floor.

The floor goes on forever.

It is made of tiles. Each one is different — some as small as my open hand, others large as a table. They are fired clay, brick-red and gray-brown, rough to the touch, with the faint marks of fingers pressed into their corners. They fit together without mortar, but so tightly that no weed could grow between them — though in truth there are no weeds here, no plants of any kind. The ground is a mosaic of hands.

It is hot through the soles of my boots. In places I can feel the individual tiles through the leather, their warmth radiating up my legs. The heat is not constant. Some stretches are merely warm, others burn. I learn to step around the hot places, though they are not visible. I know them by the shimmer in the air, the way the heat bends the dim light, and by the way the steam rises more thickly from their cracks.

Rain comes often here. It comes without warning and stops without warning, not fading in and out but arriving and departing all at once, as if a door had been opened and then closed. The drops are warm — the same temperature as my skin, so when they fall on my hands I cannot tell if they are rain or my own sweat. They fall slowly, thickly, as through a denser air, and when they strike the hot tiles they do not splash but spread and sizzle softly, turning instantly to steam.

After the rain the whole plain steams. Thin threads rise from every crack and joint. They weave upward, sometimes curling together into thicker columns that stand like pale towers across the flatness. The columns move. They lean and turn, bending toward me or away from me, not because of any wind — there is no wind here — but as if the steam itself were curious, or sensing, or reaching.

The steam is the only thing that grows.

The plain is quiet. The rain is the loudest thing, and even the rain is soft, like a hand running over cloth. My own footsteps are the next-loudest: a small, dry clack-clack of boots on clay that seems to carry much further than it should, as if the flatness is a sounding board stretched across a hollow space. Sometimes, far away, I hear a clatter — a sound like a single tile falling, or a stack of them collapsing. It comes from no direction I can fix. It is always distant, always singular, never repeated.

Underneath everything, there is a deeper sound, too low to be called a sound. I feel it in my teeth and in the bones of my ankles. It is like the rumble of a fire deep underground, or the slow grinding of stone against stone. It does not stop. It is the sound of creation, perhaps, or the sound of the plain breathing.

Scattered across the vast floor are the signs of the makers. I call them makers because I do not know what else to call them. At first I thought the place was abandoned — a great work interrupted, the builders vanished. But the evidence says otherwise.

I came upon a kiln. It was built of the same clay tiles, rising in a dome taller than my shoulder. Around it lay the tools: a wooden paddle for shaping, cracked and warped by the heat; a coil of braided rope, some fiber I could not identify; a shallow basket woven so finely it held water. The kiln was cold. But inside were tiles, neatly stacked, each one formed and dried, waiting for the fire that had never come. They were flawless. In the soft clay of one, pressed into the corner, was the print of three fingers — but the print was as long as my hand.

Later I found a line of fresh tiles, still warm, set into the floor in a long curve that ran to the horizon. They had not been there the day before — or if they had, I had walked over that very spot and not seen them. I knelt and touched one. It gave slightly under my fingers, not yet fully fired, like the crust of a loaf before the oven. The heat in it was exactly the heat of a thing that had just been made.

There was no one else. For miles in every direction, the plain was empty. Only the tools. Only the work. Only the line of new tiles running off into the shimmer.

The distances here are not to be trusted. I have walked toward a dark shape on the horizon for what seemed like an hour, only to look up and find it no nearer. At other times, things appear suddenly, as if they have been there all along and I simply failed to see them until I was almost upon them: a low wall, a pile of broken tiles, a shallow pool mirror-bright in the dim light.

The plain is mostly empty. That is the strangest part. It is made — every inch of it deliberate, worked, shaped by hand — and yet between the made things there is nothing but the floor itself, stretching away in unbroken flatness. The makers did not build a city. They built a surface, and on that surface they have placed only a few things, widely separated, as if testing the placement, or as if they are not finished, or as if the emptiness itself is the purpose.

The rules of this place are not quite the rules I know. I have noticed, for instance, that the ground is not always solid in the way it should be. Once, at the edge of my vision, I saw the floor ripple, like a cloth shaken by a hand beneath. When I turned, it was still. Another time, I watched a pool of rainwater and saw, reflected in it, a sky that was not this sky — a clear blue sky with a bright sun, and birds crossing slowly, and trees on a far shore. When I looked up, the sky above me was the same dim brown it always was. When I looked down again, the pool showed only more of the same gray light.

The light itself behaves strangely. There is no shadow source I can determine. The shadows of the standing tiles point in different directions, even when the tiles are side by side. Sometimes my own shadow lags behind me, as if it had stopped to look at something and was now hurrying to catch up.

And time — time is not even. There are places on the plain where the heat has aged things centuries in moments. I found a tool, a wooden mallet, lying on the ground. One side of it was fresh, the wood still pale and sharp with recent carving. The other side was gray and soft and crumbling, as if it had lain for generations. The two states met in a perfect line, like the seam between two different empires of decay.

I have wondered how such simple tools could make such a vast floor. The answer, I think, is that the makers have never been in a hurry. They work as slowly as stone weathers, as steadily as the rain falls. The plain is not built by power but by patience.

Where the rain pools, the water does not stay clear for long. Minerals rise into it from the clay, from the ground below, from the heat. Within hours the pools develop skins — thin membranes of white and rust-orange and pale green, like oil on water but dry and crystalline. They grow thicker at the edges, forming little terraces and rims. In some places these mineral crusts have built up into low mounds, like the nests of insects, or the first footholds of a forest that has not yet decided what it will be.

They are the only forests here: forests of salt and sulfur and iron, growing by accretion, dissolving in the rain, regrowing in the steam. They have no leaves, but when the light is dim they seem to move, expanding and contracting with some slow pulse of their own.

There are no plants here. I keep expecting them — a tuft of grass in a crack, a lichen on a stone, the green scum on a pool's edge. But there is nothing. The cracks between the tiles are empty, or filled with mineral growth. The pools are clear of life, or populated only by the crystalline skins I have described. The rain falls on bare clay and bare steam. It is the only living thing, and it is not alive.

No animals either. No birds in the sky, though I have scanned it many times. No insects landing on my skin. No tracks in the mud except my own and the strange prints of the makers. It is a place of silence not because it is empty of motion but because it is empty of voices. The only other presence is the heat, and the steam, and whatever moves beneath the floor.

Once I saw a maker. At least, I believe I saw one. The rain had just stopped and the steam was thick, rolling across the tiles in pale waves. Far off, through the veils, something tall moved. It was bipedal, I think, but too tall, and its arms were too long, and its head was shaped wrong. It walked with a slow, careful gait, and it seemed to be carrying something — a tile, perhaps, or a tool. I could not see clearly. I called out, or tried to, but no sound came from my throat; the quiet swallowed it. The shape stopped. Then the steam thickened, and when it thinned again, the shape was gone.

I went to where it had been. The tiles there were newly laid, still hot. In the mud beside them was a print. It was not a footprint. It was a depression the size of a large tile, pressed into the ground, with three long grooves radiating from it like fingers or roots. I knelt and put my hand inside it. It was warm. The grooves fit my fingers, but only if my fingers were two feet long.

This is a low place. I can feel it in the air pressure, in the weight of the sky. It is as if the plain lies at the bottom of a great bowl, a basin that drains the sky of its water and holds it here. The horizons are not raised; they simply are not there, or they are too far to see. But the sense of depth is real. The sky sits close. The heat collects. The water runs downhill from everywhere and settles here, in the low pavement, in the shallow pools, in the cracks that open and close.

Sometimes I think of the land that used to be here — the original ground, before the makers paved it. Was it a marsh? A lakebed? A sea floor? The water is everywhere, rising from below and falling from above. The place has the feel of a drained inland sea — ancient, low, heavy with salt and silence — except that it has been overlaid with this skin of tiles, this patient mosaic, as if someone had found the emptiness and filled it with the only thing they had: mud and fire and the prints of their hands.

The plain is not stable. I have learned this. One afternoon, as I sat by a pool, I watched a crack open in the floor before me — a long, jagged fissure that ran from the pool to the horizon. It opened without sound, smooth as a sleepwalker turning over. Steam rose from it, hot and sulfurous. For a long time I watched it, and then, slowly, the crack began to close. The tiles on either side shifted, grinding against each other, and the fissure narrowed, and by nightfall it was gone. The floor was whole again, but the tiles on one side were different — older, darker, worn, as if they had come from another part of the plain, or another time.

This happens often. The place is in a slow, continuous rearrangement. What is built high becomes low; what was open becomes sealed; what was distant becomes near. It is not chaos. There seems to be a purpose to it, a logic I cannot follow, as if the plain is smoothing out some kink in itself, repairing some invisible wound, or shifting in its sleep.

The rules, as far as I can tell, are these: The rain never falls while a shadow shines — though there is no sun to shine. The tiles are never broken for longer than a night, and by morning the cracks are sealed with fresh clay. The steam always rises, never sinks, even when the wind should press it down. The distant always stays distant, unless it chooses to come near. And the makers are never seen squarely, but their work is always warm.

Night here is not dark. The tiles cool from hot to gray, but they hold their warmth, and from the cracks a faint glow rises, like the embers of a vast fire banked under ash. The steam catches this light and glows, so that the plain at night is a sea of pale towers, slowly breathing. I sit and watch them. They are the only constellations this world has.

The plain is very young. I know this because it is unweathered. The tiles are sharp-edged, the tools are unbroken, the walls are straight. But here and there, I see the first signs of wear. In the channels between tiles, where the rain runs off, the water has begun to carve tiny ruts. They are shallow, no deeper than a finger's width, but they are the first. The edges of some tiles are rounded, just slightly, where water has dripped for longer than it should. In a few places, a single tile has cracked, and the rain has found the crack, and widening it, cell by cell.

The place has known no seasons, no freezing, no roots, no wind-driven sand. It has known only rain and heat and the slow work of water. And yet even here, in a landscape made by hands, the water has begun its patient undoing. The erosion has begun. I think the makers know. Perhaps they are always repairing it. Perhaps that is why the work never stops — not because the place is still being built, but because it is already beginning to be lost.

The rain begins again. I sit on a low wall and let it fall on me. The drops are warm, like hands. The steam rises. The plain stretches away in all directions, flat and vast and incomplete. For the first time, I feel that I am not an observer here. I am a part of the work. My sitting still, my breathing, my watching — it is all part of the same slow effort that lays the tiles and opens the fissures and closes them again.

The quiet is not empty. It is the sound of a place being made, and unmade, and made again. It is the sound of no birds, no leaves, no voices — only the rain, and the steam, and the great breathing floor.

And so I stay. I stay many days, watching the work. I learn which tiles are cool enough to sit on. I learn the taste of the pools — iron and salt, and something bitter, like the dust of old kilns. I learn to see the shapes in the steam. And I learn, perhaps, what the makers have always known: that the work is endless, not because it is unfinished, but because it is always beginning. The plain is emerging. It has only just begun.
