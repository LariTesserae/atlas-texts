Before it is seen, it is heard. The sound comes down through the cloud as a low, braided murmur, as if a crowd were talking just too far away to be understood. The approach is through a narrow cleft in the mountain’s lower ribs. The rock underfoot is dark and wet, not from rain but from the fine sweat the cloud leaves on everything. The air is cold, only a few degrees above freezing, and breath turns to fog that joins the general mist. The daylight is even and grey, bright but heatless, like light filtered through water.

Then the cleft opens into a small hanging cirque, a room in the mountain perhaps a hundred and sixty paces from wall to wall. The headwall rises in terraces, and on those terraces, half-dissolved into the rock, is a town.

It is a settlement the size of a hamlet or a roofless cathedral. It has streets, stairways, thresholds, a central basin, and a line of small chambers climbing the terraces. But it is old in a way that is hard to measure. The stones of its walls are rounded like river rocks. The lintels have sagged into soft curves. The stairs are worn into tilted cups. The place is not falling down; it has already fallen, long ago, and then been worn smooth by the time since. It is being reabsorbed by the mountain.

Water is the first rule. It does not rain often, but the place is never dry. At the top of the headwall, meltwater issues from a cleft and runs down through the settlement along an organized network of channels. The streets are gutters. The gutters are streets. They branch and rejoin, spill over terraces, fill shallow basins, and pass through holes in the walls. The water is cold and faintly milky with dissolved minerals. Where it runs, it lays down a translucent layer of travertine. Over centuries, the town has been thickened by the same water that erodes it. The result is a place of soft stone edges, pale and pitted and wet.

The vegetation is lush, but it is a high, cold lushness. There are no trees above the tree line. Instead the stone is covered with mosses: cushion moss in harsh green, feather moss in olive, liverworts in deep maroon. They grow over the walls, the stairs, the roofs, the cistern rim. Saxifrages and stonecrops root in the wall joints, each rosette holding a bead of water at its center. In the deepest overhang, a few ferns uncurl. Low willows creep along the wetter seams, no taller than a hand. Lichens crust the drier south faces in grey and orange. The vegetation is dense but close to the ground, a thick pelt that drinks the fog and holds the water. It makes the town look as if it has been upholstered in living green. But the density is in the surfaces, not the volume. The air in the rooms is empty; the lanes are empty; the doorways hold no doors. Only the horizontal skin of the town is crowded.

The town is empty. There is no smoke, no midden, no tracks in the mud. No birds nest in the doorways. The central basin holds clear black water and no fish. The only animals are rare: a raven that crosses the cirque once or twice a day, a few midges above the moss when the air briefly warms, pale springtails in the wet cracks. The emptiness is not desolate; it is hollow, like the inside of a bell.

And the place is loud. The spring chatters down its first chute and falls into a stone basin with a ringing note. Then it divides into a set of channels, each with a different pitch. One gurgles through a narrow slot. Another runs over a ribbed sill and gives a low burble. Another drops into a deeper cistern and echoes. The wind plays the empty rooms. Some doorways hum. Some slots whistle. When the wind shifts, the largest chamber gives a thrum felt in the chest. The stone ticks as it cools. Ice cracks in shadowed crevices. Drops patter from every moss edge. The sound is continuous and many-layered, loud enough that you notice the absence of animals only slowly: almost everything audible here is water, wind, or stone.

The light is ordinary by itself, but it is cold. At midday the cloud ceiling glows white, and the wet stone shines gray. Where the sun breaks through, the moss turns a violent green and the water sheets flash. But the sun often stays hidden behind the cloud, and the light remains even and shadowless, without warmth.

What makes the place strange is its order. The stairways are too steep for comfortable feet and too even for water alone. The largest chamber is a rounded bell-shape with a single hole in the floor, not a window, and any sound made inside returns altered, a fifth lower. There are niches too small to hold more than a hand or a voice. The walls carry shallow meanders that may be writing or the fossilized paths of water. Some buildings have no doors, only narrow slots through which the wind enters and exits. The settlement seems built less for shelter than for sound. It is a town that listens, or a town that sings. Once, perhaps, it was a dwelling for something; now it is an instrument for the mountain.

The technology is nothing. There is no metal, no worked wood, no cord, cloth, glass, or gear. The only tools that remain are gravity, water, frost, and moss. The walls were made by fitting stone against stone, or perhaps by carving the bedrock, but the work is so old and so water-worn that it is hard to separate construction from geology. It is built, clearly built—the streets and thresholds and basins are organized—but it is built without devices, without machines, and without any active hand.

The erosion is ancient. The rock has the smoothness of bones in a river. Paving has cracked into soft polygons, and moss has widened each crack. Carvings, if they were carvings, are nearly illegible. Frost has scaled the walls, and travertine has healed them. Nothing is sharp. The settlement has settled into a final form, like a jawbone on a beach, still being polished by the water.

There are rules. Water may run but never pool except in the appointed basins. Frost may crack the stone, but the moss holds the moisture and releases it slowly, so the walls do not shatter. Wind enters through the high slots and leaves through the low ones. The north faces are green with moss; the south faces are crusted with lichen. Anything that rests in the open chambers will wake wet, because the condensation comes every night. The channels clear themselves; the basins overflow; the moss filters the silt. The town does not need caretakers. The water is the caretaker.

The day runs in cycles. In the early morning, fog fills the cirque so completely that the town is only wet sounds behind a white curtain. By mid-morning the fog thins, and the water runs faster as the high snowmelt reaches the channels. By afternoon the wind rises, and the hollow rooms begin to moan. At dusk the temperature dips, and a thin skin of ice forms at the edges of the basins. At night the stone ticks and the water keeps moving under the ice. By dawn the ice melts back, and the sound begins again.

It is empty but not abandoned. It is small but holds more space than its size suggests. It is cold but green, ancient but still being made. A high stone town the size of a church, half-dissolved by water, carpeted in moss, loud as a waterfall and hollow as a bell, waiting in the cold grey light for the next drop to fall.
