It is a small place, a high hollow in the flank of a young shield volcano, a natural amphitheater perhaps two hundred paces across. The rim is sharp, black basalt, still glossy in places, cracked into blades and needles that have not yet been softened by wind or rain, because there is no rain. The land is warm, and the air is thin and dry. There is no water here anywhere—not a seep, not a drop of dew, not a film of moisture on the stone. The world is built on a different solvent. The life here runs on a clear, oily sap that smells of hot iron and cloves.

But the vegetation is lush. It is lush in the way a reef is lush, or a termite cathedral is lush: every surface is colonized, every edge softened by growth. The growth is not green. It is ochre and rust, bone-white, sulfur-yellow, dusty violet. There are mats of fine dry fiber like felt, rubbery straps hanging from ledges, hollow tubes standing upright like organ pipes, and flat plates overlapping like shingles. It looks soft, but it is dry to the touch, and if you broke a stem it would not bleed water; it would bead a resin that hardens into amber.

The light is ordinary daylight, bright and clear, and it falls into the hollow with hard shadows. The air smells of hot dust, sulfur, and something faintly sweet, like resin warmed by the sun. There is no wind, only the faintest drift of warm air rising from the rock. The density is almost suffocating. The growth and the structures and the animals are packed so close that walls touch, roofs overlap, and passages narrow to the width of a body. It is a city, though not a human one.

The animals have built it. They are everywhere—slow, many-limbed, dust-colored creatures, some the size of a seed, some as long as an arm. They move with the patience of lichen. A single step may take a minute; a gesture begun at dawn may finish at dusk. They are silent. They communicate by touching antennae, by laying trails of resin, by tapping hollow stems in rhythms too faint to hear. The only sounds are the faint rasp of dry fibers brushing together, the click of a claw on a mineral floor, the creak of a settling wall. The quiet is thick, like a held breath. There is no birdsong, no wind, no running water, no engine. Even the small sounds that do occur are swallowed almost at once by the dense growth.

What they have built is a settlement in miniature: galleries winding between living walls, chambers stacked four and five deep, ramps and spiral staircases. There are nurseries walled with soft white fiber and filled with pale young that pulse slowly, once or twice an hour. There are storehouses piled with dried flakes of the mineral vegetation. There are workshops where workers chew the flakes into a paste and shape it into walls and arches. There are no tools, no metal, no fire, no wheels. The architecture is inherited, known in the body, as regular as honeycomb. Every wall is alive or was alive; every floor is grown or made from the grown. Nothing is wasted.

In the center rises a single spire, taller than the rest, a cathedral-scale tower of mineral leaves and resin built over centuries. It is hollow, and inside the air is warmer, and the walls glow faintly with a cold bioluminescence that pulses once or twice an hour. The tower is the heart of the city, a place of assembly, but the animals pass through in single file, so slowly that the line seems like a pattern in the stone. They leave behind a fine dust of mineral waste, which the wind never stirs.

The rules of this place are simple: nothing is rushed, nothing is separate, and all waterless life returns to the rock. The city grows by the thickness of a single fiber in a season. A new chamber is added once a generation. The only perceivable change is the slow turn of the sun and the infinitesimal uncurling of a tendril toward its warmth. It is a place of patience, a dry city in a dry high volcanic bowl, where the natural world has become architecture and the architecture has stayed alive.
