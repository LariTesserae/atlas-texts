The landscape is called the Low Catchment, or sometimes the Unfinished Basin. It does not look like land. It looks like a city that forgot to leave room for the earth.

There is no true daylight. The dark is heavy but not empty; it is full of small sounds and small lights. Tallow lamps hang from rope lines, each a dull orange bead. Kiln-mouths glow like animal mouths in the gloom. The pale bloom on the walls gives off a faint green-white phosphorescence when touched. The light does not reveal the place; it only suggests it.

The ground is not ground. It is a continuous floor of brick, stone, and rammed clay, laid so tightly that no soil shows anywhere. It slopes almost imperceptibly toward the center, where the great sump called the Throat waits. The whole catchment sits low, below the surrounding upper galleries; water arrives from above and from the sides, not from the ground. The terrain is a shallow basin of made surfaces, terraced at its edges, draining inward.

Water is the weather. The air is cool and wet; every breath tastes of wet lime and damp clay. Condensation beads on every wall. The high ceiling of the district—where there is a ceiling—is hung with wooden drip-panels and waxed cloth. When the air cools, they sweat and shed a fine indoor rain. It falls not as a storm but as a million separate drops, each ticking against stone. Between the rains, the walls weep and the channels run thin and black. The sound of water is everywhere: a low, steady roar made of drips, trickles, gurgles, the hiss of steam from kilns, and the slap of chain-pumps.

The buildings rise from the pavement like teeth from a jaw. They are narrow and tall, six or seven storeys, timber-framed, brick-walled, roofed with overlapping slate or fired clay. They lean toward each other until upper floors almost touch, leaving only a slit of dark air between. Streets are crevices; two people cannot pass abreast. Rope bridges and plank walkways web the upper levels. There are no true plazas, only wider gaps where four alleys meet and a communal cistern stands. The density is such that you move by sound and memory, not sight.

Everything is new. The stone is fresh-cut, the bricks still sharp at the corners, the mortar still damp. There are no worn steps, no polished thresholds. The water has not had time to round anything; it has only made it slick. Half-built walls rise out of the dark, their top courses disappearing into scaffolding. Masons tap at blocks; laborers carry baskets of wet clay. The landscape is embryonic, still under construction, a world not yet finished. It is not eroding; it is accumulating.

The technology is primitive but pervasive. No engines. Only rope, wood, stone, copper, bone, and leather. Waterwheels turn in the deeper channels, lifting water with clay cups. Bellows of ox-hide sigh in the smithies and in the great air-drums that keep the upper galleries from going stale. Cranes are wooden tripods with counterweights of broken brick. Tools are stone hammers, copper chisels, bone awls, fired-clay scrapers. Fire is charcoal, and light is rendered fat. The work is done by hand, in shifts, in the dark.

There are no plants. Nothing green grows here. Instead there is the bloom, a pale fungal-mineral crust that appears on new brick and wet mortar, spreading in lacy patches. It is not plant; it is something between fungus and salt crystal, and it glows faintly when disturbed. The Makers scrape it off and chew it; it tastes of wet stone and metal. In the sumps and drain-gaps live the rare fauna: pale geckos with translucent bellies, blind crickets that sing inside the walls, and white thread-eels that coil in the silt. They are few, and their movements are read as signs.

Some buildings are grown as much as built. The mortar is mixed with powdered bloom, and over time it knits the bricks together; the walls heal their own cracks. Doors sometimes seal themselves shut; new openings appear where a wall has softened. The Makers say the catchment is still deciding what it is. The only rule is that water must be kept moving. A drip that falls on bare stone and is not caught is a small failure; ten thousand such failures would drown the world. When the pumps fall silent, the place grows too quiet, and that silence is the only true alarm.

The place never rests. There is a cycle: the great bellows draw moist air into the high cold galleries, where it condenses on stone plates; the droplets run into channels; the channels feed the cisterns; the cisterns feed the clay-works and the drinking bowls; the waste water is pumped back to the high galleries to be cooled again. The streets are always moving: work gangs, water-carriers, masons, bloom-scrapers, child-listeners who walk the alleys with hollow tubes to find leaks by sound. The kilns never completely cool; the hammers never stop for long.

The Low Catchment extends for miles, a regional maze of wards and brickfields, from the outer clay pits to the central Throat. It is not a single room; it is a country of made stone. It lies in darkness, cool and clammy, loud with its own construction, dense with half-finished walls and narrow passages, alive with the slow circulation of water. There is no wind, no birdsong, no green. Yet the place is loud, and the loudness is all made: water and wood and stone and human breath, building the world one wet brick at a time.
