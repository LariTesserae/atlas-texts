You find it not by seeing it, but by the way the ground changes underfoot. Out on the plain, the soil is thin, dry enough to crack, despite the nearness of the sea. Then, all at once, the ground grows soft, the heat rises, and a low, regular thudding comes up through the earth like a machine buried beneath the grass.

The hollow is hidden in plain sight. It is a shallow depression, no wider than a village courtyard, sunk into the flat coastal land. A low rim of moss and leathery leaves surrounds it, spilling over the edge like the lip of an old wound that has healed too full. From outside, the only visible sign is a handful of pale stalks poking above the rim, swaying slightly with the rhythm of the sound. You could pass within arm’s reach and mistake it for a wet patch, a minor sag in the land.

Step over the rim, and the world drops—not far, perhaps two feet—but the change is absolute. The light is ordinary, gray and bright, the way it is under a high overcast. But the air inside is hot and wet, as if you have put your head over a steaming pot. The ground underfoot is not ground at all. It is a continuous mat of living plant, a single species of liverwort or bog moss, thick and spongy, spread over the entire floor and up the sides of the basin. It is the floor, the walls, the substrate. It gives under every step, then springs back slowly, as if you are walking on a firm, warm sponge. In places, clear fluid oozes up around your boots and then closes again.

For all that vegetation, the space is not dense. The mat is total, but it is low and even. There are no thickets, no tangles, no walls of foliage closing in. Instead, the hollow is open, almost empty. The eye travels easily across the broad, wet floor. The only vertical forms are the stalks—a dozen of them, widely spaced. Each rises to waist height, some to shoulder height, hollow at the top. They are not wood, not stone, not constructed; they are made of the same plant tissue as the mat, but older, compressed, dark brown and ridged like horn. They look like organ pipes, or the vent pipes of a sunken machine. But if you touch one, it gives slightly—warm and damp—and the ridges are growth rings, not weld lines.

Between the stacks lie open pools of water. They are not separate ponds, exactly, but gaps in the mat, places where the plant skin has drawn back to reveal the water below. The water is warm, dark as steeped tea, and perfectly still. It never ripples, even when the ground shakes with the thudding from the stacks. The surface is covered in a thin film of oil—not fuel oil, but a plant wax, iridescent, breaking the gray light into faint rainbows. Along the edges, the mat has grown over the water in smooth, rounded lips, like the rim of a long-used basin. There are no raw edges, no crumbling banks, no sign of active erosion. The whole hollow has the settled, finished look of something very old.

The sound is the place’s true architecture. From the open mouths of the stacks comes a steady, rhythmic chuff—a wet, hollow pop, as if each is exhaling a bubble of gas. The rhythm is perfectly even, each stack in its own time but all of them together, so the air vibrates with a low, mechanical beat: chuff, chuff, chuff. Under it, there is a constant hiss, like steam escaping from a thousand small valves. And above that, a chorus of frogs. They sit on the mat between the stacks—small brown frogs with a single yellow line down the back—each one spaced as evenly as the stacks themselves. They call in time with the chuffs, a sharp, single click, perfectly synchronized. The whole basin pulses: chuff, click, chuff, click. It is loud, but not chaotic. It is the sound of something running smoothly, a machine idling at a constant speed.

The animals are more numerous than they first appear. In the open pools, the water is dark with tadpoles—thousands of them, black commas hanging just below the surface. Water beetles row through the oil film, leaving silver trails that close slowly. On the mat, between the frogs, small translucent larvae move through the wet tissue, visible as faint shadows. There are no birds, no snakes, no large animals; the hollow is too hidden and too hot, and the air is too thick. But the small life is dense, an abundance you feel more than see.

What is strange is not the life itself, but the order of it. The stacks are spaced with a regularity that seems more than chance. The frogs have arranged themselves at intervals that match the stacks. The bubbles rise at the same rate. The temperature is the same in every corner of the hollow: warm, damp, close. There is no wind, no shadow moving, no change. The light is flat and unchanging. The water level never rises or falls. The stacks never grow. The mat never thickens. The place is not growing, not decaying, not succeeding into something else. It is in equilibrium—a state as fixed and balanced as a flywheel spinning at constant speed.

There is no metal here, no concrete, no cut stone, no human-made object of any kind. No factory sits beyond the rim, no road, no power line; the plain is flat and empty. And yet the place feels industrial. It is not just that it sounds like an engine room; it behaves like one. The mat is a pump, drawing water up from below, filtering it, and expelling gas through the stacks. The pools are settling tanks. The frogs are timing devices. The whole hollow is a natural gasworks, a biological engine built entirely from plant tissue, running on warm water and fermentation. It has been here for a long time—long enough for the edges to smooth and the system to settle into its perfect, unchanging rhythm.

You leave the way you came, over the soft rim and back onto the dry plain. The sound fades quickly, damped by the moss. Within ten paces, the hollow is invisible again. Only the warm smell and the faint thudding in the ground remind you it is there—a hidden, low, wet pocket of the world, a machine made of moss, ticking forever.
