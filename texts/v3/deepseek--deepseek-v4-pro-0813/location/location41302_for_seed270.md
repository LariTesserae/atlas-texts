The place is not visible from above. Above it is a salt-crusted depression, a little lower than the surrounding plain, where the ground has settled over an old machine hall. The plain is cool, dry, and bare—no plants, no standing water, only a white mineral crust and a few rust-streaked stones. There is a hole, a collapsed vent no wider than a doorway. If you put your ear to it, you hear a continuous hissing-ticking, like a distant factory.

Descend through the vent, and the world changes. The shaft is made of old steel plates so corroded they look like brown bone. The air gets cooler and drier. It smells of ozone, iron dust, old grease, and something sharp like vinegar. The light does not fade; it becomes even. Its source is a row of ancient gas tubes set into a low ceiling. They burn with a flat, bluish-white light, exactly like an industrial work floor. That light is the sun here.

The room you enter is small—no wider than a barn, no higher than a tall man at the center, lower at the edges. It is a pocket, a hidden cavity beneath the salt plain. The walls are not natural rock. They are steel, concrete, ceramic, and layers of compacted rust, but they have been worn so smooth and so old that they look geological. Every edge is rounded. Every bolt hole is a dimple. The floor is a single shallow basin, polished by the movement of animals, and it dips toward a dark hole at the center.

There is no water anywhere. The air is dry enough to crack your lips. No pools, no dew, no mud. The only liquids are not water: a thin dark oil that weeps from old fittings, a weak acid sweat on copper, a grease that oozes from cracked cable insulation. These liquids are the drink of this world, and they are fought over.

There are no plants. No green, no leaves, no roots, no flowers. What looks like moss on the walls is not moss; it is a grey-gold crust, half mineral and half living, that grows toward the light tubes but does not photosynthesize. It feeds on rust and on the faint electric current still running through the rails in the floor. It is more like a bacterial felt or a slow chemical skin. In places it forms thick mats, and these mats are the pastures of this place.

The fauna is overwhelming. The floor is not empty. It is covered, nearly every inch, with small hard bodies. There are rust mites the size of rice grains, dust hoppers like tiny bullets, pale ticks with legs of black wire. They move constantly, and the floor seems to boil. The air is full of winged things—small fliers with mica wings, hundreds of them, orbiting the light tubes in tight circles. They shed a continuous rain of wing-scales that settles on everything. The density is so high that if you hold out a hand, it will be tapped within a second, and the air tastes of metal.

Among the grazers move larger animals. A creature like a hand-sized beetle of black ceramic and sinew taps the floor with a rivet-shaped mandible. When it finds a soft spot, it drills down and unspools a long pale tongue to lick up the crust. A predator the size of a cat, segmented like a centipede made of old drive chain, moves in short stops. It is blind; it listens for the specific tap of a drilling beetle, then strikes and carries it up the wall.

The walls are full of holes, and inside those holes live larger things. You rarely see them whole—a pale leg, a cluster of glass eyes, a brown carapace shifting in the dark. They wait. They only emerge when the light tubes dim, which happens on a schedule only they know.

The sound is loud and continuous. It is not one sound but many layers: the hiss of grazers, the whine of fliers, the tap-tap of beetles, the click of mandibles, the occasional shriek of a caught animal, and underneath it all a deep, regular clang. That clang comes from the center of the room.

At the center stands a single piece of old industrial machinery: a stamping press, no taller than a person, half-sunken into the living floor. It still works. A corroded flywheel turns slowly, driven by a buried motor or by the pressure of animals moving against it. Every few minutes, the press falls. The floor jumps. For an instant, every animal freezes. Then the noise resumes, louder, as if the clang had reset the room’s pulse.

Around the walls are scattered remains of the old built world. A control booth with a shattered window is stuffed with nest material and pale shed skins. A row of ceramic insulators juts from the wall, now used as perches. A pipe that once carried something—water, steam, perhaps—has become a highway for a constant stream of centipede things. The catwalks are gone, but their bolt-scars remain, polished like decoration. These are not ruins; they are the bones of the room, still in use.

The ecology is strange but strict. The first rule is that nothing drinks water, because there is none. The animals get moisture from the liquids of the machines: oil, grease, acid sweat. A leaking fitting is a watering hole, and animals fight over it. The second rule is that electricity is food. The crust on the floor grows along the current rails, the grazers eat the crust, and the predators eat the grazers. The whole food web runs on a faint current leaking from the old floor. The third rule is vertical. The space is small, so the floor belongs to grazers and small predators, the walls to nesters and climbers, the ceiling to fliers and light-feeders. The paths are narrow and always moving. If you stand in one place too long, the animals flow around you, but eventually they test you with their mandibles.

The place is active. It has cycles that feel like weather. The vents in the walls open and close, breathing cold air from deep below. Sometimes a fine rust fog drifts from the vents, and for a few minutes the room goes quieter; the grazers huddle under ledges, the fliers cling to the walls. Then the fog settles and the noise returns. There are migrations: every few hours a wave of dust hoppers moves from one wall to the other for no visible reason, and the predators follow at the edges. The stamping press sets a rhythm. The light tubes hum. The room is an engine made of animals.

It is also ancient beyond measure. The walls are not crumbling; they have been worn into their final shape. The floor is not cracked; it is polished. The steel is not bright but has a deep brown patina, like old leather. The industrial marks are fossils—stamped letters on a wall panel, almost unreadable, the shape of a gear worn to a shadow. This place has already decayed and then been eroded past decay. It has settled into a stable state, an equilibrium between living bodies and dead metal. It will remain this way until the light tubes fail or the current stops.

And yet there is one thing that remains unusual even for this place. At the very center, directly beneath the stamping press, there is a round hole in the floor. It is no wider than a fist, perfectly smooth, and dark. Every so often, a single grazer—always just one—detaches from the mat and crawls deliberately toward the hole. It does not stop to graze. It does not react to predators. It walks to the hole, steps over the edge, and drops. It does not come back. The predators do not follow. The other grazers do not approach. The hole makes no sound except a faint high singing, like a wire drawn too tight. The animals treat it as ordinary, but it is the only thing in the room that seems to have no explanation.

If you leave, climbing back up through the vent, the sound fades. The salt plain is silent again. But the ground beneath your feet is warm from the movement below, and for a long time you can feel the rhythm of the press in your bones.
