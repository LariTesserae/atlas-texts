# The High Mat

The plateau has no name among the people who live there, because they have never needed to distinguish it from the rest of the world. To them it is simply the ground. But if a traveller were to name it, they might call it the High Mat, or the Old Roof, or the Place That Breathes.

The first thing you notice, long before you see it, is the sound. It comes down through the passes like weather—a continuous, many-layered noise that has no single source. It is not the sea, though it moves like the sea. It is not wind, though the wind lives inside it. By the time you reach the rim and look out over the plateau, you understand: this is the sound of too many living things concentrated into too few places, carried across too much open air.

The plateau sits at the top of a dead mountain range—peaks that were pushed up, folded, and then worn down over hundreds of millions of years until only a high, nearly flat table remains. From the rim, the ground drops away into a sea of cloud. Above that sea, the sky is a hard, dark indigo, and the sun seems closer than it should. Yet the air is warm. The plateau stores its heat in its own body and releases it slowly through the day, and the cloud below acts like a blanket, trapping warmth against the high ground.

The ground is not soil.

It is a continuous mat of plant tissue—a single clonal organism or a fused community of them—pressed flat by ancient wind and frost until it resembles dark leather over rock. It covers the high plain like a skin. In most places it is only a few hand-spans thick, but it is alive, warm to the touch, and it moves. The surface is smooth and ridged with growth lines that run for kilometers, like contour lines made of wood. Where it cracks, the cracks open into shallow root galleries and old leaf scars. The whole landscape feels less like terrain and more like the palm of an enormous hand turned up to the sky.

Moisture arrives sideways. There are no rivers, no streams, almost no rain. Instead, each dawn the cloud sea below climbs over the rim and drags its belly across the mat. The air condenses on the plant surfaces in fine beads, which run into shallow cups formed by broken root crowns and folded leaf bases. These cups are the only standing water for kilometers. They fill with a few centimeters of brownish liquid, fringed with tiny bladderworts, and by midday they are drunk dry by animals and sun. The rest of the ground remains merely damp, carrying a smell like wet leather and crushed green leaves.

From the mat, at long intervals, rise the towers.

They are the same kind of organism, or its close descendants: hollow flowering trunks, ancient and immense, fifty to eighty meters tall, fluted by wind, their bases swollen into buttresses that spread across the living ground like the roots of a different world. They are not a forest. They are too far apart for that. A person can walk between two towers for an hour or more and see nothing but flat green-brown ground, the far heat-shimmer, and the stooped shapes of the next towers on the horizon. The plateau is empty in volume—almost all of it is air—but what it contains is pressed into these vertical islands.

Each tower is crowded to the point of crawling. Its fluted trunk is hung with nests and web-like mats, and its flanks carry a fur of gray-green moss and small epiphytes, so that every surface in the landscape is either plant or animal. The hollow core acts as a resonator, amplifying everything that lives inside. At midday the noise is physical. A metallic drone from legions of glass-winged cicadas fills the air. Piping calls and clattering wings come from the upper ledges. A low, carrying boom—like a mallet struck inside a wooden barrel—repeats every few seconds from the hollow heart of the tower. The wind adds another voice: the fluting is so regular that the gale plays the tower like a bone flute, a note that trembles through the ground. Standing close, you cannot hear your own voice. People communicate by touching hands or waiting for the wind to turn.

Between towers, the sound settles but never stops. It travels along the mat, mixed with a faint creaking that comes from the ground itself. The mat lifts a finger-width before dawn, as the plant draws water from its deep roots, and settles again at dusk. It is a slow, continent-sized breath. The people mark the day by it: the ground rises, the fog comes, the animals drink, the fog burns off, the wind picks up, the pollen flies, the bats pour out, the ground settles, the sky screams.

The fauna is not evenly spread, but it is overwhelming in total. The towers are vertical rookeries, and the mat is a highway between them. At dawn and dusk, streams of small root-hares—no bigger than a hand—run from one tower to the next, their bodies a ripple across the living surface. The raptors are waiting. They hang in the empty air above the mat, watching for the ripple, and when they fall the noise redoubles: alarm piping, wing-cracks, the shrieks of the hunters. Bats pour from the hollow trunks at evening in ribbons so thick that they darken the sky near the tower for several minutes. The insects follow. The tower hums with feeding, and the empty dark between towers fills with the sound of hunger and flight.

The place is ancient in a way that is visible from the ground. Every edge has been rounded, every face polished or cracked into its final form. The rim cliffs are not sharp; they are shoulders. The rock beneath the mat, where it shows, is banded with the memory of old mountains—folded and twisted, then cut down to a stub. Even the towers are shaped by time: their windward sides are smooth as polished bone, their leeward sides flaking in long fibrous strips. The ground has been planed so level that if rain ever fell, it would sheet off in a single film.

The people here are few and their marks are faint. There are no villages, only scattered single-family shelters: a drystone arc built against the root flare of a tower, a woven platform hung where the trunk forks, a doorway carved into the living wood and sealed with a slab of thick bark. The tools are simple—obsidian knives, bronze needles traded from the lowlands once a season, rope braided from tower-bark fiber, baskets woven from dried leaf blades. Fire is kept small and contained on flat stones, because the ground itself is flammable; a stray spark could smolder for weeks in the resinous inner layers of the mat. The people walk in single lines, and the path is marked by pale stones set into the living surface. They do not cut the ground without leaving an offering of water in the wound, and the wound heals slowly, over years, like a scar on skin.

There is one rule of the world that outsiders must learn quickly: the emptiness between towers is not as safe as it appears. It is empty of vegetation above the mat, yes, but it is full of watching things. The mat is too low to hide a human, and the raptors are not always small. Travelers go in pairs, carry long poles with bone hooks, and never cross an open stretch at dawn or dusk. The sound is both cover and warning: when the insect drone drops suddenly, it means something large is moving.

The plateau stretches farther than the eye can follow. From the rim, the towers recede in a line that bends with the curve of the world, their tops catching the light like masts. There are not enough of them to make a forest, but they make a sentence—an alphabet of dark spires spaced across a green-brown page. Each one is a world. Between them, the living ground breathes, and the cloud sea rises and falls below.

The sound never stops. It changes, but it never stops. In the fog days, when the cloud sea climbs over the rim and stays for a week, the noise becomes muffled and directionless, coming from everywhere and nowhere; the towers appear suddenly in the white, dark and wet, their flutes dripping. In the clear afternoons, the drone from a single tower can carry for miles, and the whole plateau seems to vibrate with it. At night, when the insects quiet, the wind takes over the flutes, and the deep boom of the towers answering each other rolls across the mat like slow thunder.

This is a place at the end of its own history. The mountains that made it are gone. The plants that cover it have become the ground. The animals have filled the only vertical structures left. The people have learned to fit themselves into the cracks. Nothing here is young except the sound, which is remade every second by a million small bodies and one enormous, patient wind.
