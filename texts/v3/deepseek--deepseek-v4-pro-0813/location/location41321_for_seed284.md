High on the shoulder of a young range there is a country without water. The rock is fresh, black glass and pale mineral crust, broken into blades. The air is thin and cool, like a stone cellar. There is no soil, no plant life; only bright salts and dark basalt. The sky at noon is a deep blue almost to black. The mountains have not yet been worn down: every ridge is hackly, every notch a clean fracture. Erosion has only just begun.

The city does not announce itself. You see a narrow cleft, hardly wide enough for a mule and cart, wind-bitten and dry. Follow it and it winds into the mountain until the walls fall away and you are inside the Throat.

The Throat is a vertical pocket: a collapse pit or gas vent maybe three hundred paces across and five hundred deep. It is tiny compared with the mountains around it, but it contains a city. The city is not on the floor. It is the walls. Terraces and galleries rise in stacked rings; from the rim they look like the strata of some industrial geology. There are maybe fifty levels, some no wider than a doorway, some broad enough for a rail tram. They are connected by caged ladders, spiral stairs cut into the glassy rock, cable elevators, rope bridges, and balconies that nearly touch across the shaft.

The material is cast iron, riveted steel, black brick, ceramic block, mica window, copper pipe, and something like pressed mineral board. The construction is dense, almost impossibly dense. The walls are so crowded with buildings, ducts, busbars, cableways, hoppers, chutes, and cisterns that in places the original rock cannot be seen. Residences lean over the drop. Markets occupy every wide ledge. Bridges hang in layers, some only a body-width apart. It has the density of a lowland slum folded into a well.

The city is industrial to its bones. On the upper tiers, foundries and electric arc furnaces glow behind smoked glass. There are stamp mills and crushers on the lower levels, chemical batteries and electrolytic cells in the middle, and a ring of wind-scoops on the rim that feeds a blustering array of turbines. Heat comes from the vent itself: deep geothermal gas and hot rock drawn up through pipes into radiators and smelters. Yet the place does not sound industrial. The air is too thin to carry a shout far. Machines are wrapped in hush-cloth and lead-wool; the city enforces silence by law because loud noise can trigger rockfalls. So it hisses, ticks, thrums, and vibrates, but rarely clangs. The loudest sound might be the faint pour of sand-sized crystals down the pit floor, or the whisper of wings.

Light is dim. The sun reaches the bottom only for a short time, if at all; most of the day the pit lies in blue-gray shadow. Arc lamps make pools of electric white, furnace doors throw bands of orange, and red and amber sign-lamps flicker over the market terraces. The lower floor is a salt pan, flat and white and almost glowing in its own pallor, crusted with mineral efflorescence. The dark is not empty: it crawls and shimmers.

There is no vegetation. Nothing green. The base of life is microbial: sulfur-oxidizing films that make yellow and white crusts on rock, iron-reducing stains of rust and black, and a pale blue-gray fuzz that is not plant, not fungus exactly, but something older and simpler. It feeds on gases rising from the vent, on metal dust from the mills, on acid vapors. Fauna grazes on it, and each other. Water is absent from the whole system. The organisms run on non-aqueous solvents, sulfur compounds, iron, salts, and esters. The city taps the same chemistry. Water, when it appears, is a curiosity kept in sealed glass.

The fauna is abundant—startlingly so. Glass-flies with transparent wings drift in sheets between the cables, drawn to the electric fields around the busbars. They settle on insulators in shimmering mats; the city uses static brushes and trap screens to keep them from shorting the circuits. Pale swifts nest in the warm flues and hunt the flies in near-silent stoops, their wings making a sound no louder than a hand turning a page. On the salt pan below, salt-lopers step about on needle legs, small and opalescent, grazing the mineral crust like tiny walking sculptures. There are blind rust-red smelt-mites in the slag heaps; they bore through clinker and excrete beads of reduced copper and iron, and the workers sift their tunnels for metal. Around the warm gas vents, dense colonies of pale, eyeless crickets wave their antennae in the heat shimmer; their singing is too high for most ears, leaving only a faint prickling in the teeth. The walls themselves seem alive, not with plants but with motion.

The atmosphere is cool, dry, and chemical. It smells of basalt dust, hot iron, ozone, sulfur, burnt pitch, and old batteries. It leaves a mineral taste on the tongue. The coolness is not deep winter; it is the cool of shaded stone and thin air, enough to make breath smoke at dawn but not enough to freeze standing oil. When wind crosses the rim, it curls into the pit and drives dust into slow spirals, and the whole city adjusts.

The place is turbulent, not because of weather but because of change. The vent is young. It does not stay fixed. Gas mouths open and close; micro-tremors loosen slabs; rockfalls happen from the rim, and sometimes a terrace shifts by an inch and never goes back. The city has learned to be temporary in place but permanent in form. Houses are mounted on screw jacks and greased rails. Bridges retract. Cableways are rerouted every morning. Markets move to new ledges because yesterday’s market is now unsafe or gone. The city is in constant, quiet rearrangement: hands moving, ropes paying out, light-signals flashing, carts rolling on felted wheels. It has the energy of a bazaar, but not the noise. No one shouts. Orders are given by hand-sign, lamp-flash, wooden clackers, and soft-soled messengers. The quiet makes the turbulence strange, as if you are watching a storm through thick glass.

The city is a pocket-scale world. It may hold several thousand people and millions of small animals, but its footprint would fit inside one cathedral plaza of a lowland city. The enclosure is total. Looking up, you see the sky as a small, pale coin above a cylinder of ledges, cables, signs, and smoke. The city has no streets, only levels and ways. It measures distance in height. Wealth is vertical: upper terraces get more light and cleaner air, lower terraces are older, hotter, closer to the gas and the salt floor. People live in iron cages covered with waxed cloth, in brick rooms with mica panes, in workshops suspended on steel hawsers. The rock is never far away; the wall is also foundation and ceiling and neighbor.

There is a machine at the center of the shaft called the Salt Clock. It is a bronze dish, wide and shallow, fed by a single crystal of salt dropped from a glass tube at every minute. The crystal falls with a small tink. Over time, the dish fills. When it does, the city holds a quiet festival; the dish is carried to the bottom and emptied onto the pan, and the salt becomes part of the floor. This is the closest thing the city has to a calendar, and to water, for the crystals look like ice and melt in no liquid anyone can drink.

The rules of the world are simple: do not be loud, do not waste heat, do not treat rock as solid, do not waste metal, and do not hoard water, because water is not a currency but a legend. The city is a young place in a young land, too high for rain, too dry for plants, too sharp to be comfortable. It survives by chemistry and arrangement, by being small and folded, by moving when the mountain moves. It is dense, dim, cool, alive with small creatures, and almost silent; but it is never still.

This is the pocket city in the Throat, a hidden industrial hive in a waterless high country, where the streets hang and the horizon is a well.
