At first it is mostly sound: a steady, close, dripping roar, like an enormous greenhouse after rain, only deeper, with hollow notes beneath it. The air is warm, almost body-warm, and so wet that breath and weather are hard to tell apart. Water stands in sheets and films, runs down every surface, beads on the undersides of leaves. It does not feel like ordinary rain, though a fine mist moves through the higher air. It feels like the land is sweating.

Underfoot there is no true ground. The first step lands on a thick, springy mat of roots, flattened stems, and half-digested leaves, firm as cork but warmer. In the low places the mat gives way to open water only a few centimeters deep, so the world is a mosaic of wet green flats and shining pools. The water is clear but tinted like weak tea where it sits over brown leaf-fiber. Mineral soil and stone do not appear anywhere. The entire surface is plant—alive, recently alive, or held in the strange state between.

Then the scale begins to open. The plateau is high and broad. Clouds drag across its shoulders. The wet flats extend for what feels like miles between large, isolated forms. At first these forms seem like trees, but as you approach they refuse to be trees. They are rooms. Towers. Terraces. A long, low hall of interwoven branches; a spiral stair of leaf-bases; a row of columns formed from woody roots; a dome with translucent leaf-skin panels. Nothing has been cut, jointed, nailed, woven, or carved. The architecture is grown. The plants have made walls and ceilings the way a coral makes reef, but with a regularity that looks deliberate.

The density is strange. The structures stand far apart, separated by open ground covered only by a low skin of liverwort, algae, and tiny succulent leaves. There is no thicket, no understory, no crowded competition. Each building stands alone in its own reflection. This emptiness makes the place feel larger than it is, and also more designed: the structures are presences, not clutter. Between them, the eye travels a long way over bright water and green film.

Water moves through everything. It rises through the plant bodies from some deep source below the plateau, then releases from pores in the bark, from the tips of leaves, from cracks in the woody domes. It runs in channels that are themselves curled leaves. It falls from awnings in strings of drops. It drips into cupped bracts and hollow stems, each with a different pitch. The sound is the dominant fact of the place. It is loud but not disordered. The drips make a many-voiced plinking. Wind moves through the hollow structures and produces low organ tones. Large lignified plates creak without moving. The sound is like the inside of a giant shell: continuous, resonant, wet, and green.

The light is ordinary in strength, but the sky is a high bright overcast, so there are no sharp shadows. The open flats glow silver-green. Inside the structures, the light is underwater and dim, filtered through leaf-skins that have the exact translucency of old glass. The colors are mostly green, brown, amber, and water-silver, with occasional eruptions of violet, red, or yellow when a flowering structure opens. These colors are sparse and seem significant. There is no season here, only the one warm, cloud-diffused afternoon that never ends.

The ecology is a closed loop. The plants are the substrate, the architecture, the climate, the water system, and the soil. There is no outside input. Old tissue does not rot into dirt; it is reabsorbed into the living mat. New tissue is laid down continuously, but at exactly the same rate old tissue is recycled, so the landform never changes. It is an erosion landscape in reverse: not wearing away but being deposited, yet arrested. Everywhere there is the look of emergence, of construction half begun. Arches rise and stop. Ribs extend into empty water and do not close. Staircases turn a half-turn and end in a blank wall of living bark. These unfinished forms are not decaying or growing. They are stable, held at the exact point where becoming stopped.

The rules of the world seem to be these. Nothing here is mineral; all substance is plant. Water does not leave; it loops through bodies and air and pools. Growth and decay are one continuous process, so the architecture is both being built and being replaced at all times, yet its form is frozen. Emptiness is part of the design: the space between structures is as important as the structures themselves. Few animals are allowed; the system has almost no need of them. And nothing is finished, yet nothing changes.

There is almost no fauna. In the shallows, a few transparent, snail-like grazers move over the algal film, leaving bright trails that seal behind them. In the hollow interiors, a pale, many-legged insect-like thing may appear at a port-hole and then retreat. There are no flocks, no fish, no croaking, no swarms. The rare animals feel less like inhabitants than like stray cells of the landscape, temporarily detached and then reabsorbed.

The unsettling part is the sense of intention. The rooms, the stairs, the long halls, the thresholds—they repeat, they vary, they have a grammar. There are niches at hand height. There are low platforms that could be beds. There are open places that could be gathering halls. But there is no sign of a maker, no tool, no depiction, no fire, no word. The architecture is alive and empty. It is not waiting in the way a ruin waits, because a ruin declines; this place is waiting while completely maintained, like a house whose occupants have not yet been imagined.
