The first thing is not the light, though the light is enormous, and not the cold, though the cold is steady. The first thing is the sound. It comes up through the ground before you reach the rim, a low, grinding hum that makes the bones of the hand buzz against a walking stick. By the time the plateau opens out, the sound is no longer heard; it is worn. It has parts: a deep millstone drone, a rhythmic thud like a slow piston, a high sibilant tearing from many directions at once, and a dry electrical crackle that never quite stops. You could shout your own name and lose it within arm’s length.

The plateau is high and continent-wide, a roof of the world that runs flat to the horizon in every direction. The air is thin and bright, cold without being arctic, and so dry it pulls moisture from the eyes and tongue. There is no standing water anywhere—no streams, no ponds, no dew—though the shallow depressions glitter with salt and sulfur crusts that imitate water from a distance. The only liquid is locked inside the living ground as a cold, pressurized sap of liquid carbon dioxide and dissolved salts, and when a stem is broken it does not bleed; it hisses out a dry, stinging vapor.

The ground is not soil. It is a single continuous organism, or a colony so fused that the distinction has no meaning: a thick, leathery mat of dark teal, rust, and bone-grey that covers everything like a hide. It grows in lobes and ridges, old and polished by wind, perforated by breathing pores the size of cook-pots that open and close in slow waves. Walking on it is like walking on a breathing animal. It gives slightly, and in places it is warm. The surface is not crowded; it is the opposite. Between one tall structure and the next there may be two or five kilometers of open, low mat, so the eye travels a long way across trembling emptiness before finding the next vertical thing.

Those vertical things are the forges. They are not built, but they are impossible to describe without the language of machinery. Each one is a hollow chimney of laminated, mineralized plant tissue, thirty to a hundred meters tall, grown over centuries from a swelling in the mat. They stand alone, each in its own ring of disturbance. Their walls are smooth and fluted, polished by grit and age. From the upper third of each chimney jut horizontal shafts of silica-hardened growth, and from the shafts hang turbines of long, serrated blades—obsidian leaves that rotate constantly in the wind. Some turn heavily, like millstones, with a sound you feel in your teeth. Some spin in a blur and keen like a saw against stone.

Inside each forge, the plant is grinding. At the base, root-like conduits pull up mineral crystals and salt crust into a gizzard lined with black chitinous teeth. The crushed material is carried upward in thick vessels of liquid carbon dioxide under pressure, a dry slurry that glows faintly through translucent tissue. Higher up, in bright internal chambers, the slurry meets the thin air. Spinning blades build static charge; the charge is stored in layered sacs of wax and conductive sap, then released in small arcs that jump between internal fibers. Chemical reactions flash along the walls like heat lightning. What leaves the top is a shimmering exhaust of waste gas and fine dark powder that falls downwind like snow made of iron and silicon.

The entire plateau is a factory without hands. Nothing is constructed: no wall, no frame, no weld, no artifact. The pistons are peristaltic chambers that contract in sequence. The bearings are cups of mirror-smooth cartilage. The flywheels are seed-pods grown to be flung away at great speed. It is not a landscape with industry in it; it is an industrial landscape that happens to be alive.

The animals are not incidental. They are abundant, but they do not spread evenly. Around each forge the air is full of fliers—long-winged, prismatic creatures whose wing-scales catch the glare and flash blue-white. They ride the hot exhaust and feed on the electric field itself, snapping up stunned insects with a dry click. They call in light, not sound, because sound is useless here. Between the forges, the mat crawls with millions of tiny grazers, glossy ticker-mites that move in waves, as if the ground is conducting an electric pulse. Larger creatures cross the open spaces slowly: shaggy hexapods with pads of sound-absorbing fiber on their flanks, cropping the mat’s upper lobes and leaving trails that heal within hours. They are silent. You feel them coming through your feet before you see them.

For all its noise, the place is old. It has been eroding and rebuilding for long enough that every forge is a ruin and every ruin is a nursery. Fallen chimneys lie half-digested at the base of living ones. The wind has polished the salt pans to a hard crust, and the mat has worn paths into itself. There is an equilibrium here, but not a calm one.

The atmosphere is a storm-state that rarely lets go. Dust devils rise every few minutes, some hundreds of meters tall. Gusts overspeed the rotors until one blade lets go and sails half a kilometer, tumbling like a thrown knife. When it stabs into the mat, it does not die; it roots. The blades split into leaves, the shaft softens into a stem, and a new forge begins. Sometimes a whole tower cracks under its own torque and comes down in a cascade of splintering bone-wood and electric fire. The crash is not a catastrophe. It is reproduction. Within days the mat swells over the wreckage, and three new chimneys rise from the heap.

At night the light drains to a cold grey, and the temperature drops far below freezing, but the forges keep turning. Their vents glow from internal heat. Sparks jump from blade-tips like slow lightning. The ground shudders in long, slow sequences, not random—almost like a countdown. The air smells of ozone, hot silica, and a cold, sweet chemical odor that might be sap or might be the smell of the planet’s blood. There is no water anywhere, and no quiet, and no human-made thing, only the roar of a continental engine running in its own deafening, dry, living way.
