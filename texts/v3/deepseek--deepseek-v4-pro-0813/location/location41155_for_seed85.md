The place is a drowned town in a high crater, though “drowned” is the wrong word. It was never dry. The crater sits far above the tree line, in a range so young its peaks still have edges like fractured glass. Its bowl is filled with water too salty to freeze and too still to disturb the world above. If you stand on the rim, the lake looks like a black mirror—not water, but polished obsidian. The town below is invisible.

The water is a heavy brine, six or seven times saltier than the sea. It is cold, one or two degrees above the freezing point of freshwater, and it is absolutely calm. No wind reaches the surface. No stream feeds the lake. The crater is closed, and the water has been the same since before the mountains finished rising. Below the first few meters, light fails quickly. The dark is not empty; it is thick and mineral, and the only illumination comes from things the town itself contrives.

Down the caldera walls, columns of basalt descend into the water like the ribs of a broken organ. They are sharp, with the smooth, faintly greasy shine of newly fractured rock. Nothing has worn them. There is no rounded gravel, no mud, no sediment. The lake floor is shattered glass and angular slabs. The water is so saturated with salts that it cannot dissolve the stone, and so motionless that it cannot carry dust. Erosion has not started here. The crater is still in its first morning, and the town is part of that morning.

The town is sparse. A dozen or so main structures hang at various depths, tethered by cables not much thicker than a finger. Another thirty or forty smaller objects—cells, tanks, buoys, housings—are strung between them, each separated by huge volumes of black water. It is not a city in the crowded sense; it is a settlement of distances. The water between one building and the next is the real architecture. If you could swim across the widest part, it would take twenty minutes, but the place is mostly vertical. The central structure rises from the deepest part of the lake, three hundred meters, its top just below the surface. It is a cathedral of dark stone and silver seams, built for the water, not against it.

Its technology is beyond the local physics. The buildings do not rest on foundations; they are held in place by buoyancy cells and pinning fields, by machines that make the water itself carry weight. Anchored cables run straight down into the dark, and along them are spaced small spheres—arrestors—each emitting a faint red pulse. The arrestors cancel any current before it can form. They hold the water at a single temperature, so there are no thermal layers, no convection, no slow circulation. The city enforces its own stillness. There are heat engines that take the tiny, constant warmth of the volcano below and spend it on keeping everything gradient-free. It should be impossible. The town does not care.

There is no plant life anywhere. No kelp, no algal cloud, no green film on the rocks. The water is too cold, too saline, too starved, or perhaps simply too final. The only organic-looking forms are mineral dendrites—black fronds of manganese and iron branching across the basalt. They look like ferns, but they are chemistry with a shape, not living. Nothing grows. The place is a mineral world suspended in water.

Fauna is rare. A single blind eel-like animal, pale as a wet thread of glass, returns every few hundred days to circle the cathedral and vanish into the deeper dark. A ribbon-shaped worm, long as a person but no thicker than a thumb, coils around one of the cables and stays there for months, absorbing iron from the brine. The city’s sensors classify it as an event, not an inhabitant. There are no predators, no prey, no food chain. The animals live off the rock and the salt, and they do so slowly.

Light is scarce. Above the water, the sky is often black, sometimes a dirty gray; the rim is so high and dark that even at noon the sun feels like a rumor. Below the surface, sunlight loses its way within a dozen meters. The town provides its own lights—not floods but signals: a green filament along a tether, a blue-white lamp in a deep window, the red pulse of an arrestor. They do not illuminate the water so much as punctuate it. The distance between lights is immense, and the dark between them is total.

The sound is the sound of a held breath. Underwater, sound travels far, but there is almost none to travel. A low hum, too deep to hear, comes from the cathedral; you feel it in your jaw and along the bones of your face. Every hour or so, a cable clicks as it adjusts by a fraction of a hair, and the click rolls around the crater walls and fades. No rain, no waves, no voices, no engines. The loudest thing in the world, if you were inside it, would be your own pulse.

And nothing changes. That is the true strangeness. In this place there is no weather, no tide, no season, no current, no growth, no decay. The water temperature is the same at the surface and at the floor. The chemistry is saturated. The rock is unweathered. The city’s machines have not been repaired because they have not needed repair. They do not run so much as persist. If a stone falls from the rim, it sinks at one steady rate, lands on a slab of glass, and stays there until the end of the world. The town is not old; it is new and already complete. Time does not pass here. It sits on the bottom with the stones.

There are rooms no one enters. The cathedral’s only door is a hole in its spire, open to the water, and a ladder goes down through concentric chambers, each one colder and darker than the last. Some buildings are hollow bells that would ring if the water moved—but it does not. A copper sphere the size of a house hangs at mid-depth, its only feature a narrow slit that seems to appear when you look away. The blind eel circles it and then turns, as if it heard something. No one knows what the slit is for. Perhaps the town is a lock, and the slit is a keyhole, and the key has not arrived yet.

Above the surface, if you climbed out, you would find only black rock and cold, thin air. The lake would be flat and dark, too smooth to believe. From the rim, the town below is not visible at all. You could stand there for hours and hear nothing but the blood in your ears. The place is small, dark, cold, drowned, and perfectly preserved. It is not waiting. Waiting would be a kind of change.
