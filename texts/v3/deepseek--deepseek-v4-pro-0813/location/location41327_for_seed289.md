Below a thin skin of cold ground, in a place you would pass without noticing, there is a hollow not much larger than a room. It lies beneath the surface, but only just—a pocket of void caught between plates of pale limestone and dark, unfinished rock. You enter by a seam in the earth, not a mouth; the descent is shallow, a crawl between two slabs that close behind you with the silence of stone. Inside, it is bright.

The light is the first wrong thing. It is bright enough to read by, a cold blue-white glow like noon under overcast, but it comes from no opening. The walls themselves seem lit from behind, and every surface glows with a silver, even luminescence. The rock is not bare. It is upholstered in life.

The walls, the ceiling, the floor—all are covered by a dense, continuous layer of small pale creatures. Each is no longer than a fingernail, many-legged and wingless, something between a cricket and a silverfish, with a translucent body through which the light moves like liquid. There are so many of them that no stone is visible. They stand packed antenna-to-antenna, abdomen against thorax, climbing over one another without ever breaking the surface of the colony. From a distance the room does not appear to be inhabited by animals. It appears to be lined with a living, breathing fur.

The air is cool, the temperature of a root cellar in early spring. It is damp without being wet. No stream runs here, and no pool is larger than a cupped hand. Moisture arrives as condensation, a fine silver sweat that beads on every antenna, every stone nub, every scattered leaf. The beads do not drip. The air is exactly saturated; the water forms and stays, hanging in perfect tension, then slowly re-enters the rock as vapor. The floor is uneven, ridged with small terraces of new calcite, each no larger than a saucer, each holding a shallow lens of still water that reflects the glow.

The cave is raw, unfinished. There are no tall stalactites, no broad flowstone curtains. Instead there are incipient forms: pinhead knobs of calcium, draperies thin as wet paper, helictites curling like pale hairs from the walls. The floor has the look of clay pressed once by a thumb and then abandoned. Erosion and accretion are balanced so exactly that the cave seems to be in the middle of becoming, but never advancing. It is a sketch of a cavern, a place caught between stone and void.

The sound is not water. It is a single, loud, metallic trill, as if a million tiny saws are drawn across glass. It comes from the living layer. The creatures stridulate without stopping, their legs scraping against notched plates on their sides. The sound is so continuous that it has no rhythm, no rise or fall. It is one chord held forever. It is loud enough to press against the eardrums, but after a while it becomes a kind of silence. There is no echo. The packed bodies absorb sound the way moss absorbs rain; a shout dies at the wall, swallowed by the close-pressed life.

There are plants, but only a few. In the fissures where condensation is highest, sparse rosettes of pale cave moss grow. Their leaves are reduced to translucent scales, and they glow faintly, not from their own light but from the light they trap and turn back. They are spaced far apart, each one alone, a small grey-green star on the pale rock. They do not spread. They do not flower. They are just enough vegetation to keep the word “plant” present in this place.

At the back of the hollow, something has been built. A low ring of water-worn stones, each about the size of a fist, rests on the floor. The stones are not from this rock; they are smooth, foreign, fitted together without mortar. Inside the ring, the glowing creatures do not go, leaving a circle of bare, wet stone. Next to it, a series of shallow lines has been cut into the wall—not writing, but tally marks, so faint they might be the scratches of a stick in old mud. A few splinters of bone are embedded in the calcite nearby, arranged in a fan. These are the only signs that something else once had intention here.

There is no metal, no wood, no tool-marked stone. Whatever made those marks used only finger, bone, or another stone. The built things are scattered, partial, like a sentence left unfinished.

The most strange thing is the stillness. The place is full of life, but nothing happens. The temperature is exactly the same at the floor and the ceiling. The air does not move. The light does not flicker. The sound does not change. The animals are so densely packed that there is no room for a new one. If one dies, its body is not removed; it becomes part of the mineral crust, and the others close over it before the space is empty. The population is not a number. It is a pressure.

Where two of the creatures touch, their cuticles seem to fuse, and if one is pulled away it takes a thread of its neighbour with it. The colony is not made of individuals. It is a single tissue that has taken on the shape of the cave. Its light and its sound are the same process, a steady vibration that shakes the air and excites a pale luminous fluid in their bodies. If the sound were to stop, the light would stop too.

The rules of this place are simple and absolute: nothing enters, nothing leaves. Water moves only as vapour, condensing and re-entering the rock. The creatures etch minerals from the stone, and their waste becomes calcite. The calcite becomes substrate for the glowing film. The moss feeds on the mineral seep. The light feeds the moss. It is a closed loop, a perfect, frozen equilibrium.

It is a place that has stopped happening. Not dead—too full for death—but arranged in a single stable configuration, with no gradient to push it forward. If you were to lie down in the center, the creatures would part slowly around you, as if you were only another stone, and then they would flow back over your limbs, covering you with their cool, luminous bodies. You would hear their one chord from inside yourself. You would feel the dampness, the packed weight, the coolness of the stone beneath.

And you might understand that you are the only thing here that changes, the only disturbance in a room that has found its only shape. The hollow does not resist you. It simply waits, steady and bright and loud and still, until you leave and the living fur closes over the place where you had been.
