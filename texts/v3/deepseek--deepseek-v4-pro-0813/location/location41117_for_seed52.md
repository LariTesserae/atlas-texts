Something in the light takes the edges off the world before the basin even comes into view. The sky is not dark and not bright; it is a high, warm gray-gold, like a sheet of old glass holding the sun behind it. There are no shadows worth the name. The light falls from everywhere and nowhere, so stones and leaves and skin all seem to carry their own faint illumination. The air is warm and full, the way a greenhouse air is full, and it smells of wet clay, of green things sweating, of a mineral tang that might be salt or might be something stranger.

From the rim the basin looks less like a valley and more like a room that has just been unroofed. The walls are raw. They drop in stepped terraces and vertical faces of naked stone, sharp as if cut yesterday. Streaks of color run down them—ochre, rust, a pale turquoise that glows even in the dimness. These are not old hills worn soft by weather. They are the fresh edges of a world that opened, or drained, or was set down here before it had time to be weathered. You can read the water's history on them: horizontal benches, scarred lines, a high tide mark of a lake that no longer fills the basin to anywhere near that height.

The floor of the basin is mostly flat, but it is not smooth. It is a floor still learning to be land. Gray silt and pale mud lie in beds that crack into neat polygons, the cracks so sharp they look deliberate. Sand holds the exact imprint of moving water, frozen in wave-like ripples. Pebbles litter the surface with edges that have never been rounded. Here and there the ground has slumped or split, and dark water wells up through the fissures and spreads in silvery sheets that reflect the hazy sky like polished metal.

This is a place where water is everywhere but not in abundance. The ground is damp rather than wet. Springs seep from the base of the scarps and thread across the flats in channels that are barely a hand's width, their beds lined with dark, slick mud. Pools gather in the lowest hollows, their surfaces still, their edges crusted with a fine white salt. A larger body of water lies at the center of the basin—a lake, or what remains of one—shallow and milky, glowing very faintly as if the water contains a slow, diluted light. In the cool hours, a fog rises from it and rolls outward across the flats, knee-high at first, then taller, until the whole basin is half dissolved in white.

The vegetation takes that fog and uses it. The green places are not continuous; they are islands. In every depression, every channel, every slope where the seepage gathers, life has crowded in. Thickets of broad-leaved plants grow so densely you could not push through them: ferns that uncurl in the damp, reeds tall as a person, low trees with glossy leaves that cup and catch the mist. Moss spreads in thick, velvety carpets, deep green and slick with condensation. The plants are lush in a way that surprises, because the basin is not wet the way a swamp is wet. They have the look of things built for this precise environment: leaves shaped to hold water, surfaces that bead and channel it, roots that spread shallow over the unweathered sediment or deepen into the cracks where the groundwater hides. Some of the green is not simply green. Under the leaves, in the thickest places, there is a faint bioluminescent pulse, a color like the inside of a lime, slow and rhythmic.

Between these green islands, the land is bare. Wide flats of gray and ochre stretch between the thickets, empty but for scattered clumps of a low, tough plant that seems to survive on almost nothing. The basin is open and sparse in this sense, even while the pockets of life are dense. The lushness is not a blanket; it is a constellation. The eye travels over long stretches of empty ground before finding another cluster of green.

The city is built into that same pattern. It sprawls across the basin floor and the lowest terraces, but it does not fill them. The buildings stand in loose groups, separated by wild flats and green islands, so that walking from one cluster to the next means leaving the city behind for a while and crossing ground that belongs to the basin. Paths connect the clusters—strips of a firm, slightly yielding material that follows the contours, lifts on low bridges over seepage channels, and glows very softly at their edges. The light is not much; just enough to show a direction.

The buildings themselves are quiet and strange. They do not look built so much as grown, or perhaps assembled by a process that has no need for seams or joints. Some are slender and tall, rising in smooth curves, their surfaces ridged like bark or shell, folding and unfolding in slow patterns. Others are low and dome-like, their walls dark and faintly translucent, so the soft light within shows only as a blush. Many of the structures do not touch the ground at all. They hover a hand's width above the sediment, or they stand on legs that adjust themselves in tiny increments as the earth settles. The ground here is young; it shifts. The city expects this. The buildings compensate without noise or fuss.

What technology exists here is everywhere and almost invisible. Conduits run along the paths like slow veins. Mist collectors rise from the rooftops and from the edges of the green thickets—fine arrays of mesh and filament that catch the fog and wick it down into storage basins. The water is moved, filtered, returned. The air is warmed or cooled by surfaces that seem to absorb and release heat. Sometimes, when the fog is thick, the whole city seems to breathe: surfaces flexing, lights deepening, the paths becoming slightly darker as they drink the moisture. There is no sound of machinery. Only a hum so low it is more felt than heard, a hum that has been there all day, so constant it becomes part of the silence.

About the inhabitants: there are few. The city is too large for its population, or the population is too small for its city. People move along the paths in ones and twos, unhurried, wearing loose clothes and wide-brimmed hats or hoods that keep the mist from their faces. They carry small lights or none at all; they know the paths. They tend the green islands, open panels on the buildings, walk out to the lake with their hands in their pockets. They do not often speak. The place imposes a kind of hush. It is not that speech is forbidden; it is simply that it seems to get absorbed by the air, by the damp, by the dimness.

Animals are rare, and their rarity is noticeable. The green thickets should be full of noise—birds, insects, small rustling things—but they are not. There is a lizard the color of pale clay that clings to the broad leaves and barely moves. There are insects, but they move slowly, as if light is a currency they must spend carefully. Once, from somewhere in the reeds at the lake's edge, a bird calls: two notes, soft, then silence for a long time. The scarcity of animals feels less like a failure of the ecosystem and more like an unfinished sentence. The place is still early. The plants have arrived; the rest has not yet followed.

The quiet is deep and layered. Water is the main sound: the faint trickle of the seeps, the drip of condensation from leaf to leaf, the low murmur of the lake when the breeze touches it. The wind itself is small—more a movement of damp air than a true wind—and it makes the reeds hiss very softly. The settling ground creaks sometimes, a low, almost animal groan, and the hovering buildings adjust with a sound like breath. The city's hum fills the spaces between, but it is so low, so steady, that it feels like the silence itself.

There are rules here, and they are learned without being taught. The ground must be watched, because it is still forming and will take what you build if you do not build with it. Water must be gathered rather than expected, because the rain comes rarely and only at the whim of the fog. Light must be used sparingly, because there is never enough of it to waste. The city lives by these rules. The plants live by them. Even the animals, few as they are, seem to obey them, moving slowly, speaking rarely, keeping to the edges.

Strange details accumulate as you stay. The buildings, if you watch them long enough, change shape; not quickly, but the way a sleeping thing shifts. A wall that was smooth in the morning has a new ridge by evening. The doors are not doors exactly, but openings that appear and disappear, like mouths. The plants in the green islands are connected by fine threads that run along the ground, pale as nerves, pulsing with a faint light that is not unlike the city's own. The lake, from across the basin, looks as though it is breathing, rising and falling by a few inches on a schedule that has nothing to do with the moon.

The light never changes. Night is only a deepening of the gray-gold, a soft darkening that might last a few hours, and then the haze brightens again in a dawn that is barely dawn. There are no stars. There is no sun visible, only that high, warm canopy, like the underside of something. The inhabitants measure time by the fog—its rising, its thinning, its sudden return in the evenings—and by the growth of the green islands, which expand and contract with the seasons, if seasons are what they are.

The basin, the city, the vegetation—they are all part of the same ongoing event. The ground is still settling. The lake is still retreating. The plants are still colonizing. The city is still being adjusted, tuned, extended. The whole place has the feeling of a room just before someone speaks, or a breath just before it is released. It is a low, warm, dim, damp place, and it is not finished. Nothing here is finished. The strangeness is not that something is wrong, but that everything is still arriving.

At the edge of the lake, near where the last of the green thickets gives way to open mudflats, there is a structure unlike the others: a small platform on slender legs, no walls, only a roof of fine mesh that catches the fog. Under it, a bench of the same yielding material as the paths. Sitting there, you can see the whole basin: the bare flats, the scattered islands of green, the low city with its quiet lights, the raw scarps, the milky lake. The water laps very softly. The air is warm and wet. The light is dim and directionless. The silence is almost complete. And it is possible, for a moment, to believe that this is what the world looked like when it was first imagined—before the animals came, before the ground had settled, before the light had found its way.
