It is called the Underworks, or sometimes Low Brim, and it is not so much a town as a single industrial organ that has grown a town around itself. If you came down through the water from above, you would not see it until you were almost inside it, because the darkness does not thin so much as gather differently around its few lamps. The whole place is small—cathedral-scale, no more than a drowned market square and five or six streets folded over one another—but it is so dense, so layered with pipe and cable and leaning brick, that you could spend a long time inside it and still not touch every wall.

The terrain sits in a low fold of a drowned fjord, where a glacial valley once ran before the sea came in and never fully left. Elevation is almost nothing; this is the lowest habitable shelf before the true trench. The old riverbed lies beneath the streets, buried under silt and iron scrap. On the north side a black rock face rises straight up into the dark. On the south side a row of pressure doors and a half-flooded lock separate the settlement from the open water of the fjord. East and west the old mine tunnels have collapsed, sealed with concrete and cold-cured mortar. Within those boundaries the ground is not flat. It steps and sags: sunken courtyards, flooded cellars, roofs that have fallen and become new floors. The water does not simply fill the streets; it has become the medium of every ruined room, so that you swim through doorways and over staircases rather than walking.

The water is close to freezing. Sea water here stays a little above its freezing point, but only just, and it feels thick in the sinuses, almost chewable. Cold is not an event in the Underworks; it is the default condition. Metal shrinks and ticks. The bricks are filmed with a fine white slush in the stillest corners—not ice exactly, but something the keepers call white water, a crystalline haze that forms on surfaces and dissolves when touched. Breath, in the few dry pockets, would roll out as fog and freeze on the walls.

The built form is old industrial, but it has gone beyond ruin into a long equilibrium. The first walls were likely brick and basalt, the later ones riveted steel and poured concrete. Now erosion has rounded every corner and eaten the mortar back into a kind of lace. The iron has a permanent black skin of oxide that does not flake. The windows are gone, but their frames have fused into the surrounding stone. The water has carved flutes through the masonry, yet what remains is stable, settled, in its own state. The Underworks is not collapsing. It has already collapsed, and the collapse has hardened into a new shape. The place is weathered the way a tooth is weathered: worn down to a form that no longer resembles what it was, but perfectly suited to what it now is.

The density is the most immediate thing once you move inside. All the buildings stand shoulder to shoulder, four and five storeys tall, some leaning so their upper floors touch. The streets are narrow water-alleys, some no wider than a person with outstretched arms. Overhead, cables run in black bundles from eave to eave, looped and sagging like dead vines. Pipes rib every wall—some thick as a torso, some fine as fingers—carrying cold water, warm brine, oxygen, hydrogen, or nothing at all. There are layers of occupation: a flooded printing shop on the ground floor, above it a machine hall still dry and lit, above that a breached roof where cold water pours through a broken skylight. The density is not only architectural. It is sensory. The water itself seems crowded: microbubbles rising from oxygen hoses, rust flakes turning slowly, glacial silt suspended in sheets, a faint glitter of disturbed bacteria along the current.

At the center is the pump-house called the Bellows, a round brick and iron tower built over a drowned geothermal vent. It is the reason the town exists. Its slow, enormous pistons push oxygenated water up over the fjord’s sill and draw cold brine down through the streets. The pistons take about six hours to complete a cycle, and when they reverse, the whole settlement feels a faint surge in every hallway, a pressure change that makes the lamps flicker and the loose cables sway. The old church has been gutted and filled with a rack of hydroelectric Pelton wheels, turning steadily in the constant current that spills down from the rock face. Along the main street runs a series of electrolysis stacks behind a rusted fence, splitting seawater into oxygen and hydrogen. The oxygen is fed through porous hoses that leak chains of tiny bubbles into the alleys; the hydrogen is pumped away to some holding tanks on the south side, where it burns in a pale blue pilot flame inside a wet chamber. Electricity moves through a few live cables only. The dead ones are bare and black, while the live ones carry a faint heat-shimmer in the water, and sometimes a thin crust of iron-loving snails.

The light is sparse and low. No sunlight reaches this depth. What light there is comes from a few yellow-green lamps in the dry rooms, their glow leaking out through bolted portholes; red pilot lights on valve boxes; the pale blue flicker of the hydrogen flame; and the phosphorescent trail of bacteria where the current scrapes against stone. Every few minutes a maintenance drone—a small, blunt thing with a single headlamp—drifts along the main street, sweeping its beam across the walls. The water is black, not blue, like old glass. The lamps do not illuminate; they simply make the darkness more visible.

Sound is almost absent, and what remains is low and close. The loudest thing is the sub-bass hum of the turbines, more vibration than sound, traveling through stone and steel and reaching the inner ear through bone. There is the soft click of cooling metal, the distant thud of a piston cushioning into its cylinder, the hiss of bubbles from the oxygen hoses. When a valve opens, it makes a low wooden groan, like a door moving underwater. A dropped tool makes a dull knock, not a clang. Finned boots on silt sound like a brush on paper. In the deepest alleys, the only sounds are your own pulse and the occasional pop of a tiny shrimp.

Vegetation is sparse. It grows in pale, grudging patches: cold-water kelp, not the tall forests of warmer seas but a low, dark matting that clings to the leeward side of walls; tufts of white frostmoss on the bricks near the warmest pipes; long strands of gutweed that lie flat in the current like hair on a drowned head. There are no true leaves, no photosynthesis in any useful sense. The plants here are chemosynthetic or slow filter-feeders that resemble plants. Around the geothermal vent, a thick gray bacterial mat grows on the pump-house walls, giving the central square its smell—metallic, slightly sour, like old batteries left in water.

Fauna is rare, but it is present in the way a nearly empty room still holds dust. Most of the animals are pale, slow, and blind. A single hagfish may nest in the drain of an old bakery and not be seen for weeks. Translucent amphipods drift in the oxygen bubbles. There is a species of cone-shelled snail that lines its shell with iron oxide and lives on the live power cables. Once in a great while, a deep-water shark, thin as a whip and covered in black scales like charred wood, moves through the main street with its mouth open, filtering the current. The keepers say there is something larger below the old mine shafts, but it does not come up into the lamp-light, and no one has seen it clearly.

The Underworks has its own rules, its own logic. The water is not uniform; it layers into distinct bodies that do not easily mix. A cold, oxygen-rich current flows along the roofs of the old buildings, while a warmer, saltier layer moves along the streets. Where they meet, there is a visible shimmer, like oil on water, and sound bends around it. A call made at one end of the settlement does not reach the other end intact; it turns into a nonsense of echoes, or into nothing. Compass needles here do not point north. They point toward the Bellows, as if the pump-house has become the magnetic heart of the place. The tide is not governed only by the moon; it is overruled by the pistons, which can hold water back for hours and then release it in a slow, deliberate flood. The white water in the still alleys is not ice, but if you bring it into a dry room, it turns into a cold vapor and leaves a salt rime on the walls.

The place is never still, even in its quiet. There is always process: rust turning to hard black patina; cold meltwater mixing with vent brine; bacteria oxidizing sulfur; frostmoss growing a few millimeters a year; the daily reversal of the pumps; the slow settling of silt in doorways; the creaking of walls relieving pressure. The Underworks is not a ruin. It is a dense, cold, dark, small world that has reached an equilibrium with the water, and it continues to work, quietly, under the weight of the fjord.
