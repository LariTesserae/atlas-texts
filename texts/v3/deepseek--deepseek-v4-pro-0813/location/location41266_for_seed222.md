High up, where the air has been scraped thin by altitude and the sun arrives with almost no atmospheric softening, the plateau unfolds. It is not a single landscape but a set of repeating motifs: flat-topped ridges of dark, varnished stone, broad swales of pink-grey silt, and between them a great deal of open, light-filled air. The land has the look of something finished long ago. Not ruinous, not young, but worn down to its essential planes. The cracks in the soil are old, their edges rounded. The desert pavement is fitted edge to edge, each stone glazed by eons of wind and heat and rare rain. There is no upheaval here anymore, only a slow, settled balance.

The warmth is strange at this height. The air thins and cools quickly after dark, but by mid-morning the sun lies on your arms like a held breath, and the ground radiates the heat back in long, wobbling shimmers. There is heat below, too—something deep and old, not volcanic in a violent sense, but steady, keeping the seeps from freezing and making certain stones warm to the touch. The elevation is high enough that distances sharpen and the sky at noon goes almost blue-black at the zenith, yet the heat persists, as if the plateau has its own climate, sealed beneath a lens of bright air.

Most of the water here is invisible. The place is damp only in the ways that matter to small things. At dawn, a river of fog pours up from the escarpment edge and drags itself along the swales, not a dense cloud but a visible thickening of the air. It condenses on every cool surface: the leaves of cushion plants, the polished faces of stones, the fine wires of the old dew fences. It does not rain often—perhaps a few times a year—but every morning the fog beads and drips, and the ground drinks for an hour or two. There are seeps, too, where groundwater warmed by something deep rises along invisible fault lines and wets a patch of gravel no larger than a table. These places are marked by a faint green so pale it is almost grey, and by the tracks of animals.

The vegetation is sparse nearly to the point of absence, but it is not lifeless. Cushion plants grow here and there, flattened hemispheres of grey-green wool, each one centuries old and spaced so far apart they seem like individual events rather than a community. Between them, biological soil crusts cover the ground in patches of rust, black, and olive, so thin they read as stains on the earth. Along the seeps there are sedges no taller than a finger. And in the shade of the old structures grow pale, leafless plants that seem to require no direct sun, only the coolness and the faint trace of something in the walls. The tallest plant is a yucca-like rosette with leaves like sabers, scattered singly where a rock concentrates moisture. Nothing forms a canopy. The vegetation does not fill the space; it punctuates it.

The animals are far more numerous than the plants, though you might not know it at first. The ground is stitched with their signs: scat pellets, burrow mouths, the shed skins of snakes, the tiny highways of ants. At dawn, mirror flies come to the dew fences in thousands. Each is the size of a thumbnail, its wings transparent except for a single silver line. They settle on the filaments and drink the beads directly from the threads, and for a few minutes the air is full of the faintest ticking—not from their wings but from their feet on the wire. Skitter-lizards, slender and long-toed, emerge from cracks and run across the hot stones, stopping with one forefoot lifted, heads cocked. They are preyed upon by pale harriers that ride the thermals in silence, their shadows moving over the pavement like thoughts.

In the swales, dust hares graze on the crusts, their ears so large they seem to be listening to the ground rather than the air. There are snakes with scales the color of iron, and at night the small rodents come out, and with them the foxes—or something like foxes—that walk the old roadlines as if following a scent only they can smell. The fauna is abundant in the way a desert can be abundant: not dense, but continuous, patient, organized around water and shadow and the residual warmth of old things.

The marks of people—or of whatever built here—are scattered so widely that you can walk for an hour without seeing one, then cross a low rise and find a cluster. They are not exactly ruins. Ruins imply decay, and these things have not decayed so much as been left behind. A single wayhouse stands near one of the seeps: a low, rounded structure of pale ceramic, no seams, no windows, only a rectangular doorway. The interior is always cooler than the air. The floor is covered with a fine dust that never blows in, and on one wall faint lines of light wake when someone enters, then fade. There are no furnishings, only a shelf that may have grown from the wall rather than being attached to it.

Elsewhere, an old roadline runs for miles: a single band of grey-white material about a pace wide, laid directly on the ground. It is not stone, not metal, not ceramic—something in between—warm to the touch, and it sheds dust the way a leaf sheds water. In places it has been buckled by the slow movement of the earth and forms low waves, but it never cracks. At intervals, stanchions rise from it—not separate pieces, but extensions of the same material, curving upward like grass blades. They hum at the lower edge of hearing, and in the hour before dawn they bead with condensation, which runs down their inner channels and collects in basins at their bases. The basins are often full, and the water is always clear, and it never grows algae, even in the heat.

Then there are the cairns. These are the most common built things: low mounds of fused regolith, some as small as a knee, some as tall as a person, placed on high points across the plateau. Each one contains a core of something that still holds a trace of warmth. The dust hares use them as landmarks; the harriers nest on the taller ones. If you put your ear close, you hear not exactly a sound but a pressure, as if the air were very slightly thicker there.

The place has other small strangenesses. The light at noon is so bright that shadows seem to be cut out of the world rather than cast. Near the old roadline, compasses drift by a few degrees. Some of the basins hold water that is denser than ordinary water—a pebble drops faster than it should. The mirror flies, when they swarm, align themselves along lines of the old magnetic field, so for a moment the air is patterned like a living graph. These things do not announce themselves. They are simply there, like the heat, like the fog.

Yet for all this life, the place is quiet. The high air does not carry sound well. The wind makes no whistle; the stones are too smooth, the surfaces too worn. The harriers hunt in silence. The only sounds are the faint ticks of insects, the occasional clatter of a loose pebble, the almost inaudible hum of the stanchions, and, at dawn, the soft dripping of water into water. If you stand still long enough, you can hear your own pulse in your ears, and behind it, the very low thrum of the roadline, like a note below the floor of hearing.

The landscape is active, but its activity is spaced out in time. The fog comes every morning, then burns off by mid-morning. The wind rises in the afternoon, moving dust in slow spirals. The animals follow their cycles: mirror flies at dawn, skitter-lizards through the heat of the day, dust hares at dusk, foxes at night. The seeps rise and evaporate. The roadline keeps its tiny vibration, drawing moisture from the air and passing it into the ground. Over centuries, the desert pavement shifts imperceptibly; the cairns sink a little; the plants send out roots; the crusts grow and crack and grow again. It is a place that has been active for a very long time, but never in a hurry.
