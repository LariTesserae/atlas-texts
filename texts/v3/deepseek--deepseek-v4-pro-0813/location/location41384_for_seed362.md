The light arrives without a sun. It is a cold white radiance that seems to come from every part of the sky at once, as if the whole firmament were a single diffuse lamp. There are no shadows in the usual sense; the light is so even that objects appear slightly flattened, their depths readable only by texture and the subtle parallax of movement. The air is dry, and the dryness is not a mere lack of moisture but a positive quality: it has a taste, faintly metallic, faintly sweet, like the inside of a sealed archive where nothing has ever been wet. It is cool — not the cool of winter, but the cool of a vast, steady thermal mass, a temperature that never varies, as though the entire landscape is held in a state of regulated equilibrium. You are standing in a low basin, a shallow continental depression where the cool, dense air pools and settles, and the ground slopes gently away toward a distant center you cannot see.

The ground is not soil. It is a continuous woven surface, a mat of interlacing strands that stretch in every direction. Some strands are the brown of old roots, fibrous and rough; others are the silver of braided wire, cool and hard; still others are translucent, filled with a slow-moving clear liquid that is not water — it has a slight iridescence, like a thin film of oil, and it moves in long, lazy pulses, as if a heart somewhere far below is beating. The strands are layered so densely that the ground is firm underfoot, but not unyielding; it gives slightly, with a springy resilience, like walking on a deep pile of cable and root. In the spaces between the strands, small structures protrude: pale nodes with metallic contacts, clusters of tiny leaves that are also heat sinks, minute vents that open and close with an inaudible rhythm. The ground is alive, and it is built, and there is no distinction to be made between these things. It shows its age: the surface is stratified, layer upon layer of older growth and older manufacture, each generation healing over the last with scar tissue and solder.

From the ground rises the forest. It is a forest the way a refinery is a city: dense, regular, purposeful, but grown rather than assembled. The trunks are columns of bundled tubes — some steel-gray, some the deep brown of bark, some clear as glass — wrapped in spirals of broad, flat plates that are leaf and insulation both. The plates are dark green on one side and silver on the other; they overlap like scales, and each is edged with a fine metallic rim that catches the light. The trunks are not round but polygonal, faceted, as if they have been extruded from a die, yet their surfaces carry the irregular texture of living growth: ridges, lenticels, scars, places where a tube has branched and fused with a neighbor, calluses of hardened tissue around old wounds. They have been here a long time. They rise to a great height, and then they branch, not into boughs but into articulated limbs that move with a slow, hydraulic grace, repositioning themselves over the course of hours. The limbs end in leaves that are not leaves: broad, flat panels, some dark as oxidized copper, some mirrored, some glowing from within with the same cold white light as the sky. The canopy they form is a continuous ceiling, layer upon layer of leaf and panel and luminous surface, so high that it has its own geography — swells and hollows, ridges and basins, a topography of vegetation and light.

The light comes from the canopy, and it is not constant. It pulses in slow waves, brightening and dimming, as if the entire overhead is breathing. The waves sweep across the ceiling like cloud shadows, though there are no clouds. They are not a day-night cycle; they are a rhythm, irregular, biological, like the firing of neurons in a vast distributed brain. In the bright phases, the landscape is washed in a white glare that makes colors turn pale and slightly wrong; in the dim phases, the light is still bright enough to read by, but the shadows deepen, and the glowing leaves stand out like constellations. The temperature does not change with the light; it remains cool, steady, the temperature of a machine that never sleeps.

The air itself is filled with a fine haze — not dust, but a drifting suspension of tiny fibrils and spores and micro-machines, each no larger than a mote, catching the light in brief glittering threads. The haze moves in slow currents, carried by the air that flows through the forest. The air flows in a steady, almost imperceptible respiration: a gentle push and pull, a sigh that passes through the trunks and the canopy and the spaces between. It is this movement that rustles the leaves, that stirs the haze, that carries a faint scent: hot metal, dry leaves, and something else, something sharp and clean, like a solvent that has never been named. On some of the lower leaves, a clear, oily condensate forms in tiny beads — not dew, but the exhalation of the non-aqueous chemistry that runs through the tubes. The droplets refract the light into short-lived rainbows before they slide off and are absorbed by the ground.

The landscape is dense. The trunks are spaced so closely that in most directions you cannot see more than a few hundred meters before the view is closed off by a wall of growth and metal. But the density is not chaotic; it is structured, layered, as if the forest has been designed for maximum surface area and maximum silence. The ground undulates in low swells and shallow basins, a gentle topography that seems to have been shaped by the growth of the structures themselves, as if the ground is a skin stretched over a body of roots and ducts. The scale is vast, not just in the height of the canopy or the depth of the ground, but in the horizonless extent of the whole. You cannot see a horizon; the canopy curves away and up, the ground curves away and down, and the forest simply continues, a single continuous fabric of plant and machine that might cover the entire world. The weather here is not rain or wind but the internal weather of the system: the slow surge of fluid through the ground, the pulse of light from the canopy, the migration of the haze, the faint pressure changes that make your ears pop if you stand still for too long.

In this landscape, there are few animals. The spaces are too dense, the air too dry, the surfaces too unyielding for most life. But sometimes, in the gaps between the trunks, you might catch a movement. A small creature, no larger than a hand, with a segmented body of dark chitin and brass, scuttles along a strand of the ground. Its legs are blunt and precise, each foot ending in a small soft pad that grips the fibers. It stops at a node, extends a thin, translucent proboscis, and inserts it into a socket; there is a brief flicker of light, a soft click, and then it moves on, leaving the node faintly glowing. Higher up, among the branches, a second kind of creature occasionally passes: a slow, armored thing shaped like a seed pod with legs, its back covered in the same overlapping plates as the trunks, so that when it pauses, it becomes indistinguishable from the surrounding growth. These are the rare fauna, the only things that move with their own purpose. They are quiet, and they are deliberate, and they do not seem to notice you, or if they do, they do not care. They are not separate from the landscape; they are its mobile parts, its wandering hands, its grazers and its gardeners.

The reality of the place is not stable. It is not an illusion, but a genuine breakdown of categories. The boundary between plant and machine is not a boundary; it is a zone of transformation, a gradient where one thing becomes another without any clear point of change. A trunk that begins as a bundle of steel pipes will, higher up, have the texture of bark, and then of skin, and then of metal again. A leaf that looks like a photovoltaic panel will, when you glance away and glance back, have taken on the shape of a fern frond, or a feather, or a hand. The ground, when you stand still, feels like a floor, hard and level; but when you walk, it gives slightly, like a living membrane, and you realize that the entire surface is a single continuous organ, a skin stretched over the body of the world. The structures are not separate plants; they are the villi, the bronchioles, the heat exchangers of something vast. The light is not external; it is the bioluminescence of a machine that is also an organism. The coolness is not weather; it is the metabolic temperature of a being whose body is the landscape. The whole place is one thing: a single living-industrial entity, a factory that is a forest, a forest that is a factory, a world that is a body. The only rule is that there is no rule separating life from machine; here, they are the same process.

The dynamic processes of the landscape are slow but continuous. The sap in the clear tubes rises and falls in long, regular pulses, carrying heat and charge and nutrients through the body of the world. The leaves turn to follow some invisible source of energy, some current in the air or the light that only they can sense. The ground shifts imperceptibly as roots grow and cables stretch, as the fabric of the landscape repairs itself and expands. The air cycles through the canopy, drawn in through the vents and exhaled through the leaves, a slow, planetary breath. The light pulses, and the haze settles, and the rare fauna move among the nodes. The entire place is in motion, but the motion is so smooth, so integrated, that it reads as stillness. It is a quiet world, a world of processes that run below the threshold of hearing, a world where the only sounds are the soft clicks and hisses, the faint rustle of plates adjusting, and the almost sub-audible hum of a machine that has been running so long it has forgotten how to be silent.

You could walk through this landscape for days, and it would not be the same landscape. The paths between the trunks would have shifted; the canopy would have grown thicker or thinner; the light would have pulsed in a different rhythm; the ground would have healed over your footprints. The place is vast, and it is alive, and it is built, and it is all one thing, and it does not acknowledge you at all. It simply is: a cool, bright, dry, dense, quiet world, a world of vegetation and machinery, a world without water, a world that has grown itself into existence.
