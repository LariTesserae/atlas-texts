It has no name that survives, or it has a name that is only the sound of its own water. From below, you hear it first: a continuous roar that comes down through the cloud like a long exhalation of the mountain. It is not loud in the way of a storm; it is loud in the way of a sea, or a boiler room, or the inside of a drum. The air warms as you climb toward it.

The city occupies the rim and inner wall of a high caldera, so high that the clouds lie below it like a white sea. The heat is strange for this altitude—thin air and tropical heat together. Steam rises from clefts in the rock, and hot springs spill into pools that smoke. The stone is pale volcanic rock, water-polished and soft-looking, worn into flutes, bowls, and overhangs. Every surface weeps. Water runs in sheets, in strings, in droplets that hang on moss. It is a landscape of constant wetness, not rain exactly but condensation and springs and the breath of the mountain.

Around and over the stone is vegetation so dense it reads as infrastructure. Tree ferns grow in colonnades along the terraces. Moss covers every horizontal and vertical surface. Bromeliads cluster on walls, each one holding a small pool. Lianas braid into cables, railings, and bridge-ropes. The green is heavy, saturated, and hot. The air tastes of wet clay, fern, minerals, and the faint sulfur of steam.

The city itself is built into this. Terraces step down the caldera wall, each one a street or a district. Dwellings are grown and woven: walls of living root lattice, roofs of overlapping leaves that stay green, doorways formed by bent stems and stone slabs. There are aqueducts and cisterns, staircases, plazas, galleries, and stacked neighborhoods where roofs become the streets above. The construction is organized, urban, dense—buildings touch, tunnels pass through huge trunks, bridges span the gaps—but there is no metal, no glass, no cut timber, no tool mark. Nothing has been manufactured. The city seems to have been grown, water-carved, and woven into being by a process that is neither quite plant nor quite stone.

No animals live here. None. The absence is not subtle. There are no birds, no insects, no fish in the pools, no bats in the upper galleries. The water is clear and empty. The flowers, where they exist, are waxy, scentless, and self-contained, as if animal pollination never existed. The only motion is water, steam, and the slow, almost invisible movement of plants that never grow larger or smaller. The city is full but not inhabited.

The sound is not just loud; it is complete. It is the roar of waterfalls on the western rim, the hiss of steam from a hundred vents, the low resonant hum of wind through stone flutes and hollow stems, the patter and drip of water on leaves. It is so constant and so layered that it becomes a pressure. It has no rhythm, no swell, no pause. It does not change from one level to the next. It is the same sound, always, like a note held forever.

Then there is the part that breaks the rules. On the eastern terraces, rain does not fall. It rises. From the pools and cisterns, water lifts in slow vertical threads, gathers into streams, and climbs the walls into the cloud. Some aqueducts run uphill. In the central plaza, a fountain sends water upward, and the water does not come down; it thins into mist and ascends. The western waterfalls and the eastern upward rivers seem to be parts of one closed loop, a knot in the world’s plumbing. The same water returns endlessly, never adding, never subtracting.

The geometry is wrong in quieter ways. Stairways descend and arrive at a level above. A street between two buildings emerges on the far side of the same plaza. Some windows look out onto a view that belongs to a tower miles away. Distances stretch and shrink; the far side of the caldera is sometimes a long journey, sometimes a few steps. The city’s plan is too dense and too folded to map.

And nothing changes. The same leaves are green for lifetimes. The pools neither fill nor empty. The light is always the same—neutral, diffuse, bright enough but not bright, as if the sky is a white lamp behind steam. There is no erosion, because the stone is not losing matter; the water gives back what it takes, or the stone has stopped eroding. There is no decay, no growth, no seasons. The city is in a perfect, deafening equilibrium. It does not move forward. It is not waiting. It is simply continuing.

To stand there is to feel that the place is not older than time but outside it. The water falls and rises forever. The plants are always already green. The city is always already built. And the sound—the roar, the hiss, the hum—is the only thing that tells you it is alive, though nothing here is alive in the usual way. It is a green, wet, hot, impossible city at the top of the world, where reality has been worn smooth and set aside.
