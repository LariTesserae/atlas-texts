The settlement is called Nooth, and it is built almost entirely inside the body of a dead plant.

Not a dead plant like the hollow stump of a tree, but something older and stranger: a root-mind, a vegetative city-organism that sprawled across the high plateau for a million years and then simply stopped, leaving its architecture behind. The locals still call it the Vivus, or sometimes “the old lady.” They speak of it as a thing that is not quite dead and not quite alive, a plant-substrate the size of a mountain range in its final, settled form. Nooth occupies one lobe near the old crown, where the plant’s vascular bundles were once as wide as rail tunnels.

The terrain here is high and broken: a plateau at the top of a long, ancient escarpment, so old that the mountains have worn down into wide, whale-backed ridges and deep, flat-bottomed valleys. The rock beneath is mostly grey and reddish conglomerate, but you rarely see it. The Vivus covers the summit like a fossilised bark-skin, dozens of metres thick in places, and the city is excavated into that. The plant tissue has mineralised over ages into a dense, fibrous, waxy substance, something between lignite, amber, and bone. It is warm to the touch in some places and stone-cold in others, and it sweats.

The first thing you notice in Nooth is the condensation. The plant is not fully sealed. Moisture moves through it, slowly, the way sap once did, but now it moves as groundwater, as condensation, as thin mineralised weep. The city is damp in its deepest corridors and dry on the upper terraces, but the dampness is not uniform: it comes as a regular, breathing condensation, a daily rhythm of moisture and relative aridity. At dawn, the walls bead with fine, cold dew, and by noon the dew has evaporated, leaving a salty rime. By evening, the deeper halls are slick with a clear, slightly viscous fluid that runs along curves of former phloem. The people of Nooth call it “the breath,” and most of the city’s water supply is gathered from it.

The vegetation here is not sparse in life, but sparse in distribution. The whole landscape is plant, but it is dead plant, and the only living plants are those that have colonised its surface: mosses, lichens, alpine cushion-plants, and long, hair-like grasses that grow in cracks in the Vivus’s skin. They are dispersed, each tuft separated by metres of bare plant-stone. In the protected courtyards and old sap-chambers, people grow gardens of edible cress and blue-leafed root vegetables, but these are tiny green islands in the vast grey-brown of the dead organism. The density of living things is low; the density of plant matter is absolute. It creates a strange perceptual field, because the eye reads it as a forest floor stretched over an entire city, but there are no trees, no canopies, only the endless mineralised plant body, pocked with colours of moss and bare as a moonscape between them.

Nooth was built into the Vivus in layers, like a termite city that became industrial. The oldest neighbourhoods are carved into the natural vascular chambers, their walls smooth and ridged from the original plant tissue. Later, people cut wider avenues, rectangular halls, staircases that follow the spiral of old phloem; then they lined them with brick and concrete where the plant-flesh collapsed. Above ground, the light is normal for this elevation: a cool, thin, grey-blue light that comes from a high sky, often with a high veil of cloud drifting over the plateau. At this altitude, the sun is bright but the air is cold. The tops of the old plant structures catch the light like wet stone and gleam faintly, but the streets below are often in shadow.

The industrial layer sits at the eastern edge of the city, where the Vivus dips into a huge natural amphitheatre-like depression, and there the great factories are built into the dead plant’s heartwood. They run on water power and compressed air; the sound is loud, a continuous clatter and hiss and deep, rhythmic thudding. Pipes run over and through the plant body, carrying captured condensation into reservoirs, and from there down into the pistons and boilers. Black smoke rises from chimneys that poke out of the Vivus’s bark, but the smoke does not smell like coal. It smells of dried sap, of burnt amber, like incense and hot sugar, and it drifts across the city in thin, blue-grey threads.

The people of Nooth are not numerous. The city is built for many more than it holds, and the streets feel open, with long empty stretches between the inhabited quarters. Most people live in the middle terraces, where the plant-flesh has been cut into high-backed, cave-like apartments that face the western valley. The interiors are small but high-ceilinged, and they smell of cool earth and old resin. The population is a few thousand, but the built fabric could house ten times that. They are miners, engineers, chemists, sap-refiners, and a small order of “botanists” who study the old plant’s residual behaviour, its breathing, its whispers. And there are mechanics who run the pumps, and rail-gangs who maintain the funicular lines that descend the escarpment to the warmer valleys below.

The weather is cool and active. Clouds move fast over the plateau, and showers come suddenly, leaving the whole city wet and shining. Mist rolls in from the escarpment edge and fills the lower halls. The rain is thin but frequent, a few hours of drizzle every other day. Temperature rarely rises far above a cool spring day; at night it drops near freezing, and frost forms on the upper terraces. The Vivus almost seems to respond to the cold by tightening, and some of its ancient doors shrink or warp slightly.

The most uncanny thing about Nooth is the sound. The city is loud, but the loudness is not always human. The industrial quarter is loud in the mechanical sense: compressors, steam releases, rhythmic trip-hammers. But beneath that, and sometimes over it, the old plant itself makes noise. There is a deep, low groaning that comes from deep in the substrate, as if some buried root-tendon is slowly shifting, not quite dead. There are ticks and creaks in the walls, like a wooden ship in a swell, but on a much longer rhythm: a series of creaks that build over minutes, then release with a small, concussive crack. The people call these “sap-joints.” Sometimes, in the quiet hours before dawn, the walls seem to whisper, a low acoustic effect from air moving through tiny, half-collapsed vascular canals. But the whisper is too patterned. It comes in waves, like a breathing.

And the air is loud in another way: the wind. At this elevation, the wind crosses the open plateau with little obstruction, and it rushes through the high halls and abandoned vaults. The city is an enormous wind instrument. The old openings, the spiral staircases, the chimneys, and the broken pipes create a continuous, low flute-like note, sometimes rising to a howl. The sound is so constant that the people have learned to ignore it, but visitors find it disturbing, as if the city were breathing through a windpipe the size of a cathedral.

The fauna here is rare. There are birds, but not many: a few high-circling grey hawks that ride the updrafts at the escarpment edge; small, pale orb-spiders that live in the condensation channels; and sometimes wild hares in the open gaps where living grasses grow. But there are no large animals, no flocks, no insects in abundance. The city’s own ecosystem is mostly microbial: mats of pale bacteria in the wet halls, fungi that fruit very slowly from the dead heartwood. The people have a few goats and chickens, but they keep them close, and the goats have the nervous, alert look of animals in a place that is not entirely safe.

The weirdness comes at the edges of perception. The reality of the Vivus is unstable in small, localised ways. The plant was not just a physical organism; it was a kind of world-form, a living geometry. In the deep halls, the dimensions are slightly wrong: a corridor that looks fifty metres long takes a hundred paces; a chamber’s ceiling seems to recede when you look at it directly. The walls sometimes appear to ripple, as if the plant-flesh beneath the stone were still moving, very slowly. There are places in Nooth where the city overlaps itself, where a staircase leads both up and down to the same room. The botanists call these “knots.” They are rare and usually sealed behind brick and resin, but sometimes a new one opens.

The most famous knot is the Hollow Meridian, a long, curved hall in the old crown where the city’s two main rail lines cross at right angles in space but never intersect. A train on the upper line, descending, can be seen through a long transparent vein-stone; it enters the same hall as a train on the lower line, but when the lower train emerges, it is sometimes carrying passengers who say they saw the upper train pass through them. The people of Nooth treat this as ordinary, a by-product of living in the old plant’s nervous system. They say the old lady is dreaming, and the city is her dream.

The dynamic of the place is steady but never still. The weather cycles every few days: clear, high cloud, rain, mist, then clear again. The Vivus’s condensation follows a circadian rhythm, a deeper breathing cycle that takes about twenty-six hours. The industry runs continuously, but in shifts; the rail goes up and down the escarpment twice a day. The only real movement is processual, not animal: water moving, smoke moving, sound moving, trains moving. The city feels active, but it is the activity of a machine or a body, not a wild ecosystem.

The built layer is extensive: streets cut from plant-stone, brick and concrete inserts, tramways, pipe-bridges, signalling towers made from the old fibre-bundles, iron ladders bolted into the bark. There is a central market in a repaired sap-chamber, where the ceiling is so high that it holds its own weather. There is a Church of the Vivus, a small sect that believes the plant is not dead but slowly remembering itself, and that one day the city will be digested. There is a school, a hospital, a pumping station, a rail terminal, and a factory that distils the old sap into a resin used as lacquer and as a preserving agent for meat. There is no graveyard; the dead are laid in the deep, cool heartwood, where the fibres slowly close over them.

At the far edge of the city, where the Vivus’s edges crumble into the escarpment, the plant-root body is exposed in a great broken cross-section, two hundred metres high. You can see the fossilised growth rings, thousands of them, some pale, some dark as burnt toast. A waterfall emerges from a broken vascular bundle near the top, and falls free for a long way before hitting the valley floor below, where it forms a thin silver river that runs into a vast, dry plain. That is the only real watercourse: a single falling river from the old plant, like a wound that never heals.

At night, Nooth is mostly dark, but the industrial quarter glows with furnace light, and along the main terraces, a few electric lamps run on steam-driven dynamos. The light is sparse and yellow, and it does not reach far into the black of the dead plant, which gleams faintly where wet. The wind continues, the pipes hiss, the old halls breathe. The city is like a fossil skull with a small, warm brain of industry and ten thousand empty rooms.

The place is ancient and high and cool and wet, built into a plant that is the whole world, a plant that is dead but still present, still breathing, still dreaming. Nooth is an open place, a city of sparse people in a dense vegetal architecture, with loud machinery and louder wind, and a deep reality that occasionally forgets itself in small, almost beautiful ways.
