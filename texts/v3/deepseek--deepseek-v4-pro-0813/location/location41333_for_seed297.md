The first thing is the dryness.

You come down from the low ridge into a broad basin, and the air changes: warmer, thinner, sharp with minerals. It has no moisture at all. Your mouth dries, your eyes sting faintly, the skin on your hands feels tight. There is no free water here—no dew, no seep, no dark line of moisture at the base of a stem. What little water exists is bound inside crystal lattices and plant films, chemically unreachable. Even the shadows are dry, as if they could be swept away as dust.

The light is ordinary daylight, but the sun is too high and too white, and it does not move. Shadows are short and hard, printed on the ground without softening. The place is warm, not scorching—the kind of warmth that has been there so long it has become a property of the air.

Below the ridge, the land opens out. The basin is low, a shallow depression perhaps ten kilometers across, its rim visible only as a faint change in mineral color. The floor is a salt-crust, but not the salt of any sea. It has the blue-grey of tarnished magnesium, cracked into hexagonal plates. The edges of the plates are sharp, smooth, unweathered. This is a young landscape. Nothing has been rounded by water, because there has never been water. No streambeds, no rounded stones, no silt. The crust looks as if it was broken open yesterday and then simply stopped.

And yet the place is lush.

The vegetation begins a few meters from the rim and grows denser as the ground drops. It is not green in the way of wet grass or humid forest. It is a lushness of glass and mineral fiber, of filaments and blades and spirals. The stems rise in clusters, some as thick as an arm, branching at precise angles. The leaves are thin, translucent, mica-like; they overlap like scales. Their colors are the colors of chemistry, not chlorophyll: copper-green, cobalt-blue, sulfur-yellow, iron-red, silver-white. Where light passes through them, the ground is striped with colored shadows as sharp as stained glass, and just as still.

Underneath the taller growth, there is a dense understory—curled shoots like frozen fiddleheads, mats of hair-thin fiber that glint like optical wire, pale nodes that could be flowers or could be lenses. The plants grow so close that you cannot see more than a few meters in any direction. In places the growth forms corridors that are not paths, just gaps between near-touching stems. The ground is bare salt where it is not covered by roots. There is no leaf litter. Nothing has fallen. The air beneath the canopy is warm and still, with a smell like hot electrical insulation, crushed camphor, and the faint sweet chemical tang of benzaldehyde.

The plants do not use water. If you break a leaf—it snaps cleanly, with a faint glassy sound—there is no sap, no wetness. The interior is layered, metal-organic films and microscopic cavities, veins of copper that are not veins but circuits. The roots run deep into the salt crust, not to drink, but to draw current and minerals. The whole forest is a vast distributed machine, an array of solar collectors and chemical reactors that has taken the shape of vegetation. There are no buildings, no roads, no antennas, no tools. There are no made artifacts at all. The technology is not built on this landscape; it is the landscape.

Then there are the anomalies.

Thirty meters away, a cluster of pale stems is also visible ten meters to your right, identical to the last bent frond, like a double exposure. It does not flicker. It is simply there. The horizon, when you look at it directly, is level; at the edge of your vision it curves upward, as if the basin is the inside of a vast bowl. Some leaves cast no shadow. A few small shards of salt hang in the air at chest height, motionless, as if the moment the ground broke was never allowed to end. In one place, your footstep sounds half a second late; in another, the sound is swallowed entirely. These things are not alarming. They are too stable, too much a part of the place's anatomy. Reality here has not collapsed. It has crystallized into a configuration that should not exist, and then stopped.

Animals are rare. In a whole afternoon, you might see one: a long, ribbon-like organism of black mirror, moving through the canopy without disturbing a single leaf. It has no obvious head or legs. It swims through the still air, bends the light around itself, and is gone. You might hear a single click from deep in the thicket, once, but no answer comes.

The sound of the place is mostly quiet. Not empty quiet—pressurized quiet. There is no wind, no water, no birdcall, no insect. Your own breathing sounds close and too loud. If you put your hand on a stem, you feel a very slow vibration, or maybe it is only your heartbeat returning through your palm. The only continuous sound is a subaudible hum, as if the entire basin is a tuning fork struck long ago and still ringing below hearing.

And nothing is changing. Nothing at all. The sun does not move. The shadows do not lengthen. No leaf falls, no new shoot emerges. The dust has already settled. The salt grains in the air have hung there for as long as the place has existed. The circuits in the plants are closed and perfectly balanced; no charge accumulates, no gradient dissipates. The forest runs without running. It is a landscape that time has forgotten to enter, or that time has been made unable to enter. The lushness is mature, complete, saturated—a climax without succession. It is not waiting for rain. It is not waiting for anything.

You leave, and the place remains exactly as it was, as if you had never been there.
