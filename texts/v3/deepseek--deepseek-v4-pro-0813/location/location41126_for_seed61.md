**The Weeping Vault**

It is a single chamber, hidden beneath the oldest quarter of a city whose name has been forgotten above—a forgotten cistern that became a world. The only way in is a low, water-worn passage that begins behind a false stone in a cellar and ends in a drop of twice a man’s height onto a narrow ledge. The chamber is not large—perhaps a hundred and twenty paces long, forty wide, thirty high—but it is a city. Every surface is built, carved, stacked, hung, and inhabited. The air is cool, not cold, the kind of cool that seeps into the bones after an hour; the water is colder, and the stone is coldest of all. The air is so saturated that the stone sweats and the breath does not steam.

The first thing is the sound. It is not a single sound but a many-layered one: the high ping of water falling into a copper basin, the low plunk of a drop into a deep clay jar, the rattle of a channel, the hiss of a sheet of water sliding down a wall, the gurgle of a drain. There are hundreds of basins, jars, gutters, and flutes, all tuned, all fed by the same unchanging seep from the rock above. The sound is so loud that it fills the chamber like water fills a pool; it is the city’s weather, its clock, its music. The people speak in a kind of sung rhythm that fits between the drips, and they would find silence unbearable.

The light is dim, blue and gray, from a few clay lamps burning a thin, blue-flamed mineral oil and from a seam of faintly luminous stone that runs across the ceiling like a vein of old moonlight. It is never bright enough to cast sharp shadows; everything is seen as if through deep water. The city is built vertically. The walls are terraced into ledges and galleries, each one crowded with dwellings carved into the rock and built out with stone slabs and clay bricks. The lanes are so narrow that two people cannot pass without turning sideways; the balconies nearly touch across them, and from every edge hang ropes, hooks, buckets, and cages of bone and hide. The city houses perhaps three hundred people in a space that would be a single courtyard above.

There are no plants, no wood, no cloth of fiber. Nothing grows here. The water is too mineral, the light too dim, the air too still. Instead, the walls are covered with stone that grows: gypsum flowers, aragonite needles, calcite crusts that spread like frost. The people tend these mineral gardens, scraping away the excess to keep the shapes from changing, and they call them the orchards of the deep.

The water is the city’s blood. It seeps from the ceiling in a thousand places, each drop caught and channeled. The channels are carved into the stone, lined with clay, and they run down the walls in a network of gutters and pipes. The water is cold and clear and heavy with dissolved stone; as it flows, it deposits a microscopic layer of calcite, so the channels are always being repaired by the water itself. The people do not build new things; they maintain the old, and the old is so well maintained that it looks both ancient and new. The steps are hollowed by countless feet, but the hollows are filled with new stone, so the steps are level again. The carvings are worn soft, but the softness is the shape they were meant to have. The city is finished. It has been finished for so long that no one remembers a time when it was otherwise.

There are no seasons, no weather, no day or night. The lamps burn at the same height, the water flows at the same rate, the basins ring with the same notes. The people are few, and they move slowly, as if they are part of the stone. They are born and they die, but the city does not change. The only wild creatures are a few pale, blind newts that live in the deepest cistern, and they are so rarely seen that their appearance is an omen. The people say that when a newt is seen, the water has chosen a new note, but no one has heard a new note in living memory.

The rules of the place are simple and absolute. The water must not be stopped. The sound must not change. Nothing new may be brought in, and nothing old may be taken out. The city is a closed system, a single instrument, and every part of it is tuned. On the north wall, the water does not run down; it climbs, drawn by the stone’s fine pores, and hangs in a permanent silver fur of droplets that never fall. That wall is called the Weeping Mirror, and it is said that if you look into it long enough, you can see the city as it was when it was first built, and as it will be when it is last seen, and they are the same.
