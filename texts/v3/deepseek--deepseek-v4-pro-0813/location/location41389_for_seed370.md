Two ledges below the room, the sound arrives before the place does. It comes up through the dry air as a pressure in the chest, then in the teeth, then in the back of the eyes: a single chord, low and continuous, like a note the world makes when it has no reason to stop. The path climbs a cliff that is not rock. The cliff is brick. It is not a natural mountain capped with a building; it is a building that has become a mountain. Every handhold, every step, every shadowed alcove is made of mud-brick, fired clay, or stone blocks cut and set so long ago that the courses now read as strata. At this height the air is warm and thin, and it has no moisture in it at all.

The chamber at the top is small. It has the scale of a modest chapel, eleven strides long, five wide, and no more than twice a person’s height at the vault. But it does not feel small because it is so full. It feels like the interior of a chest: every surface covered, every interval used. The walls are lined from floor to ceiling with niches, and in every niche sits something. There are round-bottomed clay jars, hundreds of them, their mouths stopped with clay or dust. There are hand-sized stone heads, worn smooth, each with a single hole drilled where the mouth should be. There are stacks of clay tablets, some of them sagging in the middle as if they were stacked before they were dry. There are strips of hammered copper hanging from clay hooks, motionless.

The light is dim. It comes from a shallow channel in the floor, filled with a mineral powder that glows a dull orange without flame, like coals that have forgotten how to burn. The same powder has been pressed into holes in the ceiling, so the upper dimness is scattered with steady, unblinking points. It is warm light, red-leaning, and it gives just enough to see the room by, but not enough to read the far wall. The shadows are crowded.

The warmth is constant. It rises from the floor and from the wall behind the pipes. It has no dampness in it. The air smells of hot clay, hot dust, and a faint metallic mineral tang, like rain that has never fallen. The dry air pulls moisture from the skin. Nothing grows here. There is no water anywhere, and no soil that has not been baked or swept away. No leaf has ever uncurled in this room, no insect has crossed the lintel. The only living or once-living things are the absent makers, and the many small faces they left facing inward.

The room is full, but it is not a storage room. Its center is occupied by a low stone screen, and behind the screen are the pipes. There are seven of them. They are thick as bodies, made from coiled clay, set upright in the wall. Warm gas rises through a shaft from below, out of the lower parts of the built plateau. It is not steam; it has no water in it, only the hot dry exhalation of the lower brickwork. It is pushed across the lips of the pipes and then up through hidden flues. The pipes are tuned to one another. Their notes lock into a chord that is not quite music. It is the sound you heard below. It is deafening. It is not a sequence, not a melody, not a rhythm. It does not pause for breath. It is a single standing pressure, too steady for the ear to follow as change.

Inside the room, the sound is physical. The floor hums. The dust that should settle does not settle; it hangs in a fine line at chest height, a plane of red mineral held in a node of the standing wave. It does not move, though the air trembles. The sound is so complete that after a time it stops being sound and becomes the shape of the place: the room as a bell that has been ringing for longer than the language that named it.

The density of the room is absolute. There is no bare wall. Between the niches are smaller niches. Between the floor altars are clay pegs, little posts, low stone bowls. One must walk sideways in places. The path across the floor is not an empty space; it is a deliberate aisle left by the builders, and it winds because the room is packed with objects that cannot be moved. The objects are not random. Every jar has been placed; every tablet is stacked to a measured height; every copper strip is stamped with rows of small marks, like a tally of something no one now counts.

The place is ancient, but it has not collapsed. It is ancient in the way a riverbed boulder is ancient: every edge is gone. The brick corners are rounded. The niches are soft as old wax. The stair outside has been hollowed by the pressure of feet until it shines. The features of the stone heads have worn away, leaving only the drilled mouth-holes. The clay pipes’ lips are polished to a vitreous smoothness by the constant gas. This is not ongoing erosion; it is the final condition. The room has been scoured down to its final shape, and now it holds. The dust it lost long ago has either blown away or hangs in the sound.

Because it is high, the world outside is not visible except as a suggestion from the ledge: tier after tier of roofs and baffle walls and vent mouths descending into a brown haze. The sky, if sky exists, is a permanent bronze dim. There is no weather. The temperature is the same at the floor and at the ceiling. The glow from the light-channel does not flicker. The gas does not gust. The sound does not change pitch. The room has reached a time-invariant state: no seasons, no decay, no growth, no cycle. It is not frozen; it is exactly balanced. The only movement is the warm gas in the pipes, and even that is so steady it is less an event than a condition.

The rules of the world are simple here. Everything is made; nothing is natural. Everything is dry; nothing decays. Everything is full; nothing is empty except the door and the passage, which are full of their own function. The sound is the law. It keeps the dust up, keeps the air pressed into order, keeps the place from falling into silence. A visitor can make no sound of their own, not because they are silent, but because their sounds are eaten by the chord before they reach the walls.

The room is a landscape in a small form. It has its own horizon, which is the far wall. It has its own climate, which is warm and dry and dim. It has its own inhabitants, which are the hundreds of made heads and jars and copper leaves. It has no water, no plants, no animals. It has no change. And at the center of it, beyond the low screen, seven clay pipes hold a note that has been going so long it has become not a note but a place—the room itself, singing the only word it has left.
