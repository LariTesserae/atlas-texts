It is a hole in the shoulder of a mountain, high enough that the sun is pale and huge and the cold gets into the lungs like water. From above it might look like a socket, a single small opening no larger than a cathedral nave, not a valley. Its walls rise on three sides, fluted and old. They are not jagged. The water has been at them long enough that every edge has softened, and the stone has the look of wax drips or ribbed bone. The whole place is a single room.

The rock weeps. Water seeps from every crack and pore, not in gushing falls but in thin sheets and slow threads. It is slightly briny and glistens on the walls like sweat. It collects in a series of stone-lipped terraces, some natural, some cut by generations of stone tools, and spills from one basin to the next in smooth unbroken sheets. In still corners, ice forms a skin and breaks and forms again. The cold is deep and wet, and it gets into clothing and bone. But most of the water moves too quickly to freeze.

The light is even harder than the cold. It comes down from above and then bounces between wet walls until it seems to have no origin. Shadows are thin and gray, even at noon. The air itself is full of mist and spray, and the mist catches the light, so the hollow is not just bright but filled with a cold white substance, like being inside a cloud. The people here squint through pale eyes.

Green is scarce. There are no trees. Gray-green lichen follows the seep-lines on the rock. Cushion plants the size of fists grow in cracks. A few wiry shrubs hold themselves sideways on ledges, folded against the cold. But the true base of the place is not green: it is a pink-white mineral film that thickens wherever the light hits the wet stone. It is not quite vegetation, but it feeds the hollow.

Inside the bowl is a settlement. It is too dense to be called a village and too small to be called a city, but it has the organization of a city. The buildings are stacked into the rock face, roofed with slate and stretched hide and the bones of large animals. There is almost no wood. The people build with stone, mud, hide, and bone, and their tools are stone and obsidian and splinters of antler. Rooms are carved into the walls and extended with drystone, connected by ladders, rope bridges, gutters, and stone stairs. The water is the main infrastructure: channels carry it through rooms, under floors, between walls; it supplies drinking water, carries waste, and keeps the terraces working. Paths are only gaps between walls, narrow and slick with mist. The density is nearly unbearable. Roofs almost touch roofs. Windows face windows at arm’s length. You could cross most of the settlement by stepping from one roof to the next, and people do. It is not a planned town. It is an accretion, a swallow-nest colony, a coral of human making. Every ledge holds something: a basket, a cage, a sleeping animal, a shrine of small bones.

The hollow is full of animals. They are pale and mostly silent. White birds nest on the high ledges; their wings are soft as moths, and they do not cry. Six-legged grazers like thin goats move over the wet rock, licking the mineral film. Fish school in the channels so densely that the water seems to move sideways. There are pale eels, insects with water-colored shells, and small furred things that sleep in stacks in doorways. The people keep some of these animals in roof pens, and the animals keep the people warm. Everything is packed so close that the settlement and its animals form a single warm-bodied system. The base of the food web is the mineral film and the lichen. The grazers eat it. The fish eat the film that sloughs off the rock. The people eat fish, birds, eggs, and the six-legged grazers. Nothing is wasted.

And yet the place is quiet. The quiet is not emptiness; it is dense. The air is so cold and heavy with water that sounds die within a few paces. A dropped tool lands with a dry tick. A footstep is a soft press. Voices are kept low. A shout would only be swallowed, so the people have stopped trying. The water, which should gurgle and splash, moves in smooth sheets and covered channels, and where it falls it falls as an unbroken curtain, so it makes no splash. The loudest thing is the inside of one’s own head. It is the silence of felt, or deep snow, or a bell jar.

Under the quiet, the place is turbulent. The water constantly rearranges the terraces. Small channels shift. Pools overflow in new directions. A wall will crack in the night, sag silently, and be rebuilt by morning. The cold splits stone; the wet lubricates its fall; the people carry the pieces away and fit new ones. Animals move up and down the rock in waves, white bodies flowing from ledge to ledge. The settlement is an anthill with the sound deleted. There is no pause. Ladders are shifted, roofs repaired, gutters cleared. Even the air churns with spray and mist; little whirlwinds spin water off the walls without noise. It is a storm in slow motion. The erosion is established, but not finished; it is a steady process of falling and rebuilding, not a stable form.

Some things here do not behave as they should. The light comes from too many directions, so shadows are weak, and sometimes a person seems to float because nothing dark anchors them. The mineral film glows faintly when the sun is low. Some water runs uphill along threads of stone, pulled by the wind or by the surface tension of the wet rock, so the terraces fill from below as well as above. The animals are pale and often blind, navigating by touch and heat. The people do not sing. They say the mountain listens, and if it answers, the whole hollow will come down. True or not, the result is the same: a bright, wet, cold, crowded room in the world, silent while everything inside it moves.

This is a place that never repeats itself, though it has been here a long time. It is a small urban storm wearing the shape of a stone bowl, full of animals and water and light, with the sound turned off.
