It is a seam in a mountain that has just been born.

The mountain is a black spine of fresh volcanic glass and scoria rising out of a high cloud deck, so young that its edges have never been rounded by weather. At its upper reaches, the outside air is thin and cold, but heat still moves through the rock like a fever. Just below the summit steam cap, behind a curled overhang of fused ash, there is a hidden slot no wider than a pair of shoulders. From below it is invisible. From above it is only a wet black line exhaling steam. Inside, it opens into a narrow keyhole of rock—one step deep, two steps long, with a ceiling that narrows into a bright fissure.

The walls weep. Not with rain, but with the mountain’s own water: hot condensation rises out of hairline fractures, beads on the glassy stone, and slides down in slow, wrong paths. The rock is black, smoked silver, rust-red in places where iron salts have lacquered the cracks. Sulfur dusts the deeper seams a dirty yellow. There is no soil here. There are no plants. The surfaces are bare mineral, sharp as unhealed fractures, and they have not been smoothed by all the wetness because the place is too new and, in some places, time does not let erosion finish its work.

The air inside is dense and hot. At this altitude, water should boil away, but the pocket holds its own breath: a private atmosphere of steam and mineral vapor that keeps the liquid just on the near side of boiling. Breathing it feels like drinking. Daylight enters from the fissure overhead, but the steam turns it into a soft amber glow without a clear source. Droplets hang in that glow like bright seeds. Some fall. Some roll sideways along a wall. Near the center of the pocket, a slow knot of droplets circles a point of warmer air, orbiting one another as if the room has its own small, liquid solar system.

The place should hiss. Steam in a stone throat should hiss. But it is quiet. The quiet is not empty; it is crowded and held. The water makes only the smallest ticks, and even those seem to come from a few inches away from where they happened. Occasionally the cooling glass pings once, a single high note, and then the silence closes back over it. The animals make no calls. They move through the hot wet air without scraping, without song, without any sound at all.

Nothing green lives here. The bottom of the food web is not light-harvesting but chemical: sulfur-oxidizing films spread over the wet stone in gray and yellow sheets, and iron-loving colonies turn the condensate rust-colored. They are not plants. They are not fungi. They are older, simpler, and they coat the rock like varnish.

Above them, the walls are more animal than stone. A dense lacquer of tiny comma-shaped grazers covers every surface, overlapping like translucent scales. They feed on the chemical films and the mineral crusts, and their movement makes the rock appear to sweat. Over them crawl larger seed-sized animals with ring-shaped mouths and too many legs. Many carry splinters of obsidian in their front limbs. The tools are not made; they are chosen from the sharp debris that flakes from the ceiling as the rock cools. The animals use them to pry mineral salts from the cracks, then discard them. The floor, the walls, the undersides of ledges—all of it seethes with this silent, layered population. Every droplet of water holds smaller animals still, round and slow, turning in their own bright spheres.

The predators are harder to see. Some are like ripples of heat, visible only when they move. The pocket’s geometry is unreliable, and the hunters have learned to swim through that. One may be on the left wall, then on the right wall, then folded into a corner that should not exist, all without crossing the air between. It pushes prey into spaces where distance repeats and escape paths become loops. There are also mirror-skinned things whose bodies copy the rock so precisely that when they move, a patch of wall seems to lag half a second behind the rest. The density is not just crowding. It is stacking: grazer upon grazer, tool-user upon tool-user, predator folded inside predator, all packed into one small wet cut in the mountain.

The rules of the place are not human rules. Heat drives water out of the rock. Water condenses, falls, is reabsorbed by the same fractures. Mineral salts build, peel, and are grazed. The glass-fleas prune the crusts; the predators keep the glass-fleas from overgrazing. It is an ecosystem in motion, but silent and without architecture. The animals do not build. They make no nests, no middens, no lasting tools. Anything arranged in one of the time folds might come back twice, or as a different object, or not at all. Their technology is primitive and temporary: a borrowed splinter of glass, used for a few heartbeats, then dropped.

Inside this pocket, the mountain has not finished becoming. The rock is newly exposed, the heat is still immense, and in that strangeness the old certainties fray. Gravity runs in eddies. Light tacks sideways. Shadows sometimes point toward warm stone rather than away from the light. A bead of water may fall, then be at the ceiling again, then fall a second time with a faint double image. The animals have adapted to this. They use the loops to redo a failed strike, to travel a short distance by folding the space between two breaths.

The whole place is small enough that one person could touch both walls with their elbows, but hidden enough that a traveler could pass within arm’s reach and see only a wet black seam. Outside, the mountain drops away into cloud for thousands of meters. Inside, the world is hot, wet, overfull, and nearly silent—a crowded wound in new rock, where the physics have not yet sealed themselves shut.
