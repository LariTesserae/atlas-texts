On a high volcanic shelf above a sea of cloud, the city sweats. The heat does not come from the sun—there is no direct sun—but from the ground, from the walls, from the seams in the paving stones. The air is thick, warm, and wet, like a long-closed factory at the end of a shift. You are high up; the air behind the humidity has a high-altitude thinness, but the heat overrides it. The light is dim, sepia and brown, filtered through a permanent ceiling of steam, fume, and condensate that sits over the city. The sky is not a thing with weather; it is a lid.

The terrain is dark basalt, columnar in places, cut into broad terraces. The plateau is large and open: from the upper streets you can see the city step downward through haze, and beyond it the land falls into a deeper haze that might be canyon or cloud. The stone is old, settled, polished by centuries of steam and mineral water. Nothing here is raw. The edges of rock are rounded, the joints filled with mineral crust. The city has grown over the heat like a scab, and the plateau has grown around the city in turn.

Water is everywhere, but not the kind that nourishes. It runs down walls, beads on iron, drips from lintels, collects in gutters, spills over the edges of terraces into steaming channels. It is warm to the touch, faintly mineral, tasting of sulfur and copper. The humidity is so high that each breath feels like a drink. There are frequent rains, quick and violent, that appear from the steam ceiling and stop as suddenly, leaving the streets wet and steaming. The buildings weep. Not just from condensation: the walls themselves exude a thin film of moisture, which runs down in white or yellow streaks. Pipes, riveted plates, concrete, stone—all are damp. The word for the city might be “weeping.”

Nothing green grows here. There is no soil in the streets, only mineral crust. In cracks where plants might be expected, there are fans of salt crystals and branching accretions of travertine and sulfur, pale and delicate, growing a few centimeters a year. They are not alive; they accrete. The only biology is simpler: in the hot drainage channels, a faint pink-white film of heat-loving microbes, and in the cisterns, perhaps, blind pale things. But no trees, no moss, no weeds. It is a mineral world.

The built environment is vast and intact, not a ruin. It is a city of brick, iron, concrete, and dark stone, built in layers. Oldest are the blocky basalt warehouses and stone foundations; newer are the riveted steel towers, pipe gantries, open iron stairways, brick smokestacks. It is industrial: factories with long sawtooth roofs, boiler houses, transformer yards, tram lines on concrete posts, shaft heads, hoppers, conveyor bridges. The city is organized into districts. The upper terraces capture water and steam; the middle terraces hold the machines; the lower terraces release the hot mineral outflow into a canyon that fades into steam. Streets are wide enough for trucks and trams, but they are empty.

The emptiness is the city’s most pronounced feature. It was built for thousands, but nothing now fills it. A single working pump may be the only movement for a hundred meters. A factory hall can have a dozen machines running and still feel like a cathedral. The plazas have no one. The buildings are not shells; many are lit by small amber control lamps, their interiors clean but empty. The spaces between functioning things are measured in minutes of walking. This emptiness is not absence; it is a quality, an echoing hollow. The city has a population of machines.

And the machines are still running. The industry is old, electromechanical, steam-driven, with exposed gears and glass insulators, but not dead. Underground, turbines turn. Steam pipes knock and hiss. Overhead, cables hum faintly. Some of the factories still produce—what, it is not clear. Perhaps they process the mineral water, extracting salts or cooling it, or perhaps they make parts for each other. The city is a closed loop of maintenance and production with no visible consumers. Occasionally a tram car moves, empty, on its scheduled route. There are no workers, no operators. The automation is clunky, industrial, thorough. It has kept the city alive long after the people left or died.

Day and night are only slightly different. The steam ceiling glows from within, a deep orange-brown, but the light stays low. There is no direct sun. Shadows are soft and long at all hours. At night, the city is almost black except for the small red and amber signal lamps, the glow of furnace doors, the occasional white flash of a spark. The dimness is constant, like a foundry at dusk.

The city is mostly quiet. The dominant sound is water: a low hiss of steam, the tick and patter of condensation, the gurgle of drains, the distant rumble of a geyser. Machinery adds a low-frequency hum, felt as much as heard, like the breathing of the city. Every few minutes a valve opens with a sigh; every hour or so a bell or whistle sounds far away. There is no human noise. No voices, no engines revving, no clatter of industry—only the soft, patient sounds of a machine running itself.

Living creatures are rare. In the hot drainage channels, there are milky, eyeless fish or amphibian things, no longer than a finger, drifting in the mineral-rich water. On the walls near vents, tiny translucent arthropods pick at the salt crusts. There are said to be larger things in the deep cisterns and the old tunnels, but they are seldom seen. The ecology runs on sulfur and heat, not on sunlight and leaves. The rare fauna are pale, silent, heat-adapted, and they stay away from open spaces. Sometimes a white, sightless thing will be seen in a puddle, and then it is gone.

The strangeness of the place is not that it is empty, but that it is alive. The city seems to breathe. The steam vents open and close in slow waves across the terraces, like gills. The water in the gutters sometimes reverses its flow for a few seconds, as if the city is swallowing. The weeping of the walls is rhythmic, not passive. At certain times, the entire upper terrace exhales a white cloud that rolls down the streets and then is pulled back into grates. In places, the pipes have grown into the rock and the rock has grown into the pipes; it is impossible to tell where geology ends and construction begins. The city is less a thing built on the plateau than a mineral growth that has learned to conduct steam and water. It is a heat engine with the shape of a city.

The rules of this world are roughly these: the city sits on a thermal gradient between the hot rock below and the cooler cloud above, and it converts that gradient into work. Water is the working fluid. The city’s body—pipes, towers, cisterns, terraces—moves heat from below to above by evaporating water and recondensing it. The factories are organs of that process. The steam ceiling is its exhaust; the rain is its condensate; the weeping walls are its temperature regulation. No plants because the heat and minerals are too extreme; no people because the city no longer requires them. The city is not waiting for anyone to return. It is not a ruin. It is a place in its own state: hot, wet, dim, empty, and quietly, steadily working.

The cycles continue. Every few hours, a deep thump runs through the ground, then a column of steam and hot water shoots from a concrete stack, and the streets run with warm water for a few minutes. The factories shift their output; a machine that was still begins to turn, then stops. Trams run empty. The cloud ceiling thickens and thins but never clears. This is an active place, but its activity is slow and steady, like a chemical reaction that has been running for a very long time. The city sweats, and the sweat runs down the terraces, and the water rises again as steam, and the city breathes.
