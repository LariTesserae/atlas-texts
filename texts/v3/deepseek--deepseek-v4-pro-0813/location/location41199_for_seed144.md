It is a high salt basin, a plateau folded between old volcanic shoulders at something like 4,600 meters. The thin air makes the heat strange: in full sun it arrives as a physical weight, pressing on the back of the neck, bouncing off the white ground, but step into the shadow of a pipeline trestle and the warmth falls away instantly, the way a light goes out. Nothing holds heat here. Yet the basin is hot, hot enough to soften boot soles on the salt, hot enough that the salt pan shimmers all afternoon like a lake that is not there.

The scale of the place is regional. The southern rim is visible as a pale jagged line, but it takes half a day to reach; the northern edge dissolves into heat haze and white distance. There is almost no green. Vegetation is a rare event. In a few places where fresh water leaks from the base of the mountains, there are cushion plants—hard, resinous, centuries old, growing in dense gray-green mounds no bigger than a sleeping cat. Elsewhere, only a wiry grass in fist-sized tufts, lichen stains on stones, and the dry inflorescences of plants that bloomed during a wet decade no one now remembers. The emptiness of vegetation is total enough that a single green cushion a kilometer away draws the eye like a signal fire.

But the basin is not empty. It is saturated, packed in a way that has nothing to do with plants. Every square meter of ground is claimed by something. The salt pan is divided into polygons, each the size of a tabletop or a door, their edges raised into low walls of white crust. The cracks between them collect everything the wind and the animals and the old industry have left: wind-drifted gypsum sand, windrows of dead brine flies, the bones of small birds, green copper wires corroded to fur, bottle caps, glass worn milky, a fish vertebra from some long-vanished lake. There is no vacant ground. Even the air above the pan is full—heat shimmer, small flies, a few pale birds, dust suspended so finely it never seems to settle.

Water is intermittent and mostly hidden. Rain comes rarely, theatrical, with clouds piling over the western ridge and dumping enough water to turn the entire pan into a brief mirror. For ten minutes, the basin becomes a perfect reflection of the sky, and then the water drains into cracks and vanish. Beneath the surface, brine sits only a few meters down, moving slowly, and it seeps upward along the polygon edges, keeping the salt doughy and damp. At dawn, every metal surface is beaded with condensation. Fence wire drips. The flanks of old machines are wet as if they have sweated through the night. Where the brine concentrates in shallow depressions, the ground is dark and soft, and the air has a smell like wet zinc, like the taste of a battery.

Life is abundant but localized. The wet seeps are black with brine flies; they rise in mats when a shadow crosses. Lizards with salt-white backs do push-ups on concrete plinths and bolt into cracks too small for a finger. Small finches nest inside junction boxes and in the elbows of pipework, and they fly in nervous arcs from one machine to another. At dusk, a red fox works the pipeline track, walking the raised berm with that odd, patient gait, pouncing on rodents that have dens in the loose fill. Long-billed birds—some kind of highland snipe—stitch the sky above the seep lines, their calls thin and metallic, like a gate swinging. In the evaporation ponds, the water is pink with halophilic bacteria and crowded with tiny brine shrimp, and from a distance flocks of flamingos stand in the heat, too bright to be plausible, pale pink objects against the white.

Human presence is scattered rather than gathered. Every half-kilometer or so there is a concrete pumphouse no larger than a horse cart, painted once in pale blue and now flaking to salt. One has a wooden door gone soft as felt. Another has no door at all and contains a diesel motor so corroded it resembles a cave formation. There are single-room shelters of stone and corrugated iron, most roofless, their interiors wind-piled with salt and bird droppings. Half-collapsed A-frames mark old test wells. Survey stakes of rebar stand in lines, each with a scrap of colored cloth tied to it. A narrow-gauge rail spur runs from an old loading platform to the edge of the pan, its rails red with rust and partly buried, each tie furred with white crystals. A few solar panels on a pole face the sun beside a pump, their glass scoured milky. These are not ruins in a sad sense; some are still in use, or half-use, because a pump starts on a timer somewhere and runs for eleven minutes and then stops.

The technology is old industrial, electrical and mechanical. A single line comes across the southern ridge on lattice towers, sagging over the pan, feeding a transformer that steps down current for a dozen pumps. The pumps are cast iron and brass, with greased leather gaskets and pressure gauges that have not read true in years. Brine flows through a mix of black plastic and rusted steel pipe to evaporation ponds lined with cracked rubber. There is a diesel generator in a concrete bunker, its exhaust pipe rising like a stovepipe, and a bank of lead-acid batteries on a wooden rack, their cases bulging. At the northern end, an old loading derrick stands over a dry pit, its cable slack, its bucket half-filled with salt that has hardened into a single block. The system is not dead. It has simply been working long enough that its own casts and leaks have been absorbed into the mineral order.

Erosion here is established, settled. The wind has had decades, perhaps centuries, to round every edge. Nothing is being destroyed quickly. Salt has grown up around the feet of machines in deliberate-looking encrustations, white coral around iron. Pipes that leaked have built their own mineral terraces, small stepped mounds of pink and white. Concrete has lost its aggregate at the corners but still stands. Metal rusts to a certain depth, then stops, protected by a skin of oxide and salt. The place is not falling apart. It is becoming itself, the human objects slowly entering the same long time-scale as the rocks. A rusted bolt half-buried in the pan has the same presence as a stone; it belongs.

For all this, the basin is mostly quiet. The heat makes a sound you feel more than hear: a low pressure in the ears, a faint ticking of expanding salt. The pump, when it runs, is a distant rhythmic mutter, more vibration than noise. Wind is rare and low, a whisper through the cable loops. The flies make a continuous tiny crackle at the wet margins. A bird calls once, far off, and the silence closes over it. Sound does not carry far here. The open bowl of the basin seems to swallow it, or the thin air lacks the substance to push it along. You hear your own breath, your boots on the crust, the clink of a wire against a pole. Then nothing.

Do not mistake quiet for stillness. The salt is growing. Each night the crust cools and contracts, and before dawn it snaps along the polygon edges, a sound like someone walking far away on broken glass. The brine moves beneath the surface, drawn upward by evaporation, and the ponds lose a centimeter a day under this sun, leaving rings of pink and white. At midday the mirages return, filling the southern flats with water that does not exist. When a storm crosses, it does so violently: a wall of dust, then rain so sudden the salt hisses. Flash floods pour down the alluvial fans, carrying red gravel over the white pan, then vanish into swallow holes. The animals follow these moisture pulses within hours. New flies appear at new seeps. The birds come after them. The fox comes after the birds. The industrial system cycles too: pumps switch on, brine flows, a valve shudders, pond levels drop, a float switch somewhere trips in a pumphouse no one has entered for months.

There are small strangenesses woven through the ordinary. The salt has grown over a lost boot in such a way that the boot resembles a sleeping hare. A pipeline flange is wrapped in what looks like a matted bird’s nest but is actually a colony of alkali-tolerant moss that grows only on warm metal. In one evaporation pond, the water has separated into magenta and turquoise bands, and the boundary between them wavers as if something breathes below. At the far edge, a dead vicuña lies on its side, mummified by heat and salt, its legs extended as if running; a white ridge has begun to build along its ribs, and soon it will be just another polygon in the pan. An old wooden crate, half-dissolved, shows stenciled letters that now read only “NITR—” because the salt has eaten the rest. It could mean nitrate, or niter, or nothing at all.
