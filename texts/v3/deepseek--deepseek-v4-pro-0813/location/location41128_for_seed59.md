Mossgrip, they call it, or sometimes the Weep. It occupies a single high cirque, a scooped bowl in the mountains where the clouds come to rest. The bowl is not large—maybe a quarter-mile across at its widest—but it is packed so densely with vegetation, water, animals, and human construction that it feels like a city folded into a pocket.

From the rim you would not see a town at first. You would see a basin of green fur, a single continuous surface of moss, fern, dwarf tree, and wet leaf, stitched through by a silver thread of falling water. Then the green resolves into roofs, bridges, walls, ladders, ledges, and the movement of hundreds of small bodies going about their business. The sound reaches you before the shapes do: a deep, churning roar of water, overlaid with the shrill chitter of birds, the ring of frogs, the creak of wet fiber, and human voices pitched high to carry.

The rock walls of the cirque are old and rounded, weathered by centuries of frost and seep, but almost none of the stone is visible. It is upholstered. Moss grows in thick mats over every ledge. Ferns unroll from cracks. Dwarf trees, gnarled and slow, lean out from the walls and lace their branches together. Water does not run in a few neat channels here; it weeps from the cliff faces, condenses on leaves, drips from rope, pools in hollows, and seeps upward through the ground when you step. The floor of the cirque is not soil so much as a soaked mattress of roots, peat, and half-rotten wood. Footprints fill with water almost immediately. The air is cool enough that breath shows in the morning, but not freezing; it is the steady coolness of high altitude and permanent cloud. Newcomers feel their lungs working harder on the ladders.

The vegetation is not a backdrop or a resource. It is the substance of the place. There is no bare ground, no clear boundary between forest and settlement. The town has been grown and woven into the living plant mass rather than carved out of it. Dwellings are lashed into the crotches of trees, their walls made of woven willow and root, plastered with living moss. Roofs are mats of sedge and sphagnum that continue to grow, so every roof must be thinned and shaped like a living thing. Walkways are braided ropes and trained branches, slung from trunk to trunk across the wet air. Some houses are not built at all but guided: years of patient bending and tying have turned five or six trees into a single roofed chamber, their trunks grown together into walls, their crowns interlaced overhead.

It is a town, not a camp or a scattering of huts. There are streets—if you can call them that—of rope and root, winding upward through the layers of the cirque. There are terraces stepped along the seep lines, each one a shallow pool planted with watercress, bitter tubers, and cloud-berries. Water is channeled through hollowed alder trunks and split stems, spilling from one terrace to the next. There are workshops, storage baskets hung high in the branches, communal platforms where the market gathers, and a central longhouse grown from a ring of ancient trees. It is a settlement of maybe four hundred people, but because the space is so small and so vertical, it has the density of a city. You could cross the bowl in ten minutes if the bridges were empty. They are never empty.

The technology is simple but highly developed in its own way. Most tools are stone, bone, tooth, and wood. There is a little copper, cold-hammered from bog ore, but it is rare and precious. Rope is the true technology of Mossgrip: rope of bark fiber, vine, animal sinew, and hair; rope that swells in the wet and must be knotted and re-knotted; rope that creaks constantly under the weight of people, animals, water, and growing plants. The people cut with obsidian and copper knives, but the law is explicit: you may cut only where there is already a knot, you may pull only along the grain of living wood, you may dam water only for a day. Water has the right of way. A nest is a room. If a bird chooses a doorway, the doorway must change.

The fauna is not a few birds and frogs. It is overwhelming, a constant moving skin over the whole town. Fork-tailed swifts nest in the undersides of the bridges, their mud cups packed in rows. Small bright frogs live in the roof gutters and cisterns, and at dusk their calling rises to a metallic ringing so loud that conversation becomes a game of lip-reading and raised fists. The air in the afternoons is thick with midges, and the swifts harvest them in screaming arcs. Pika-like creatures the size of fists run along the ropes, stealing dried moss and bits of fish. White-throated birds nest directly in the walls of houses; their fledglings beg from every doorway. There are weasel-shaped animals in the root mat, owls in the cliff holes, and long-tailed mice that swim the water channels as easily as they climb. Every basket, every fold of cloth, every pile of cut sedge must be shaken before use. Children are posted to whistle away the moss-squirrels from the granaries. The people do not so much share the town with animals as live inside an animal city that tolerates them.

The sound never stops. At the bottom is the waterfall, a deep brown roar where the stream enters the cirque at its head and drops down the headwall. Above it is the creak and groan of wood and rope, the constant small sound of wet fiber under tension. Over that is the chitter of birds, a harsh and continuous conversation. The frogs rise like a tide at dusk, fill the whole bowl, and then fall silent only when a hard rain comes. Human sounds float on top: the clack of looms, the knock of wooden mallets, the slap of wet rope, the call-and-response of children and water-wardens. When the wind rises, the entire cirque hisses with wet leaves, as if the mountain is exhaling through a million small mouths.

The light is normal but altered by the place. There is no dim cavern here, no perpetual gloom; the days are a green-gray glow, diffuse and even, as though the sun is behind frosted glass. When the clouds thin, a pale white light makes every wet surface glare, and rainbows form in the spray of the waterfall. At night the darkness is thick, cut only by small fish-oil lamps and moss candles, their points of light trembling in the wet black. The eyes of frogs glitter from every wall.

The place is old in its bones and turbulent in its skin. The cirque itself is settled-form, a landscape that has long ago found its shape. The stream has its bed, the terraces are ancient, the walls are rounded and stable. But the surface is never still. The town is in constant small repair. A lashing unties under the weight of growing moss and must be replaced. A sluice clogs with eggs and leaves and must be cleared. A tree sends a new root through a sleeping platform, and the platform must be moved. The stream shifts its gravel after every storm. A flock of finches arrives and strips a berry crop in an hour. The frogs lay their eggs in the water channels and the people unclog them with bare fingers, carrying the eggs to safe pools as carefully as they would carry their own food.

At midday, often, a squall rolls over the rim. The temperature drops a degree or two. Rain hits hard enough to silence the frogs for a breath. The waterfall doubles, turns the stream brown, and sends a pulse of water through the channels. Children run along the ropeways beating wooden clappers. People appear in doorways to open the sluices, shift the dams, tighten the lashings. A wall of moss shrugs off a sheet of water. Then the squall passes, and the frogs resume louder than before. In the wet afterlight the whole town seems to steam.

Nothing here is supernatural. It is only excess: too much water, too much plant, too many animals, too little space, and a people who have learned to tie themselves into the excess rather than fight it. The dead feed the living; waste goes into the moss pits, which steam in the cool air, and the moss grows thick on the heat. Fire is kept small and rare, in clay jars and stone basins, because to burn the living walls would be to burn the town itself. The people do not own land; they own knots—sections of the woven fabric they are bound to tend. If they leave, the knots unravel.

At night, when the rain stops for an hour and the clouds part, the moon turns the wet ropes to silver. The whole cirque glitters with the eyes of frogs. The waterfall keeps its deep note, the ropes creak in the wind, and somewhere a child is mending a bird-net by lamp-light while a small animal sits on the beam above, watching. The town breathes, drips, and holds. It is a small place, but it is dense enough to contain a world.
