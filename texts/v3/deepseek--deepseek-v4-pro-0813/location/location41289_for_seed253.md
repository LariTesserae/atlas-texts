The first thing is the sound. It comes from everywhere and nowhere at once: a long, low chord that seems to be the darkness itself vibrating, a note like a wooden pipe the size of a mountain being breathed through. It never stops. It never changes pitch. Then, as your senses settle, you realize you are not in air but in cold water, and the note is traveling through it—not sound as you know it but a pressure that shivers teeth and ribs.

The place is a single vertical landscape, a drowned mountain range made almost entirely of vegetable matter. It rises from an abyssal plain at six kilometers depth to a plateau still far below the sun. The entire thing—every slope, wall, tower, plain, and chasm—is built. There is no exposed rock. There is no raw mineral ground. Every surface is plant tissue: living bark, compressed leaf, root braid, peat, translucent frond, lignified wood. The first inhabitants were a clonal colony of black phytoids, tree-like organisms that grow in tubes and plates. Over millions of years they built their own mountain, generation after generation dying and becoming the substrate for the next, until the structure reached three thousand meters of relief and settled into something that no longer grows or erodes.

Down on the low slopes, the terrain is smooth, almost polished. The ground is an ancient mat of compressed stems, gray-brown, planed by the same unvarying current. There are broad channels cut through it, like roads worn into stone, but they are not stone—they are hardened cellulose. The water moves through these channels in a steady, laminar flow, cold, about four degrees, and so clear that you can see the next structure rising half a kilometer away before it dissolves into the black.

As the slope steepens, the plant architecture becomes vertical. The middle walls are a forest of giant stems, each ten meters across, twisted together into cables and pillars, then separating to form arches and overhangs. Between them, hollow galleries run through the mountain like the corridors of a cathedral. Some are natural gaps where the stems failed to fuse; others were burrowed by animals long after the tissue died. The water flows through these galleries too, and each one has its own note. In the largest, the sound is so loud it becomes a kind of silence. The walls are covered with a living veneer—maroon, amber, black—dotted with pores and hair-like filaments.

Higher still, the structure breaks into a city of towers, balconies, and amphitheaters. These are not buildings in the human sense; they are the crowns of individual phytoids, each a hollow, fluted chimney where water is pushed upward by its own buoyancy and expelled through slits in the wood. The cold deep water, the slight warmth of decomposition below, and the pressure of the water column create slow convection cells. The plants have shaped their growth to exploit these cells, building conduits that keep water moving through their tissues. It is this perpetual, perfectly steady circulation that produces the organ-note. The current never changes because the water column is never disturbed: no tides, no storms, no seasonal mixing. The temperature and chemistry are the same now as they were a million years ago.

The place is dark. No sunlight has ever reached this depth. The only light comes from living things. Scattered in the open water are constellations of bioluminescent plankton, blue and green and occasionally pale violet, each a point of light the size of a pin. They drift slowly, but their distribution is patchy: vast stretches of black, then a cloud of sparks. Along the walls, some of the phytoid bark is faintly luminous—not enough to see by, only enough to show the edge of a stem or the rim of an opening. The overall impression is not darkness with light, but light with darkness: the black is the default, the lights are exceptions.

The fauna are abundant but scattered. There are blind fish with long, translucent fins that hang motionless in the galleries, facing into the current, waiting for whatever the water brings. There are eel-like creatures, some no longer than a finger, others as long as a house, that live in the hollow roots of the phytoids and come out only to hunt. There are enormous isopods, slow as stones, grazing on the living bark. There are squid that drift in schools of a hundred, flashing in unison. There are whale-sized filter feeders, their bodies pale and soft, that cruise between the towers with their mouths open, singing in frequencies that overlap the mountain’s chord. There are tripod fish standing on their extended fins in the channels, perfectly still. There are colonial siphonophores draped over the balconies like curtains of glowing thread. The density is low: you can drift for minutes through empty water, hearing only the mountain, and then pass a single animal, huge and slow, that ignores you completely.

The ecology is not based on sunlight. The phytoids are not green; they are not photosynthetic. They feed partly by filtering organic particles from the water—dead plankton, fecal pellets, the dust of organisms from above—but mostly they rely on symbiotic bacteria living in their roots. These bacteria consume methane that rises from the compressed lower layers of the structure, where ancient dead tissue, under the weight of the mountain, slowly breaks down. The phytoids supply the bacteria with oxygen and sulfate; the bacteria supply the phytoids with carbon. It is a closed loop. The animals feed on the phytoids, each other, and the microbial mats that grow on every damp surface. Their waste sinks and becomes part of the structure. Nothing leaves the system except a little heat, and nothing enters except a fine, constant rain of marine snow from above.

The rules of this place are simple. It is old. It is cold. It is always the same. The plants grow no faster than they decay; the animals reproduce just enough to replace their dead. The mountain is not eroding. The water is not warming. The sound is the same note it has always been. There is no day, no season, no weather. If a structure broke, the phytoids would not repair it quickly—but structures do not break here. There are no devices, no tools, no artificial light; everything is flesh and wood and water. The whole landscape is a machine of living matter that has settled into its final form, and it will likely remain exactly as it is until the ocean itself changes or the planet cools.

And yet it is not dead. The surfaces are warm with a slow metabolism, like the skin of a sleeping animal. The water is full of breath, of currents, of voices. The mountain is alive in the way a coral reef is alive, but darker, colder, older, and louder—a place where the line between built and grown, between plant and structure, between sound and silence, has been erased by time.
