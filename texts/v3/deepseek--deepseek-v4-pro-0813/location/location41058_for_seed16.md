The hollow is a small, low, hidden world in the floor of a salt desert. It is no more than a kilometer across, a single contained pocket of hot, dim air beneath a lid of dust and fume. From above it is only a seam in the white plain, a heat shimmer like a banner of dirty amber. The rim is a broken lip of halite and wind-polished gypsum, and the only way down is a spiral of hand-cut steps so old that their edges have softened. The walls drop two hundred meters to a floor of cracked salt polygons and fissures that breathe.

The light below is dim, not from night but from the thick haze of mineral dust and hot gas that hangs over the hollow. The temperature rises as you descend, until the stone itself feels feverish. The air is dry beyond any desert; there is no water here, not in pools, not in the air, not in the soil. This dryness is not a lack but a condition, a rule of the place. Nothing rots; it desiccates, then mineralizes. Metal does not rust; it stays bright or turns to a frosted copper. The walls are fluted and settled, shaped by wind and temperature, old and in balance.

Yet the vegetation is lush. It begins halfway down the wall, a dense band of growth that fills every ledge and crevice. It is not green. It is a forest of dry stalks and plates: jointed stems of sulfur-yellow and bone-white, fans of mica-thin leaves, swollen pods the color of old bronze. The plants grow in thickets, layered like a reef, but they do not drink. Their roots burrow into fumaroles and mineral veins, taking in hot gases and dissolved salts. Their metabolism is a non-aqueous chemistry, a dry photosynthesis that uses heat and sulfur compounds instead of water and light. Their sap is a dry resin that beads on their surfaces like amber. In the dim light, some of them give off a faint heat-glow, a deep orange under the dust. They are lush not because of moisture but because the ground here is rich in the chemistry they need. The air around them smells of sulfur, hot copper, and something sharp like resin.

The city sits on the lower benches of the hollow, built from the same salt rock as the walls, so that it seems to have grown out of the ground. It is a town-sized city of terraced compounds and low domes, colonnaded streets and wide plazas, all carved and stacked by hand. The walls are of dressed salt blocks and baked clay, with simple copper fittings and hinges, and the marks of chisels are still visible. There is no glass; windows are thin sheets of mica or open grilles. The roofs are often gone, replaced by the spread of the vegetation, which climbs through doorways and over parapets. The city is layered: older walls lie under newer floors, and some buildings have been rebuilt on the stumps of earlier ones, so that the lower streets feel half-swallowed. Despite the emptiness, it is undeniably urban: there are boulevards, stepped platforms for markets, shared courtyards, and channels that, instead of water, direct the hot wind and gas from vent to vent.

But the city is empty. Not ruined, not collapsed—empty. There are no animals, no insects, no birds, no people. The density of the place is low, almost hollow. The plazas are vast, the streets too wide, the buildings spaced far apart. You could stand in the central plaza and hear your own breath echo from a wall a hundred meters away. The emptiness is not abandonment; it feels like a condition of the place, as if it was built for a smaller number of presences than it once held.

The sound is the loudest thing. It comes from everywhere at once. The wind funnels through the hollow and over the city, moaning in the colonnades and hissing through the dry leaves. Gas vents, not water, erupt from the floor at irregular intervals, each eruption a tearing roar. The vegetation pops and cracks as it grows, and some of the swollen pods burst with a sharp report, scattering dry spores. Beneath all of it is a deep organ note, the breathing of the earth, as the whole basin inhales through fissures and exhales through the tall chimneys. It is a sound you feel in the chest and in the soles of the feet. The city is built to catch this sound; the colonnades and domes amplify it, and the empty streets act as pipes.

The place is active in cycles. At the hottest part of the day, the updrafts carry plumes of dust and spores high above the rim, and the gas vents play the city like an instrument. In the evening, the cooler air sinks, and the wind changes direction, rattling the dry forest in waves. The plants move slowly, turning their fans toward the heat, and over hours they creak like old trees. New sinkholes open in the salt floor, and old walls settle with a grinding crack. There is no predator and prey, but there is a slow competition: the vegetation encrusts the older structures, and the city sinks one stone at a time into its own foundations.

There is a strangeness to the place, but it is not loud. The main processional way is too wide for human traffic, and it ends in a blank wall of salt that vibrates faintly. The spiral plaza at the city’s center is not decoration; it is a map of the gas vents, and when a new vent opens, a new line of paving is added, though no one is there to add it. Some of the buildings seem to have grown rather than been built, their bricks aligned like plant cells. It is as if the city follows rules slightly different from the outside world, not impossible, only tilted.

The rules here are dry. Water is absent, and its absence is a law. The plants are the only living things, and they are not animals; they are more like slow fire, turning heat and mineral vapor into structure. Their reproduction is a storm: on the hottest days, the pods release clouds of dry spores that drift upward, and the air becomes thick with pale dust. No animal eats them; they feed on the earth’s breath, and when they die they collapse into their own mineral skeletons, which the city’s foundations slowly absorb. The city, too, is a kind of organism, built by hands that are no longer present, maintained by the same dry chemistry. It breathes, it settles, it sings, but it does not live in the way animals live.

To stand in the center of the hollow is to stand inside a single contained world: a low, hot, dim, dry pocket of earth where the vegetation is lush and the city is empty and the sound never stops. It is hidden not because it is small, but because it is folded into the salt plain, a different set of rules held close under a lid of dust and heat.
