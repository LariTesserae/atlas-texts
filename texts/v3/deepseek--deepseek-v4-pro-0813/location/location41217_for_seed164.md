**The Vent Shelf**

The first thing is the noise. It is not one sound but a wide, continuous pressure: the low roar of boiler halls, the rattle of piston rods, the hiss of steam valves, the rhythmic knock of expansion joints, the constant drip of water onto stone and metal. There is no birdsong, no insect hum, no human voice. The sound is all machinery, water, and steam, and it never stops.

Beyond the noise, the land appears. It is called the Vent Shelf, a high plateau lifted recently, so large it makes its own weather. Below it, a sea of cloud stretches to the horizon. Above it, a bright grey overcast seals the sky. The air is cold and thin. At this elevation, breath comes short and water boils at a lower temperature, but the cold keeps the air dense with moisture, always near the dew point. The cold is not arctic; it is the cold of high places, constant and wet, only a few degrees above freezing.

The terrain is black and sharp. Basalt and slate and glassy dikes rise in clean fracture faces. Nothing is rounded; there are no old river valleys, no glacial polish, no talus fans. The uplift happened only a geological moment ago, and erosion has not had time to soften it. But the reason is also the city. The city has intercepted every natural drainage. Streams run in concrete flumes; cliff faces are channeled by gutters; rain and condensation are gathered into canals and sumps before they can cut the rock. The land is young and preserved in its youth, kept wet but unworn.

Water is everywhere. It comes less from rain than from condensation. Steam from the city’s vents rises into the cold air and settles on every surface. The rocks weep. The metal fins of condenser arrays run with water. Leaf tips drip. Puddles stand on the concrete, perfectly still except for the impact of new drops. Canals run in long straight lines across the plateau, full of cold water. The water is clean but empty. No fish, no water beetles, no larvae.

The vegetation is lush but low. Moss is the dominant life form. It grows in thick carpets over basalt and concrete, in many colors: copper, grey, acid green, dark red. Ferns unroll near warm vents. Low shrubs, like dwarf willow or birch, form dense thickets in sheltered places; their leaves are leathery and waxy, shaped to drip. On steam pipes, a spongy, rubbery plant grows directly on the insulation, drinking the leaked heat and condensed water. There are liverworts and hornworts in the cracks. The plants are wind-pollinated or water-dispersed. They reproduce by spores, by runners, by broken fragments that the city’s fans distribute. Nothing has flowers bright enough to attract an animal, because there are no animals to attract.

That is the central fact: no animals. No birds, no insects, no mammals, no humans. The vegetation is not grazed, browsed, or pollinated by anything with muscles. The food web has no animal tier. Plants grow, die, and are decayed by fungi and bacteria. Dead moss accumulates as peat. The only moving things that resemble animals are machines: slow trains, sweeping arms, rotating fans, automatic loaders. The absence is not peaceful. It is structural, as though an entire frequency of the world had been silenced or never existed.

The built environment is urban but sparse. It is a city spread over continental distances. There are apartment blocks with blank windows, but they are empty. There are streets and tram lines, but the trams run empty on schedule. There are factories, powerhouses, rail yards, switching stations, water treatment plants, and pipe racks. The architecture is industrial: brick, concrete, steel, copper, brass, ceramic. Sometimes older brick buildings sink into the moss and newer steel scaffolding rises above them. Between the structures are huge open spaces—fields of moss, empty platforms, abandoned foundations, long corridors of steam.

The technology is heavy industry. No digital screens, no aircraft, no radio chatter. Steam pistons drive pumps; belts connect turbines to generators; pressure gauges shiver; diesel locomotives pull cars of peat and ash along rustless tracks. The machines are not sealed or silent. They hiss and knock and roar. The air smells of wet rock, moss, hot metal, ozone, coal smoke, and boiler chemicals.

The scale of the place is so vast that the city’s own heat and moisture stabilize the weather. Steam from hundreds of thousands of vents rises and condenses into a permanent grey lid. The light is normal but diffuse; no hard shadows, no bright sun, only a steady bright overcast. At night, the cloud glows with the city’s lights, and the darkness is never deep. The plateau has its own cold, its own humidity, its own wind, but all of it is held in a narrow range by the city’s exchange systems.

The place is in a frozen equilibrium. Water level in the canals does not rise or fall. The temperature away from the boilers stays near freezing. The moss grows to a certain thickness and is harvested at exactly the same rate. Steam produced equals water condensed. The machines are replaced before they fail. There are no seasons, no storms, no ecological succession. Time passes as maintenance cycles, not as change. The city is not dead; it is a flywheel spinning at constant speed.

The strangeness grows the longer one looks. The moss grows in geometric lines along pipe seams. The empty apartments are clean, their windows washed by condensation. The trams stop at empty stations, open their doors, wait, close them, and continue. The city performs the acts of a society that is not there. It is an urban body with no inhabitants, a factory with no workers, a garden with no animals. The only living beings are plants, fungi, and bacteria. The rest is metal, stone, steam, and electricity.

Perhaps people built it once, or perhaps the city built itself. The world does not say. What is clear is that the city and the vegetation have become a single system. The plants need the warmth and water of the condensers; the condensers need the insulation and filtration of the moss. The peat feeds the boilers; the boiler steam waters the plants. The loop has been running so long that it has become the landscape’s only metabolism.

At the edge of the plateau, the city stops in a line of railings, warning lights, and drain holes. Below is the cloud sea, a white-grey ocean. The cliffs are sharp and newly broken. Even here, the city has built gutters to carry the condensation away. Water falls in long thin streams into the cloud, and the cloud swallows it without sound.

Behind, the noise continues: the roar and hiss, the drip, the hum. It is a vast, cold, wet, high, empty, industrial, loud, unmoving garden-city without animals, preserved at the moment of its birth.
