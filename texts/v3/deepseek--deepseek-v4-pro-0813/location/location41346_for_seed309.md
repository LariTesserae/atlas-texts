The Cask

The place is called the Cask, and it is a room inside a root. You come to it from the dry thorn-plain above through a sinkhole that narrows into a wet throat, down ladders bolted into living wood. The descent is not far—thirty or forty feet—but it passes through the underside of the world. At the bottom, the ladders end on a wooden dock floating in warm water, and the Cask opens around you.

It is not a cave that contains a root. It is a root that contains a cave. Long ago, water dissolved a hollow in the limestone around a taproot, and the root slowly grew to fill the cavity. Now the walls are all tree—if tree is the word—a vast cylindrical trunk running vertically, its inner surface ribbed and fissured like the inside of a heart. The chamber is cathedral-sized but not huge: maybe forty meters long, fifteen wide, and the root walls rise and close overhead in a tangle of rootlets. There are side pockets, but the Cask is the main space, and it feels bounded, almost intimate, despite the industry.

The floor is mostly water. A shallow sump fills the lower third of the chamber, warm and dark, with a film of pollen and sap. Floating boardwalks and iron pontoons make the streets. The water level is never still: it rises at seep-hour, when rainwater from the surface percolates down through the root, and falls again as the drainage channels take it into deeper stone. At high seep, the lower walkways are wetted; at low seep, a ring of pale wood shows the high-water mark.

The walls are the place. They are plant, all plant. The root’s bark is ridged and sweating, covered in mosses that grow in the dark: pale green, liverwort, mat-forming. Small ferns sprout from the cracks, their fronds almost translucent. Aerial roots hang from above in curtains, some as fine as hair, some as thick as a wrist, each dripping condensation. The root is alive and warm to the touch. In places it has grown over the town’s old iron ladders and railings, swallowing them like slow water. The buildings are set into this living wall: houses of riveted iron, salvaged wood, and brass, built on terraces cut into the bark. They are scattered, not crowded. Much of the chamber is empty air and water; the town clings to the edges, leaving the center to the sump and the mist. There is perhaps room for two hundred people, and they use only half. There is a market ledge near the middle, a communal bath fed by a warm seep, a school niche hung from the north wall, and a shrine to the Choir where the root moans loudest.

The light is dim. The Cask has no daylight. A few caged electric bulbs hang from the gantries, running off a turbine in the sump outflow; they glow a low orange, just enough to see the walkways. Between them, the walls give off a faint blue-green luminescence from sap-nodules and from the eyes of animals. The water reflects scattered gleams. Most of the chamber is in shadow, and the upper root-ribs are hidden in a kind of warm dark that moves. The lights are not steady; when the sump outflow surges, the bulbs brighten for an hour, and the whole room seems to breathe.

The sound is loud and constant. There is no silence here. The main noise is the pump house at the south end: a deep, rhythmic pounding that drives water through the root’s natural pipes. Steam vents hiss from the sap-boilers. Conveyor chains rattle in the upper gallery. Water drips from a thousand rootlets. The root itself creaks and groans as sap pressure changes, sometimes letting out a low, musical moan that the inhabitants call the choir. Over it all is the animal sound: crickets trilling, bats shrieking in the dark ribs, moths fluttering against the lamp cages, the wet grunting of sump-pigs wallowing below the boardwalks. You have to raise your voice to be heard, but people are used to it; they speak in short bursts between the machine cycles.

The fauna is overwhelming. It is not just presence; it is saturation. The Cask is a rookery, a hive, a wallow. Cave crickets cluster in mesh pens and also loose along the walls, their black bodies covering the bark in patches. Pale millipedes move over the moss in slow waves. Moths the size of hands settle on the lamp housings, and their wings scatter the light. Bats roost in the highest root-ribs, thousands of them; their guano falls like fine rain and feeds the root. Blind salamanders live in the sump, and fish with no eyes swim in the warm water. There are also larger animals: sump-pigs, pale gill-necked hogs that wallow in the organic film, and root-crawlers, small sticky-toed lizard creatures that clean the sap residue from the pipes. The animals are not separate from the town; they are the town’s livestock, its garbage disposal, its silk-farm, its protein, its watchmen. They are everywhere enough that the walls seem to move if you watch too long.

The industry is built around them and the root. At the south end, a boiler house burns dried root-scrap and guano briquettes, producing steam. A pump house sends water through the root’s vessels and into the sump. A copper distillation column separates the root’s sap into alcohol, syrup, resin, and a thinner that smells of pears. There is a tannery for the hides of sump-pigs, a machine shop, a ropewalk, and a conveyor that brings raw plant scrap down from the upper galleries. Pipes and cables cross the chamber overhead, wrapped in condensation and fluttering with moths. The whole thing is industrial but not coal-and-iron in the usual sense; it is a biological factory that runs on the root’s slow pressure and the animals’ fast bodies.

The rules of the place are simple and old. No open flame near the sap; the air is rich in volatile alcohols, and a spark can run blue across the mist. Walk only on the marked boards; the root floor is alive and can grow over a boot in a night. Do not kill the root-crawlers; they keep the pipes from clogging. The root is not to be carved without the permission of the rootwrights, who know which vessels can be tapped and which will bleed too much. The animals are not to be silenced; their noise is part of the machine, and the root needs their leavings. The pumps must not stop for more than an hour; if the pressure builds, the walls swell and the doorways change shape.

The place is old. The rock behind the root has been dissolved into smooth flutes, and the iron is rusted to a stable orange. The paths are wear-polished by generations. The town looks less like construction and more like a shell grown around the root, settled into its own state. It is active, always moving, but balanced: the seep-cycle, the sap-tides, the animal migrations, the machine shifts all overlap into a single living rhythm. The Cask is warm, wet, loud, dark, and full of animals, and it has been so for a long time.
