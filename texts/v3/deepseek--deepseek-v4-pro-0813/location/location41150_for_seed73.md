It does not announce itself as a high place. It announces itself as heat first: a saturated, greenhouse heat that presses in from all sides and makes the shade no cooler than the sun. Then the sound arrives—a continuous, many-layered roar that is not one thing but many. Insects, frogs, falling water, the low hum of something mechanical that has outlived its makers. The ground underfoot gives slightly, and water wells up around the edges of your footsteps. Only later does the land’s elevation become clear, when the forest opens at the rim and the world drops away.

At the rim, the escarpment falls for two thousand meters, but not in a clean cliff. It has been weathered into ribs and flutes and green-hung ledges. Far below, clouds collect like a slow silver sea, their currents independent of the sky above. Through gaps in that sea you can see the tops of lower ridges, dark with heat and rain. The drop is extreme, but it is not young. The rock is black basalt in some places and pale tufa in others, and both have been worked over by water for long enough that every edge has softened. Some of the cliffs weep constantly, not from springs but from the air itself condensing on the cool interior stone and oozing through the moss. The heat does not thin with altitude. If anything, it thickens here at the top, as if the high rim traps the warm air the way a pot holds steam.

Turn away from the edge and the plateau opens, but not into bareness. It is open in the way a cathedral is open: there are long sightlines, but every surface has been colonized. The land is a tilted, terraced country of black rock and red soil, broken by steam basins, wet forest, and lines of old stone. It is broad enough to have its own weather, its own rivers, its own interior distances. Yet the density never really eases. There are roots overhead, vines between trunks, moss underfoot, and a constant rain of small debris—flowers, spores, insects, droplets. The place is crowded, layered, near-touching, but it does not feel cramped because the sky is large and low and the next escarpment is far away.

The soil is a thick red-brown laterite, and beneath it lies a layer of clay that holds water near the surface. The ground squelches even on slopes. Streams do not run clear and fast here; they are slow, tea-dark, braided channels that spread into marshes and then gather again into pools. Some of them issue from hot springs. Others begin as cloud drip, falling from leaves so heavily that they form their own small creeks. The rock is visible in places: basalt columns, pale mineral terraces, boulders rounded like old bones. The landscape has had time to settle. The valleys are wide and flat-bottomed, their walls sloped rather than sharp. The cliffs have been fluted by water, but the water is not tearing the land down quickly. It is in balance.

Vegetation covers almost everything. The forest is not the tall, dark rainforest of the lowlands. It is a cloud forest of stunted, wide-crowned trees, tree ferns, and thickets of bamboo-like grass that grow higher than a person. Moss covers the north sides of trunks and the bare rock. Bromeliads and orchids grow in branch crotches, their roots holding pockets of rainwater. The understory leaves are enormous, soft, permanently wet. There are no strong seasons, only a daily rhythm of mist, rain, and brief clearing, so the vegetation layers into a permanent now: new growth pushing through old growth, not as decay but as accumulation. Even the dead wood remains in place for a long time, furred over with life. In the steam glades, where the ground is too hot for roots, the vegetation pulls back and pale mineral terraces step down one into the next, colored orange and white by thermophilic bacteria. Steam rises there in slow columns, and the air smells of hot clay and sulfur and wet leaves.

The marks of built things are scattered, never gathered into a settlement. The first one you notice is not a ruin but a path. It runs along the contour of a slope, a line of fitted stones so even that the forest has had to grow around it rather than over it. The stones are warm to the touch, even in hard rain. They are not carved exactly; they look poured, or grown, and polished by a long use no one remembers. At intervals the path widens into a small platform, and on the platform there is a shelter: a single room, walls of the same stone, a roof that overhangs like a shell. The shelters are mostly empty. A few hold a low bench or a basin that fills from a hidden source. Some hold things that are harder to describe—a patch of wall that reflects the forest upside down, a doorway that is slightly too narrow for a body, a corner where the air tastes like cold metal. These are not ruins in the usual sense. They are still warm. They have simply been left, or perhaps not left, only unattended.

The technology here has gone feral. In the high bogland there are towers, not many, maybe one every few kilometers. They are no taller than a tree, built of a material that seems neither stone nor metal, and they hum at a frequency that feels like the pressure before a storm. They are condensers. You can see their work at dawn. As the first light touches the mist, each tower releases a ring of vapor that widens across the plateau. For a few minutes the whole landscape becomes a system of expanding circles, and the dew that forms on every leaf is so heavy it drips like rain. The towers are not derelict. They are in their working life, whatever that means. They have been running long enough for the roots to recognize them. Some of the paths are longer than they should be. You notice it only over time: a walk that should take a hundred paces takes a hundred and twenty, and the extra twenty steps do not feel like motion, only like being in the same place more thoroughly. The water moves too slowly for the slope. It is not thick, not exactly. It is full of something—filaments too fine to see—that the old paths shed into the soil. Droplets cling to leaves a half-second longer than they should. A stream will pause at a stone before sliding over it, as if remembering the shape.

The animals are inseparable from the sound. The air is thick with small winged things—dragonflies, moths, tiny iridescent beetles. Frogs call from every pool, their voices layered into a wall of trills and clicks and barking notes. Birds move in noisy troops through the canopy, long-tailed and bright blue or black with white shoulders. Their calls cut through the insect drone: long whistles, electric chatters, the low booming of something that sounds like a drum but is probably a bird with a hollow crest. In the understory there are mammals, though you usually hear them rather than see them—a rustle, a high piercing whistle, the sound of a body moving deliberately through the wet leaves. Near the steam pools, the ground is too hot for most animals, but a few have adapted. Pale frogs sit in the hot mud, their skin ridged with the same mineral colors as the pools. Slender lizards run across the surface of the water, not skittering like surface-tension insects but moving with an odd smoothness, as if the water agrees to hold them. Some of the flying things are wrong in small ways: a large beetle that flies backwards, a moth whose wing markings rearrange when it lands, a hummingbird with a beak like a spiral needle. The place is strange, but the strangeness is not alarming. It is the strangeness of a system that has been running long enough to develop quirks.

For all the noise and water and growth, nothing hurries. This is a slow landscape. The water runs slowly, the trees grow slowly, the terraces around the hot springs accumulate so gradually that a single ripple in the mineral surface might record a hundred years. The towers release their rings of mist on a daily schedule, but if you stay long enough you begin to suspect there is a second schedule beneath it, a pulse that takes decades. The whole place is in equilibrium. Uplift still happens below, but so slowly that erosion keeps pace. The heat from below and the rain from above balance each other. The forest does not spread or retreat; it thickens. The only rapid cycles are diurnal: mist at dawn, rain at midafternoon, frog song at dusk. The light at noon is ordinary and clear, the kind of gray-white tropical light that makes every color true. After rain, the clouds pull apart and the plateau stretches out for many kilometers—green on green, black stone, silver water, and the distant vapor towers standing like quiet exhalations.

At night the temperature barely drops. The heat stays in the wet ground and leaks into the air. Fireflies or their equivalents rise from the bromeliads in dense spirals. The frogs continue. The towers glow faintly, not with electric light but with something older, like the moon seen through water. The whole place seems not built and not grown, but something in between: a long-running system where stone, water, plant, animal, and machine have dissolved into one another. It is a high, hot, wet country, loud and almost too alive, and it gives the impression of having been that way for a very long time, and of planning to remain exactly so.
