You come to it from the west, across a plain of bare, pale earth and scattered stones, where the ground is cracked with dryness and the only shadows are the faint ones cast by low, wind-carved rock. The sky is high and hazy, a warm, milky white that seems to diffuse the sunlight rather than let it fall. The day is warm, but not hot; the air is still, and the light is silvered by a low mist that hangs over the ground even in the middle of the day. You almost miss the hollow until you are upon it. The rim rises only a meter or two, a gentle lip of weathered rock curving around a depression like the edge of a shallow dish. Only when you climb the last few steps and look down do you understand that the place is there.

The hollow is small. Perhaps thirty paces across, no more, and oval in shape, as if it had been cupped out of the earth by a hand that then grew tired and left it unfinished. Its walls are low and rounded, worn into smooth shoulders and benches of sedimentary rock that descend in irregular steps to a floor of stone and water. The rock is pale gray, nearly white in places, and it is laid down in thin horizontal seams that the weather has etched into fine parallel lines, like the pages of a book left too long in the damp. In places the strata have separated, forming shallow overhangs and narrow ledges, and under one of these overhangs the floor is darker, shielded, as if the hollow keeps a shadow of its own. The whole depression has the look of a place that has been worn down over a very long time, not by violence but by the patient chemistry of water and air. Its edges are blunt, its corners rounded, its surfaces smooth. There is nothing sharp in the entire hollow.

As you descend the worn steps of rock, the air changes. It grows warmer, heavier, and damp, and it carries a faint mineral tang—a taste of salt and a suggestion of sulfur, like a hot spring but muted, as if the water had long ago given up its fierceness and now only remembered heat. But the warmth is real. The rock under your hands is warm, not unpleasantly, and the air is the temperature of skin. A thin steam rises from the floor, not in billows but as a continuous, barely visible exhalation that gathers in the hollow and drifts slowly, too slowly to be called mist, refracting the light into a diffuse, directionless glow. The sky above is pale and featureless, a bright haze without a sun, so that shadows are soft and the whole space seems to be lit from within, like a room behind paper walls.

The floor of the hollow is not a single surface but a mosaic of stone and water. The stone is pale gray, coated in places with a white crust that crunches softly underfoot, like frost or old salt. The crust is thickest near the water, where it forms raised rims and scalloped edges—tiny terraces, no larger than a hand, that look as though they were poured layer by layer from the same source that feeds the pools. The terraces are covered with a delicate, irregular pattern of ridges and hollows, like miniature mountain ranges, and they catch the dim light in a thousand tiny glints. In some places, the crust has grown over the edge of a pool, forming a thin, brittle shelf that overhangs the water, and from these shelves hang tiny stalactites, no longer than a fingernail, that drip in slow, perfect time.

The pools themselves are small. The largest is no wider than a bathtub, the smallest no bigger than a cupped hand, and each is a distinct basin, separated from its neighbors by low ridges of mineral crust. The water in them is so still that at first you mistake it for a sheet of dark glass, and only when you kneel and look closely do you see the faint shimmer of light on the surface. The water is clear at the edges, but the bottoms are lined with a pale, flocculent sediment that stirs if you move your hand through it, clouding the water like smoke, then settles back into the same smooth, pale floor. The water is warm—warmer than the air, warmer than skin—and it leaves a faint metallic taste on the tongue, a salinity that dries the mouth. It is not water you would drink, but it is water that has been here, in this same place, for longer than the rocks around it have been smooth.

You can see the seep now, the source of it all. It is not a spring in the usual sense; there is no bubbling, no current, no clear point of origin. Instead, water emerges from the base of the back wall as a general dampness, a glistening on the rock that beads and gathers, then runs in thin, silent runnels down to the pools. The seep is continuous and unchanging, as if the rock itself were sweating, and the water it releases is the same temperature year-round, the same salinity, the same mineral balance. It does not flow, exactly; it seeps and collects, the way condensation collects on a cold surface, except that here the surface is warm and the water comes from within. The dampness extends a short way up the wall, darkening the rock in a broad, irregular stain that has the shape of a map, or a lung, or a cloud—something that seems to breathe.

The vegetation in the hollow is sparse, but not absent, and it has a quality of great age. A few ferns grow from cracks in the rock, their fronds dark and leathery, almost black in the dimness. They are ancient-looking things, each one solitary, with stiff, arching leaves that droop at the tips, the way old things droop. They do not form a groundcover, and between one fern and the next there are meters of bare stone, wide stretches of emptiness where nothing grows but the pale crust. The ferns are not lush; they are survivors, each one holding its place with roots that have found a thin film of water in the rock. Higher up, on the ledges where the steam does not reach, pale lichens spread in slow, perfect circles—gray-green and ochre, their edges crisp, their centers cracked like old paint. They are the only other plants, except for a single stunted tree that grows from a crack high on the north wall. It is a juniper, or perhaps a tamarisk, a twisted, half-dead thing with silvered, splitting bark and a few living branches reaching outward as if searching for light. It has the look of a tree that has been growing here for centuries, not growing taller but simply persisting, its roots reaching down through the rock to the seep below. Its needles are dusty and sparse, and in the dimness they look gray, not green, as if the tree has begun to take on the color of the stone around it.

There are traces of people, but they are faint, and they have been almost absorbed by the place. Near the largest pool, three flat stones lie in a shallow arc, their surfaces worn smooth. They are the same color as the native rock, but their edges are too regular, their spacing too deliberate, to be accidental. They might once have been a low wall, or a bench, or a place to set offerings, but now they are half-buried in the mineral crust, their outlines blurred, as if they had been sketched and then erased. A fourth stone stands upright behind them, broken at the top, its base encased in a thick collar of travertine. On its face, a pattern of lines has been incised—a spiral, perhaps, or a series of concentric circles, or a wave—now worn almost beyond recognition, filled with the same white mineral that coats the pool rims. It is visible only when the light shifts, a ghost of a mark, and then it disappears again. There are no other structures: no walls, no foundations, no paths, no shards of pottery. Whatever people came here did not build; they only marked, or placed, and then they left. The stones have been taken up into the same slow processes that shape everything else, so that they seem less like artifacts than like a kind of natural formation that happens to be arranged.

The silence in the hollow is not an absence of sound but a presence, a substance as real as the steam and the stone. It is a thick, warm silence, saturated with moisture, that seems to press against the ears. The only sounds are the water and the air: a faint, continuous seeping, so soft that it is felt more than heard, a kind of atmospheric pressure at the edge of hearing, and, every few seconds, the clear, musical note of a drop falling from an overhang into one of the pools. The drop is so regular, so perfectly spaced, that it becomes a kind of clock, the only marker of time in a place where time seems not to pass. There is no wind, no birdsong, no insect hum, no rustle of leaves. Even the steam does not swirl; it hangs in place, as if exhausted. If you stand still, you can hear your own breathing too loudly, and the sound of your own heartbeat echoes in your ears, and you become aware of the silence as a listener, waiting for a sound that never comes.

The rules of this place are simple, and they have been in effect for a very long time. Water rises from deep underground, warmed by the earth's own heat, and seeps into the hollow through the porous rock. It carries dissolved minerals—carbonates, sulfates, salts—and as it reaches the surface, it evaporates, leaving those minerals behind. The evaporation is slow, almost imperceptible, but it has been happening for millennia, building the crusts and rims, the terraces and ledges, in layers so thin that a century adds only a few millimeters. The temperature of the water never changes; the temperature of the air never changes; the humidity never changes. The light is always dim, filtered through the steam and the pale sky, and direct sunlight never reaches the floor. The plants grow slowly, at the very edge of what the light and minerals will allow; a single frond may take years to unfurl, and the lichens spread by fractions of a millimeter per year. No animals live here. The water is too saline, the air carries too much carbon dioxide, and the energy available is too meager for anything that moves. So there are no insects, no birds, no amphibians, no mammals, not even the smallest mite or springtail. The only life is plant, and the slow, colorless slime of microbes that coat the pool edges in a slippery film—not animals, not yet, but the first, faint stirrings of a world that has not finished being born.

The place is stable, in equilibrium. It has been this way for so long that it has forgotten how to change. The water level never rises or falls; the pools never dry, never overflow. The mineral deposits grow, but so slowly that the eye cannot detect it; the rims look the same today as they would have a thousand years ago. The ferns do not spread; the juniper does not grow taller; the lichens do not cover the rock. Even the built stones do not shift; they have been fixed in place by the mineral crust, sealed into position like bones in amber. The hollow is a closed system, a small world that has reached the end of its evolution, and now simply persists, warm and dim and damp, in perfect stillness.

It is not a place of dramatic beauty. It is too quiet, too gray, too sparse for that. But it has a quality that is rare in the world: it is exactly itself, and it has been exactly itself for longer than any living thing can remember. Nothing disturbs it. Nothing changes. And in that stillness, there is a kind of completeness—a sense that this small depression, with its seeping water and its solitary ferns and its ghost of a carving, is not waiting for anything, not becoming anything, but simply being, the way a stone is a stone, or a pool is a pool, or a silence is a silence.
