The first thing is the dryness. It is not merely absence of water; it is an active dryness, a dielectric hush in the air. At this elevation the sky is deep-toned even in full daylight, because there is no vapor to scatter the light. The sun comes down hard and white, and shadows lie on the stone like cut metal. Warmth rises not from the air but from the rock itself, as if the plateau is still cooling from the inside. You breathe and your breath does not fog. There is no dew, no cloud, no dampness. The air smells of hot stone, ozone, and a faint metallic bitterness like freshly machined parts.

The ground is high and newly made. Nothing here has been smoothed by rain or wind; there are no rounded boulders, no sand, no soil. Every surface is a fresh fracture. The rock is banded in layers of hematite, bornite, black silica, graphite, and copper so bright it looks like exposed wiring. The plateau opens outward in terraces and scarps and broad pavements that run for miles. Between the landmarks there is an enormous emptiness: acres of bare stone with almost nothing on them, only heat-shimmer and the receding edge of distance. The world is open, regional, but sparsely filled, as if the land has not yet decided where its features belong.

Then you reach the vegetation.

It is not soft, and it is not green. It is lush in the way a fractal is lush. The growths stand in groves and thickets scattered across the stone, each one a dense bloom of metallic branches, crystalline needles, looped glassy fibers, and plates that fan like leaves made of tarnished bronze. A single frond divides into ten, each into ten more, until the eye cannot follow. They are copper, iron-black, silver, violet, sulfur-yellow. They do not flower or fruit. They grow from the rock as though drawn out of it by an electric charge, and at their tips the dry air carries a faint corona glow, too faint to hear. Metal atoms plate onto them grain by grain. Up close, the groves are full of detail; between them, the plateau is almost void.

The place is a factory without builders. Fissures exhale hot gas that smells of sulfur and chlorine. Where the gas meets the high air, metals condense into fine dust and fall in layers, sorted by weight and charge. Terraces act as natural assembly lines: on one, iron nodules sit in neat rows as if counted; on another, sulfur crystallizes into pale yellow bars. There are no walls, no tools, no paths, no constructed structures. Yet the landscape is industrial in every meaningful sense—an electrochemical refinery running at perfect equilibrium. It is all geology, but geology behaving like a mechanized plant with no moving parts.

Nothing animal moves here. There are no birds, no insects, no tracks, no bones. The only sound is near-silence: a faint hiss of corona discharge at the edge of hearing, or the occasional click of a cooling rock so rare you might wait days for it. The air is so thin and still that sound does not carry; a shout would fall away into the stone.

Time is suspicious. The process is stable to an uncanny degree. The growths lengthen, but their bases dissolve back into the substrate at the same rate. The vents blow, but the metallic fog condenses exactly where it did before. The light does not shift. There are no seasons, no dust storms, no erosional wear. The place is caught in the middle of its own creation, so perfectly balanced that it appears both unfinished and eternal.

Stand there long enough and it becomes clear: this is not a desert with strange plants. It is a vast, open-air engine—silent, dry, warm, and empty—whose only product is more of itself, held at the exact edge of being.
