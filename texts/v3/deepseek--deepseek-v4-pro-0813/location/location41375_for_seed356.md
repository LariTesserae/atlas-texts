At first you think you have entered a cave, but the ground is soft and alive. The darkness is not empty; it is full of surfaces you must feel before you see them. Warm air, saturated, moves only when you move it. It tastes of iron and hot vegetation, a little sweet, a little caustic, like standing at the exhaust of a factory that has been running clean for a thousand years.

Then your eyes find the forms.

They rise all around you: not trunks and branches, but cylinders, columns, flanges, ribs. The first thing you take for a smokestack is a hollow stem as wide as a house. The first thing you take for a gantry is a horizontal vine, thick as a road, grown between two uprights. The first thing you take for a pressure vessel is a huge, translucent pod, faintly lit from within by a deep violet-blue. None of it is built. All of it is growing.

The ground is not soil. It is a continuous mat of roots, tubers, air bladders, and small overlapping leaves that lie like scales. When you press it, warm water oozes up between the leaves. Where the mat is torn, you see the native rock below: black, glassy, sharp-edged, as if it cooled yesterday. There are veins of quartz and crusts of sulfur around the cracks. The water seeping up is hot and smells of minerals. The land is low—so low that the water table seems to be just beneath the surface—and very young. The stone has had no time to round, no time to soften. It has been covered, not worn. Every edge is still a knife.

The plants have not simply covered the rock. They have taken it into their circulation. Roots descend into every fissure not to split the stone but to tap it, like pipes driven into a mine. Some roots are hollow, their inner walls lined with a dark, rubbery tissue. Warm mineral water is drawn up through them by a slow, continuous suction. You can hear it sometimes: a faint gurgle, a sigh, the tick of a valve opening somewhere in the dark.

The stems are the most industrial thing. Many are vertical, divided into segments by raised rings, like bamboo made of black metal. But they are not metal. They are lignified plant tissue, dense and wet inside. At intervals, side-branches bear structures that look like pistons: long, polished rods that move in and out of sheaths with a slow, regular stroke. The motion is not fast. You might watch one for a full minute and see it advance only by the width of a hand. But the place is full of them, thousands per acre, all moving at the same beat, in the same direction, as if the entire continent is one engine idling.

Above the piston-stems are the condenser vanes: huge, lobed leaves arranged in stacks, each leaf ribbed and channeled. They are not oriented toward any light—there is no light to orient to—but are held open to the warm, wet air. Water condenses on them in sheets, runs down the channels, and collects in cup-shaped growths at their bases. From there, smaller stems carry the water to other places. Some water is released as steam through the chimneys: tall, hollow reeds with a vent at the top that emits a steady, warm column of vapor. The steam rises only a short way before the moist air holds it, and it returns as a fine drizzle. The whole place waters itself.

Between the stems run cable vines. They are sheathed in a waxy, translucent coating, and inside you can see bundles of conductive fibers. Every so often, a faint spark of blue light travels along one—not a flash, but a slow pulse, like a charge moving through a nerve. It is one of the few lights in the world. The other light comes from the battery bladders: round, pendulous growths, each one filled with layered membranes and ion-rich fluid. They glow deep red or violet, the color of oxygen-starved fire. The light does not carry far. It makes the darkness more architectural, picking out the underside of leaves, the rim of a piston-sheath, the edge of a root-pipe.

There are no animals. Not one. No insects, no birds, no worms, no hidden things with eyes. The movement you see is all plant and water and gas. The only sounds are the ones the processes make: the low thrum of the pumps, the hiss of steam, the drip of condensate, the occasional electric crackle. It is almost silent, but not empty. After a while the regular sounds settle into a kind of humming stillness. You realize the place is not quiet because it is not doing anything; it is quiet because it is doing exactly one thing, forever, without variation.

That is the strangest rule of this place: it does not change. There is no breeze, no season, no growth spurt, no decay. The pistons move, but always the same distance, always the same rhythm. The steam rises, condenses, returns. The water percolates through the root-mat, is drawn up through the hollow roots, is pumped through the stems, is condensed on the vanes, and runs back down. The minerals enter the roots and leave as new tissue, but old tissue is reabsorbed so completely that nothing falls. Nothing rots. The ground is not littered with dead leaves. There is no litter at all.

The place is an industrial process in a living body. It runs on heat from the young, thin crust below and on the chemical difference between the warm brine rising up and the water in the air. The plants stack membranes like cells in a battery of impossible size. They separate acids from bases, store the electrical potential, and use it to pump water, fix minerals, and grow new structures. The waste heat is vented through the chimneys. The waste gas is reabsorbed by the mat. Every output is an input. The system is closed. It should run down, but it does not. It has reached an equilibrium that is also an engine.

This is why there are no animals. There is no loose energy for a mouth to steal. The entire metabolism of the place is already accounted for, every joule already circulating inside the plant-machine. An animal would be like a gear dropped into an engine: unnecessary, and in the way.

The terrain under the vegetation is young and sharp. Black basalt blades break through the mat here and there, crusted with white and yellow minerals. The water does not erode them; it deposits minerals on them. The roots bind the stone. The sharp edges are preserved, even reinforced. In places, the rock is still smooth and conchoidal, as if it cracked open only a few centuries ago and the vegetation rushed in before the cracks had cooled. The low elevation means there are no hills, only a vast, almost flat plain interrupted by fresh fault scarps and low spines of glassy rock. Some of these spines are hundreds of feet high, but still knife-edged, still black, still young.

The scale is the hardest thing to hold in your mind. The same forms repeat in every direction, not because they were planted, but because this is the only way the chemistry works here. The pistons, the cables, the chimneys, the vanes go on for hundreds of miles. The steam from the chimneys forms a permanent ceiling, a low, warm fog that touches the tops of the tallest condensers. The ceiling is not uniform: there are columns where the steam rises, and clear zones where the air sinks, so the atmosphere is divided into cells. It is a weather system, but a stationary one. The rain falls in the same places, the mist flows outward in the same channels, the heat columns stand in the same spots day after day. The place makes its own climate, and the climate has stopped moving.

If you climb one of the basalt spines and look out, you see the dark, breathing continent laid out like a circuit board under a low, glowing fog. There are no lights of cities, no roads, no fields. But you could almost believe there are. The pattern is too regular, too filled with purpose. You see rows of chimney reeds venting steam in unison. You see cable vines strung in straight lines between uprights. You see glowing battery bladders arranged in grids, pulsing slowly. The whole scene looks like an industrial district seen from a distance, if the factories had grown from seed and the smokestacks were alive.

There are no artifacts. No nails, no cut timber, no scraps of wire. There are no walls, no roads, no foundations. The only architecture is living architecture. If you break a stem, it does not snap like dead wood; it tears with a wet, fibrous sound and weeps a warm, slightly yellow sap. The water in the sap is the same water as the pools. The mineral crust on the stem is not paint; it is a living exudate. The electrical charge in the cable vines is not carried by copper; it is carried by chains of organic molecules aligned in the tissue. This is not technology in the sense of tools. It is technology in the sense of process—a process that has learned to shape matter without any mind to shape it.

The darkness is not absolute. It is the darkness of a control room at night, where the only light comes from indicators. The red glow of the bladders, the violet pulse of the cables, the occasional blue spark—they do not illuminate the world; they only make the darkness visible. The air is so wet that the light seems to hang in it, as if the darkness itself could condense on your skin. Your own breath is the only irregular thing. Your own footsteps are the only sound that does not repeat.

The sound, when you stop moving, is a low, almost subsonic hum. It is not exactly a hum; it is more like the memory of a hum, felt in the soles of the feet and the roots of the teeth. It is the sum of thousands of pistons moving at just below the threshold of hearing, of water moving through thousands of miles of root-pipes, of gas pushing through valves. There is also a very faint hiss, like steam escaping from a gasket, and a tick, tick, tick, as regular as a clock. The drip does not come from a leaf. It comes from a condenser vane, and it always falls at the same interval. The sound is so consistent that after a while it becomes a kind of silence. The only true silence would be if the machines stopped, and they never stop.

The rules of this world are simple to see but difficult to accept. There is no decay. There is no solar cycle. There is no food chain. There is no competition, because the entire vegetation is one interconnected system—perhaps a single organism, perhaps many but so thoroughly fused that the difference does not matter. The plants do not grow toward light; they grow toward gradients—of heat, of salt, of pressure, of charge. They are not individuals; they are organs. The pistons are not separate plants; they are extensions of one root-mat that covers the plain. The battery bladders are not fruit; they are storage organs. The chimneys are not trunks; they are exhaust ports. The whole land is a single, continental body—a plant that has become an engine, or an engine that has become a plant.

Every few minutes, a cable vine releases its accumulated charge, not in a lightning flash, but as a slow, silent glow that travels along the cable and sinks into a battery bladder. The charge is not wasted. It is used to power the next pump stroke. The whole system is a loop. There is no beginning and no end. Time here is not a line; it is a circle. The world does not change because it has no need to change. It has solved all the problems that other ecosystems solve by growth and death. It has become a single, stable, industrial metabolism, half-lit, half-audible, breathing in steam.

You stand in the dark, warm and wet, and you realize you are the only thing in this place that is not part of the machine. The machine does not care. It hisses and ticks and glows. The roots under your feet are warm. The air smells of hot metal and wet stone and something sweet. Far off, a chimney reed vents a column of steam that rises and fades into the low fog. It looks like a factory in the distance. But there is no factory. There is only the landscape, running.
