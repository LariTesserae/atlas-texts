At first the dark seems close and warm, almost indoor, with a faint mineral odor. Then the dark opens. It is not a room but a high, broken plateau, and the warmth does not come from the air so much as from the stone underfoot and the air rising from cracks. There is no sky, only a lid of dust or fume so thick that no star shows. The plateau glows by itself, sparsely: a rift shows orange heat; a newly split slab of what might be quartz or gypsum gives off a green-white phosphorescence; some surfaces shine with the dull red of rock still cooling. This is the only light. It moves as the ground moves.

The place is not flat. It is an extreme high country, a tableland lifted far above something unseen. Its edges are vertical, dropping into blackness. Within it, there are blade-edged ridges, glassy flows, upright plates, sinkholes, isolated spires, and terraces of salt that rise in steps. The materials are raw and waterless: black basalt, reddish scoria, anhydrite, gypsum, crystalline salts, sulfur, obsidian. There is no soil, no water, no ice. The dryness is not simple absence; it is a chemical condition. The rocks are anhydrous, sharp, unweathered. No river has ever smoothed them; no rain has dulled their edges. The only rounding comes from heat, not erosion, and even that is rare. Debris lies where it fell, angular and new. The place is being built faster than it can be worn down.

It is warm, almost hot in places. The high elevation should mean cold, but the heat comes from inside the world and gathers under the dark atmosphere like a fever. Some slabs are warm as skin, some too hot to touch. Heat shimmers invisibly in the still air except when it passes over a glowing fissure and makes the light tremble. The air smells of sulfur, ozone, hot metal oxides, and powdered rock. It is so dry that the mouth aches. Salt crusts bloom from any patch of exposed skin. The only liquids are not water: molten sulfur burning with a low blue flame in a few vents, and a slow red glow in fresh cracks where the interior of the world is briefly exposed.

Movement here is constant but uneven. For long minutes the silence is nearly perfect, except for the tick of cooling stone, a faint hiss of gas, a deep creak as if the plateau is adjusting its weight. Then something gives. A fissure opens with a ripping sigh, exhaling warm air and a smell like struck flint. A pinnacle leans, pauses, and falls; the sound comes late, low and physical, more through the ground than the air. Dust rises in black sheets and hangs. The floor of a salt pan swells into blisters, then bursts into fans of white needles. A ridge may rise a hand’s breadth in an hour, or a slab may drop whole into a new crack. There are no animals, no cries. The only turbulence is mineral and thermal: rockfall, uplift, gas, dust, crystal growth, collapse.

Across this open region, presences are sparse. You can travel for long stretches over bare heaving stone and see nothing but the land itself. Then, scattered, there are signs of structure. A ring of basalt columns has been fitted together without mortar on a promontory, each column chosen for its shape. A low wall crosses a saddle between two spires, then ends at a fresh fault; its stones spill into the crack. A stairway cut into a warm scoria dome goes down five steps, then is buried by new growth of salt. In a shallow basin, a floor of mica tiles reflects the glow of the surrounding fissures, though no tool marks show on their surfaces. There is no wood, no metal, no cord, no paint. The structures are stone on stone, fitted with an exactness that seems less like craft than like a thought imposed on mineral. They are not ruins exactly; they are interrupted. They lack the erosion of age, and they lack inhabitants. Some are so new-looking they might have been finished moments ago; others are half-swallowed by crystal.

Faint marks appear on some rock faces: long parallel grooves that turn at right angles, spirals that stop, rows of shallow pits. They might be weathering, but the pattern is too regular. In one place, a basalt wall has a doorway-shaped opening too smooth to be a natural fracture; no chisel marks, no rubble. It opens onto nothing but more dark plateau.

The weirdness of the place is not flamboyant. It is in the way the dark seems to have a texture, the way some sounds arrive too late or too early, the way some stones are body-warm with no source, the way the horizon occasionally appears to shift. Distance is unreliable. A feature that looked close is a kilometer away; a distant spire seems to stand just at your shoulder. Sometimes, when you pass a standing slab, its shadow points the wrong way—toward the only light, or toward no light at all.

The rules of this world are not hidden. The land is still being made. It has not settled. Erosion has not begun its slow smoothing; the ground is raw, embryonic, and violent. What vegetation or animal life would do elsewhere is done here by mineral growth and fracture. The only ecology is a cycle of uplift, crystallization, collapse, and re-formation. The quiet is the quiet of enormous forces held in a temporary balance. The scattered structures suggest that something—someone—began to build, or is still building, or will build, but not now. The dark is warm and open, and the high plateau extends beyond the reach of the faint glowing seams, into a blackness that is not empty but unfinished.
