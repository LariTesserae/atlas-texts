Before you see it, you hear it. It is not a single sound but a stacked, continuous roar—water over stone, metal under strain, the chitter of millions of small animals, the low hum of something that might be machinery or might be the earth itself. The sound arrives before the horizon does, and by the time the city becomes visible you are already inside its acoustic weather.

At first it looks like a reef, because nothing built should be this large. It stretches to the edge of the world, a low, dark, complicated country of terraces and spires and causeways, half sunk in a warm shallow sea. There is no clear boundary between land and water. The streets are canals half the time; the canals become streets when the tide or the city's own systems decide. The ground is only a few meters above the water at its highest, and in many places it is not ground at all but a lattice of stone, metal, and living shell held just above the waterline.

The wetness is not a feature; it is the medium. Water moves everywhere. It sheets off platforms, drips from overhead pipes, wells up from seams in the pavement, condenses on every surface, and runs in thin, bright threads down the faces of walls. The air is warm and dense, heavy with moisture and the smell of wet stone, hot metal, ozone, and animal musk. Sometimes it rains from a clear patch of sky; sometimes a geyser opens in a street and turns a plaza into a steaming pool. The city does not resist water. It is built the way a river delta is built, by deposition and redeposition.

There are no plants. The eye keeps searching for green and finds none. Instead, the upright world is mineral and constructed. There are spires of laminated sinter, fans of pale crystal, terraces of salt and silica that look geological but follow the lines of walls and conduits. Some structures are clearly engineered—black alloy struts, plates of self-healing glass, stairways that fold into ramps—but everything is crusted with the same mineral scale, so old and new share a single weathered skin. Erosion has been at work here so long that it has become a style: edges are rounded, surfaces are furred, and every wall has a lower band of salt and stone like a tideline.

Then the animals resolve out of the wet air, and you understand that the city is not occupied but saturated. In the warm shallows, pale segmented swimmers coil and uncoil between drowned foundations. On every vertical surface, shell-backed scrapers the size of plates graze the mineral film, moving their mouths in slow circles. Long-legged waders with too many joints pick along the submerged ledges, dipping clusters of feelers into the water. Overhead, clouds of dark flying things rise and settle in waves, their calls a sharp metallic clatter. Larger grazers, humpbacked and slow, move in herds across the wide terraces. Some animals are not entirely animal: one carries a carapace studded with sensor-like nodules; another breathes through vanes that might be gill or might be machined. They are all ordinary in the way a horseshoe crab is ordinary—old, improbable, but not impossible.

The built environment is not something that was finished and then inhabited. The city is still being made, and unmade, and remade. A crack in a wall closes while you watch, filling with a pale slurry that hardens into laminated stone. Water flows uphill along the underside of a bridge, gathers into a trembling sphere, and rolls itself into a cistern. A street rearranges itself from roadway to staircase to spillway without any visible machinery. The city is a machine made of weather and mineral and living tissue, and it is too large to be called a machine. It makes its own weather. Heat rises from it in shimmering columns; moisture gathers over the densest districts and falls as local rain. It might be raining on one side of a canal and steaming on the other.

The sound is constant and deafening. There is the water: slap of tide, hiss of steam, rain on metal and stone. There are the animals: millions of small calls, scraping mouths, splashing bodies, wing-clatter. There is the city itself: the groan of stone, the grind of metal, the low continuous hum of whatever force moves matter and water and heat. You feel it through the soles of your feet and in your chest. You could shout and lose the words less than a meter from your mouth.

And the place is turbulent. A canal that was full a moment ago empties, its water released through sluices you never saw. A tower on the far edge of sight folds downward without collapsing, disassembling into wet sand that already rises again as a new wall. A geyser erupts through a pavement seam, and a herd of grazers shifts around it without breaking rhythm. The animals do not seem alarmed by the changes; they are the changes, or part of them. The city is not a container for their life but an extension of it, built and rebuilt by their movements, their excretions, their deaths, their machines.

The rules are simple, once you learn to see them. Nothing stays dry, so everything must be able to be wet. Nothing grows green, so everything alive must eat mineral or eat something that eats mineral or eat the city's synthesized food. No space stays empty, so every gap is a niche, a passage, a den, a market, a machine. Nothing stays finished, so nothing is built to last; it is built to be replaced, and the replacement is already being made.

After a while, the city has no outside. The water that leaves it returns as rain. The material that erodes from one district is deposited as stone in another. The sound is the sound of a place with no edges, packed to the point of pressure, warm and wet and moving. It is not beautiful exactly, but it is alive in a way that makes the word feel small.
