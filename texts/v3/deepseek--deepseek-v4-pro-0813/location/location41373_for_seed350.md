# The Churn

The first thing is the sound. It does not come from any single direction. It is a continuous, full-body pressure: the drone of turbines buried underwater, the shriek of steam through cracked flanges, the thud of loose iron, and behind it all a granular roar like a landslide that never stops. The rain is not falling. It is being wrung out of the air. You move through a warm fog that is half water, half breath, and you cannot tell where the sea ends and the sky begins.

The valley is a recent wound. The basalt ridges on either side are black and glassy, their edges unweathered, so sharp they look poured last night. No beach, no rounded stone. The floor is a tangle of raw lava tubes, sinkholes, and a broad drowned channel where the water deepens. The land is low, only a few meters above the sea, and much of it isn’t land at all—just a shelf of silt and slag held together by roots. Erosion hasn’t had time to soften anything. But the plants have. They cover the sharp rock like bandages.

The vegetation is not like a forest; it is like being inside a body. Thick braids of ribbon weed hang in the water, each blade longer than a person and moving with a slow, muscular pulse. Black kelp rises in columns, their tops opening into gas bladders that look like sooty lanterns. There are ferns with fronds the size of doors, uncurling and folding in the warm current. A pale yellow grass grows on every flat surface, even on the steel. It does not need sunlight. The light here is dim, a green-brown murk like the inside of a bottle of river water, so the plants have learned to eat heat and sulfur. Their leaves are copper-black, sulfur-yellow, bruise-purple. They turn toward the gas flares the way sunflowers turn toward a sun that no longer exists.

The gas flares are the brightest thing. Every few hundred meters, a pipe rises from the water and pours a column of black smoke and steam that flames a dull orange at the top. The flames do not cast shadows; the shadows go the wrong way. Around each flare, the water is boiling, but the plants grow thicker there, drinking the heat.

Scattered through the basin are the machines. Not ruins, exactly—many still run. A row of pump-heads stands on long legs in the shallows, their turbines spinning in the current, each one wrapped in vines. Their cables run down into the murk and disappear. There is a transformer station on a grating of iron, humming at a frequency you feel in your teeth. Its porcelain insulators are cracked, but sparks still jump between them. A narrow-gauge rail line comes down from the ridge and vanishes under the water; the rails are bright, not rusted, and pale shoots are growing between the sleepers. An old gantry crane leans over the channel, its hook still raised, motionless except for the vines that swing from it. The control rooms are half-flooded, their gauges still lit, their needles flicking to numbers no one is reading.

Nothing watches. There are no fish, no crabs, no birds, no insects. The water is full of shapes that look like fish from the corner of your eye, but they are leaves, bubbles, or shadows cast by nothing. Nothing swims except the debris. The absence of animals is so complete that when a frond brushes your leg, you flinch; then you remember there is nothing here that hunts. But this is not a dead place. It is too loud, too hot, too mobile. It simply has not invented animals yet.

The air smells of hot iron, sulfur, and a faint floral rot, like crushed orchids in a machine shop. The water is blood-warm and slightly thicker than water, as if it has been mixed with a thin syrup. It fizzes where the cables enter. It does not reflect light properly. Look into it and you see not your face but a spreading gray bloom, or sometimes a different sky.

The sound is the hardest thing to stay in. It is not noise; it is a pressure. The turbines thrum underwater. The steam whistles through broken seals. The rain has no gaps. The metal groans as the plants twist it. And underneath, there is a continuous granular roar, like gravel in a drum, that might be the water itself boiling against the rock. Sound here is unreliable: you hear a thing break before it breaks. The echo of your own breathing comes back with extra syllables. Sometimes the sound goes away for a second, and the silence is worse—it is the silence of a place holding its breath.

The rules are not fixed. Gravity is a suggestion. The water level rises and falls not with the tide but with the breathing of the basin. A dropped stone may fall upward if you do not believe in it. The same pump station appears on both sides of the channel, one mirrored, each with the same bent ladder. A swim that took an hour may take four, or four strokes. The water sometimes forgets to be wet; for a moment it holds you like glass, and you can walk on it, then it shudders and you are chest-deep.

The vegetation is not stable either. A frond can unroll, darken, and rot within a minute, then begin again from the same bud. The root mat underfoot is not soil; it is a living scar tissue, growing faster than the water can erode it. The place is still being made. That is why it is so sharp, so unweathered. Nothing has had time to soften, because every edge is being re-cut by the same forces that made it.

If you stay long enough, you will learn the rules. First, do not trust boundaries: the line between water and air, plant and machine, inside and outside, is a habit, not a law. Second, do not trust distance: the far side of the basin may be closer than the thing next to you. Third, do not trust your memory: the place will remember you differently.

The Churn is not a landscape you visit. It is a landscape that is still happening to itself. You can swim through it, climb its raw ridges, follow its cables, but you will not find an end. You will find only a deeper layer of the same condition: warm, submerged, overgrown, industrial, dim, and very, very loud.
