The first thing is the light. It comes at you from everywhere—not just from the sky but up off the ground, off the wet leaves, off the seams of standing water, off the rusted metal flanges that stud the plateau like half-buried vertebrae. The sky is a pale, scrubbed blue, almost white at the zenith, and the sun sits in it like a cold coin. There is no shade anywhere. The light is so bright it seems to flatten depth, so that the distant line of low hills looks like a cutout pasted against the horizon, and the middle distance shimmers with a vibration that is not heat but the sheer intensity of photons bouncing off saturated surfaces.

Then the sound arrives. It is not a single noise but a pressure, a continuous roar that fills the skull and makes your teeth ache. The wind. It never stops here. It pours across the plateau from the west, a river of air that has been accelerating for two hundred kilometers over open ground, and when it hits the first low ridge of vegetation it doesn't break—it lifts, howls, and drives straight through. The sound is like standing inside a turbine housing, or next to a waterfall that never lands. Beneath the wind, there are other sounds, but you have to strain to separate them: a low mechanical thrum from somewhere to the north, a rhythmic clanking that might be a loose panel on an abandoned pumphouse, and the constant, subtle squelch of water being squeezed out of the ground under your feet.

The ground is not ground. That is the first true shock. You walk on a living mattress, a spongy, waterlogged mass of vegetation that gives under each step and then slowly rebounds, leaving a footprint-shaped depression that fills with clear water before your next stride. The surface is a patchwork of mosses—emerald, rust-red, olive, and a pale, almost white species that grows only in the brightest spots and crunches faintly underfoot like frost. Beneath the moss, there is no mineral soil, no rock, no clay. There is only more vegetation, layer upon layer of it, the compressed and half-decayed remains of ten thousand years of growth. You can dig down with your hands—the water is cold, shockingly cold—and pull up handfuls of brown, fibrous peat that smells of tannin and iodine. This is the substrate. The plants are the ground. The ground is alive.

The terrain is high, a broad plateau that extends to the edge of sight in every direction, broken only by the faint rise and fall of what might be ancient beach lines or the first, hesitant wrinkles of a landscape still being born. It is a raw place, geologically speaking. The rocks that do emerge—where the vegetation has been scoured away by some local catastrophe, or where a stream has cut a narrow slot—are sharp-edged, unweathered, dark basalt or pale granite, as if they were quarried yesterday. There are no rounded boulders, no smooth cobbles. Every stone is a fresh fracture. The erosion is minimal, not because the land is hard but because it is protected: the vegetation mat holds everything together, absorbing the rain, slowing the water, stitching the surface with a billion tiny roots. The streams that do exist are clear and cold, running in shallow channels between moss hummocks, so new they have not yet carved a proper bed. Some of them simply disappear into the peat, sinking into the sponge and re-emerging a hundred meters away as a spring that weeps from a low bank.

Water is everywhere. It is in the air, condensing on every surface as the temperature drops in the late afternoon. It is in the ground, a few centimeters below the surface, so that every step produces a soft, audible squelch. It is pooled in the slight depressions between the moss mounds, forming dozens, hundreds of shallow ponds—some the size of a dinner plate, some the size of a tennis court—each one perfectly clear, each one reflecting the bright sky with a mirror-like intensity. The ponds are connected by slow, almost imperceptible seeps, so the whole plateau is a single, vast, shallow wetland, a skin of water held in place by living tissue.

The vegetation is total, but it is not dense. This is the paradox. Everything is covered, yet there is space between presences. The moss forms a continuous carpet, but the distinct plants—the ones that rise above the surface—are few and far between. There are no trees. Even shrubs are rare. Instead, the vegetation is low, wind-clipped, and scattered. Pools of space lie between individual tufts of sedge, between the occasional wiry shrub that clings to a slightly raised hummock, between the solitary, twisted stems of a plant you cannot name. The overall impression is not of jungle or forest but of an open, almost savanna-like expanse where the ground cover is absolute but the vertical elements are sparse, isolated, each one a small event in a vast emptiness. It is as if the landscape is a continuous green ocean with only a few scattered masts rising from the swell.

Scattered across this ocean are the remnants of industry. They are not ruins, exactly—too recent, too functional, too small. There is a concrete pad, half-swallowed by moss, with four rusted anchor bolts still set in the corners. There is a length of steel cable, twisted and half-buried, leading nowhere. There is a small metal building, no larger than a garden shed, its walls streaked with rust and its door missing, revealing a dark interior that smells of diesel and old rubber. Inside, there is a generator, silent, its fuel tank empty, and a panel of gauges with cracked glass. Farther off, a single wind turbine stands on a low ridge, its blades motionless in the wind—why they don't turn is a mystery, perhaps the brake is on, or the bearing has seized—and it emits a low, almost subsonic hum that you feel in your sternum rather than hear. There are paths, too, of a sort: not made by feet but by machinery, straight lines of compressed peat where tracked vehicles once passed, now slowly filling with water and colonized by a different, paler moss.

The fauna is rare. In a full day, you might see one bird, a raptor hanging motionless against the wind, or a small, dark shape that flits between sedge clumps and is gone. There are insects—tiny midges that rise in clouds from the ponds at dusk, but not enough to bother. No mammals. No tracks in the soft ground except the occasional, half-erased mark of something that passed weeks ago. The place feels empty, not in a dead way but in a patient way, as if the animals have simply not yet arrived, or have left and not returned.

The dynamic is slow. Everything here happens on timescales that dwarf a human lifetime. The peat accumulates at a rate measured in millimeters per century. The moss spreads, dies, and becomes soil in a cycle so gradual it is invisible. The streams shift by centimeters each year. The rust on the industrial remnants flakes away one cell at a time. The landscape is not static, but its motion is a deep, slow breathing, a geological patience. You could come back in a hundred years and find the same scene, almost, except that the generator has sunk a little deeper into the moss, and the wind turbine has lost another blade, and the path has become a string of ponds.

Stand still for a while, and the place begins to work on you. The roar of the wind becomes a kind of silence. The brightness becomes a kind of darkness, like staring at the sun through closed eyelids. The wetness becomes a medium you move through, not a condition you endure. You begin to understand that this is a world under construction, an embryonic landscape, a place where the rules are still being written. The plants are the ground. The water is the blood. The wind is the voice. The industry is a recent, faint scar that is already healing. And the light, the terrible, beautiful light, is the first thing and the last thing, the thing that will outlast everything.
