You find it by accident. Not a door, not a marked way — a seam in the wall of a much larger cave, a crack in black basalt no wider than your shoulders. If you weren't already lost, you'd miss it. The air that comes out of it is hot and dry, so dry that it hurts the inside of your nose. It smells of struck flint and sulfur and a metal left too long in the sun. There is no drip of water. There is no cool cave-breath. There is only heat, and a dry exhalation, and the faint suggestion of a sound.

You turn sideways and enter.

The passage runs downward at a steep angle. The rock is glassy and sharp, full of tiny edges. Nothing here has rounded. Nothing has been worn smooth by time or water. The floor is a jumble of angular blocks, as fresh as if the mountain cracked open last year and forgot to heal. Every edge is a knife. There is no dust. There is no moisture. The sharpness is pristine, almost surgical.

And then the passage ends, and the room begins.

It is not a cavern. Caverns are vast and grand and slow. This is a room — a single chamber the size of a small chapel, maybe twenty meters long, ten wide, five high. A pocket. A bubble in the body of the earth. The ceiling is low and uneven, and the walls are black obsidian and basalt, broken into hundreds of small facets, each catching whatever light exists and throwing it back like the eye of a crow. The fractures are fresh. The surfaces are glassy. The room looks like it was shattered open a day ago, and then time stopped.

It is warm. Not a summer warmth — a body warmth, a living warmth, the heat of the earth bleeding upward through the floor. The air is heavy and absolutely, completely dry. There has never been water here. There is no film of condensation on the walls, no dampness in the rock, no moisture anywhere. Your lips begin to chap within minutes. The dryness is a presence, a pressure, a thing with edges.

And there is sound.

It is loud. A low, continuous drone, like the lowest note of a pipe organ held forever, like a didgeridoo played by something that has never needed to breathe. It comes from everywhere and nowhere. It seems to rise through the floor, through the walls, through the bones of your jaw. It is not unpleasant. It is not music. It is a single chord, never wavering in pitch, never changing volume, never stopping. You realize, after a long while, that the room itself is an instrument — a throat somewhere below, a bellows of hot gas, and this chamber as the resonant body. The note has been sounding for centuries. It has never once been silent. It is so constant that, after a few minutes, it becomes a kind of silence — not an absence of sound, but a sound so steady that the mind folds it into the background of the world.

Then the light. Because there is light, though you can't find its source.

It is dim. Not darkness — a twilight, a dusk-light, like the hour after sunset when objects still exist but colors have gone gray at the edges. The light seems to come from the walls themselves, from the floor, from the air. In places, the basalt glows with a faint orange, as if remembering the heat that made it. In other places, there is a cold blue-white phosphorescence, the color of certain sea-creatures, of fungal glow. And between these two lights — warm orange and cold blue — the space is lit in a way that makes everything look like a photograph of itself, precise and unreal.

And things are growing here.

It takes you a moment to understand, because the word *growing* belongs to water and soil and sunlight, and none of those things are here. But things are growing. They rise from the floor and from the walls in clusters — dense, profuse, *lush* clusters, separated by wide stretches of bare black rock. At first you think they are crystals. Of course you think they are crystals. They look like crystals: branching needles of yellow sulfur, rosy spires of arsenic red, white fans of gypsum, rosettes of brown and silver baryte. They are beautiful in the way minerals are beautiful, and frightening in that same way, because they are sharp and geometric and absolutely indifferent to the idea of softness.

But then you look closer. You crouch. You see that the clusters are not simply precipitated from solution — they are *structured*. Each cluster has a central stem, a core like a trunk, from which the needle-branches spread. The branches divide and subdivide in patterns that are not crystalline but organic — not random, not fractal, but organized, with a logic of growth. They reach upward, toward the warm air. They are crowded with tiny, repeating structures that might be leaves: flat blades of sulfur arranged in spirals along the branches, minute fans of gypsum that would flutter if anything here moved fast enough to stir the air. Some of them are broken — snapped needles lying on the floor, splintered trunks, pale chips scattered across the black rock. They have been eaten. They have fallen. They have grown too heavy and cracked. And where they are whole, they glitter, thousands of tiny facets catching the orange-and-blue light.

They are alive. They are plants in the way a plant is alive — they grow, they metabolize, they die, they leave fragments. But they have no water in them. No green. No softness. They are made of sulfur and arsenic and calcium sulfate, of silica and iron. They draw their energy from the hot, sulfurous gas rising through a fissure in the floor, oxidizing compounds, building crystalline lattices in place of cellulose and leaf. The word *vegetation* is wrong, but it is also the only word that fits. Because this is a kind of forest. A dry forest, a mineral forest, a forest that would shatter if you touched it too hard.

And the lushness — the lushness is real. Within each cluster, the needles are so dense you cannot see through them. The branches overlap and interweave. There is a profusion, an exuberance of growth, the riot of a greenhouse translated into stone. But the clusters themselves are sparse. Between one grove and the next, there are meters of empty floor — bare, black, mirror-smooth rock, interrupted only by a fallen needle or a drift of yellow powder. The lushness and the emptiness exist together. Each cluster is an island, a small world, a grove in a desert of glass.

Then the creatures make themselves known.

You see them first as movements at the edge of your vision — small, slow, patient. They are hard to see because they look like parts of the landscape. They are made of stone. Their shells are obsidian, or flint, or banded agate, or a matte black basalt that drinks the light. They are the size of a fist or a hand, sometimes larger. Their bodies are segmented like beetles or trilobites, armored plates fitting together with the precision of carved jigsaw pieces. They move on short legs, or on a single muscular foot, or on rows of rippling cilia in their mineral flesh. They move slowly — so slowly that watching them is like watching a minute-hand. But they do move. They are everywhere. Not in swarms — never in swarms — but you count a dozen in the space of a minute, scattered across the floor and climbing among the crystal clusters, each one occupying its own small territory, its own patch of sulfur and rock.

They are abundant, but not crowded. There is space between them, always. They graze. One of them — a dark, beetle-like creature with a shell of banded agate, no larger than a mouse — works at the base of a sulfur needle, scraping off the soft bloom of yellow crystals, leaving behind a polished, slightly shining surface. Not wet with water — wet with something else, an oily trace, a sheen that catches the light. They eat the mineral flesh of the plants. Their mouthparts are crystal. Their teeth are silica. And here and there, where one has died, its shell sits empty, slowly being covered by new growth, a dusting of sulfur, a splinter of obsidian.

The room is a closed loop. The animals graze exactly as fast as the plants grow. The plants grow exactly as fast as the animals eat. The dead are consumed, their minerals returned to the floor, and new growth rises from the same points. There is no change. The numbers stay the same. The positions shift in slow, repeating patterns. It is a system so perfectly balanced that it has stopped being a process and become a place. A thing that simply *is*.

This is the deepest rule of the world. Nothing changes here. This is the key that unlocks the whole place. The gas flows at the same rate it always has. The temperature is the same at every moment. The sound is the same note. The sharp edges of the walls stay sharp because nothing erodes them — no rain, no wind, no freeze, no thaw, no organism strong enough to wear down the basalt. The fractures stay fresh. The handprint — you will find a handprint — stays red. The room is a single moment, extended forever. It is not frozen in cold, but frozen in warmth. A warm stasis. A hot equilibrium. A place that has been exactly this way for so long that time has stopped meaning anything.

And then, at last, you see that someone has been here.

At the far end of the room, where the ceiling is lowest, there is a half-circle of stones. Not natural stones — they were placed. Each one is a chunk of vesicular basalt, gray and pocked with holes, and each one is turned so that its flattest face points inward. Together they make a shallow enclosure, a low wall no higher than a knee. A windbreak. A sleeping-space. A shelter against a wind that has never blown here. Inside the circle, the floor is slightly concave, polished — not by water, but by the pressure of a body lying there, sitting there, living there over some unknowable length of time.

Nearby, in a natural niche in the wall, there is a bowl. It is carved from a single piece of basalt, the size of a pair of cupped hands. The carving is crude but careful — the inner curve is regular, the surface smooth, as if someone spent whole seasons grinding it with sand and patience. It contains nothing. Next to it, a cluster of flaked blades lies in a careful row — obsidian, black glass, sharp enough still to cut. The flakes are small, perfectly struck. A hunter's kit, a cutter's kit, left behind in this hot, dry, resounding room.

On the wall above the niche, someone has made marks. You have to lean close to see them, because the light is dim and the marks are faint. A series of vertical scratches, shallow in the basalt. Seven short lines, then a longer line, then a double line. A tally of days. A map of something. A prayer, maybe, or a name. Below it, pressed flat against a rough patch of rock, is a handprint. Not black, but red — the red of iron oxide, of ochre mixed with something that has dried and hardened into the stone. It is small. A human hand, or something very like one. Four fingers. A thumb. The lines of the palm are visible in fine detail, as if the owner pressed it there only yesterday. It is not painted. It is printed. The print is perfectly preserved. Nothing has faded it. No dust has settled in its creases. The room holds it the way amber holds an insect.

There are no other marks. No bones. No fire-pit, despite the stones. No tools beyond the blades and the bowl. Whoever came here stayed for a time, and then they left. Or they stayed, and they died, and their body was returned to the mineral cycle, consumed by the same slow, stone jaws that eat the crystal plants. Either way, the room has kept only these faint traces. A bowl. A circle of stones. A tally. A handprint.

When you finally sit down on the warm floor, with the drone in your chest and the dryness cracking your lips, you notice the source of everything. In the corner of the room, there is a fissure in the floor — a crack no wider than a hand, glowing faintly orange at its lips, exhaling a steady stream of hot, sulfurous vapor. The note comes from there. The heat comes from there. The life comes from there. It is a single crack, a single wound in the earth, and the whole room is built around it. It has been exhaling for so long that the version of the world where it did not exist is not a world worth counting.

This is the place. A pocket of time and sulfur. A room where nothing changes, where the vegetation is lush and dry, where the animals are stone and slow, where the sound is a single endless note, where a handprint on the wall is older than you can imagine and yet looks fresh. It is strange. Deeply strange. The strangeness never resolves into familiarity, no matter how long you stay. The plants are not plants. The animals are not animals. The silence is not silence. The light is not day. And yet it all works. It is alive, in its dry, hot, mineral way. It has been alive for a very long time.

When you finally stand and turn back toward the narrow crack in the wall, the room does not watch you go. It simply continues — balanced, bright-dim, loud-silent, unchanged. The stone animals keep grazing. The crystal groves keep growing. The note keeps sounding. And as you slip through the passage and the warm dry air gives way to the cooler air of the upper cave, you hear the drone begin to fade behind you. But it does not fade. It stays in your head, vibrating in the bones of your jaw.

The note. The single, endless note of a place that never changes.
