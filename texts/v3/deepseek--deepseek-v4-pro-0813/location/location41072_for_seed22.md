The first thing is the sound. It arrives before the landscape, a stacked confusion of tones: a low, almost physical vibration in the ground; a choir of wet flutes; a continuous high insect shriek; distant clattering like halyards against masts. The air feels tuned. You cannot tell if it is noise or music, and the distinction probably doesn’t matter here.

The land emerges slowly from the dim. It is a broad low basin, sea-level or near it, open to the sky but shaded from it. The floor is black stone, fine-grained, split into narrow ridges and shallow troughs, as if the whole valley had been snapped upward yesterday and not yet rounded by rain. The edges are raw. Escarpments rise abruptly in sharp-edged walls, and the rock faces show no moss or weathering in their upper reaches. The ground is wet, but not with rivers. Water seeps from cracks, beads on stone, drips from leaves. There are channels that carry only a finger’s depth of water, and basins that fill with mist more than flow. Warmth comes from the ground and the air together: a humid, mouth-like warmth.

Then the structures appear. They are miles apart, some so far they look like pale scratches on the low clouds. They are clearly urban—ramps, terraces, arcades, stairways, cisterns, broad empty thresholds—but not dense. The spaces between them are enormous. You could walk an hour from one tower’s shadow to the next and meet nothing but open stone and shallow watercourses. But the city is not small; it extends beyond sight, an infrastructure spread thin over the valley. The towers rise two or three hundred meters, fluted, perforated, covered in circular openings and narrow slots. They look grown, not built. Their material is pale gray or bone-white, like pumice or packed salt, with no seams or joints. Some of the upper parts look blurred, too soft to focus on, as though they are vibrating at the wrong frequency for the eye. A few bridges span between distant towers, and they ripple like heat when the wind passes.

The light is dim. There is a high haze of spores and water vapor; the sun is a pale bruise behind it. Near the ground, a blue-green bioluminescence comes from patches of moss and from the edges of stone. It outlines things without illuminating them. The city absorbs much of the remaining light. On a dark stone wall, condensation glints like the skin of a mirror. The whole valley is drowned in permanent amber dusk.

Vegetation is everywhere, but not evenly. The ground is often bare except for wet mineral crust. The lushness rises: walls of fern, shelves of broad-leafed trees, aerial roots, bromeliads, vines that hang from the high ledges in curtains. The plants seem to prefer the structures, growing from the holes and flutes, thickest inside the towers where the air is full of moisture and sound. Some of the larger trees are rooted in midair troughs, their roots reaching down but not into soil. Many have waxy leaves that funnel condensation into reservoirs at their bases. The vegetation gives the city a half-swallowed look—green tongues from every opening, root systems like exposed tendons.

There is much animal life. The air is thick with small flying things: pale moths, long-winged wasps, tiny bats, clouds of spores that may or may not be alive. On the stone, lizards and snails and beetles move in pulses. In the wet pockets, frogs call in metallic voices. Flocks of birds move between towers, gray and white, turning in synchrony. Colonies of something with papery nests pack the upper holes. Larger forms pass in the far distance—long, many-legged, slow—their footfalls knocking against the rock. Predators move too: a dark wing falls from a high ledge, a chorus stops, resumes. The animals are not dense, but their presence is abundant; every open vista holds a few, every vertical surface vibrates with hidden ones.

The sound never lessens. It is a whole climate. Wind moves through the tower flutes and produces changing organ chords. Water drips into hollow basins with bright, high rings, and some basins are shaped to amplify those drips into drumbeats. Insects in the greenery produce a sustained, shrill drone that seems to come from the air itself. The structures add deeper notes—subsonic thrumming, as if the city is breathing. When the wind strengthens, the valley roars. When it drops, the insects and frogs and dripping take over. There is no silence. Even the ground has a low seismic murmur.

The place is not abandoned, exactly. It is maintained. The towers sweat a clear resin that heals cracks. Some of the spires tremble and condense fog: their vibration produces a fine rain at their bases, and the water runs into channels. It is a technology that treats sound, moisture, and growth as convertible. The city may have built itself, or been built by something that no longer needs to be present. There are no inscriptions, no doors that open, no signs of current human occupation. The structures may be storage for something—memory, sound, living systems—but if so, it is written into the resonance.

The basin is active. Rain arrives in sudden warm squalls, fills the channels briefly, then drains into porous stone. The mist stirs. The plants respond to the noise by orienting their leaves. Animals move on a schedule not keyed to daylight, because daylight is uncertain. The whole place feels newly made, as if it had been switched on only a few decades ago; edges are still sharp, fresh surfaces bare of erosion, the city’s shape unsoftened. Yet the vegetation is old, layered, gnarled. It is not a ruin, and not a wilderness, and not a city. It is something else, holding all three together under a roof of sound.
