**The Underpan**

Below the white desert, after you descend through a fissure in the salt, the world opens into a hollow lit from above. The ceiling is not sky; it is the underside of the pan itself, a roof of packed salt and gypsum thin enough to glow. Light passes through it in a cold, water-white radiance, so the whole place is bright without a visible sun, shadowless, like the inside of a porcelain bowl held up to the light. The hollow is broad enough that the far walls dissolve in white air, but shallow enough for that light to reach almost everywhere. In places the ceiling is only a few spans thick, and if you press your ear to the stone you can hear the hiss of wind moving across the desert above.

The air is cool and wet. It never warms beyond a well-water chill. The wetness is not rain, though rain is the only weather the roof allows in: a dimming of the light, a faint drumming through salt. The wetness is seepage, condensation, the breath of the river. Every surface is filmed. The river is white with dissolved stone. It braids across the hollow floor, pools in old solution basins, then drops into a swallow-hole at the far end. Its channels shift every year.

The floor is ancient. The rock has been worked by water for so long that it has lost its edges. The walls are fluted, scalloped, undercut into broad ledges. It looks soft, like old cloth, though it is stone. Even the fallen slabs are rounded with mineral crust. The people call it “the place that has already been worn smooth.” It is not a ruin, though it contains ruins. It is the final form of a certain kind of erosion: not a landscape in decline, but a landscape that has settled into its oldest shape.

Against this, the city is sparse. It is urban by organization, not by crowding. There are streets, cisterns, water gates, shared courtyards, salt-kilns, curing racks, fungal paddies, a council ledge carved into the wall. But between the neighborhoods lie wide white flats, sometimes empty except for dust devils and the slow drift of salt. The city is too big for its population; many houses stand empty, their doors sealed with salt. Houses are low, thick-walled, built of cut salt block, tufa, and lime. Roofs are corbelled or arched because wood is too precious to burn or waste. The city looks less like an imposition on the hollow than like part of the floor that has learned to stand up.

There is no green. No leaf, no blade, no stem. The eye eventually stops looking for plants and begins to see the local vegetation of minerals: gypsum flowers that open in the wet dark, halite hoppers stacked like tiny stairs, helictite shrubs that look like roots but are stone. The only living things that can be called crops are fungal mats, white and gray, like fleece or old tallow. They spread in shallow stone pans and along the damp walls. The people scrape them with bone knives, press them into cakes, and tend them as carefully as any orchard. In the deeper side-galleries there are mushroom-like forms, pale as wax, that open toward the ceiling light. But these are fungi, not plants.

The animals are abundant. The hollow is full of movement, but the movement is quiet. White beetles the size of thumbs graze the mineral scum along the river, ticking. Blind pink salamanders coil in the cisterns. Pale mice with huge dark eyes slip between houses, leaving no tracks. In the pools, glassy shrimp twitch. In the deeper sumps there are fish with no pigment and no eyes, their fins moving like slow fingers. Small ash-colored birds nest in the ceiling cracks, going to and from the surface through vents in the pan. They are mostly silent; when they call, it is a two-note sound so soft it seems to come from inside your own ear.

The silence is not empty. It is built. The roof is fragile; a sharp noise can shake it. The inhabitants believe, with reason, that a shout can bring down a slab of salt the size of a house. So they speak in signs, taps, and close whispers. Children learn to walk softly before they learn to talk. The streets are dusted with sound-absorbing powder. The walls are porous. Even the animals have learned. The loudest sound in the open hollow is the thud of a ceiling fall, and it is more a vibration in the breastbone than a noise in the air.

Yet the place is turbulent. The river shifts. The ground heaves as salt grows under it, pushing up ridges and splitting walls. Silent dust devils spin across the flats—white columns of gypsum dust that wander like ghosts, uncovering old doorways and burying paths. There is a place called the Needlefield where the salt grows in a night into crystals as tall as a person, and by morning they are already cracked and falling; the people collect the pieces for building. The ceiling is constantly falling. Somewhere in the hollow, every day, a salt stalactite lets go. It drops in a white bloom of dust, takes an hour to settle, and leaves a new bright wound overhead. The people do not look up. They listen with their feet.

This is the law of the Underpan: the city does not fight erosion; it rides it. A wall is not expected to last. A house is a task, not an heirloom. Tools are simple—stone hammers, bone chisels, fire-hardened levers, rope of sinew and fungus-fibre, baskets of hide and the long hair of the pack-beasts. Metals are rare; a little copper is worked cold, used for needles and small knives. Everything else is made by hand and patience. When the salt takes a structure, the people rebuild it a few feet away, using the same stones. The map of the city is more like a cloud than a plan.

The light changes with the desert above. At midday the whole ceiling is a white blaze, brighter than a surface street but without shadow. At night it fades to gray, then to black, and the only lights are the small, waxy flames of fat-lamps in windows, each one a warm point in the cool dark. The sound, if you stop and listen, is not silence exactly. It is the faint sift of settling dust, the tick of a beetle, the far-off hiss of water finding a new course, the creak of salt cooling. It is a quiet so deep you can hear your own blood. And under it, if you press your hand to the stone, you can feel the slow, turbulent life of the place still moving.
