Darkness first. Not night—night implies a dawn—but the permanent dark of a sky that never clears. The cloud deck over the Karrask Plateau sits low, maybe two hundred meters above the highest towers, a lid of black vapor that reflects only a faint violet from the city below. Rain falls as a fine, almost-frozen drizzle, so light it seems to hover rather than fall. It settles on skin, stone, metal. The air is cold, a few degrees above freezing, and so saturated that every surface runs with water. The stone weeps. The metal sweats. The sound of water is everywhere, a roar that never lets up.

The plateau is high and new. It is raw basalt from a slow upwelling that has not yet been worn down. Edges are sharp, glassy, conchoidal. There are no rounded stones, no pebbles, no sand. Erosion has barely begun. The water moves over the rock without shaping it; the channels are straight cuts made by the city. In the open country beyond the city, the ground is black and wet, steaming where warm brine emerges and meets the cold air. The land looks unfinished, like a sentence still being spoken.

No plants grow here. None. No leaf, no stem, no root, no green. The only growth on the bare mineral is a pale chemotrophic film—the locals call it silt-skin—a bacterial mat that feeds on iron and sulfur in the seep water. It looks like spilled milk dried and cracked. In the humid galleries, pale fungal spires rise from the mats, but they are not plants; they are soft, fleshy, heavily grazed. All food here is microbial, mineral, or animal. Nothing photosynthesizes. There is no light for it anyway.

The city is Vess. It is a dense, layered settlement built into the rim of the caldera and down into its bowl. Towers of sintered basalt and carbon composite climb one against the other. Streets are narrow and roofed, because open air is too wet and too cold for public life. Upper lanes pass over lower lanes; buildings lean so close that their upper floors join into shared galleries. There are no open plazas. The city’s true life is interior, where the air is warmed and the water is channeled. Vess is not a collection of buildings; it is one continuous structure, a terrain of roofs and conduits and inhabited walls.

The older districts are layered, patched, accreted. The newer districts are unfinished, but not ruined—just not yet complete. Walls end in clean, sharp edges. Towers rise from the rock as if the basalt itself is being poured into form. Machines work slowly: some are force-field scaffolds, some are semi-organic extruders that secrete a living-mineral composite. A single building may take two hundred years to finish. The city does not mind. It is still being made, and so is the land.

The technology here is beyond the modern. Heat is drawn from deep thermal sinks and moved through corridors by resonant magnetic fields. Water runs uphill in gravity-pinned channels. Some walls are made of composites that change opacity with the load. Lights are cold blue-green, tuned to not blind the sonar of the animals. There are elevators that fall in vacuum shafts, and doors that recognize you by the weight of your breath. The infrastructure is dense, layered, and partly biological—grown as much as built. It is machinery current physics describes only in fragments.

And the animals. They are overwhelming. Every surface moves. The dominant grazers are sump-mites, translucent, many-legged, the size of a fingernail, so dense that the walkways shimmer as they feed on silt-skin. In the drains, blind pipe-eels, pale as milk, hunt the mites. Above, hull-bats roost in their thousands under the heat exchangers, their leathery wings folded, their sonar a constant patter. Rust limpets, like small mineral cones, cover every pipe, eating the metal and excreting an insulating foam that the city harvests. Glass midges, no bigger than a grain of rice, hatch in the sump water and fill the air in clouds so thick the automatic shutters must close.

The city does not fight them. It has been designed for them. Buildings have crawl-spaces, nesting shelves, guano traps. The animals outnumber the human inhabitants a thousand to one. Their noise is part of the city’s sound: a continuous click-chitter, a tide of calls that rises and falls, merging with the drip of water and the low hum of thermal pumps.

It is loud. The water alone is a roar: thousands of rivulets, drips, and sheets falling from ledges. Wind through the pipe arrays hums like a horn. Hull-bats’ sonar clicks like rain on tin. The city’s pumps add a subsonic thrum you feel in the sternum. Silence is not a thing here. The wet, cold air carries sound far; the dark makes it feel closer.

Light is scarce. The sky never brightens beyond a faint violet, the glow of the city’s waste heat reflected from the cloud deck. Street lights are dim, and most are off in the animal quarters. What light there is comes from bioluminescence—the pale blue of silt-skin, the moving points of sump-mites, the soft green glow of nutrient vats. Eyes adjust to the dark, and the dark becomes legible: the restless constellations of the animals, the edges of wet stone, the distant tiers of the city.

The dynamics are slow. The plateau rises a few centimeters a century. The water table creeps higher. Animal populations cycle over decades, not seasons. Construction is so gradual that streets are rerouted before they are finished. The only fast things are the animals and the water, and even they follow slow rhythms. It is a place of patient processes, weak gradients, long durations.

Stand at the city’s rim and look out across the Karrask Plateau: a black, wet, open country, steaming where warm seep meets cold air, its surface crawling with life that needs no sun. Vess is a dense, glowing, grinding knot on the edge of that dark plain, still being born from the rock. It is not hostile, but it is not gentle. It is a place for those who can hear the slow pulse under the roar, and who do not need the light to see.

The rules here are simple and old: keep the water moving or it will freeze and split the stone. Keep the dark; light is for close work, not for the sky. The animals are not pests; they are the city’s other citizens. Build slow, because the ground will shift. Nothing green will take root. Heat is the only currency that matters. And the city, wet and black and loud and cold, keeps on.
