The air is loud before it is light.  
The noise has texture, like a wall of dried reeds being struck by a thousand small hammers. Before the path opens, you feel it in the sternum, in the teeth, in the hinge of the jaw. It is not one sound but a braid: a low tectonic grind, a continuous dry rattle, high whistles from somewhere above, and underneath it all a chittering that never quite syncs.

The place is a young rift, a gash in a plateau that has not yet learned to be a valley. The ground has dropped away from the old surface in sharp terraces and raw fracture faces. There are no riverbeds. No seeps, no glisten of groundwater, no dark stains of moisture. The stone is warm to the touch, glassy in places, pitted in others, flaking into knife-edged plates. The only rounded stones are the ones carried and polished by animals, scattered along the paths like beads.

Sun comes down through the open rift in broad shafts, turned yellow-green by the canopy, and fills the middle air with a thick, dusty glow. The floor lies tens of meters below the rim—not so deep that the sky disappears, but deep enough that the horizon is a jagged line of stone and vegetation. The basin opens outward, stretching for kilometers; the far wall is visible only as a dark band of texture through the drifting fibers.

The vegetation does not cover the land so much as it constitutes the land. The floor is not soil. It is a continuous living mat of roots, runners, fallen leaf-plates, and old compressed growth, all of it connected. When you step, the ground gives more than soil, with a fibrous resilience, and it is warm. The plants have taken over every surface. The walls are ribbed with root-ladders. The boulders are upholstered in tight scales. The air itself is strung with hanging stems and drifting seeds. It is not a forest on a landscape; it is a landscape made of forest.

The plants are strange. Many have leaves that are narrow, folded, waxy, silver or rust. Others have broad black vanes that angle toward warm updrafts, drinking heat instead of water. The trunks are not single columns but bundles of fused stems, twisted together like cables, rising out of their own root-mat. In places the canopy is inverted: roots descend from the fractured ceiling, meet stems rising from the floor, and fuse in midair, forming pillars and arches. The middle distance becomes a lattice of living wood, dense and near-touching, every cubic meter occupied.

Nothing here weeps. When a leaf is torn, it releases a dry cloud of golden spores and a smell like turpentine and hot iron. When a branch breaks, it does not snap wetly; it cracks like a dry bone, and the wound seals within seconds under a bead of black resin. The sap is not water. It is a volatile, oily substance that evaporates into a white film and a sweet, caustic scent. The plants run on it, pumping it through channels that show as ridges under the bark.

The animals are overwhelming. They are not hiding. The vegetation is full of them, on every surface, at every scale. Small six-legged things with glassy shells race along stems in waves, their legs tapping out a rapid code. In the crotch of a fused trunk, a creature like a bundle of thorns unfolds into a long, flat, many-jointed shape and screams, then folds again. Overhead, gliders with parchment skin leap between columns, landing with a rattle of dry membranes and claws. The air is thick with small fliers—some are insects, some are seeds, some are both—hovering, biting, dying in drifts on horizontal surfaces.

A single step sets off a ripple of movement. Things scatter, other things rush in to investigate. The ground mat is alive with burrowing forms, visible only as shifting lumps and the occasional eruption of a segmented head. The trunks are encrusted with shelled animals that look like bark until they move. The noise they make is the loudest part. It is not just the calls. It is the sound of their bodies: chitin scraping on stem, claws on stone, wings rattling, jaws grinding, all of it multiplied past counting.

Here and there, the living scaffold has been manipulated. A hoop of greenwood bent and tied with fiber to form an arch. A platform of woven stems lashed to three trunks, swaying but intact. A windbreak of black stone blades and dried hide, stitched with sinew. A spiral of pale pebbles around a steaming vent. A tripod of long bones tied at the top, from which hang clusters of seed pods. None of it is monumental. It is scattered, partial, provisional. Some structures are fresh; some are half-overgrown, being absorbed back into the plant mat. They mark routes, resting places, caches.

The builders are not obvious. Occasionally a small figure appears on a high branch: leathery skin, large dark eyes, long fingers, a face more flat than pointed. It holds a flaked stone knife or twists fiber into cord. It does not call out. It taps its feet against the wood, and the taps are answered from far away. Then it is gone into the leaves. There are others—taller, paler, slower—that tend the sap-bleeding stems and collect the black resin in hollow gourds. They use simple tools: stone scrapers, bone awls, twine, resin. There is no metal. Fire is rare and dangerous; the land is too dry and too alive. The builders use the heat of the vents to soften resin and bend wood, but they do not open flames.

The place is in a state of constant construction and destruction. It is not erosion in the usual sense; there is no rain, no streams, no ice. The rounding forces are biological. Roots split the rock. Falling debris crashes into the mat and is immediately overgrown. A corm bursts with an audible pop, scattering seeds that sprout within an hour. A section of the wall shears away, and the dust rises in a yellow cloud; before it settles, pale runners are already probing the new surface.

The growth is visible. A stem uncoils across the path in the space of a minute, then hardens. A new leaf unfolds from a bud with a sound like paper tearing. The trunks grow in jerks, not slowly but in pulses, as if responding to some deep rhythm. The animals respond in waves: a ripple of alarm clicks passes through the canopy, a flock of gliders launches, a mass of small grazers surges from one patch to another. The place is not stable. There is no baseline. The map would be obsolete by evening.

The sound is not separate from the landscape; it is a terrain feature. It rises and falls but never drops to silence. At times it becomes a roar, as when a nesting colony takes flight or a grove of seed pods detonates in a chain reaction. At times it settles into a dense tapestry of small sounds, like rain on dry leaves but with no rain. The animals use the noise: calls cut through it, rhythms repeat, silence is a warning. The plants use it too. Some seed pods only open in response to a particular frequency; some stems resonate, amplifying the calls of the creatures that pollinate them. The whole valley is a kind of instrument, playing itself.

The rules are these: water is not absent as a lack but as a rule. The living things here do not rely on it. They run on oils, resins, solvents that evaporate and condense in the dry air. The animals get what they need from the fat-rich pith of certain stems, from the black sap, from the bodies of one another. They excrete dry pellets of salt and silica. Their breath is a fine dust, sweet and slightly caustic. The plants are the primary substrate. When a creature dies, it is not buried. It is overgrown, pierced by rootlets, digested by the mat. The mat thickens. The land grows upward, not by sediment but by the slow accumulation of living tissue.

The warm air rises from vents in the floor—narrow fissures that exhale heat and sulfur and fine mineral crystals. The plants gather these minerals and turn them into bark, spine, wax. The animals lick the crystals from leaves and cast off shells. There is no soil. There is only the mat, and beneath it, the raw stone.

The strangeness is not one thing but a combination. It is the way the ground breathes. The way a stone, when split, has veins of fiber. The way the animals move in coordinated pulses without any visible leader. The way the canopy sometimes parts to reveal a wall of yellow eyes. The way the sound, after an hour, begins to sound like language.

If you stand still for too long, the mat begins to grow over your feet. The animals become accustomed to you and start to use you as a surface. A vine loops around your ankle, not aggressively, but as if you are just another stone in the land being assembled. The noise fills your skull until you cannot tell if it is outside or inside.

That is the place: not a landscape to observe, but a process to be inside.
