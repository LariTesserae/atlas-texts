The sound comes first, even before the cold. It is not exactly wind, though wind is in it. It is a drone made of many instruments: a low organ-note from the great vertical chimneys, a wet crackle from growing fibers, a chorus of thin animal cries riding the air. The whole place is loud enough that your own breathing disappears into it.

Light is scarce. The sky above the high country is almost permanently closed by a lid of cloud the vegetation itself has made. What reaches the ground is a gray-blue twilight, so dim that shadows are not cast but suggested. The air is thin and cold. You are high on the shoulder of a young range that has not yet been worn down. Every ridgeline is sharp as cracked bone. The rock is black and fresh, with edges that have known no rain but the freeze-thaw of a few hard seasons.

But the rock is not what you walk on.

The ground is a single living organism. It covers the mountains like a knotted skin, hundreds of feet thick in places, thinning to a scum on near-vertical faces. It is not moss, not forest, not meadow. It is all of those folded into one continuous body. There are no individual trees. The world is one plant, or a web of plants so fused that the distinction no longer matters.

From a distance, the range looks furred, or plated. Close, it resolves into overlapping fans, coils, bladders, tubes, scales. Some parts are smooth and waxy; others are rough as bark or soft as lung tissue. The plant has architecture because it grows into useful shapes: arches over ravines, spiral staircases of braided root, wide shallow basins that hold water. It bridges gaps that rock cannot.

And almost all of it is hollow. The solid living rind is thin compared to the empty galleries beneath. The mountains are wrapped around a honeycomb of air. Tunnels the width of roads run through the plant mass. Vertical shafts drop for hundreds of feet and open into caverns large enough to hold a fog bank. The density of the world is low—it is a scaffolding, a net, a blown-up lung. You feel it when you walk: the floor gives slightly, and your footsteps return with an echo that takes too long.

Moisture is intermittent. The plant wrings water from the clouds. When a fog bank rolls over a ridge, millions of tiny waxy cups condense it into drops. The water runs down channels, gathers in hollows, freezes in the darker pockets. There are no true rivers, only seasonal threads of melt that follow the plant’s veins and vanish into the interior. In some chambers it rains upward from the warm breath of the plant condensing on cold stone above.

Most of the light is not from the sky. The plant’s deep veins glow faintly, the color of old ice or sea-washed glass. Some fruiting bodies hang like shuttered lamps and open for an hour, then go dark. Animals carry their own light, or none at all. The darkness is not total, but it is close, and it has texture.

The air in the hollows is crowded with life. Small winged things—wire-flies, the wanderers call them—spin in the updrafts. They are nearly silent until they strike a hollow stem, and then they ring it like a bell. There are larger things: blind pale lopers that run along the resonance-ribs, listening with their whole bodies for the footsteps of prey. Bladder-hawks ride the thermals above the plant canopy; when they dive, they make a sound like tearing cloth. In the deep galleries, herds of sap-aphids move slowly, each one the size of a child’s fist, tended by pale six-fingered shepherds that are themselves only partly animal.

The ecology is fast and violent in pulses. A herd of sap-aphids will strip a vascular cord in a day; then the lopers come. The plant responds by flooding the wound with bitter sap, and the aphids move on. At night, which is nearly all the time, the hollows ring with pursuit, feeding, and the wet crack of seeds.

Human or near-human presence is scattered and old. On a flat lobe of the plant above a warm duct, someone built a single dome of bent stems and hide. The doorway is a braided tube, and inside there is a fire pit made from black stones carried up from a rock spine. The ashes are cold. A copper knife lies half-under a sleeping mat of shed fronds; its edge is still sharp. Outside, a line of bone hooks hangs from a tendon, each one carved with a different pattern of notches. Farther along the ridge, there is a signal tower: a stack of stones around a hollow plant stem, so arranged that the wind makes it moan in two tones. These structures are not ruins. They are simply empty, waiting for the season when their owners return.

This is a place large enough to make its own weather, and it does. The plant’s transpiration raises a permanent cloud deck above the high valleys. Storms are born inside the hollow spaces, small and violent, with rain that falls only on one side of a gallery. Cold air pours off the high peaks at dusk, which is no darker than noon. The plant’s growth is slow but audible: a new frond can unroll in a night, and a gallery can close by a hand’s width in a month. The mountains themselves are young and sharp; they would be sharper still if the plant were not holding them together.

The rules of this place are simple. The ground is a body; treat it as you would an animal. It remembers pressure. A footprint leaves a pale bruise that fades over days, so paths are visible as long calluses. Cut it only if you must, and if you do, be ready for what the bleeding attracts. Water is not found but heard: listen for the small silver sound of drops on a waxy cup. Sound is the only reliable sense here, and it travels far. Silence is rarer than light.

In the deepest hollows there are pockets of air that have been breathed by the plant for centuries. When you enter them, your ears pop, and for a moment you can feel the whole mountain breathing—a long in-out that has nothing to do with your own lungs. It is said that if you sleep there, you wake with the plant’s pulse behind your eyes. Some fruits are not fruits. They are lures shaped like the cry of a lost animal; when a predator comes close, the fruit seals shut.

The place is full and empty at once. Every surface is life, but the volume is mostly air. You can walk for an hour inside a single hollow stem and meet nothing but your own footsteps, your own breath, and the endless braided sound of the world inhaling.
