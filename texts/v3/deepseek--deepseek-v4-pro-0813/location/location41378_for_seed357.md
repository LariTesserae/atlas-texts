It is not a wilderness, though nothing human has ever touched it. It is not a city, though the land is made. It is a high, hot, weeping place where the world has been rebuilt from living plant matter, and the building never stops.

At first you take it for a ruined city, because the forms are too sharp and too purposeful to be forest. Then you see the joints are healed, not mortared; the arches are boles, not blocks. It is a high plateau almost entirely made of living plant tissue, wet and loud, under construction and demolition at the same time.

The ground underfoot is not soil. It is a woven mat of roots, compressed stems, and old leaf sheaths, many paces thick, springy and warm. Where it has torn, the edges are pale and fibrous, ringed like a huge log. Steam rises from the tears. There is no rock anywhere. The original mountain, if it still exists, is far below, buried under generations of growth.

The land is open. Between the great towers and terraces of vegetation, the gaps are wide and filled with mist. You could walk for half an hour from one structure to the next. The plant mass is enormous, but it is scattered: a sparse archipelago of giants standing in a sea of warm air. The horizon is a rhythm of spires and absences.

The shapes are too sharp to be old. Nothing is rounded. The edges of leaves are crisp, the spire tips are needle-thin, the broken stems are clean and pale. This is a young landscape, newly made and still making itself. It has not had time to weather; before anything can soften, it is replaced.

Water is everywhere. It beads on every surface, drips from every rim, runs in channels of waxy cuticle. Some channels are as wide as roads, but they are not stone; they are curved leaves fitted edge to edge, sealed with resin. The water in them is hot, almost too hot to touch. In places it spills over the lip of a leaf and falls in a thin, steaming sheet into the mist below. Ponds cup themselves in the rosettes of enormous bromeliad-like plants, their water clear and green with algae, or brown with fermenting pulp.

The air is saturated. It is always just after rain, though no rain has fallen. The plants transpire so heavily that the high air cannot hold the moisture; it condenses on every cool surface. Breathing is like drinking. The altitude makes the sky vast and bright, but the steam turns it into a white-green haze. The light is normal, even bright, but shadowless at noon, as though the whole world were inside a wet greenhouse.

The materials of this world are few and strange. There is a pale, fast-growing bonewood that makes the rigid frames of towers and bridges. There are dark, rubbery tendon-vines that swell and shorten when wet, pulling loads slowly. There are broad translucent leaves used as roofs and catchments, and spongy, air-filled tissue that floats on water. There is resin, exuded to seal joints and wounds. Everything is alive or recently alive. Even the dead matter is quickly softened by heat and water and fungi, then reabsorbed by the living roots. There is no soil, no stone, no metal, no fired clay. There is no tool here but the hydraulic, growing body of the plants themselves.

The sound is loud and vegetable. It comes from every side. Deep, slow creaks and groans as woody stems expand and rub. Sharp pops as seedpods burst. The hiss of steam escaping from a tear in the ground. The drumming of hot water on a leaf. The low, hollow moan of wind moving through tall empty stems, like an organ built by no one. When something collapses—and something is always collapsing—there is a long, tearing sound, then a splash, then a cloud of yellow spores. There are no animal sounds. No birds, no insects, no voices. After a while you notice that the loudness has no mouth. It is all water, wind, and the strained tissue of plants.

No animal life has ever touched this place. There are no grazers to crop the leaves, no pollinators to visit the flowers, no fish in the hot ponds. The only consumers are fungi, bacteria, and older plants that parasitize younger ones. Reproduction is by wind-borne spores, water-borne fragments, and the rooting of fallen limbs. The absence of animals does not make the place still. It is turbulent, restless, full of motion. But the motion is the motion of growth and collapse, not of creatures.

The vegetation is total, but not in the way of a closed forest. It is total in that it is the world: the ground, the walls, the roofs, the reservoirs, the bridges. Yet the density is sparse, because each giant organism keeps space around itself. The gaps are not empty; they are territory, filled with steam and falling water and light. The plants need the gaps as much as they need their mass. They grow outward into emptiness, then fill it, then fail, and the emptiness returns.

The landscape is a construction, but the construction is not made by hands. It is grown. Specialized plants act as scaffolds: fast, brittle, tall. Others are binders, twining around the scaffolds and pulling them into arches. Still others are bladders, swelling with water and lifting great plates of tissue. And there are dissolvers, fungi and parasitic plants that eat the scaffolding from inside, triggering collapse. This is the cycle: a tower of bonewood rises in a day, its joints bound by tendon-vines. Water is pumped into its hollow core. The pressure swells the walls, opens fissures, and the tower groans. Then the dissolvers find the fissures, and the tower falls. By the next day, a new shoot has risen from the wreckage.

It is a kind of storm-state. The whole plateau has the energy of a bazaar or an anthill, but there are no workers. Every shoot, tendril, and root is a worker. The terrain rearranges itself not in geological time but in the time of weather. A map drawn at dawn would be wrong by noon. The only constant is the wet heat, the steam, the green light, and the noise.

Some of the structures look deliberate. There are spiraling towers whose side-branches follow the same logarithmic curve. There are gutters that route water into reservoirs with an uncanny economy. There are membranes that curl open when the steam pressure rises and close when it falls, like valves. There are bridges that tighten in the morning heat and sag at night. It is easy to think of them as designed, but there is no designer. The design is the blind result of growth, stress, failure, and regrowth. It is a kind of intelligence without mind, a calculation in cellulose and water.

At the edge of one basin, a thick leaf the size of a door slowly curls and uncurls as the water level changes, diverting overflow into a different channel. It moves because its upper and lower surfaces swell at different rates. It is not an animal. It has no muscle, no nerve. But it is watching the water in the only way a plant can watch: by being shaped by it.

The place is high and young and full of falling. The chasms between the towers drop into green mist, not into rock. The air is thin but so wet that it feels heavy. The sun is hot on the skin, and the steam is hotter. Every surface is slick. The ground flexes underfoot, and when you stand still too long, a warm puddle begins to form around your feet, condensing from your own warmth. A human visitor here would be an intrusion, an animal in a world without them.

The rules are simple. Everything is grown, not made; the built world is plant. Nothing is permanent; the landscape is a process, not a place. Water is the engine; heat and water drive expansion, contraction, steam pressure, and collapse. There are no animals; the only motion is plant, water, wind, and heat. The gaps are structural; emptiness is part of the architecture. The land is young and will not get time to soften.

And the strange thing is not that the plants build. It is that the building never stops. The place is not a wilderness. It is not a city. It is a high, hot, weeping machine of living tissue, taking itself apart and putting itself back together in the steam.
