The valley lies below the surrounding world, a long sunken trough whose rim is broken basalt and fresh gravel. Its floor sits far enough down that the water table is not a distant idea but a presence: water stands in every hollow, seeps from every crack in the wall, drips from every branch. The air is cold enough that breath turns to vapor, and the light is the ordinary grey of an overcast morning—bright enough to see color, dim enough to make the pools look like polished slate.

It is a staircase of water, not one lake but dozens of pools stepping down the length of the valley. They are held back by dams—not a few, but hundreds, curving across channels, linking boulders, arcing over spillways. The dams look new: sticks still pale, mud still dark and raw, edges sharp where teeth have stripped bark. The banks of the canals between pools are freshly cut, unsoftened by age. The valley floor was recently carved by retreating ice and flood, and the land still has a sharp-elbowed, unfinished quality. Boulders sit with unweathered faces. Gravel bars are clean and angular. Nothing here has had time to round itself down.

The builders have been busy. This is a settlement in the fullest sense: lodges rise from the pools, shaggy domes of sticks and mud, some clustered into neighborhoods, some solitary, all with underwater entrances. Canals braid between them, straight enough to be deliberate, their sides packed smooth by use. There are side channels, bypasses, small weirs, food rafts of willow branches anchored in backwaters. Every pond has a dam; every dam has a lodge or a burrow; every stretch of bank has a slide or a path. It is urban in density and organization, but built entirely of living and once-living things: chewed wood, carried mud, rolled stones. No metal, no fire, no glass. The only tools are teeth and paws.

The vegetation is lush in the way cold wetlands are lush: willow and alder thickets so dense they lean on one another; sphagnum moss in red and amber mounds; ferns and horsetails in the wet shade; pale lichen furring every trunk. Moss covers the dams and lodges until the structures look like low natural hills. There is no bare ground. Even the fresh mud at the spillways is already greened with algae and crossed by tracks. The saturation is total—the ground is a sponge, the air tastes of wet wood and cold mud, and every surface drips.

The animals are everywhere but quiet. Dark fish hang in the cold pools. A heron stands motionless at a spillway. Muskrat trails part the cattails, and otter and mink prints overlap on the dam tops. Small brown birds move through the willows without sound. The cold and the moss swallow noise; even the water over the dams is a soft hiss, more texture than sound. There is no wind. Occasionally a drop falls from a branch, or a far-off tail slaps the water, and then the silence closes back over it.

Yet the valley is active. Water moves from pool to pool, carrying silt. A willow tilts and falls, felled silently, then floats down a canal. Below one dam, a spillway is working a raw notch into the mud, and by morning it will be patched. Stakes pushed into the dams have begun to sprout, so the structures are slowly becoming living hedges. Fish find their way upstream through gaps. An otter surfaces, looks, and sinks. The place is under continuous construction, a quiet, cold, saturated ecosystem reorganizing itself with teeth and roots and running water.

From the valley floor you can see the scale of it: a long open reach of pools and green, stepping down between dark walls, dense with lodges and dams, extending far enough that the farthest dams are only lines in the grey air. It is below the plane of the ordinary world—a young, crowded, sunken marsh-city, alive and still being built.
