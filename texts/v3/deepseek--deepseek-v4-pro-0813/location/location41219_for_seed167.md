The place is a drowned city, and it has been drowned long enough that the water no longer feels like an event. It is the climate, the medium, the whole sky. The water is warm, close to skin temperature, and so full of silt, tannin, and drifting life that light is a memory. At noon the surface is a brown-green rind; a few meters down the water is bronze; by the streets it is black. You move through it not by falling but by being held. The city occupies a low coastal plain, a former river delta that the sea took slowly, so the streets are shallow—six meters, twelve, twenty—but the darkness is not from depth. It is from thickness. The water carries the land in it: mud, leaf-rot, pollen, old plaster, bone dust, the faint mineral breath of distant vents. This is not open ocean. It is a warm, silty sea over a town, a wall of water that has replaced the sky.

The city itself is still urban, stubbornly so. It was built of stone and brick and tile, and those materials remain, though softened by time. The buildings stand shoulder to shoulder along narrow lanes; there are arcades, stairwells, a central market hall, a bridge whose arches still hold. But the edges are gone. Water has eaten the corners, rounded the lintels, turned roofs into slumping shoulders. It is a settled ruin—not collapsing, but settled. The city has weathered into a permanent shape, a reef made of architecture. Every wall is crusted with tubeworms, barnacles, sponges, and coral-like animals that have laid their own skeletons over the stone. The old surfaces are no longer clean; they are layered, encrusted, near-touching. The buildings are dense, and so is the water between them. It tastes of lime and plankton and something faintly strange, not from metal—no metal remains—but from the chemistry of thousands of small lives.

Vegetation is scarce. There are no forests, no kelp, no bright gardens. In the dark, only a few pale sea grasses grow on the silt banks, and near the old cistern there is a patch of dark algae that waves in the current like fine hair. The plants are outnumbered by animals. The city is packed with fauna. Small translucent fish hang in the lanes in sheets, flicking direction without sound. Moray eels live in the doorways. Groupers sit in the ground-floor rooms like boulders, their gills opening and closing with a slow bellows. Urchins graze the walls in herds, leaving pale trails on the encrusting life. Crustaceans clatter in the rubble. Above the market square, a school of jacks circles for hours, and a cloud of larvae makes the water grainy. There are predators too: a shadow passes, and the school folds into a stairwell as if pulled by a string. The whole city is a vertical reef, and every window, every balcony, every collapsed roof is a territory.

The place is not silent, but it is mostly quiet. Sound travels strangely in the warm, silty water. You hear the city as a series of small, close noises: the snap of shrimp, the creak of old masonry, the click of barnacles, the low grind of a fish’s teeth. There is no surf, no wind, no engine. The loudest sounds are the deep thud of a grouper’s tail against a wall, or the distant crumble of a wall slumping into an alley. Twice a day the tide turns, and the streets become rivers. The current picks up silt and shell dust and old mud, and for an hour the water is a moving fog; then it settles, and the streets are redrawn in new drifts. This is the city’s weather: a slow, warm, underwater weather of currents and sediment and spawning. There are seasons, too. In the warmest month, the water turns faintly milky with spawn, and every surface becomes a nursery.

There is almost no light. The darkness is not empty; it is full of small bodies, and occasionally they make their own light. A comb jelly blooms pale blue in an alley. Green pinpricks of tiny crustaceans rise from the silt like sparks. A fish passes with a line of bioluminescence along its belly, and for a second the old market square becomes a map of itself.

The rules of this place are simple. There is no air. There is no fire. There is no tool. The only technology is biological: settlement, secretion, growth, predation, decay. The city is not abandoned; it is inherited. Its architecture now belongs to the animals that live in it, and they are slowly rebuilding it in their own way. In the oldest district, a kind of coral has grown so precisely into the shape of windows and doorways that it is hard to tell what was built and what was secreted. Some of the walls have become a living tissue of polyps and shell. The city is not dead; it is becoming an animal. And there is a strange thing about the water itself: at the level of the old windowsills, a layer of warmer, saltier water lies beneath the upper water, and it shimmers like a glass floor when a fish crosses it. It is not harmful, but it divides the city into two districts—the drifting life above and the crawling life below. Creatures pass through it slowly, as if stepping through a membrane. That is the border between the upper city and the lower.

You can follow the old boulevard for kilometers. It runs from the market square, past the customs house, through the tenement rows, out to where the buildings thin and the mud takes over. The city is not a single image; it is a series of neighborhoods, each with its own depth, its own current, its own smell of stone and life. In one, the roofs have fallen and the streets are wide; in another, the buildings still rise five stories, and you must swim through their interiors. The place extends beyond the immediate—there are whole districts you cannot see, further out, where the water is darker and the fish are larger. It is a drowned town, but it is a town still, with its own order, its own quiet, its own slow architecture of water and bone.
