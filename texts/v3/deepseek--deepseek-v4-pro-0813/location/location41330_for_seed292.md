The first thing is the noise. It is not one sound but a braid of them: water falling, water pushing through stone, the cries of birds by the thousand, wind singing in old steel cable, and underneath it all a low mechanical hum that never quite stops. The air is cold and wet, about eight degrees, and it tastes of salt and lime.

The place is a drowned karst plateau. The old mountain range is gone. What remains is its skeleton: hundreds of flat-topped towers and stacks of pale limestone, some no wider than a door, some broad enough to hold a wet meadow or a small wind-bent wood. Their tops are all nearly the same height, the remnant of an ancient plain that has been dissolved and cut apart over millions of years. The sea fills every old valley, every sinkhole, every collapsed cavern. The result is a labyrinth of channels, sounds, and blind bays stretching forty or fifty miles in every direction. In the narrows, the stacks are so close their ledges almost touch; the water between them is dark and moving.

The cliffs drop straight into the sea. They are not clean walls; they are fluted, undercut, riddled with sea caves and vertical runnels. The rock has been smoothed by weather until it looks like old candle wax or wet bone. In places it has collapsed into arches and natural bridges. The stone is gray-white, stained black by lichen at the tide line, streaked green by moss above, and rust-red where minerals bleed from the rock. The oldest surfaces are polished almost to a shine. Even the rubble at the cliff bases is rounded and settled. This is an ancient landscape, near its final form, but the sea is still working on it. The tops stand several hundred meters above the water; from sea level the cliffs are dizzying, and from the top a boat is a speck.

Water is not a boundary here; it is the main medium. The channels are deep, often thirty or fifty meters a stone’s throw from a cliff. The water is cold enough to numb a hand in a minute. It is tidal, with a range of several meters. At mid-tide the current runs fast enough to pull a boat off course. Where two channels meet, the water folds into standing waves and whirlpools that open and close without pattern. When the tide turns, the whole system seems to reverse with a sigh. Rain comes several times a day, dense and fine, and every cliff carries its own temporary waterfall. Fresh water pours off the tops in thin silver threads, mixes with salt spray, and makes the air taste like stone and brine.

The light is ordinary. It is not a dim place or a bright one. Much of the time there is a high maritime overcast, a silver-gray that makes the green of the vegetation almost glow. When the cloud breaks, the water turns the color of old bottle glass and the wet rock dazzles. At night there is no great darkness; the sky is often low and reflective, and a few small electric lights burn on the inhabited stacks, red and white, reflected in the moving water.

The vegetation is lush but cool. Moss grows over everything that is not bare rock, thick as a mattress and dripping. Ferns grow out of vertical walls and hang in mats. Dwarf birch, rowan, alder, and juniper grow on the larger tops, wind-sheared and horizontal. There are bogs and tarns on the broad summits, full of dark water and sphagnum, with sundews and cotton grass. The green is dense because water and light are constant, but the cold keeps the trees small and slow. In the splash zone, kelp lies in dark, slippery sheets. Below the surface, a kelp forest rises and falls with the surge, stipes as thick as wrists, blades streaming. The green continues underwater, a vertical meadow going down until the light fails.

The fauna is abundant and loud. Every ledge and every hole seems to have a bird in it: guillemots, kittiwakes, gulls, cormorants, puffins, black guillemots in the sea caves. The noise of one colony is like a factory floor; the noise of many colonies braided together is the background of the place. Their droppings paint the rock white. Seals haul out on low skerries and sing in the evening. Below water, fish move in silver threads, and crabs pick through the kelp. In the moss on top, voles and shrews move. Midges cloud in the still moments, and bats come out at dusk. The abundance is not just visible; it is loud and smelly and constant.

Human presence is scattered and small. There is no city, no town, barely a hamlet. What exists is sparse infrastructure, mostly industrial, mostly old, some of it still running. On one broad stack there is a small hydroelectric station: a concrete blockhouse with a green copper roof and a penstock dropping down the cliff. It uses the water from a summit tarn and the tide to turn a turbine. When it runs, it sends a low thrum through the rock. It powers a few things: a string of navigation lights, a winch house, a radio repeater, a workshop. The power cable sags across a channel between two stacks, and the wind makes it sing. There is a cableway, old but working, with a steel basket for moving supplies and sometimes people. Stone steps cut into a cliff disappear underwater at high tide and reappear at low. A few huts of driftwood, corrugated iron, and salvaged ship plating cling to ledges. Most are empty. One has a small chimney that smokes on wet mornings. There are abandoned quarries and mine adits, rusted winches, barrel hoops, a rail track ending in air. The industrial remains are not grand; they are the marks of small-scale extraction and transport—stone, ore, fish, people—left to weather. Some machines still run because the water and wind are constant. A diesel generator coughs on one island when the tide turns, then settles into a low thrum. A pump clanks somewhere. A wind turbine on a high stack turns with a sound like a slow heart. But these are only points of noise in a larger noise.

The weather is turbulent. Storms come through quickly, piling waves into the narrows. The sea can change from glass to white-capped chop in the time it takes to cross a channel. Waterfalls shift with the rain. Rockfalls thunder from the cliffs, and the echo rolls around the stacks. The bird colonies rise in panic and settle again. The cables sway. The kelp writhes. The tide is always working, moving water through the maze, lifting and dropping the boats, exposing and covering the stone steps. It is a place of constant small change, though the large forms are ancient. The limestone is still dissolving, a little at a time, and the sea is still cutting, but the changes are slow enough that the place feels eternal.

The rules of the place are verticality and water. Everything either climbs, clings, swims, or flies. To move horizontally you need a boat or a cable or a very good sense of the tides. The rock is limestone, so the tops may hold small lakes that drain through holes; caves and sinkholes are common. The sea is salt, but rain and streams are fresh. The lower cliffs are marine, the upper cliffs are terrestrial, and the line between moves with the tide. The machines are maintained by a few people or not maintained at all; power is intermittent, but the water is always reliable. Nothing here is supernatural. It is only an old, wet, loud, green, cold, half-drowned place, still being worked on by the sea.
