From above, the sea is a flat gray-blue, and then the drowned ridge shoulders upward out of the abyss. Its two highest fangs rise to just below the surface, and between them hangs a saddle of rock not much longer than a cathedral nave. Sunlight reaches it. It is ordinary daylight, blue-shifted, bright enough to cast shadows on the raw stone.

The water is cool, around seven degrees, and it moves slowly but never quite goes still. The saddle is high ground—not a beach or an island, but high in the sea, a summit plateau surrounded by deeper black. The rock is young. It has not been worn down. It is black basalt, glassy, split into sharp splinters and fresh plates. There is almost no sediment in the hollow; the floor is bare stone, cracked in places, as if the mountain had newly broken apart and the sea had not yet begun to soften it.

From the cracks come seeps. Cold seawater has traveled into fractures below, met mantle rock, and returned changed: loaded with methane, hydrogen, sulfide, dissolved carbonate. Where the seep-water meets the sea, pale carbonate precipitates out. Over time it builds fluted pipes, stacked flanges, branching tubes, knobbed chimneys. Some stand as tall as a person; most are waist-high, clustered around the fissures. They are not built structures. No hands made them, and there are no artifacts. But the place looks industrial. It has the appearance of a small factory floor—chimneys, flues, settling basins, pipework—all grown by chemistry and pressure. The basalt and brine make weak electrical potentials; mats of bacteria grow in patterns like circuitry. It is a machine made of stone, water, and bacteria, with no designer.

There are no plants. No kelp, no eelgrass, no sea lettuce, no drifting sargassum. In the clear light, the absence of green is strange. The floor should be a meadow, but it is mineral and animal instead. The only living mats are bacterial—orange, rust, white, violet-black—spread over the rocks in fine loops and blistered crusts. They are not plants. They feed on the chemical seam where the seep-water rises.

The animals are dense beyond comfort. Mussels are layered over mussels, their black shells wedged into every fissure, forming drifts that cover the rock. From the beds rise clumps of tubeworms, red plumes out, snapping back when a shadow crosses. Crabs with pale, hairy arms move over the mussels, prying. Small armored snails graze the bacterial mats. Amphipods thicken the water like smoke; they drift in clouds and scatter when fish pass. Fish are everywhere—silver schools that move in sheets and braids, parting around the carbonate chimneys. Skates lie half-buried at the saddle’s edges. A wolf eel watches from a crevice. A small octopus flows across a basalt slab, turning from black to white to black. Glass sponges stand in the dimmer overhangs, animals that resemble living lattices. The density is not just abundant; it is near-touching, layered, crowded into every volume.

The sound is loud and continuous. Snapping shrimp crackle like a field of static, so many that the noise becomes a bed under everything. The carbonate chimneys thrum as gas moves through their flutings. Some huff with a regular, piston-like beat. The mussel beds click as crabs work them. Fish schools turn with a low whump. Far away, whale song passes through the water and the rock, too loud to ignore, as though the mountain itself were singing. The whole saddle roars.

The strangest things are the brine pools. They sit in shallow depressions, too salty and too heavy to mix with the surrounding sea. Their surfaces are mirrors. Looking into one, the black fangs and the blue light reappear upside-down, sharper than the real world. Fish avoid them. At their margins, the water shimmers like oil, and a few specialized worms live exactly where the salt begins. Above strong gas seeps, the water loses buoyancy; a swimmer who strays into the bubble column can sink as though the sea had opened its hand.

For all the noise, the place changes slowly. The chimneys grow a few centimeters in a decade. The mussel beds thicken over generations. The gas paths shift only across decades and centuries. The rock is young, but on a geologic clock; the sea has not had time to round its edges. The whole place feels like a small, loud, crowded, mineral engine running on cold chemistry, under normal light, with no built thing in it—only stone, water, bacteria, and too many animals.
