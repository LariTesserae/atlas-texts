High on the rim of a still-rising scarpland, the first thing you notice is the heat’s strange intimacy. It comes from overhead—a close, bright sun in a high, thin sky—and from underneath, where the rock is too new to have cooled. The elevation makes the horizon fall away more sharply than it should, and the sky overhead has a darker, almost violet tint, while the lower world beyond the scarp sits in a pale, dusty haze. This is not a desert, though it looks like one from a distance. Up close, it is wet in unexpected places: steam leaks from hairline fissures, pipes weep condensate, shallow basins of black rock hold brines that gather at night and vanish by afternoon. Water does not yet know how to run here. It gathers, drips, seeps, and condenses rather than flowing.

The terrain is embryonic. There are no rounded cobbles, no soil, no sand of the familiar kind. Every surface is raw basalt, scoria, andesite, tuff—edges sharp as broken glass, ropy lava frozen mid-turn, vertical fresh faces where the plateau is still separating along unseen faults. The landscape has not been worn smooth by time. It is still being built from below, and the city is built into it like a scaffolding around a patient wound.

The settlement begins at the scarp edge as a cluster of condenser towers: tall, flanged, wrapped in coils of pipe like industrial spires. From them, terraces step down the new rock, each level crowded with structures that seem less designed than accumulated. The density is intense. Habitation stacks lean against factory blocks. Cableways cross at seven or eight different heights. Bundled pipes form the walls of narrow alleys, and stairways are cut directly into cooling lava. There are no plants—no green at all except the pale verdigris of copper oxidized by sulfur steam. The city is a single continuous mass, a vertical district that clings to the raw cliff and drills inward into the hot rock for heat and stability, while reaching outward on cantilevered platforms for space.

The architecture is industrial, but not like a factory district from an older and gentler world. Here the machinery is becoming geology. Steam turbines hiss inside stone housings. Flywheels turn so slowly they look still from a distance. Electric cables sag between towers, not from neglect but because their insulation has begun to mineralize, thickening with the same crystalline crust that forms on the rocks. Streets are not paved so much as ground flat where necessary, then covered with metal gratings and pipeline bundles. The older districts have been buried by fresh lava flows and continue to operate inside the rock, reachable only through tunnels. Maps of the city include strata, not just streets.

Light is ordinary in its daily rhythm—the sun rises and sets, day follows night—but at this elevation it has a punishing clarity. There is no vegetation or soil moisture to soften it. The glare off metal, salt, and mineral surfaces is bright enough that people move in the shaded canyons between buildings or wear narrow goggles. Yet shade is not cool. Heat radiates from every wall, and the ground itself is warm through boot soles. Steam banks roll through the lower terraces at dawn and dusk, turning the light silver and diffuse for a few minutes before the sun burns through.

The place is mostly quiet. There is a low background of turbine whine so steady it becomes a kind of silence, the way a familiar heartbeat is silence. Water drips. Steam escapes in thin jets that sound like whispered consonants. A pressure valve lifts somewhere with a long, slow hiss. Metal joints settle with groans that travel through the whole structure. Footsteps are rare. There is no birdsong, no insect drone. The loudest events are geological: the distant crack of a new fissure, the low thump of a lava tongue spilling into an abandoned courtyard, the creak of the city adjusting to a ground that never stops rising.

The ecology is mineral rather than vegetal. Without plants, the base of the living system is chemical—thin mats of sulfur-reducing microbes in hot sumps, so sparse and intermittent they feel more like a rumor than a biome. More visible are the crystalline growths. Needles of gypsum and halite form around leaks. Calcite spires accumulate under dripping condensate. Salt crusts creep across the rocks in fern-like patterns. People tend these in places, not for food but because the mineral growths are useful: they track the chemistry of the steam, indicate where water will condense, and slowly seal cracks in the city. There are gardens of stone. The gardeners do not plant; they adjust the flow of water and the temperature of pipes to shape the accretion.

Fauna is rare. A few pale, many-legged creatures live in the condensation sumps, moving between droplets. Birds are almost absent; at the highest terraces the air is too thin for anything but an occasional black-winged scavenger riding thermals up from lower valleys. The most common animal is a small heat-tolerant mite that lives in the mineral crust and emerges only when a pipe cools enough to gather moisture. Seeing one is an event, a small proof that life can still find a purchase.

The city’s rules follow from the landscape. Water is not owned by individuals but by vent factions—guilds that maintain the condensers and the mineral gardens. Heat is abundant, but it must be moved carefully; a closed valve can raise the temperature of an entire terrace by twenty degrees in a day. Construction is slow because the ground is still shifting. Buildings are not designed to resist erosion but to survive the opposite: the land is so new it is still adding material. Lava wells up in a lower courtyard and is left to cool into a new floor. The city grows by accretion as much as by design. The population is not large, but the built density is enormous—the density is of infrastructure, machinery, pipework, stored heat, and layered construction rather than crowds.

The strangeness of the place is not that it is alien, but that it has become geological. The older parts of the city are no longer distinct from the cliff. Pipes have been encased in mineral layers until they look like natural tubes. Steel beams have rusted and then been infiltrated by salt until they resemble stone. Factories have been buried by lava flows and continue to operate inside the rock, their vibrations felt through the ground rather than heard. There are districts that exist only as layers of abandoned machinery now fused into the escarpment. Some of the machines are still running.

At the boundary where the city meets the raw plateau, the world is still forming. New land rises slowly. Steam vents open. A sulfur crust forms overnight, dissolves in the morning dew, re-forms in a different shape. Erosion has not yet begun. There is no soil to wash away, no old valleys to deepen. The only dynamic is patient: the mountain range is being built from below, and the city is building itself into it, layer by layer, pipe by pipe, mineral by mineral. The land is a fresh page, and the city is the first mark on it—not a settlement placed on the world, but a settlement becoming part of the world’s own unfinished body.
