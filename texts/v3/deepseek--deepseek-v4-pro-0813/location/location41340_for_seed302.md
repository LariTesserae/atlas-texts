The first thing is the sound. It comes up the valley like weather, not as separate noises but as a single continuous pressure: a bright, rattling shriek of water over stone, all overlapping, so dense that the air seems to have its own tone. At the valley’s bottom, you feel it in your sternum. It is not a waterfall’s roar exactly; it is a chorus of small falls, ten thousand thin tongues of water hitting mineral lips, each a different pitch, none pausing. If you cup your hands over your ears, the sound gets worse, because it is coming through the ground. The ground is ringing.

Above the sound, the light is almost violent. At this altitude the sky is a dark, bruised blue even at midday, and the sun is a white point that seems to focus on the valley floor. There are no shadows except the sharp ones cast by the rims of the terraces, and those are so bright-edged they hurt. The air is cool, maybe just above freezing, and it is saturated: every breath tastes like wet stone, and a fine mist hangs over the lower pools, not warm fog but cold veils that condense on your skin and on the stone.

There is not a single blade of grass, not a cushion of moss, not a lichen. The ground is mineral all the way down. The only colors are the whites and pale ambers and blue-greens of the stone and water.

The valley floor is a broad sloping shelf between two ridges, eight or ten kilometers from one side to the other. The slope is not steep, but it is continuous, and it is entirely covered with terraces. They start below the ridgeline as narrow, scalloped bowls and multiply as you descend. By the middle of the valley, they are packed so tightly that one terrace’s lip is the next one’s back wall. There is no interval, no beach, no boulder field. The whole floor has been divided into pools, and every pool overflows into the next.

From above, the terraces are not a single staircase but a labyrinth of nested crescents, each opening downslope. Some are large enough to lie in, others smaller than a hand. They interlock like fish scales or the surface of a brain. Their rims are razor-thin at the top and wider at the base, built of centuries of mineral deposition.

The water does not arrive in rivers. It seeps from the ground itself. At the heads of the valley, cold springs pour from low openings in the stone, not violently but steadily. Elsewhere, water beads on the surface of the rock and runs down in sheets. The air is so humid that water condenses even without rain. The cloud layer that often fills the valley as low fog is itself a source; every stone drips, every wall weeps. By the time a single drop has traveled from the ridgeline to the valley’s mouth, it has crossed a thousand terrace lips and has been divided a hundred times.

The stone is unusual. It is a sinter, but almost glassy, not powdery like common tufa. It looks like frozen flowstone, pale as milk, with a faint blue-green translucence in the thinnest edges. In the brightest light it seems to glow from within, as though the mineral holds the sun. Some rims are ribbed, some smooth, some covered with tiny dendrites of darker mineral that are not plants but manganese films. The pools are mostly shallow, a few centimeters to a few meters deep, and their floors are rippled with growth rings, each one a season of mineral deposition. The water is clear to the bottom, but not blue; it is white-green where deep, like thin jade, or completely colorless where it is only a film.

Close up, the noise resolves into components. One pool might make a high, bell-like note where the water falls over a hollow lip; another makes a low, wet ruffle where a sheet slides over a smooth slope; another a rapid ticking where droplets fall onto a ridged stone. The sounds do not blend into white noise. They stay separate but too many to count, so the ear cannot follow any single one. It is a chord with thousands of voices. There is no silence anywhere. The sound bounces off the ridges and comes back doubled. If you stop walking, the ground underfoot continues to vibrate.

The place is not dead, though it has no plants. In the water, on the underwater faces of the rims, there are films of bacteria, gray-pink and rust-colored, that oxidize dissolved iron and sulfur brought up from below. They are the base of the food chain. They are the only producers, and they do not grow upward. Above them, the water is almost lifeless, but not empty. Rarely, a white thread-worm, thin as a hair, drifts in the low current, grazing on the film. In deeper pools there are blind copepods, so transparent that their only visible parts are two tiny black eye-spots. They are scattered, a few per square meter. There is also a kind of flightless midge that skates on the surface film; it appears singly, lives for a day or two, and dies without a sound. The density of life is tiny; the density of the land is overwhelming.

One of the strange things is that the water surfaces are not smooth, even where the flow is slow. They carry standing waves, cross-hatched patterns that do not move, as though the water has been poured into an invisible mold. The waves are stable from day to day. If you disturb a pool with a stone, the ripples race outward, bounce, interfere, and then slowly settle back into the same pattern. The water returns to its old shape exactly. It is not memory; it is the shape of the current and the shape of the basin, but the exactness is eerie.

From the ridgeline, the terraces look too regular. They are not random. They form concentric arcs, spirals, radial fans, as if the whole valley had been rippled by a single vast drop that struck and was frozen. Perhaps that is what happened, in the deep past, when the water first found its paths and the mineral began to build. Each generation of rims echoed the previous one until the echo became the landscape.

The most important rule of the place is that it does not change. The water flows, but it never erodes in a way that matters. As fast as the water can wear a channel, the mineral waters deposit a new layer to fill it. As fast as a rim grows outward, the same water undercuts it just enough to keep its profile. The two processes are in equilibrium. The terraces are stable over centuries, perhaps over millennia. This is why the sound is the same now as it was a thousand years ago, and why the standing waves are unchanged. There are no seasons, no floods, no droughts. The temperature rises and falls with day and night, but never enough to freeze the water or change the mineral. The clouds come and go, but they do not alter the water’s course. Nothing collapses. Nothing falls. There is no room for a new feature, because every place is already occupied.

There is no trace of people here. No path, no cairn, no carved mark, no structure. If a person stands here, they are the only made thing. The stone does not record footprints for long; the water films erase them, or the mineral encases them and turns them into another faint rim. There are no tools, no fire rings, no shelter. The place is not hostile; it simply has no need of anything except its own cycles.

If you stay too long, the sound and light begin to erase your thoughts. The constant ringing makes it hard to remember that there are places without water. The brightness flattens depth until the valley seems like a single white plate. Then, slowly, you notice the detail again: the tiny standing waves, the blind copepods, the white thread-worms, the way each pool has a different note. The place is not empty. It is full—too full of water, stone, light, and sound—and every part of it has been the same for longer than any living thing can measure.

It simply continues, weeping and ringing, bright and deafening, unchanged.
