It is not under the sea, though at first the ear believes it. The sound is wrong for a forest and wrong for a mountain; it is the sound of a wet hand moved slowly over stone. But the water here is mostly overhead, and underfoot, and all around as mist. The place is a high plateau drowned in its own cloud.

You would not know the elevation except by the thinness of the air and the way your breath comes a little harder until you have been there for a few days. There is no view down to any lower world. The cloud seals the plateau like a lid. Warmth rises from the stone itself, from deep conduits that have no name, and when the cooler air of the heights meets that warmth, the sky begins to sweat. This is the Drown: a high country that is never dry, where the main weather is not rain but a kind of perpetual atmospheric water, a fog so dense it runs in rivulets down the face of the stone.

The land is not old. This is the first and strangest fact. Elsewhere, hills are worn down, valleys deepened, boulders rounded by time. Here the world is being made, not unmade. The rock is raw and pale and still uncertain in places, like wet clay that has just begun to set. The terraces are formed of soft travertine, the same kind of stone that builds around hot springs, and it does not so much erode as accumulate. The water is milky, faintly greenish, warm as a bath. It carries dissolved stone, and whenever it slows it lays down a fresh rind of rock. Edges curl. Pools rim themselves. Channels grow shallow. A fallen leaf left in a warm pool will be covered in a brittle skin of mineral before the week is out; after a month it becomes a little stone ghost of a leaf, veins and stem intact.

The terrain is a staircase of these pools and terraces, descending in broad, lumpy steps through warm mist. There are no sharp ravines, no cliffs of shattered rock, almost no sand. The shapes are rounded, beaded, draped, as if the landscape has been poured rather than carved. Streams do not cut downward. They slide over the edges of the terraces in thin sheets, each one adding another layer. At the bottom of the larger basins the water stands hip-deep, warm and perfectly clear, with a white mineral floor that is still thickening. The high points are not dry; they are only less wet. There is no dry stone in the whole region.

The vegetation is dense beyond belief. It is not a forest of tall, straight trunks but a layered mat of mosses, ferns, and broad-leaved plants that grow over one another in a slow competition for what little light there is. The moss is the main forest. It covers the terraces, hangs over the rims, climbs the tree roots, pads the edges of the pools, and grows over the houses. It is not the low moss of a northern rock but a deep, soft, many-layered growth, sometimes waist-high, sometimes shoulder-high. Ferns unroll from its surface. Tree-like plants stand in the pools on bundles of dark aerial roots; their leaves are wide, leathery, and nearly black, because they must catch every scrap of gray-green light. They do not reach for the sun, which is barely present. They spread outward, making fans and shelves and canopies just above the water.

The light is like the inside of a shell. The sun is not visible. It is not dark, but it is not day either. There is a permanent twilight, gray-green, evenly scattered, with no strong shadows. The mist makes distance collapse. A wall ten strides away looks like a drawing done in charcoal and moss. A terrace fifty strides away fades into a pale greenish wall. The edge of the world is rarely more than a suggestion. In the deep hollows between terraces, the cloud sometimes forms standing curtains, so thick that you cannot see your own feet, and you walk through water that is not falling so much as hanging.

The sound is mostly quiet. The main note is a fine, continuous hiss, as if the whole landscape is breathing through its teeth. Under it there is the drip of water from leaves and stone edges, each drop a tiny clock in the dark. Wood creaks somewhere in the town. Rope stretches with a low, fibrous moan. The moss makes a sound when the air moves, like a hand brushing suede. Voices are swallowed by the wet air, so a person standing one terrace away speaks as if from inside a closed room. There is no birdsong and no insect drone. The water is the only music, and it repeats.

The animals are few, and they are strange. It should be a place of swarming life, given the heat and the water and the green growth, but it is not. There are no deer, no monkeys, no flocks of birds. The fauna is scattered and mostly small and mostly hidden. In the warm pools live pale shrimp, almost transparent, that move backward when a shadow crosses the surface. On the leaves there are slow, soft-bodied creatures like slugs with feathery gills, grazing on the film of algae. In the dimmest hour, gray moths with wet-looking wings gather at the cisterns and do not make a sound. Sometimes a larger thing is glimpsed at the edge of the mist, long and low, moving with too many legs, but no one speaks of it except to say that it was probably only a shadow. The people do not hunt, and they keep no herds. There would be nothing to herd.

The settlement is not a village. It is a town, dense and layered, built along the main mineral terraces where hot water rises strongly enough to be shaped. At first you do not see it, because it is the same color as the stone and the moss. The houses are packed close, sharing walls, stacked four or five deep against the slope. They are made of two things: a framework of dark wood, and a crust of stone that has been encouraged to grow over it. The builders raise a lattice of branches and split cane, then direct the mineral water to run down it. Over months the water lays down a paste. Over years the paste becomes shell. The people scrape it, smooth it, redirect the flow to leave openings for doors and windows and shelves. Older houses are solid stone, their original wood long gone, but the grain of the lattice still shows on the outside, like fossilized basketwork. Newer houses are still wrapped in reed and cane, with white mineral paste weeping through the gaps, giving them the look of unfinished nests.

The town is crowded. One family’s roof is another family’s terrace. The streets are not dry streets but narrow channels, some paved with rounded stone, some simply flowing ankle-deep. Stairs are cut into the living rock or lashed from poles, and they wind between walls with no clear plan. There are public cisterns, shared by several houses, where the water pools and thickens at the edges. There are rope-and-wood bridges across the wider streams, just wide enough for one person, and they sway without making much noise. The main avenue is a watercourse: a long wooden trough, dark and greased with algae, carrying hot water from the upper springs down to the lower gardens. People walk beside it or along its raised edges. It is the spine of the town.

The town has no stone quarries and almost no foundations. Digging is nearly useless here. The ground is too young, too porous, and it heals itself with new stone. Instead, the people grow their foundations upward. They lay bundles of twigs in the water in the shape of a wall and wait. The mineral water does the rest. A season’s crust can support a floor. A year’s crust can bear the weight of a sleeping loft. In ten years, a new room has become part of the hill. The people are not builders in the usual sense; they are gardeners of stone. Their tools are simple: wooden mallets, chisels of hard volcanic glass, scrapers made from the ribs of the larger pool creatures. A few families own knives of hammered metal from the lowlands, traded at great cost, but most work is done with patience and wet lime.

The daily work is not what you would expect. Elsewhere, people fight erosion, shore up riverbanks, fear the loss of soil. Here the danger is accretion. The water wants to fill things in. A path left untended becomes a low wall. A drain becomes a dam. A doorway will close like a wound if the water is allowed to run over it for too long. Every morning, before the light strengthens, the townspeople scrape the edges of the main channels and break the thin rinds that have formed overnight. The sound is a soft, patient scraping, like the sea heard from a long way off. It is quiet work, and it never ends.

The world changes slowly. A terrace widens by the thickness of a fingernail in a year. A stalagmite grows a thumb’s length in a generation. The town looks unfinished because it is unfinished, and always will be. The oldest houses are no longer houses at all. They have swollen and lost their edges, become mounds of smooth stone with the faint impression of a doorway, a window, a wall. The people say that a house is not complete until it has forgotten its door. They do not say it with sadness. It is the local way of saying that a building has become part of the world.

Heat comes from below, so the springs are warm even when the cloud is thick and cool. The people cook in the hot pools, lowering bundles of root and moss into the water in woven baskets. Fire is rare and precious, because there is almost nothing dry enough to burn. A few households keep a small fire in a stone bowl, fed with oil pressed from the dark waxy leaves, and the flames burn low and orange, more for light than for heat. At night, which is only a deepening of the gray, the only glows are those little fires, the pale green phosphorescence of certain wet mosses, and the shifting reflection of the sky in the mineral pools.

The region is not one town but several, scattered along the strongest warm springs, each one built where the stone grows fast enough to shape and slow enough not to overwhelm. Between them there are no roads in the ordinary sense, only stone paths that are already beginning to grow over. Travelers walk from terrace to terrace, through moss and cloud, following the sound of water. The settlements are close enough that a person can go from one to another in half a day, and far enough that each town has its own way of shaping the stone, its own pattern of walls and cisterns and bridges. The people call the whole highland by one name, but the name is not for the land. It is for the condition of being inside it: the Drown.

There is one rule the people repeat to strangers: do not trust the ground to stay where it is. Not because it will move, but because it will grow. A tool left on a ledge will be crusted by afternoon. A footprint in soft lime will be a hard bump by the next week. A dry place, if it is made, will not stay dry unless it is scraped and maintained. The world is not finished, and everyone in it is engaged in the long, quiet work of keeping a little space open against the water’s slow determination to close it all in stone.

And yet it is not an unhappy place. It is dim and wet and strange, but the water is warm, the moss is soft, the dwellings hold heat, and the people move through their narrow alleys with the ease of those who know every surface. They lean on walls that were once their grandparents’ lattice and will one day be their grandchildren’s hillside. They walk through the Drown as through the inside of a great unfinished body, patient and enclosed, listening to the hiss of the world being made.
