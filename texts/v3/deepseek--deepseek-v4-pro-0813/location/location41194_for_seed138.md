You come into it through the chain-link gate at the southwest corner of the intake yard, and the first thing is not sight but sound: a continuous tearing hiss, as if the air itself is being split by steam, undercut by the low drum of a pump. Then the heat and wet reach you. The air is thick, a warm mineral breath that condenses on your skin and on every surface—handrails, tanks, black rock, concrete. The light is dim, the color of tarnished silver: no direct sun, only a hazy white sky and a few weak sodium lamps burning over the walkways as if it were always just before dawn.

The ground is a shelf of young basalt, very low, almost at the level of the lake. It has not been smoothed by rivers or time. It preserves the shapes of its own making: ropy coils, burst blisters, cracked plates with edges sharp as broken crockery. Where men have needed flatness they have poured concrete slabs or bolted down steel grating. The rest is bare black stone, in some places dusted white with soda crystals, in others oozing warm water from invisible seams. Water is not an event here; it is the floor. It weeps from the rock, trickles under the fence, stands in milky pools in the hollows, runs down the outside of the intake pipe in a constant sweat. The lake itself begins a few steps beyond the causeway: a wide alkaline mirror, the same gray as the sky but warmer—too warm to touch for long—and so full of salts that it does not ripple like fresh water. It seems syrupy, almost still.

The yard is small. The whole district inside the fence is roughly the size of a cathedral nave: an intake channel, two shallow evaporation pans, a pump house, a pipe trestle, a low black causeway. You can see its boundaries at once—chain-link on one side, lake on another, a cracked concrete road and a row of power poles on the third. It is urban, not wild, but sparsely built. The structures are set far apart with wide bare flats between them, as if the emptiness were poured as carefully as the concrete. The pump house is a blocky, riveted iron shed, painted gray, its paint blistered by salt and heat. A steam vent stands on its roof, roaring. Along one side run steel pipes, thick with white mineral crust, some sweating hot water, others trembling with the rhythm of the pumps. A narrow-gauge railway comes in from the town past a conveyor and a hopper, built to carry away sacks of dried soda ash. Transformers hum on a raised platform; wires run low over the water, their ceramic insulators furred with salt.

There is almost no vegetation. No reeds, no trees, no grass except a few bristly patches of glasswort growing in a crack where rainwater dilutes the brine. The green life here is mostly slime: mats of cyanobacteria in the shallows, rust-colored and olive; a bright orange bacterial bloom at the hottest inflow; a film of green-black film growing on the wet concrete. Plants cannot root in this water; it is too hot and too alkaline. But the scarcity of plants is not a scarcity of life. The animals are abundant, more abundant than the bare stone first suggests.

The mud along the pan edge moves if you look at it long enough. It is not mud but a carpet of alkali flies, tens of thousands of them, gray and small, packed so densely that their upper layer lifts in a sheet, hovers a few inches, then settles exactly back. The shallows are clouded with brine shrimp, small red specks that turn the water rusty where they gather. At the edge of the first pan, a flock of flamingos stands in the warm, colored water; there are more of them than seem possible, a long pink-and-white line reflecting in the gray mirror. They make a constant low murmur, a barnyard sound hundreds of voices wide. A few small silvery fish, no longer than a finger, live only in the cooler channel where lake water enters the plant; they gather near the pipe outlet. On the concrete walls, white snails cluster. Hermit crabs drag shells from one wet crack to the next. Around the lamps and over the brine pools, midges whine in heavy clouds. The air is thick with small wings.

The ecology runs by a simple rule: the water is too harsh for plants, so food begins as slime—bacteria and algae that feed on the mineral springs and soda. The flies and shrimp graze on the slime. The fish and some birds eat the flies and shrimp. The flamingos filter shrimp and fly larvae from the water. The town adds a second source: spilled grain, offal, garbage at the far side of the road feed gulls and crows, which roost on the plant structures. It is a simplified world, not a dead one: many individuals, very few species, all kept in place by the water’s chemistry and the heat.

The sound is loud, but it is not chaotic. It is a steady chord. The steam vent on the roof has a white roar, like a jet at a distance. The pump in the pump house beats a low, unvarying thud, so regular that it disappears into the body after a while. The transformers whine. The conveyor clacks once every three seconds, always at the same interval. Under it all, the flies hiss when they lift; the flamingos mutter. You would have to raise your voice to speak across the yard, but after an hour the noise becomes a kind of silence, because it never changes.

And it never changes. This is the strangest ordinary thing about the place. The light does not brighten or fail; the sky stays that same steamed gray. The water level in the pans does not rise or fall; the intake pump and sluice gates and evaporator fans hold it exactly. The temperature is always the same, hot and wet, with no cool hour before dawn and no afternoon peak. The same flock of flamingos stands in the same shallows. The same black patch of flies covers the same stretch of mud. If you returned after a month, or a year, you would not know whether you had been gone an hour. The place has reached a steady state. It is not frozen—water moves, animals move, machines turn—but it is a moving equilibrium, with no seasons, no tides, no storms, no growth, no decay beyond the slow rust that the salt arrests by forming a crust.

Just outside the fence is the rest of the town: low whitewashed concrete houses in a loose grid, a water tower, a rail siding, a market shed, a row of tamarind trees planted by the road—one of the few places in the basin where vegetation looks ordinary. The town is small and spread thin, the edge of a city, or a city so young it still has gaps. The works dominate its skyline with stacks and pipes, but inside the yard all of that falls away into this one room of the world: hot, wet, loud, dim, crowded with tiny and pink and silver lives, and as stable as a held note.
