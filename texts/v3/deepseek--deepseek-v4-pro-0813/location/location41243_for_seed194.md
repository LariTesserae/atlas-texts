The place is called the Dry Nave, though no one has spoken that name in a long time. It is not a landscape you can walk across. It is a pocket, a hidden chamber tucked inside the flank of a root so large that its outer surface passes for the world. You would not find it unless you already knew where to look. The entrance is a crack in the bark no wider than a door, and it opens into a space the size of a chapel: a hundred paces long, maybe forty high, but mostly empty. The floor slopes gently down toward the far end, where the cold air pools. It is low-lying, lower than the surrounding galleries, lower than the old surface, which no longer exists except as a band of darker rings in the walls.

No light comes here. Not a glimmer. The darkness is not the absence of light; it is a substance. It presses on the eyes. After a while you stop expecting your eyes to adjust; there is nothing for them to adjust to. You begin to see with sound.

And the sound is enormous. It is the first thing you notice, before the dark, before the cold. It is a low chord, loud as an organ, coming from everywhere at once—the floor, the walls, the air. It shivers through the sternum and the teeth. It is not constant; it wavers, slowly, as if the whole chamber is a throat trying to speak. The sound is the plant breathing. But the breath is slow: an inhalation can take a year, an exhalation longer. You are standing inside a note that began before you were born.

Everything is plant. That is the first law. The floor is not stone; it is wood—but wood so old, so dense, so mineralized that it rings like slate when you tap it. The walls are not rock; they are wound fiber, pressed leaf, bark plates the size of houses, all fused by time and pressure into something harder than concrete. The dust is plant dust: tiny fragments of cell wall, dried resin, broken spines, sifted into drifts in the corners. There is no soil. There is no mineral that did not pass through a plant. Even the air is full of plant: a faint pollen that never germinates, a fine crystalline exhalation that settles on the skin.

There is no water. The air is dry as old paper, cool as a root cellar. When you disturb the dust, it rises in clouds and hangs, refusing to settle. The walls do not sweat. If you touch them, they are cool and dry, like the inside of a casket. Nothing rots here. Nothing decays. The plant does not live by water. Deep in its vessels moves a clear, cold solvent that smells faintly of ammonia and iron; it dissolves minerals, not salts. It has never known rain.

But the chamber is not a natural hollow. It has been shaped. There are doorways cut into the walls—oval, low, their edges smoothed by uncounted years. Stairs spiral around the thick root-columns that rise through the dark, spaced far apart like trees in a desert. Balconies of woven fiber sag from the walls, still holding after the people who built them are gone. The city is vertical, stacked in layers; you can trace the levels by the ledges and the patterns of holes where beams once supported floors. Some rooms are carved, some are grown; the plant has swallowed the carved parts, and the carved parts have opened the grown parts, until architecture and anatomy are the same. A whole district is folded into this one hidden pocket—settlement-scale, but compressed into the space of a single chamber.

The tools left behind are simple. There is no metal. The knives are blades of glassy mineral that the plant secretes around its wounds. There are baskets woven from root-hair, pots of baked root-clay and resin, hammers of dense plant-stone, wooden awls, spindles still wound with thread. The only technology is fiber and stone and time.

There are no animals. Not one. The silence between the notes is absolute; no chittering, no scurry, no song. The dust lies undisturbed except for your footprints. There are no bones, no droppings, no nests. Whatever built this place left no animal remains—or perhaps they were not animals at all. The only living thing is the plant, and the plant is not exactly living in the way you know.

The chamber is ancient beyond measure. The edges are rounded, the carvings softened into suggestions of themselves. The steps are worn into gentle curves, the doorways widened by the brush of passing bodies until they are almost arches. It has been eroded, but not by water; by the plant’s own slow movement, by air that has circulated for eons, by the touch of hands that are no longer here. This is the final form of the city, the shape it has settled into after everything that could fall has fallen and everything that could smooth has smoothed.

The plant still moves. It is the only living thing here, but its life is so slow it is easy to mistake for stone. The walls flex, a hair’s breadth in a year. The pillars lean, imperceptibly. The great chord changes pitch as the tension in the root shifts—not over minutes, but over decades. The place is not dead; it is asleep, or awake in a way that uses centuries for seconds.

There are rules to this place, if you stay long enough to learn them.

First, there is no water. Nothing drinks. Nothing decays.

Second, everything is the plant. Even the city is the plant. The plant is the world; what you think of as terrain is its body.

Third, no animal lives here. If you hear a footstep, it is the plant settling.

Fourth, the dark is absolute; do not expect your eyes to adapt.

Fifth, the sound is not a warning. It is the breath of the place. In time you will hear the rhythm, and your own heartbeat will begin to follow it.

And if you stay even longer, you may begin to understand that the city was not built by hands. It was grown, or it grew itself. The plant has been here so long that it has begun to dream rooms, to dream stairs, to dream tools. The city is the plant’s memory of a time when there were others. Now there are only the rooms, the chord, the dark, and the slow, dry breathing of the world.
