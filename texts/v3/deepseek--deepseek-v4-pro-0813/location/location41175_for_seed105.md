# The Drowned Choir

The place is called the High Water, or sometimes the Drowned Choir. It is a drowned mountain valley: a high plateau basin that now lies under a cold, mineral-thick brine. The old valley floor is nearly three thousand meters above the lowlands, yet it sits hundreds of meters below the surface. The surrounding peaks are not visible from below; they are sensed only as a shallowing, a change in current, a place where the water grows slightly less dark. The brine is dense with magnesium and calcium, dense enough that the cold does not freeze it, dense enough that a falling object drifts rather than sinks. From the valley floor the surface is only a rumor—a grey-green lid far above, often hidden by cloud and rain.

The light that matters here does not come from the sky. It comes from the moss.

Between the groves the land is sparse and open. Pale silt flats stretch away, crossed by old stone walls. Limestone ridges rise smoothly, their edges water-worn and settled. There are sinkholes, terraced slopes, drowned saddles, and one long pass—the Narrows—where the brine pours between two ridges like a slow, cold river within the sea. The stone is not jagged. The valley has been underwater long enough to have entered its old age: the terraces have settled into the slope, the sediment is sorted into fine layers, the walls have become more geology than architecture.

The vegetation is lush where it grows, but it grows in islands. The groves are scattered across the bare water like gardens separated by emptiness. There is no green. The plants here do not feed on sunlight; they feed on minerals and heat and the gases that seep from the earth. The chime-reed groves are the most common: dense stands of hollow, stone-crusted stalks, taller than a person, growing in old channels and leats. The current blows across their open ends and produces a low, continuous chord. In the Narrows, the reeds sing so loudly the water itself seems to tremble, and the sound carries for kilometers.

Anchor-trees rise on the flanks of the ridges, sometimes alone, sometimes in loose stands. They look like drowned oaks made of pale cartilage and sponge, with long netted branches. They bend in the current and creak—a sound that travels far in the brine. Their roots run down to mineral seeps. Around them grow bladderweed: translucent globes the size of helmets, filled with methane and other gases. They swell and burst in slow chains, popping like distant wood blocks.

On the terrace walls and the rims of sinkholes, glow-moss spreads in thick, spongy mats. It gives off a faint blue-green light, not steady but pulsing in slow waves that pass from one colony to the next. There are no eyes in this light. It is the light of one-celled plant-life, not of fire, not of sun.

The human marks are scattered. Drystone terraces step down the drowned slopes, their walls still holding the old mountain soil. A few corbelled huts stand with their doorways turned away from the current. In one, clay pots are still stacked. In another, a stone axe rests on a wall, its wooden handle blackened but intact. There are standing stones, each carved with a shallow cup or groove, and leats—narrow stone channels—that once carried snowmelt to fields. Now the leats carry the deep current, and they hum. The builders used no metal. Their world was stone and wood and fired clay. They are gone.

This is the strangest rule of the water: there are no animals. None. No fish, no mite, no worm in the silt, no midge at the surface. The water is too cold, too mineral-rich, too saturated with plant chemistry for animal tissue to organize. A fish would die before it could twitch. The only moving things are water, gas, stone dust, and plants. What a diver might mistake for fish are only bubbles: bladderweed gas, methane from the sediment, the slow exhalations of the anchor-trees.

Yet the place is loud. The chime-reeds never stop their low chord. Rain on the surface arrives as a vast hiss from above. Gas bubbles thump and pop. The anchor-trees creak. Mineral seeps hiss along the faults. In the Narrows the current roars between the ridges, and the whole valley becomes an instrument. The cup-marked standing stones may be a notation; the reeds may have been planted, or may have planted themselves, in spirals that follow the old field divisions. The current shifts, the note bends. Sometimes a phrase repeats for minutes, as if the valley is remembering a song.

The place also has its own weather. Dense brine sinks and slides down the old valleys, forming submarine rivers. The plants pump water with slow contractions, adding their own currents to the greater one. Gas eruptions rise in chains from the silt. Glow-moss blooms pulse in waves. Silt clouds drift and settle. At times the water turns milky with spores; at other times it clears, and the far ridges seem to stand too close, because the mineral brine bends light strangely. Distances are not reliable here. A wall may appear suddenly out of the murk and then recede, though it never moved.

The ecology is plant-only, but it is not simple. The anchor-trees tap reduced minerals from the seeps. The glow-moss feeds on their exudates. The chime-reeds filter calcium and silica from the water. The bladderweed scavenges methane. There is no predation, no herbivory, no mating. Death is by starvation, burial, or old age, and the dead plants settle into the silt and feed the next growth. The lushness is real, but it is clumped. Between the groves, the water is empty and cold, and the only sounds are distant ones.

If you hung above a drowned terrace and looked north, you could see the valley open out: a broad shelf of pale silt, a line of standing stones vanishing into black water, a far ridge that might be ten kilometers away or ten meters, its flank lit by slow waves of blue-green moss. Ridge after ridge, bay after bay, the drowned country continues for days. The sound would be all around you—low, continuous, almost too loud to notice after a while. The only absence would be the absence of animals, and even that would feel full: no small movements in the corner of your eye, no fish fleeing, no insect in the silt. Only the plants, breathing, ringing, glowing, growing in the dark.
