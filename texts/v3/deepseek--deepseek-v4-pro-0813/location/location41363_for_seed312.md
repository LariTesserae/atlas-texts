The plateau is a country of bare rock and thin air, so high that the horizon seems to bend away rather than meet the sky. It is cold even in direct sun: the wind has a mineral edge, and shadows are another season. The land is not flat, though it pretends to be. It is broken into low escarpments, sudden shelves, and fields of shattered basalt that shift underfoot. There is no soil to speak of, only a crust of grit and the slow rust of lichen. What vegetation exists is barely there—cushions of moss, small hard plants in cracks, yellow and gray-green scales on the rock. It is not a desert of heat but a desert of cold and bareness, and yet it is not entirely dry.

Moisture hides in the land. Rain is rare, but it comes sometimes as fine needles that freeze on the stone. More often, water appears as fog and seep. At dawn the rock exhales: condensation beads on every surface, drips from ledges, collects in small basins. Springs rise along the fault joints, cold and slow. The water is intermittent, a live thing rather than a constant. In the harshest months, it freezes into white fingers that grow from the cliff until the sun finds them.

Then the ground breaks open.

The rift is a young wound. It is not a valley carved by a patient river but a crack where the plateau has pulled apart, leaving raw edges and a long fall. The rim is sharp; in places, slabs of stone have peeled away and lodged halfway down. The walls are columnar basalt, fluted and dark, broken by pale bands of ash and rust-colored cinder. Nothing has been rounded by time. Boulders sit where they fell, their edges clean enough to cut leather. Far below, a river runs, dark and narrow, too small for the size of the canyon. Its sound reaches the rim as a hush rather than a roar.

The first sign of the city is smoke. It rises from holes in the rock, thin blue-gray threads, then hundreds, then thousands. As the eye learns to see, the cliff becomes structure: terraces, stairways, low walls, windows, bridges. The city is called Kessil, or the Seam, and it is not built on the plateau nor at the bottom of the rift. It is built into the rift, across it, up and down it. It stretches for days along the canyon, but it is not broad. Its density is vertical, a saturation of life in the one place where water, shelter, and warmth meet.

The stone of the city is the stone of the walls. Rooms are cut into the rock and fronted with drystone masonry; roofs are slabs of slate or stretched hide. There are no grand facades, only accumulation: a new room set above an older one, a stair cut beneath a granary, an animal pen placed over a dwelling so that its warmth sinks down through the floor. Doorways are low and narrow, windows small and covered with oiled hide or scraped gut. Wood is too scarce for beams; ceilings are corbelled, or made of small stones fitted with patience. The streets are ledges and stairways, some wide enough for two, some so narrow a person must turn sideways. In places, the path passes through a shared room, and the boundary between house and street is only a curtain of heavy felt.

The two sides of the canyon are joined by bridges of braided wool and leather. They sway underfoot, some of them so light that a single person's weight bends them in the middle. The city's infrastructure is all like that: rope, felt, stone, and bone. There is no wheeled cart. Goods move on backs—human, animal, or rope. Bronze is known and treasured, but most tools are stone and bone. Fire is made from dried dung and fat lamps, and its fuel is too precious to waste.

The density is startling. Every ledge has a room, every room has a household, every household has animals. People live above and below one another, in layers of sound and smell and warmth. Yet the city is quiet. This is not emptiness but discipline. People speak in low voices; tools are muffled. A hammer may be wrapped in wool. Doors are hide curtains rather than wood because hide does not slam. The common sounds are the hush of the river, the drip of water into cisterns, the soft cluck of birds, the low cough of animals, the scrape of stone on stone, the creak of leather. The loudest event is rockfall: a single sharp crack, a scatter of echoes, then silence again.

The animals are everywhere. The city keeps long-wooled camelids, which graze the sparse cushion plants and lichens of the plateau in slow herds and return to the upper terraces at night. Their wool is the city's main wealth, its cloth and rope and bridges and felt. There are also rock-pigs, small bristly animals kept in dark pens and fed on scraps, moss, and the leavings of the camelids. Every household keeps a few; their bodies are part of the heating system. Above the city, wild birds nest in the crevices: black chough-like birds, gray finches, rock doves. Pikas and rock rats live in the talus. At dusk, bats pour from the high caves like smoke; at dawn they funnel back. A bearded vulture may hang over the rim for an hour without moving. The river has fish, slow and dark, caught in stone weirs by the lower terraces. The canyon concentrates all this life into a narrow vertical band, so that the air is never still with small movements, though the overall sound remains low.

The light is ordinary but intense. At this altitude, the sun is hard and white, and the sky at noon is a deep blue, almost violet at the top. Shadows are so cold they feel like a different climate. In the canyon, light is uneven: the upper terraces get long hours of sun, warm on stone and wool; the lower city lives in blue shadow, where breath fogs and water freezes even in summer. Morning cloud fills the rift from below, and the city appears to float on a white sea; by midday the cloud burns off, and the walls stand revealed, black and green and gray. Weather on the plateau is a thing of its own: you can watch a storm form far away, a gray curtain dragging over the stone, and it may never reach the rift.

Vegetation is sparse but not absent. Along the seep lines, moss and ferns grow in bright green strokes down the dark rock. On the plateau above, lichens and cushion plants form a thin living crust, the only pasture. There are no trees, no grasses to speak of. The city's gardens are small stone troughs filled with moss and a few bitter greens, watered by hand from the fog cisterns. Sparse as it is, this vegetation feeds the animals that feed the city, and the animals' dung feeds the moss, so the whole place is a slow loop of scarce nutrients.

The city's rules are written in heat, water, and silence. Heat is hoarded: rooms are small, doorways low, and animals are kept above people so their warmth sinks down. Water is assigned by family; each spring and cistern has a keeper, each fog-frame of woven wool is inherited. The lower city, near the water, is for leatherworking, dyeing, washing, and fish traps. The middle terraces are for living and storage. The upper terraces and rim, where the sun is strongest, are for animals, drying wool, and the households that can afford the light. Silence is both custom and necessity: sound carries far in thin air and loose stone, and loud noise is thought to disturb the animals and shame the speaker. Movement is slow because the air is thin and the paths are narrow. To rush is to waste breath and risk a fall.

Change comes slowly. The rock is young and still breaking. Frost works into the joints of the basalt and splits off slabs. Each spring, the people clear fresh scree from the lower paths and use it for building. The river rises a little with snowmelt, then falls. Earthquakes, perhaps once in a generation, crack terraces and shake down bridges. The city rebuilds. New rooms are cut a generation at a time. A bridge is re-woven every few years. The fog comes every morning, the ice melts every noon, and the place persists by not moving faster than the stone.

From the rim, the plateau seems empty and the city only a seam of smoke and shadow. Inside it, you understand that the density and the vastness need each other: the city is packed because the land around it is so bare; the quiet is attention to a world that is always slowly breaking. There is nothing uncanny about it. It is only a high, cold, crowded place, built into a wound in the earth, full of animals and wool and water, waiting for the next rockfall.
