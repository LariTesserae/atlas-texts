The sound does not begin at the edge; it begins far down the ridge, a low continuous roar that you mistake for wind or water until you get close enough to feel it in your teeth. By then the air has already changed: warm, close, sticky, and ripe. You are high enough that your breath should be cool, but it is not. The warmth comes from the valley itself, from the bodies in it, from the slow decomposition of everything that grows and dies there.

At first the way down from the pass looks like a trail through dense cloud forest. The ground is soft and dark. Ferns brush your legs, dripping. The mist closes behind you. But then you notice the ground is too even in places, too level for a mountainside, and when you stumble you do not hit stone. You land on a surface that gives slightly, like a mattress of roots and old straw. You look closely. The path is not a path. It is a ledge of compressed plant fiber, dried mud, feather, bone, and little pellets of guano, all knitted together by fine roots. It continues along the slope as a terrace, with a low ceiling of interwoven branches above it.

That is when the place begins to reveal what it is. You are not walking through a forest that has grown over a mountain. You are walking through a structure, or on top of one, or inside one. The distinction hardly matters. The whole valley has been built over. The mountain underneath is gone, not removed but buried beneath an accumulated architecture of nests, mounds, galleries, and living rootwork. There is no exposed stone anywhere. Where a stream has cut a channel through the built ground, the banks are a cross-section of old nests, pale fungal rind, black compost, and glistening mineral stain—no geology, only biology.

The place is high. Through gaps in the canopy you can see the ridgeline across the valley, and beyond it another, and another, all of them furred with the same gray-green hummocks of built vegetation. The valley is wide enough that the far wall is blued by mist, but the construction does not stop at the valley floor. It steps down in terraces, bulges outward in domes, rises into pillars and buttresses, and in some places arches over the watercourses so that the streams run in tunnels. The scale is regional: you could walk for a day and still be inside it, never touching raw earth.

The light is old and poor. Cloud sits in the valley for much of the day. What sun comes through is strained by leaves, by hanging moss, by the breath of condensation, until it is the color of tea. The upper galleries are dim; the lower ones are black. The darkness is not empty. It is full of small movements, and it has a texture: the slick reflection of water on the walls, the pale fuzz of fungus, the glow of a few blue-green mushrooms that are not magic, only ordinary rot.

The air is almost wet enough to drink. Water does not fall as steady rain, most days. The cloud forest makes its own moisture. The warm built walls exhale. The leaves gather fog and drip it. Every moss cushion, every cup of bromeliad water, every hollow in the guano holds a little pool. The water is the only non-living architect here. It dissolves the mud walls and redeposits minerals; it carves smooth runnels down the sides of the galleries; it fills collapsed chambers and turns them into ponds. The place has been worked by water for so long that its edges are soft, slumped, rounded, like sea-worn wood.

And over all of this, the sound. It is not a background. It is a physical medium. It comes from the frogs that sit by the thousands in the wet hollows, each one a bright pinpoint of noise; from insects that whine and click and rattle in the leaf litter; from birds that pour in and out of holes in the walls, crying and chattering; from bats roosting in the deeper chambers, rustling and piping at frequencies that make your skull hum. There are moments when the sound seems to organize itself into a pulse, and moments when it collapses into chaos. You stop talking because your voice is just another small animal sound, lost in the wall of it.

The walls themselves are alive. They are not uniform. In some places they are made of round mud cups, each the size of a fist, glued one to another. In other places they are papery and gray, the old cells of wasps or the silk tents of caterpillars. Roots have grown through the mud, swelling and splitting it, and then the builders have repaired the splits with fresh material. Moss covers the horizontal surfaces. Ferns uncurl from every crack. On the larger ledges, tree ferns and small palms grow, their skirts of dead fronds adding to the structure. The vegetation is lush but not bright; the greens are deep, blue-black, bronze, and the only reds are the new leaves of a few shade plants.

The animals are not visitors. They are the structure, as much as the mud and roots. Small soft-bodied insects, pale and blind or nearly blind, move in lines across the walls, each carrying a bead of red clay or a splinter of stem. They are the masons, or one of the masonic species. They do not use tools. Their architecture is secretion and chewing and time. Above them, small birds with long toes cling to the walls and pick at the insects. Lizards flatten themselves against the warm mud. A snake like a dark rope pours over a root and disappears into a round hole. Troops of small primates move through the upper lattice, shaking down a shower of water and leaves. Moths the size of hands flop through the dim air. Beetles bore through the softer walls, and the frogs wait for them.

The ecology is a closed loop. The birds and bats bring food in and drop guano. The guano feeds the plants. The plants feed the insects. The insects feed the frogs and birds and lizards. Their bodies and wastes feed the builders. The builders make the ground. The ground makes the plants. It is dense, layered, near-touching. There is no bare soil, no vacant niche. Every surface has something growing or living or dying on it.

The pace of the place is slow. The structure looks permanent, and in human terms it might as well be, but it is not. Chambers collapse under their own weight. Roots pry walls apart. Water undermines a terrace. Then the builders seal off the old chamber and build over it. The collapse becomes a pond. The pond fills with leaves. The leaves become a garden. The garden becomes a wall. The overall shape changes the way a cloud changes: you can see it has moved only after a long time. The place is not raw or new. It has settled into itself. It is an old, established country, in balance between growth and decay.

At a break in the outer wall, you can look out and see the scale. The built terraces step down into the valley mist, tier after tier, studded with holes and plumed with ferns. The stream tunnels open here and there into dark mouths. The far side of the valley is the same: a continuous constructed slope, rising into cloud. The whole landscape is one made thing, but no mind made it. It is built the way a coral reef is built, by billions of small lives, each doing what it must, in the dark, in the noise, in the warm wet air.
