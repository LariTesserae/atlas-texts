There is no proper way in. The path drops through a slot in the rim, a place where two shear planes meet, and for the last twenty meters you descend a ladder bolted into damp rock, its rungs coated in a black algal skin. At the bottom the noise comes up like a physical pressure: water falling, steel singing, air rushing.

The floor is maybe forty meters long and fifteen wide, a rough oval bowl closed by walls that lean in at the top. It is not a valley. It is a bellmouth in the crest of the range, a punched hole in the high country, and everything in it is close. The back wall is a dike of columnar basalt, columns not weathered into round knees but snapped off in straight, sharp-edged bundles, as if the mountain was broken yesterday. There are no cushions of rounded gravel underfoot; the ground is mostly angular spall, fist-sized and plate-sized, half-buried in moss. Water runs everywhere, not in defined channels only but as a general mobility: thin sheets over rock, drips from every leaf, a mist that condenses on your forearms. The air at the top of the world is somehow steeped, warm as a greenhouse, and it smells of wet stone, crushed fern, hot oil, and slightly of sulfur.

The waterfall at the head of the notch is not tall, perhaps ten meters, but it is loud because it falls hard through a narrow slot rather than spreading. It lands in a circular pool that the falling water has not had time to round. The basin is young, steep-sided, its rim jagged with fresh basalt cobbles that shift when the water hits them. Steam rises from the pool—not much, but enough to make the ferns behind it shiver. The water is warm, fed by a hot seep higher up; where it strikes the moss on the rock walls, the moss grows in dense, copper-green hummocks that smell like cooked green.

Around and within this water lives vegetation so thick it is almost architectural. The trees are not tall; at this elevation they are dressed-down, wind-trained, but here in the pocket they are protected and warm, and they tangle into a continuous canopy over the upper third of the notch. Trunks are skinny, moss-sleeved, leaning on each other. Branches cross and touch; epiphytes fill every intersection. There are orchids with white roots spread like nerves over bark, and bromeliad cups holding miniature ponds, each one with a skim of pollen and drowned insects. Ferns grow from the leaf axils of other ferns; moss grows on moss. The lower layer is mostly a wet carpet of liverworts and filmy ferns, so thick that you cannot see the rock in places. When you step, water wells up around your boot. It is not a forest so much as a single saturated organism bound to a rock wound.

The light is ordinary, a soft gray-gold haze—no visible sun, just an even brightness that makes the wet leaves look lacquered. Every surface holds water long after the rain has stopped.

The industrial part is not large enough to dominate, but it is impossible to ignore. A penstock comes down the left wall—black steel pipe, maybe thirty centimeters across, held off the rock by iron brackets. It vibrates with the water inside it, and where it passes through a dense knot of roots the roots have grown around it, so the pipe is partially swallowed. Near the base, the pipe divides: a smaller line into a steel housing no bigger than a dog crate, where a Pelton wheel spins too fast to see, and a bypass line that spills into the pool. The turbine housing is an industrial green, now patched with rust and mineral scale; its bolts have left orange tears down the casing. A white-faced pressure gauge, old and yellowed, sits at eye level with its needle flickering in a nervous arc. A metal catwalk, gone patchy with brown paint, runs from the housing to the cliff ladder. There is one caged lamp on a bracket, unlit in the daylight but full of dead midges and a pale spider’s silk. A cable loops from a junction box, follows the wall, and vanishes upward through a crack into the cloud. That is the only indication that the place is still connected to anything: a single black wire, no thicker than a pencil, running up into the dripping sky.

The machine has made itself part of the hydrology. Warm water sweats from the turbine housing where the cold stream meets the warm air; that side of the casing is furred with a dark moss that grows in unusually straight lines, following the seams and rivet lines. Where the bypass water splashes out, it has built a tiny travertine terrace, not big—a staircase of rimstone pools the size of hands—but it is growing. The trapped pools are ringed with bright green algae and flickering with gas bubbles that rise very slowly, as if the water were thicker than it should be. Around the base of the penstock there is a circular patch of ground where the soil is too warm to touch; there the roots of the nearest tree have lifted into a low cage above the ground, dying at the tips but thriving further back, forming a hollow that steams faintly.

The sounds are layered. There is the deep white noise of the waterfall, underpinned by a mechanical whine from the turbine—a high, clean note that sometimes wavers as water pressure changes. The pipe rings when a stone falls on it. Water drips from a hundred leaf tips in different pitches, so the whole place ticks and plinks. Every few minutes a valve or pressure release gives a sharp hiss, like a kettle lifted, followed by a cough of steam from the turbine house. The combined sound is loud enough that you feel it in your teeth more than hear it. There are no birdcalls. The place is not silent by any means; it is so full of water and metal noise that it empties out the middle frequencies, leaving only rush and whine and drip.

Fauna is rare. There are midges that dance in the warm cloud above the pool in a slow vertical column, never far from the steam. A few pale frogs the size of a thumbnail sit on wet leaves with their throat sacs fluttering, but they do not call; the noise would drown them. A single gray-and-rust bird, some highland wren or spine-tail, works the moss pads near the ladder, picking at insects too small to see. Once a small snake, slender and glossy black, unrolled itself from the warm pipe and dropped into the ferns without a sound. But mostly the living things here are rooted or too small to notice. The noise and the heat and the minerals in the water seem to have left the place for plants and machines, with only a few animals passing through.

The world here changes slowly. The pool is deepening by maybe a millimeter a year, the travertine terrace accreting in wet films. The wall behind the waterfall is beginning to undercut, leaving a dark brow of unsupported columns; one day, years or decades away, it will fall, and the whole geometry of the notch will change. But that has not happened yet. The turbine will need a new bearing soon—there is a dull knock in the housing for ten minutes after the water pressure spikes—but soon here might mean another winter. The moss covers the rockfalls at the pace of a tide, so that a fresh black boulder becomes a green mound only after many seasons. The place gives the sense that everything urgent has already happened long ago, and what remains is the patient work of water, growth, rust, and falling stone.

The rules are simple. The water is warm, so do not assume it is pure; it carries minerals that crust the lips of the pool and leave a white ring on your skin. The stone is young and sharp, so every handhold is a blade. The vegetation will take any surface that stays still long enough, including metal, but it prefers the warm edges of the human things. The machine is not abandoned; it is tended occasionally, and the moss is not allowed to grow over the gauge face. The sound belongs to the place as much as the waterfall does. And the boundaries are near: a wall on every side, a cloud above, a wire going up into the cloud. You cannot see far from here, only up, into the rising mist of a warm, small, high notch that is still learning what shape it will become.
