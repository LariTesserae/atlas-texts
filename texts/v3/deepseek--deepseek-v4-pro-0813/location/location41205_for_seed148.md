# The Hollow Below the Works

It is a place that is mostly air, but the air is inside something alive.

It sits low in the land, in a bowl of root-mounded ridges, so that from outside you would have to descend through a slot in a green wall and then down a staircase that is half timber and half iron. The temperature drops as you go. By the time you reach the floor, your breath gathers in a small cloud, and the darkness is almost complete.

The ground is new. It has the look of a thing that has not yet been weathered: no rounded stones, no worn paths, no dust. The floor is a mat of living roots, flattened by their own weight, some of them thicker than a person, braided into a false geology. Between the roots there are shallow depressions where water has collected—not lakes, not even puddles, just black mirrors of condensation and slow sap-leak, cold and still. In places the water has mineralized into a thin crust of white and amber, as if the land is trying to make stone and has only just begun. This is a landscape under construction, embryonic, pre-erosion. The forces that will eventually carve it have not arrived. The world here is still being laid down.

Above the floor, the space opens. It is not large—about the size of a cathedral nave, perhaps smaller than that, a single hollow chamber with side galleries—but it feels larger because it is so empty. The walls are not rock. They are the inward curves of a living plant body, a chamber grown rather than dug. Ribs of xylem rise from the floor and meet in a crown of shadow overhead. They are cool to the touch. Their surfaces are smooth, almost polished, but punctuated by rows of pores that open and close with a slow, wet breath. The air from these pores smells of yeast and cold cellar and something floral, faintly sweet, like the first stage of decay.

Into this hollow, an industrial town has been built. It is small, hamlet-sized, but it has the density of organization rather than of matter. There are riveted platforms bolted through the living bark, gantries that follow the curve of the ribs, iron staircases spiraling around the great stems. A few brick-and-metal buildings stand near the center: a boiler house with a low stack that leaks steam rather than smoke; a machine gallery with rows of brass dials and cracked porcelain insulators; a switchboard where cables have been spliced into the plant’s own vascular bundles. Pipes run from the walls and floor into the machines, tapping the sap-pressure as a power source, or drawing off gas from fermentation bladders that swell under the roots. The settlement has the logic of a factory town—numbered valves, painted walkways, a suspended tram-line of wire baskets—but it is spread thinly across the dark. The buildings are separated by broad intervals of empty air, so that walking from the boiler house to the switchboard is like crossing a small plaza made only of shadow and distance.

The light is not so much absent as it is hoarded. Most of the lamps are unlit. A few gas jets burn with blue pilot flames no larger than a thumb. The walls themselves give off a faint green phosphorescence where the tissue is thinnest, just enough to see your hand in front of your face but not enough to see the far side of the chamber. Some of the machines have glass windows in their iron sides, and inside them there is the dull red glow of a furnace, pulsing like a heartbeat. The rest is darkness, but it is a darkness with texture—some places darker than others, as if the shadow has settled more thickly there.

The damp is everywhere. It is not a place of standing water; it is a place of condensation. The walls sweat. The iron railings are slick. The air is heavy enough that your clothes become damp without rain. Every few hours, the temperature shifts a fraction, and the moisture gathered on the ceiling lets go in a fine, cold mist that falls through the dark like a sigh. Water collects in the hollows between roots, in the basins of old leaf-bracts, in the upturned lids of abandoned machines. It drips from the pipes into copper pots that someone has set out. The sound of that dripping is one of the loudest sounds in the town.

The place is mostly quiet, but not still. The quiet is an active quiet, full of small sounds that your ears learn to sort. The roots creak as they grow. The sap tide rises and falls twice a day, and when it rises, the pipes knock softly and the floor swells by a finger’s width. The vents open and close in slow waves, like a breathing you can hear only if you stand very still. The machinery, when it runs, is muffled by the living insulation that has grown over it; you feel it as a low shudder in the floor more than you hear it. The town has no bells, no steam whistles. Signals are sent by tapping on the root-braids, or by small colored lights that blink in the dark.

The fauna are abundant, but they are quiet too. In the wet furrows between the roots there are pale grubs the length of a thumb, each with a row of light-dots along its back, grazing on the sap-skin. They move in herds, slowly, and their glow makes the floor look like a field of stars if you lie down and look across it. In the black pools there are translucent newts, blind and slow, their organs visible as faint shadows. Moths with eye-spots crawl over the brass gauges, and their wings leave a dust that smells like licorice. Above, in the high dark, bats or bat-like things hang from the crown. They are silent when they fly; you only know they are there because the pilot lights flicker when they pass.

There are larger things too. Something like a fox but with six legs and a ridge of green moss down its spine runs along the gantries at dawn, though there is no true dawn here—only a change in the bioluminescence. There are insects the size of sparrows that build nests of chewed root-fiber and resin, and sometimes short-circuit the switchboard when they swarm. Predators move through the upper shadows: a long, low shape with too many legs, or a bird-like thing whose feathers are leaves. When it passes, the small sounds stop. Then they start again.

The ecology is process. The plant is the base of everything: it grows the floor, the walls, the ceilings, the air. The animals eat the plant, or eat each other, or eat the sap that leaks from the machines. The machines eat the plant too: they burn its oils, drink its gas, consume its sugars. The waste from the machines feeds the plant: smoke and ash and metal salts sift down and are absorbed by the roots. Nothing is separate. The plant grows around the pipes, and the pipes tap the plant, and the animals nest in both.

But the rules of the world here are not entirely reliable. That is the deepest atmosphere.

The first rule is that categories fail. The plant is not just plant; it is architecture, geology, water-system, electrical grid. The machines are not just machines; some of them have bark, and if you scratch them they bleed green. There are pipes that are also roots, and roots that are also wires. The animals blur into the vegetation: there are caterpillars that root themselves and sprout leaves; there are flowers that detach and crawl away when the lamps come on. The town’s inhabitants do not speak of “plants” and “animals” and “machines” as separate. They speak of “the living” and “the built,” and even those terms are provisional.

The second rule is that space is conditional. The chamber has been measured many times; the measurements disagree. A maintenance corridor behind the boiler house is sometimes three meters long and sometimes thirty. A doorway that was a meter wide yesterday may be a slot today, or a broad arch. The town is small, but it contains more rooms than its volume should allow. Some of the rooms are only there at certain times, when the plant’s breath is slow or the sap is low. The inhabitants navigate by habit and by chalk marks on the bark, but the chalk marks sometimes drift, or are overgrown, or reappear in a different place. They say the town is still being dreamed by the plant, and that the dream revises itself. When a new street appears, it has no people in it, and no one walks down it until they have all agreed it is safe.

The third rule is that darkness has weight here. In some corners the shadow is thick, like cold velvet, and you can push your hand into it and feel resistance. In other places the darkness is thin and dry, and the green glow of the walls passes through it easily. The shadows do not always fall away from the light. Some of them lean.

And the fourth rule is that the place is small, and it knows itself. It is a single chamber, a grove-sized world, a hamlet of platforms and tanks. But it is not a closed world. There are passages: one down into the root-engine room, where the great sap-pumps are bolted into the living rootstock; one up into the canopy dark, where there are sleeping platforms and a few glass panes set into the plant’s skin to show the outside weather. The outside exists—there is cool rain, and a low green landscape—but it feels far away, like a memory.

You could stand on the highest gantry, where the cableway ends, and look down. You would see the town as a scatter of small lights: the blue pilot flames, the red furnace glow, the green wall-glow, the moving star-fields of the grubs. The distances between the lights are so great that you could count them. The air is full of mist. The plant breathes. The machines idle. The animals go about their quiet lives. The place is not finished. It is growing, and dreaming, and the dream is a small industrial city inside the body of a plant, in a hollow at the bottom of the world, where the dark is cool and the land has not yet been eroded into being.
