The first thing you become aware of is the hush. It is not a true silence—there are small sounds—but they are wrapped in moss and distance. The air is dim and warm, like the inside of a lung. Light comes through the canopy in a green-amber haze, as if the sun has been strained through a hundred layers of leaves. You cannot see the sky. Where the sky should be, there is only another layer of the weave, far overhead, filtering light. The air is thin with elevation, but it does not feel thin; it is heavy with water and heat.

You are standing on what appears to be solid ground, but it is not. It is a woven mat of roots and stems, each strand as thick as a body, braided and pressed together over centuries. The surface gives slightly underfoot, like a suspension bridge, and through the gaps between strands you can see warm mist rising from below. The mist carries a smell of wet bark, humus, and something floral.

This is the High Loom, or maybe the Reach. No one here calls it that. It is a world made entirely of plants, but not in the way a forest is. There is no soil, no mineral earth, no exposed rock. Everything underfoot and overhead, to the extent you can see, is living or recently dead plant tissue. It has been shaped. That is the unsettling part: every branch, every root, every surface is deliberate. The stems have been bent and tied, pruned and grafted, coaxed into floors and walls and bridges and stairways. This is not a natural landscape with buildings in it. The landscape is the building.

The first place you enter is a hollow—a gap in the weave the size of a cathedral or a valley. Its walls are vertical or overhanging, made of braided lianas and woody stems, thick with moss and epiphytes. The floor curves down at the edges and drops away into a warm blue-gray haze. Some of the larger hollows are big enough to hold their own clouds; you can see them drifting below, white against the haze, raining into lower chambers. The space is mostly empty. The living material that forms its boundaries is a thin skin around a vast volume of still air. You could shout, and the sound would not echo; it would be swallowed by the damp moss and the porous wood.

Water is everywhere. The air is saturated. The walls weep. Moisture condenses on the leaves and runs down the braided bark in fine silver threads. Small streams form in the grooves of the largest strands, then pour over the edge of the floor and fall silently into the depths. Drops fall from the ceiling with a soft, irregular plink, like the world counting time. There are pools in the hollows, lined with a thin layer of dead leaves and the bones of small animals. The water is warm, almost body-temperature, and it is never still for long; the slow exhalation of the vegetation above sends ripples across it.

The warmth is unusual for the altitude. This place is high—very high. Looking down through the gaps, you see layer after layer of woven branches fading into a warm abyss, with no visible bottom. The air is thin but humid, like a high cloud forest, except there are no mountains. The entire structure is elevated, suspended above something lower, or perhaps it is the top of something so enormous that the ground has been left behind in another climate. The heat comes from below, rising through the gaps, and from the plants themselves, which seem to breathe heat as they breathe water.

The vegetation is not just abundant; it is total. It is the substrate, the structure, the atmosphere. There is no bare ground, no rock, no open water that is not contained by leaf and root. The air is full of pollen and spores, so thick that the dim light makes them visible as a slow golden drift. Moss covers every surface, not as a thin fur but as a deep, spongy layer that grows over the braided wood and blurs its edges. Ferns and orchids and strange, pale plants with no names grow from the moss, their roots clutching the weave. The dominant organism is not a single plant but a community of thousands, woven together into one continuous fabric. When a stem dies, it is not removed; it becomes part of the soil, a dark, fibrous compost that fills the crevices and feeds the next growth.

But the place is not crowded. The density is low. The living matter is arranged like a net, not a solid. The hollows between the strands are enormous. You could walk for hours along a single limb without meeting another strand. The floor is often just a few meters wide, and on either side the world falls away into mist. The emptiness is not empty of life, though. The fauna are abundant, but they are quiet and mostly small. Pale geckos with translucent toes cling to the undersides of branches. Beetles with polished, seed-like carapaces crawl over the moss. Blind crickets live in the damp recesses, their legs making tiny ticking sounds that travel through the wood. Moths and wingless wasps drift through the air, drunk on nectar and pollen. In the deepest shade, the glow of bioluminescent fungi and the soft green light of lantern-galls—swelling plant bladders that pulse slowly—replace the dim sky light.

The larger animals are there, too, but you rarely see them. They move slowly and deliberately, their feet padded with moss or soft scales, their bodies colored like wet bark. There are gliders—small, furred creatures with skin stretched between wrists and ankles—that launch from high strands and sail across the gaps, silent as leaves. There are herds of grazers that feed on the moss and the tender shoots, moving in slow, silent groups. And there are predators—long, sinuous things that flow through the weave like water, sensing vibrations in the strands. Their strikes are quick, but the sound is muffled; the victim gives a small, breathy squeak, and then the quiet returns.

The makers of this world are still here. They are called the Tenders, or maybe the Weavers; the names are not important. They are a primate-like species, though more patient than any human, with long fingers and large, dark eyes adapted to the dimness. They use tools of polished flint, bone needles, and sharpened thorns. They carry coils of fiber rope made from the inner bark of the lianas. There is no metal here; the very idea of smelting is foreign. Their technology is the technology of knots and growth. They do not build so much as cultivate. A bridge is not cut from a tree; it is two branches persuaded to grow together over decades, then reinforced with woven cord. A staircase is not carved; it is a living vine tied into a spiral and allowed to thicken. The Tenders are not numerous—density low—but their work is everywhere. The entire landscape is a garden, or a tapestry, or a basket, perhaps all three.

The Tenders move through the weave alone or in small family groups. They are almost silent, communicating with hand signs and low vibrations tapped on the wood. They stop to trim a shoot that grows out of pattern, to re-tie a loose fiber, to press a seed into a gap. Their work is slow, but it is continuous. They have been doing this for so long that the world has settled into itself. The surfaces are polished by water and touch, not raw or rough. The edges are rounded. The spaces are stable. There is a sense of great age, of a form that has been found and maintained, not imposed.

And then there are the holes. The true holes. They are not the normal gaps in the weave, which are filled with air and mist and the possibility of falling. The true holes are different. They are places where the braided strands simply stop, as if the pattern has been cut out of the world. Beyond them is not the lower haze or another chamber, but a flat, colorless absence, like a blind spot in the eye. The edges are clean, not ragged. No water drips into them; the drops seem to hesitate at the edge and then run sideways, as if the hole refuses them. No animal goes near them. The Tenders do not weave over them. When they must pass, they do so on the opposite side of the hollow, making a gesture with their long fingers—a tying-off motion, as if to keep the hole from unravelling further.

The holes are not always the same. A gap that was there in the morning may be gone by evening, or shifted a hundred meters to the left. Distances in this place are not reliable. A bridge that looks a few hundred strides may take an hour to cross. The light sometimes falls at the wrong angle, and shadows point toward the source. The Tenders say the world is a net, and the holes are where the net has missed. They do not fear the holes exactly, but they respect them. To fall into a true hole is to leave the world, and no one knows what remains after that.

For all its strangeness, the place is not static. It is in constant, slow motion. The plants grow; the water cycles; the animals migrate, feed, reproduce, die. New shoots curl across the gaps, and the Tenders guide them. Old strands die and crumble, raining fine dust and fibers into the depths. Sometimes a whole section of the floor gives way, and for a few moments the air is full of tearing wood and falling moss, but the sound is swallowed almost before it starts. Then the Tenders arrive, not quickly, but within a season, and begin to reweave the gap. The cycle is ancient and balanced. The place is active, but it is not loud. It is a world that changes the way a breathing body changes, or the way a slow river carves a canyon—so gradually that you only notice if you look away and look back.

The rules of this world are simple, if you stay long enough to understand them. Everything is made. Nothing is wasted. The empty spaces are as important as the solid ones; they give the world its form, like the holes in lace. To fall through a gap is not necessarily to die, but to end up somewhere else, a lower layer or a pool of water or a tangle of vines. To fall into a true hole is different. Do not do that. The Tenders will not help you, not because they are cruel, but because they know that some absent threads cannot be re-woven.

The light never gets bright here. It is always dim, a perpetual late afternoon under heavy clouds. The warm air presses against your skin, and the water drips, and the moss drinks it. The silence is not empty; it is full of tiny, unhurried sounds—the creak of a settling strand, the whisper of a leaf, the distant plink of water, the faint vibration of a distant animal moving along the same line. You could stand for hours and not see another person, but you would feel the world around you, alive and woven and mostly hollow, holding itself together over the vast, warm, hazy deep.

This is the Reach. Or the High Loom. Or perhaps it has no name, because the Tenders have never needed to name it. It is simply the place where the threads hold, and where the holes do not.
