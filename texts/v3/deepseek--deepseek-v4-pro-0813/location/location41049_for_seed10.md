The plateau has no name in any living language. It is high enough that the clouds below it look like a second, softer ocean, and cold enough that every exhalation becomes a small personal weather system. The bedrock under everything is black and glassy, worn to a featureless flatness by an era of erosion so long that the stone no longer remembers being mountain. Nothing here has been built; the only architecture is grown. The ground is not mineral but vegetable—a living mat of peat, leaf, mycelium, and vascular cable, several metres thick, saturated with water. It is a continuous organ, the true body of the place, and it covers the old stone like a wet skin.

In the dim, high-latitude light, photosynthesis is too weak to support such mass, so the mat has learned other ways. It ferments its own decay, taps electrical potential between the wet sky and the mineral floor, and condenses the cold fog directly into sugars through chemistries that do not quite belong to biology. The result is industrial. Across the open tableland, spaced so far apart that each appears alone, rise the engines. They are not constructed—no hands have shaped them, no foundations hold them—but they are impossible to call anything else. Some are hollow reed-chimneys thirty metres high, pulling moisture out of the cloud ceiling and releasing it as warm rain from vents along their shafts. Others are flattened pods the size of chapels, their surfaces covered in stomatal apertures that open and close with a slow piston rhythm, hissing steam. Some have leaf-blade turbines that turn in the wind and crackle with blue static discharge. The whole plain smells of ozone, wet iron, and anaerobic peat.

Between the engines the space is enormous. You can walk for an hour through knee-deep mist and see only three of them, each announcing itself long before you arrive by a change in the sound—a deeper thrum, a rhythmic clank of lignified parts, a hum like a distant electrical substation. The density of presence is low, but the presence itself is total. The ground underfoot is alive, and it is working.

Water is everywhere but seldom as open water. The air is balanced on the edge of condensation; every leaf, every stone, every chitinous back drips. The mat is saturated like a sponge. Your footsteps press cold water up around your ankles. The rock below weeps through cracks, filling shallow depressions that have no banks because the land is too flat to hold them. Sometimes the pools reflect not the sky above but a sky from another hour—darker, lower, or lit by a sun that is not there. Rain falls often, but thinly, more like a heavy dew that keeps the world at the exact point of wetness.

The place is loud. It is not loud in the way of cities or storms, but in the way of a factory running unattended. There is a continuous hiss from the vents, a low mechanical pulse from the mat itself, and the constant tick and creak of growing structures. The animals add their own noise. Fauna is abundant but scattered. Long, glassy grazers move in loose groups across the mat, their jaws cropping the photosynthetic skin with a sound like many pairs of scissors. Swarms of small winged things wheel between the engines, attracted to the electric fields; their bodies arc blue in the mist. Inside the hollow reeds, something heavy bellows, and the sound emerges as a cloud of steam before the sound itself arrives—a small looping of cause and effect that the place does not bother to correct.

The landscape is turbulent in a way that geology is not supposed to be. The mat does not sit still. Domes of vegetation push up overnight, growing from the flatness like blisters, then burst in the cold morning, releasing gas and seed. Channels that carried water at dawn are dry by noon, rerouted by a new root dam. In storm-state, when the cloud ceiling lowers and the wind shifts, the engines roar. The ground undulates visibly. It is less like walking on terrain than on the flank of an enormous, wakeful factory that is continually dismantling and rebuilding itself.

This is an ancient place, but it is not stable. Its age is the age of the stone beneath, not of the life above. That stone has reached the end of erosion—there is nothing left to remove, no slope left to flatten. It is the final floor of the world. In places the mat thins and the black rock shows through, polished to a mirror. If you look into it too long, the reflection is wrong: the sky in the stone is from a different age, the horizon is closer or farther, and sometimes there are figures standing where none stand in the present. The plants seem to be metabolizing time as well as light, digesting old weather and exhaling it as new. In the hollow bowls of negative space where the mat grows inward rather than outward, the air is still and cold and tastes of static. Creatures that enter these bowls sometimes come out smaller, or older, or not at all, and the other animals have learned to walk around them.

There are no walls, no roads, no artifacts. The only things that resemble structures are organs. The only things that resemble machines are living. The plateau is not a ruin of industry but an industry that has never needed a builder. It is a high, wet, cold, dim plain where the world is continuously manufactured from fog and time, and where the ancient stone beneath has already finished all the erosion it will ever know. To be there is to feel like a temporary inconsistency in a process that has been running forever—and may, at any moment, be corrected.
