It is a city at the bottom of an old, dry everything, below the surface of a world that has forgotten rain.

The first thing is the descent. The surrounding land is flat and high, a stony plateau scarred by wind. Then it gives way, as if the earth has taken a slow breath inward. The ground slopes down and down, not in cliffs but in wide, tired steps. The rim behind you becomes a low line; the floor below opens out until you cannot see the entire depression at once. It is not a cave—the sky is still there—but it is a place that sits below its surroundings, a bowl in the world.

The light is dim. Even at noon the sun does not seem to fall straight down; it is diffused through a permanent haze of fine dust, salt, and maybe spores. The sky is the color of unpolished silver, darker near the rim, pale at the zenith but never bright. Shadows are long and soft; distances go gray. The air is cool, not cold, and it grows cooler as you descend. It has a dry, mineral smell—chalk, powder, a faint chemical bite of salt and old urine from the animals. Your breath does not fog, but the stone is cool under your hand.

There is no standing water anywhere. No streams, no pools, no seeps—only the memory of them: old channels, dry basins, rounded stones set in cement-like sand. At night, a fog fills the basin, thin and pale, rolling down from the rim. By morning it has settled as a fine dew on every surface—on stone, on dust, on leaves. The dew is not enough to drink, but it is enough for life.

And life is here. The vegetation is lush, but not in the way of water-green jungles. It is a dry lushness, a fur of gray, rust, and olive that grows over the ruins in thick mats. There are lichens, crusty and branching, covering walls and boulders. There are low shrubs with small, waxy leaves, spaced just far enough apart that you can walk between them, but abundant enough that the ground has a texture. There are larger trees, if you can call them that—gnarled, many-armed, with bark like cracked leather and canopies of fine, dark needles or scales. They grow in the shelter of old courtyards, their roots pushing through paving stones. Hanging plants trail from the ledges of the high terraces, pale and silver, combing the fog. The vegetation is patchy: dense in some places, absent in others. But where it grows, it grows thickly, and it gives the ruin the feel of a garden.

The city is everywhere under that vegetation. It was built on the floor of the basin and up the lower slopes, in tiers and platforms. The stone is the same as the cliffs—a pale, gray-brown rock—but cut and fitted. Now it is ancient beyond memory. The walls have lost their corners. The edges of doorways and windows are rounded, as if the whole city has been tumbled in a slow river of wind. Stairs have become ramps. Towers have become tall, fluted hoodoos. Arches have widened and sagged, some of them more like cave mouths than engineering. Streets are shallow depressions filled with dust, and the dust is deep in places, as fine as talcum.

But you can still read the urban form. There are large plazas, their flagstones cracked but orderly. There are colonnades, their columns leaning but still standing in rows. There are market squares, with the remains of low stone tables or platforms. There are lanes and alleys, narrow enough that your voice would have echoed once. There are grand stairways that climb from one district to another. There are cisterns, dry now, their plaster linings crumbled to greenish sand. There are walls, some still ten feet high, that once separated neighborhoods. The city consists of several distinct clusters—maybe districts—linked by raised causeways that cross the empty stretches of pan. The whole thing is open and vast, not a single dense core but a sprawl of stone across the floor, fading into the haze. From a high terrace you can see the far side of the basin as a dark line, ten or fifteen kilometers away, with more structures indistinctly visible there.

There is no technology here, not anymore. No metal, no fire, no tools. The only technology is the stone itself, shaped into walls and arches and stairways. It is inert, crumbling, occupied only by the animals.

The animals are abundant. The place is a city-sized rookery, warren, and grazing ground. Large herbivores, slow and broad, move through the lichen fields in small herds, cropping the growth with wide mouths. They have thick coats the color of the dust, and their horns—if they have them—are worn smooth from rubbing against stone. They are not fast; they move with the patience of animals that have little to fear and little energy to waste. Their calls are low, resonant notes that seem to come from the ground rather than the air, and they repeat at long intervals. Smaller grazers, like stocky rodents, sit upright on hind legs and nibble at shrubs; they whistle to each other in thin, high bursts. Predators—long and low, with pale eyes—slip between the ruins, but they are rarely seen, only their scat and tracks in the dust.

Above, the flyers fill the dim air. They roost in the holes and arches, in the hollow bellies of the old buildings, by the thousands. At dusk they pour out, a sky-wide cloud of needle-winged bodies, and their chittering is like gravel sliding down a slope. They wheel over the basin, feeding on the small insects that rise from the lichen. At dawn they return, and the sound of their settling is another kind of roar.

The insects themselves are abundant, too: tiny pale flies, beetles, moths with dusty wings, and things that click. They emerge from the cracks in the stone and the bark of the trees. Their noise is a constant ticking, like the cooling of a hot rock, or the patter of dry rain. In the still air between calls, it is always there.

The sound of the place is the hardest thing to get used to. It is not just the animals. The wind moves through the fluted stone and the eroded arches, and the whole city seems to sing. It makes long, hollow notes, like a flute. The calls of the animals bounce off the basin walls—not once but many times, fading and overlapping, so that a single cry becomes a chord. In the center of the city, near the amphitheater, the echo is so strong that the air seems to press against your ears. You cannot hear your own footsteps; you cannot hear your own breathing. You feel the sound in your chest and in the soles of your feet. At times, when the herds all call at once, or when the flyers pass overhead, the noise is a physical thing, a wall of vibration that sends little avalanches of dust down the walls.

And yet the place is not crowded. The sound lies about density. Most of the city is empty—long, silent avenues between one patch of life and the next; empty plazas where nothing moves except wind-driven dust; rows of roofless rooms with no sign of occupation. The animals cluster in particular places: around the thickest lichen fields, in the old cisterns where dew collects, in the high arches that catch the morning light. Their territories are large. The grazers move slowly across miles of open ground. The flyers only gather at certain ledges. The city itself is a skeleton, mostly hollow, sparsely tenanted. In between the clusters is a wide, open quiet, broken only by the ever-present wind.

The dynamic of the place is slow. Nothing rushes. The fog comes in at night and leaves at dawn. The animals call at intervals—not constantly, but in waves, as if the basin is breathing. The plants grow a little each season, their roots widening the cracks in the stones. The city erodes grain by grain. Dust settles, then wind redistributes it, then it settles again. Over centuries, the walls sink; over millennia, the towers become hoodoos. The herds follow the fog, migrating from one end of the basin to the other, but so slowly that you would have to watch for weeks to see it.

The ecology has rules. Water is the first rule: there is none, except for the nightly fog. Everything is built around that. The plants have waxy leaves and deep roots; they close their pores during the day and open them at night. The animals get most of their moisture from the dew that collects on leaves and stone, and from the bodies of the insects. The herbivores are territorial; their low calls are warnings, not songs. The flyers return to the same roosts every night, and the sound of their arrival is part of the order. The predators are quiet, using the noise to hide. The city itself, though empty of its builders, shapes everything: it provides shelter, perches, shade, and the stone that holds the dew. The animals move through it as if it were a natural canyon system, not architecture.

The place has settled into its final form. The erosion has softened it. The vegetation has claimed it. The animals have filled it. The sound has become the most prominent thing, but underneath the sound is a vast patience—a place that has been the same for so long that change has become almost imperceptible. It is a dry, cool garden growing through an ancient city, below the surface of the world, in a dim light, under a constant, deafening, slow-moving voice.
