The darkness is not empty. It has texture—a dry, fine grain, as though powdered stone hangs permanently in the air. It has warmth. Not the warmth of a sun; there is no sun here, not anymore, or perhaps there never was. The heat rises from the ground itself, a slow geothermal exhalation that has been going on so long it has become a fact of the world, like gravity, like the color of the sky. The air is so dry it pulls at the breath, at the corners of the mouth, at anything soft it can find. It tastes of hot metal and old salt. When you move through it, you feel it as a faint resistance, a kind of mineral breath against the skin.

This is a high place. The plateau stretches in every direction for distances that exceed the eye, a vast elevated tableland lifted above the darker lowlands that fall away at its edges into cliffs, into terraces, into escarpments carved back by wind into scalloped and cove-like forms. But the interior is almost flat. Not perfectly flat—there are shallow swales, low ridges, the memory of a river system that has not carried water in epochs, its channels now mere undulations, its floodplains merged into a single smooth expanse. The whole surface has been eroded to a kind of terminal smoothness. This is the final form of a landscape, the shape it settles into when all the violence is finished, when every mountain has been planed away, when every valley has been filled, when wind and time have had their last word.

The stone underfoot is dark—basaltic, iron-rich, sometimes almost metallic. It has a sheen to it, a polished quality that comes from unimaginable ages of wind-driven abrasion. In places the rock is covered with desert varnish, a thin coat of iron and manganese oxides that has built up over millennia, so smooth and dark it reflects the faintest light like a mirror of black glass. Ventifacts stand in the open ground—stones that have been faceted by the wind into strange, angular shapes, three-faced, keeled, sometimes balanced on a single worn point. They are scattered across the plateau, each one alone in a wide space, like monuments to the patience of the wind. None of them move. None of them have changed in longer than history.

There is no vegetation. Not a lichen, not a spore, not a trace of green or brown or of anything that might once have lived. The world is purely mineral. The ground is bare rock, regolith, dust. The only color, if color there is, comes from mineral deposits—salt crusts, sulfate blooms, the rust-red staining of oxidized iron, the occasional pale band of quartzite, the deep black of the basalt. The absence of life is total, and yet it does not feel like death. It feels like a place where life never needed to exist, where the world has simply continued along a line of its own, indifferent to biology.

And yet there is a city.

It emerges from the darkness gradually, as the eye adjusts, as shapes resolve from the black. At first it reads as natural—low ridges, a distant line of hills. But then the regularity becomes apparent. Right angles. Straight lines. A grid that has been softened by erosion but not erased. The city is built from the same dark stone as the plateau, and it has been worn by the same wind, so that the boundary between construction and geology has blurred. It is hard to say, looking at a particular mass, whether it is a building or an outcrop, whether it was placed there or grew there. The city is the landscape now, and the landscape is the city.

It is not a dense city. The buildings stand far apart, each one isolated in its own expanse of bare ground, connected by wide streets that are more like cleared corridors of rock. The streets are paved with fitted stone blocks, each block worn smooth and flush with its neighbors, so that the road surface is a single continuous plane, polished by centuries of traffic. The gaps between the buildings are vast—open plazas, empty yards, stretches of nothing where the wind has deposited a thin film of dust and then swept it away again. The city is a scattering of presences across a huge space, a low-density grid of structures that seem to have been placed with a deliberate restraint, as though the builders feared crowding.

The architecture is low and massive. The buildings are rectilinear, one or two stories high, built for function and not for beauty. Walls of cut stone, reinforced with iron beams that have rusted to a deep brown-black. Flat roofs. Few windows—most structures are blind, their interiors hidden. But time and the wind have given them a kind of austere grandeur. The corners have been rounded. The edges softened. The surfaces polished. The city looks as though it has been petrified, as though it has been turning to stone for so long it no longer remembers being anything else.

This is an industrial city. The evidence is everywhere, even in the dark. Smokestacks rise from some of the structures—tall cylinders of brick and steel, some still complete, some broken at the top into jagged profiles against the near-black sky. Conveyors snake between buildings—long, low gantries with rusted belts and idler wheels. Pipes run along the ground and hang in catenaries between supports. Storage tanks, hoppers, silos. A rail yard at the city's edge where the tracks have been worn flush with the stone, the rails themselves barely visible as parallel lines of slightly different color, the ties long gone, replaced by dust and mineral cement. Switches, signals, loading platforms. The entire apparatus of a scaled industrial operation, now silent.

But not entirely dead.

There is power here still. Not much, but some. A network of indicator lights is scattered through the darkness—tiny points of red and green and amber, each one marking a valve, a junction, a sensor, a piece of machinery that continues to function on timescales too slow for human attention. They do not flash. They burn, steady, weeks and months and years of uninterrupted glow. A few sodium lamps still shine, a deep orange, casting pools of light on the stone streets, each pool separated from the next by a hundred meters of pure black. The lamps are so old the glass has frosted, and their light is diffuse, warm, almost liquid. They hiss faintly, or perhaps that is the wind.

The city was built around heat. Deep fissures in the plateau, cracks that descend into the world's interior, release a constant upwelling of warm air. Around these fissures the city clustered. Heat exchangers, great arrays of fins and tubes, still stand over some of the vents. Pipes carry the warmth to other structures—district heating for a population that may no longer exist. The ground near the vents is warmer, sometimes hot to the touch, and the air above shimmers faintly even in the dark, a distortion of the already faint light. The heat is the only dynamic process in the city, and it is so steady, so constant, that it has become geological. It does not pulse. It does not vary. It simply is.

There are machines still running. Slowly. Impossibly slowly. A flywheel somewhere turns once every few decades, grinding on bearings that need no lubrication because there is nothing left to wear. A pump cycles brine through a closed loop at a rate measured in centimeters per year. A ventilation fan, its blades visible through a shattered housing, rotates so slowly it seems still, but if you return after a month you might see that it has moved. These machines are quiet beyond silence. They do not hum. They do not vibrate. They are the sound of a city dreaming.

The wind is the only constant sound. It is not strong—a gentle, unceasing flow from the east, the planet's slow exhalation. It carries fine dust, so fine it is more like smoke than sand, and it deposits it in thin drifts against the buildings' windward sides. The wind makes a sound at the edges of things—a low, smooth hiss where it passes over broken glass, a soft whistle through a pipe, a steady hush across the open stone. It is so constant it becomes silence. The ears stop hearing it after a while, and then the true quiet reveals itself: a deep, almost subaural stillness, broken only rarely by a distant creak, a trickle of loose dust, the very occasional sound of animal movement.

The animals are rare. You might go for days without seeing one, weeks without hearing one. But they are here. They have adapted to the dark, the dry, the warm stone. Some are small—pale, eyeless things that live in the fissures, drawn to the heat, never emerging into the open. Others are larger, slow-moving, armored, their bodies the color of the rock, so well camouflaged that you see them only when they move, and even then only as a disturbance in the darkness. Once in a great while, something crosses the open ground between two buildings—a long, low shape, segmented, moving with a deliberation that suggests great age and great patience. It leaves a trail in the dust, a shallow groove that may persist for years before the wind erases it. The creatures do not seem to notice the city. They move through it as through any other part of the landscape, following the heat, following some slow instinct, following nothing at all.

The sky is almost featureless. A thin dust haze hangs in the upper atmosphere, a permanent veil that blocks most of the stars. But not all. At the zenith, if you lie on the warm stone and wait long enough, a few of the brightest stars emerge—distant, cold, irrelevant. There is no moon. There is no aurora. The darkness is not caused by the dust alone; it seems to be a property of the world itself, a baseline condition, as though light has never been abundant here. The horizon is marked only by a faint difference in tone—the sky a deep brown-black, the ground a deep blue-black, the line between them barely visible.

The city's center is a great shaft. It descends into the plateau, a vertical bore of unknown depth, lined with concrete and steel. It was a mine once, or a well, or something else entirely—an access point to the world's interior heat. Now it is covered by a grate of rusted iron, the gaps between the bars wide enough to feel the warm air rising, steady and stale, carrying a smell of deep stone. The shaft is the city's reason for existing. Everything else—the factories, the pipes, the rail lines—was built to serve it. The grate has not been moved in centuries. But the heat still rises. It always has.

The city is not a ruin. That is the strangest thing. A ruin implies decay, and there is no decay here. The dry air preserves everything. The constant wind erodes, but so slowly that the erosion is a kind of patience, not destruction. Nothing collapses. Nothing falls. The buildings stand as they have stood for millennia, their stones fitted so well that no moisture can penetrate, their iron already rusted to a stable state, their foundations settled into the bedrock. The city has reached equilibrium with its environment. It no longer changes. It has become part of the landscape's final form, one more feature in a world that has finished becoming.

And yet there is a sense of waiting. Not tension, not expectation, but a kind of suspended attention, as though the city is not asleep but simply resting, pausing between operations. The machines that still run do so without purpose, maintaining themselves, keeping a process alive for no one. The indicator lights burn because they have always burned. The heat rises because the world is warm below. The wind blows because the atmosphere turns. Nothing here requires explanation; nothing here requires purpose. The city simply is, and it has been for so long that its existence has become indistinguishable from nature.

In the deepest part of the dark, between the pools of sodium light, there are places where the eye begins to invent. Shadows that seem to move. Shapes that resolve and then dissolve. The mind, unaccustomed to such stable darkness, starts to populate it with things that are not there. But sometimes it is wrong, and the shape is real—a rare animal, a folding of stone, the outline of a doorway that was always there, leading into a building that no one has entered for longer than any record.

The plateau extends beyond the city in all directions. To the west, a dry lake bed—a vast salt pan, flat as glass, its surface divided into polygons by contraction cracks, each polygon a meter across, the salt hard as concrete. In the faint light, the salt pan seems to glow, a pale white expanse that holds the darkness differently, reflecting a light that does not exist. The polygons creak occasionally, a sound like distant ice, as the salt adjusts to infinitesimal temperature changes. The sound travels for kilometers in the dry air, arriving at the city's edge as a faint, brittle whisper.

To the east, the ventifacts multiply, a field of faceted stones stretching to the horizon, each one oriented to the prevailing wind, each one polished to a dull shine on the windward face. They are the only things that still seem to be changing, and their changes are so slow they might as well be eternal.

The city fades into this landscape gradually. The outermost buildings are low and broken, their roofs gone, their walls half-consumed by the ground. Beyond them, only the foundations remain, rectangular outlines of stone flush with the surface. Beyond those, nothing but the plateau, the wind, the warmth, the dark. The city does not end so much as it dissolves, scattering its structures wider and wider until they are no longer structures at all, just stones that happen to be arranged in right angles, then just stones.

This is a place that has outlived its builders. It has outlived their names, their purposes, their reasons. It has outlived the water, the plants, the light. It is a place where the distinction between made and born has ceased to matter, where industry has become geology, where a city has become a landform. The darkness is warm. The wind is patient. The heat from below never stops. And the plateau sits high above the world, dry, silent, ancient, stable, and utterly indifferent. It is not waiting for anything. It is simply continuing, the way stone continues, the way heat rises, the way darkness rests on a world that has forgotten the sun.
