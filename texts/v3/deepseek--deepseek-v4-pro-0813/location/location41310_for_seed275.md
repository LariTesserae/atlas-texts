The first thing is the light, because there is no sky. It comes from the ceiling: a long, irregular vault of black mineral studded with thousands of round, translucent bulbs that glow the color of noon through cloud. They do not flicker, and they do not sway. The light lies evenly across the lower world, and by that light the landscape appears like a city that has been grown rather than built.

You are below the surface—so far below that “surface” is only a story people tell. The air is cool and extraordinarily dry. No drip, no seep, no condensation on the rock. The walls are sharp and raw, broken into planes and splinters, as if the stone was cut last week and has not yet learned to weather. There are no rounded edges here, no smooth water-worn stones, no stalactites. This is a place still being formed. The rock is fresh, black, shot through with quartz threads and copper-green seams. The only signs of wear are the places where roots have begun to pry the cracks apart.

The ground is not ground. It is plant. A single continuous mat of interwoven stems and small leaves, pale gray-green, felted and tough, covers everything in sight. It stretches for kilometers, over low rises and down into broad basins. It is the substrate: there is no soil under it, only more plant, and below that the mineral. When you step, it gives slightly—not soft but springy—and it smells of dry spice and hot oil. There is no bare rock on the floor. Even the roadways are made of this living mat, pruned flat and compressed by generations of passing feet and wheels into a smooth, dense hide.

The plant is not like any water-drunk forest. Its sap is a thin, clear oil, cool to the touch, slightly astringent. It does not run with water; the whole ecosystem is non-aqueous, chemical and slow. The plant draws its moisture from the mineral breath of the deep earth and converts it in ways that make the air taste faintly of aldehyde and warm copper. In places, the sap oozes from cracks and pools as heavy, clear beads, but it never drips like water. It is too thick, too deliberate.

From this mat rise the structures of the city. They are widely spaced, so the place does not feel crowded. Wide open root-plains run between districts, covered in the same low vegetation, scattered with small, pale animals. But there is no doubt that this is urban. Tall buildings rise out of the plant as swellings of the same organism—walls of smooth, ridged tissue, some dozens of meters high, shaped into towers, gantries, arched sheds. They look grown, but they have been finished by machines: doors cut into the living tissue and framed with blackened steel, windows of resin set in layered stems, bolts and cables embedded where the plant has healed around them. Entire factories are built this way, half plant and half industry, their smokestacks rising like hardened seed-heads, their sides patched with riveted iron plates.

The city is called Low Meridian, or just the Undergrove, depending on who is speaking. It is an industrial settlement spread through this open rift. Trams run on rails of polished iron laid over roots that have been trained into long, straight ridges. The cars are small, open, their mechanisms humming dryly. There are loading yards where hoppers filled with mineral nodule and chopped root-fiber wait on sidings. Factories operate continuously but not loudly; the plant material that surrounds them absorbs sound in strange ways. The engines thump at the bottom of the audible, more vibration than noise. The great cooling stacks vent a warm, clear vapor that smells of eucalyptus and ozone, but it leaves no dampness on the skin.

The light is normal, but it has no single source you can point to. The glowing bladders on the ceiling are alive, cultured from some deep-root form, and they produce a steady daylight. It is not magical; it is industrial light, powered by the same grid that runs the trams and the mills. There are shadows, but they are short and slightly greenish. The far wall of the rift is barely visible as a blue-gray band, too far to make out, and the city simply stops at the edge of the current lamps. The underground world is large, regional, open.

Sound is sparse. A place this active should be loud, but it is not. The plant absorbs acoustic energy: the root-mat swallows footsteps, the walls of vegetal buildings dampen the churn of machinery. The result is a kind of quiet that is not silence but a low, steady presence. You can hear the dry creak of the plant flexing, the intermittent click of signal-relays, the soft whir of exhaust fans. Voices, if any, sound close and papery. There is a feeling that the whole landscape is holding its breath.

Fauna is abundant, but most of it is small and quiet. Needle-moths cluster on the warm glass of the ceiling lamps, their wings the color of ash. Six-legged grazers, the size of large dogs, move slowly across the root-plains in loose herds, cropping the surface with comb-like mouthparts. There are colonials of pale, translucent ants that live inside the walls of the buildings, tending aphid-like animals on the plant’s sap. High in the stem-towers, dust-thrushes nest in holes that the plant grows around them. Occasionally a long, thin predator—something between a ferret and a centipede—flows from one root shadow to the next, silent as an oil leak. The animals do not cry out much; their signals are chemical or low-frequency, and the plant carries those signals better than air.

The ecology is active and continuous. The plant is growing, but slowly enough that you cannot watch it, only infer it. Streets must be pruned back daily or they would heal shut. In some unused districts, the roads are already soft, the walls blurred with new stems. The city is under construction and under maintenance at the same time. Work crews with steam-cutters and acid-sprays move along the perimeter, carving new neighborhoods from the raw mat. At the edges of the settled area, there are rows of scaffolding made of living bamboo-like rods and steel, where the next factory is being grown into shape.

The geology is equally young. The walls of the rift look like they were cracked open yesterday. There is no erosion, no weathering, no accumulation of sediment. The plant is the first erosive force: its roots have begun to splinter the rock, seeking mineral salts, and the city exploits that. The plant’s deep roots draw up copper, iron, sulfur, and rare earths from the fresh fissures, and the factories extract them from the sap. That is the basis of the industry here: a slow, biological mining that leaves the rock shattered but clean.

The rules of this world are different. Water is not the solvent of life; oil and mineral slurry are. The cold slows everything, but it makes the plant stronger and the machinery easier to cool. The plant is a single organism, or perhaps a colony of mutualists so joined that the distinction no longer matters. It is the ground, the buildings, the food, the mine, the insulation, and the roadbed. People and animals live upon it, inside it, and because of it. When something dies, it is folded into the mat by the plant’s slow pressure and becomes part of the substrate. There are no dead leaves, no rot, no waterlogged decay. Things dry and are reabsorbed.

At the center of one broad plain is a tall stem-tower with a spiral staircase grown around its outside and an engine at its heart. The engine is not like a motor; it is a living organ, a muscle-oven, pulsing and glowing faintly through translucent walls. It winds the plant’s growth, or is wound by it. From the top, the city stretches out as a system of low, scattered structures and light, greenish and gray, in a cavern so large it might be a sky. The quiet is so complete that you can hear the plant breathing, a long, slow creak like ice on a frozen sea.

And then a shiver passes through the root-mat. The ground humps slightly, the buildings lean a fraction, and the animals pause. It is a grow-quake, a small one, the plant adjusting its hold on the world. Somewhere beyond the lamp-light, a new root has found a seam of ore and pushed into it, tearing the fresh rock. Another part of the city has begun.
