It is a small place, high and dry: a hanging bowl near the top of a plateau, a couple of hundred strides across, closed by wind-fluted walls. It feels less like a valley than a roofless cathedral, a single room open to a pale, ordinary sky.

There is no water. The floor is a pale crust of salt and mineral dust, cracked into dark polygons. The air is cold, thin, and dry; your breath makes a little cloud, but there is no dew, no ice, no seep. The cold has a metallic edge. The daylight is normal, gray-gold, but the sky is colorless and far away.

And yet the ground is lush. In every trough, in the lee of every boulder, a dense carpet of vegetation grows. It is not leaf or stem as you know it. It is a mat of fine filaments, like steel wool, like heavy fur, in rust, indigo, and pale greenish blue. It grows in thick swirls and hides the stone. From a distance it looks soft, almost wet. Touch it and it is dry as paper, cold, brittle; it crackles. It is alive, but water is not part of its chemistry. It feeds on mineral salts, trace gases, and the electric charge of the wind. The lushness is real, but it is a lushness of texture and surface, not of juice.

The volume above the carpet is nearly empty. A few dozen tall masts rise at wide intervals, twenty or thirty meters high, each a thin column of dark greenish metal-like material, fluted and slightly curved. They are too widely spaced to be called a forest; they are more like organ pipes set far apart in an enormous hall. Between them the air is vast. The place is small in area but feels huge in three dimensions because there is no canopy, no understory, only the low vegetation, the scattered masts, and the open sky.

The masts are not natural. They look grown rather than built. Their bases swell into the rock like roots, and their sides carry fine spiral ridges. At their tops, each splits into a fan of narrow vanes, like a dry seedpod or a heat exchanger. Some are broken. Some have collapsed and begun to regrow from their own debris. Around the basin are other traces of a technology current physics does not fully describe: a low ring of fused blocks with no visible joints, humming faintly; a single arch near the far wall made of a material that seems to breathe; a few half-buried cables that loop into the dust and vanish. The arch does something to the wind. When a gust passes through it, the air inside flickers with a lattice of light, and the sound in a narrow cone becomes a perfect, impossible silence.

The place is deafening. The wind is the main instrument. It pours over the rim and drops into the bowl, and the masts turn it into a sustained chord, low and physical, a little different with each gust. The wool-vegetation shivers and hisses. The dust moves with a sound like sandpaper. Above this there is an entire layer of animal noise. The air is full of creatures, though they are scattered across the large volume. Translucent bell-kites drift on gas-filled bodies, each the size of a fist or a dinner plate, trailing fine sensory threads. Spiral midges ride the dust devils in long ropes. Larger leather-winged things live in the flanges of the masts and sing by forcing air through holes in their carapaces. Their keening weaves through the low chord, sometimes harmonizing, sometimes fighting it. The total noise is less a sound than a pressure. You feel it in your teeth and sternum.

The bowl is turbulent. Dust devils form constantly, spin across the floor, pull up filaments of vegetation and dust, braid them into ropes, then collapse and drop them. The ground cover is being rearranged before your eyes. Pieces tear loose and tumble across the basin like dry tumbleweeds. The masts shed their outer skins with sharp cracks; the shed material spirals out and disintegrates into fine flakes. Some of those flakes catch in cracks or against other masts, and within hours they begin to grow into new small masts. The arch occasionally sends out a pulse of flattened silence that knocks a dust devil out of the air. Everything is busy, but not with animal hunger. It is more like a machine or a bazaar, a constant making and unmaking.

For all that, the place is old and settled. The cliffs are polished into long flutes. The boulders are rounded. The floor polygons are stable. The masts are pitted and weathered. The turbulence is not an event; it is the place’s normal state, and it has carved the bowl into exactly this shape over a long time. The chaos has an equilibrium.

There are strange touches, quiet but unmistakable. The vegetation pulses with faint waves of blue light, as if a slow electric current passes through its roots. The air smells of ozone and iron filings. Sometimes a dust devil holds its shape a second too long—a spiral, an arch, a hand—before collapsing. The silence from the arch is too clean, like a hole in the world rather than an absence.

No water anywhere. The cold is dry, the dust is dry, the plants are dry, the animals are dry. Thirst is not the right word. This is a place that has left water behind entirely. Life here uses charge, pressure, mineral chemistry, cold wind.

It is a chamber, not a country. It is never still. It is a small, empty, lush, dry, deafening, self-remaking room at the top of the world.
