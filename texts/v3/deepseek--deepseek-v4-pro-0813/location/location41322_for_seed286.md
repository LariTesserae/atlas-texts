# The Drowned Factory Plateau

Come down from the surface. The sea is a pale roof of light overhead, but it is not far; this is high water over high land. The plateau’s tabletop lies perhaps forty meters below the waves—shallow enough for ordinary daylight to turn the water a greenish blue, shallow enough for plants. Beyond the plateau’s edges, the bottom falls away into dark trenches. Here, on the drowned highland, the world is one vast, cool, submerged country.

The terrain is not stone. It is living.

A continuous sheet of plant matter covers the ancient rock, so thick in places it has become the landscape itself. The old mountain ridges are buried beneath a fibrous, mineralized mat—part root, part leaf, part callus. Where the buried rock rises, the plant rises with it, forming smooth hogbacks and terraces. Where the rock drops, the plant drapes over the edge in curtains of interwoven stems. There are no bare surfaces, no sediment fields, no sand. Everything solid is alive or was alive and has not stopped being alive. The original geology is only a rumor, a hard shape underneath.

It is not crowded, though. The living floor is broad and low, and for long stretches the water above it is empty. The place is vast, and the tall structures are separated by kilometers of open, slightly green water. Here and there, a tower or grove rises from the plain, dark and regular. In the blue distances they look like smokestacks, refineries, cooling towers—but they are not built. Nothing here is built.

The towers are plant organs. They are hollow, cylindrical stems, twenty or thirty meters across, rising two or three hundred meters from the floor. They are made of a dense composite of cellulose, silica, and metal-bearing fibers that the plant has extracted from the sea. Their surfaces are smooth and ribbed, with rows of vertical slits that open and close like gills. At their tops they flare into slow-turning rosettes of flat, blade-like leaves. These leaves are toothed and translucent, more like turbine blades than petals. They rotate, not quickly, but continuously, with a heavy, inseparable motion. They are part of the plant, and they are part of a machine. The distinction has collapsed.

This is an industrial landscape without any industry but the living one. The plant is a factory. It pulls minerals from the water through its roots—through the whole lower mat—and processes them in cold, chemical ways. It uses light and the gradients between fresh and salt water to do work. Its vascular pipes are like pipelines. Its heart-like bulbs pump water. Its leaves are gears and flywheels, and in some places they actually mesh with one another, tooth to tooth, turning in a pale, living gearwork. It does not burn fuel. It is cool, never hot. The water around it stays at a constant chill, just above freezing, and the only warmth is the faint metabolic heat of animals, thin and dispersed.

The sound is enormous. Underwater, sound travels far and fast, and this place is never quiet. There is a low-pitched thrum, as though the entire plateau is humming. From the tower groves comes a rhythmic drumming—pumps contracting in unison, perhaps twenty per minute. The gill-slits hiss and click as they release bubbles. The bubble columns rise in silver curtains, and the water around them roars softly, like a distant falls. The sound is not chaotic. It has a pulse, a repeating pattern that never varies. It sounds like the engine room of a ship, if the ship were the size of a continent and made of plants.

The animals add their own sounds, but they follow the same fixed rhythm. There are schools of slender fish, pale as polished tin, that trace long loops through the open water. They are abundant but not everywhere; they travel in loose, scattered ribbons between the towers. They crop the feeding lobes that the plant extends from the floor, and the plant repairs those lobes at exactly the rate they are eaten. There are eel-things that live in the tower gills, their bodies banded with copper-dark pigment. They click and chirp in time with the valves. Larger animals, slow and heavy, move along the corridors of current between the groves, their flanks glowing faintly with bioluminescent lines. They might be grazers, or filter feeders, or something like whales, but they do not migrate. They travel the same route, over and over, with no variation.

This is the central rule of the place: it never changes. It is in a finished state. The erosion that shaped it is old and already complete; every edge has been smoothed, every channel has been cut. The plant grows just enough to replace what is eaten and worn away, and no more. The animals are born and die at a rate that keeps their numbers exactly the same. The currents flow in the same braided paths. The water chemistry is held at the same balance. The light is always the same ordinary, aqueous daylight, as if the sun has been fixed at noon. There is no night, no storm, no season, no succession, no catastrophe. There is no before or after. There is only the one moment, sustained.

The strangeness is not that the place is alive. It is that the living and the mechanical are the same thing. The plant grows gears. The towers are not constructed, but they are clearly designed—by natural selection, by some old and unguessable evolution, or by the slow intention of the organism itself. There are vanes that funnel current into root-mouths. There are transparent lenses grown from clear leaf tissue that focus light into the mat. There are rows of mineral teeth that file away old growth. There are electrical pulses that run along metal-rich fibers, sending signals across the plateau, like a nervous system and a telegraph system at once. But there are no wires, no fasteners, no welds, no artifacts. If you cut into one of the towers, you would not find a machine inside. You would find tissues, wet and cool, and channels full of fluid, and the curled shapes of leaves that have become pistons. It would not be possible to say where the plant ends and the factory begins.

The atmosphere—the water—tastes of clean metal and cold green. It is oxygen-rich, so rich that bubbles form in your wake and on the surfaces of the plant. It is clear but not empty; dissolved pigments cast faint gold-green banners in the distance. The pressure is slight at this depth, only a few atmospheres, and the cold is steady. The current is always moving at the same speed, a river of water within the sea, driven by the plant’s pumps. It carries a fine suspension of spores and mineral dust, never too much, never too little.

If you waited long enough, you would learn that the sound is not random. The thrumming repeats every eleven minutes, or every hour, though you could not count it because the light never marks the time. The bubble columns rise in exact columns, each one at the same height. The school of silver fish passes the same root-like hummock at the same moment in the cycle. A particular tower to your left always clicks three times before the bass note. It is a world like a stopped clock, except the clock is still ticking; it is the hands that never move. It is an engine running in place, a factory that produces only itself, a forest that has become a machine and a machine that has become a forest.

And the scale of it is too large to see. From any point, you see only a scatter of towers, a curve of the living floor, a few ribbons of fish. But you know, from the way the sound carries and the way the current runs, that the plateau goes on for thousands of kilometers. The high drowned country is continental. It has its own weather of currents, its own atmosphere of dissolved gases, its own topography of living, weathered tissue. It is not a place you can hold in your mind all at once. It is a place that holds you.

The water is cool. The light is steady. The plant hums. Somewhere in the blue distance another tower clicks, another column of bubbles rises, another school turns in its fixed, slow loop. The drowned plateau does not notice. It has been this way for a very long time, and it will not change.
