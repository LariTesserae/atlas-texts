The place is a low, dim, hot basin, maybe ten or fifteen miles wide, where wetness is less a weather than a condition. It has no name; it does not need one, because nothing here records or repeats anything beyond itself. Coming into it from the surrounding higher ground, you first notice the sound: not the sound of wind or water running, but a continuous, close roar of small voices, so dense it seems to fill the ears. The air is warm, wet, and still, and the light is a shadowless dusk, as if the sun is permanently behind a lid of cloud and canopy.

The ground is raw. It is not exactly rock, not exactly mud, but a pale, warm mineral paste with the consistency of soft cheese, and it is seeping water everywhere. The landforms look half-made: shallow terraces with rims no thicker than a lip, blisters of tufa that have set only enough to hold their shape, pools that are not fully separated from one another. There are no stream channels, no rills, no gullies. The water does not travel; it wells up through the ground, lies in sheets and lenses, beads on every surface, and stays. Rocks weep without dripping; the droplets grow until they touch and merge. This is a landscape caught before erosion could begin. Its edges are new, unweathered, unrounded, unscarred. The mineral terraces might be forming, but they are also not growing; the water is saturated, and the chemistry is exactly balanced between deposit and dissolution.

Heat rises out of the earth and out of the water, and because there is no cooler air, there is no breeze. The atmosphere is a warm bath. Fog hangs in stationary bands among the trees, and the moisture condenses on leaves and skin and stone, not because of cold but because the air cannot hold any more. It does not rain; the sky is a dim, flat glow, and the water moves only as condensation and seepage.

The vegetation is lush beyond any usual meaning of the word. Trees grow out of the mineral paste with their roots arched and exposed, their trunks wrapped in ferns and moss. The lower layers are packed so tightly with broad-leaved aroids, ferns, vines, algae, and fungi that the ground is almost invisible. Every branch is coated; every gap is filled. The leaves are heavy with water, many of them drip-tipped, some waxy, some frilled. Flowers are rare and pale in the dimness, but there are fungus clusters and soft, swollen stems. The plants do not form clearings; they form a continuous, saturated mass, as if the whole basin is one organism with a million leaf-shaped mouths.

The animals are not scattered through this vegetation; they are the vegetation’s second layer. The air is thick with insects, hanging in place like suspended motes, each on a small fixed circuit. Dragonflies hover at eye height, their wings making a low, steady drone. Tree frogs sit in dense rows on every wet leaf and along the edges of every pool, each throat pulsing, their calls overlapping into one continuous, ringing note. The water is opaque with tadpoles and small fish and freshwater shrimp; it dimples from below with their movements. Snakes drape over branches in braided tangles; lizards grip the trunks; birds sit shoulder to shoulder, their feathers nearly touching. Bats hang beneath leaves in soft, breathing clusters. There are larger shapes too: a tapir-like animal standing knee-deep in a warm pool, motionless except for the slow blink of an eye; something like a long-necked turtle half sunk in the mud. The density is so great that the place feels crowded from ground to canopy, not with open space between bodies but with bodies almost touching, layered one over another.

There are no nests, no burrows, no webs, no dams, no tools, no structures of any kind. The animals rest directly on the terrain, in the forks of branches, on the surface of the water, on the wet stone. The plants support them without having been shaped for it. Nothing has been built, cut, arranged, or marked. The only impressions are the temporary ones of feet and bodies in the soft ground, and they fill in slowly and vanish, not because the ground erodes but because it returns to its previous form.

The sound is a constant roar, but it is also a still roar. It has no swells or lulls. The calls repeat in exact cycles; the frogs take turns according to some pattern that never varies. The insects’ wings never change pitch. The water drips in the same rhythm, always the same drop, or a drop exactly like the last. This is the place’s deepest rule: it does not change. The heat is uniform, so there is no wind. The water is saturated, so there is no flow. The chemistry is balanced, so there is no growth or erosion. The animals move, but each movement is paired with a return; a bird launches, circles, and lands on the same branch. A frog jumps, and another jumps in the opposite direction, and the ripples cancel. The chorus is endless but identical, like a held chord. The place is not dead; it is teeming. But it is frozen in a kind of perfect equilibrium, an embryonic landscape that has not yet begun to weather and never will.

Because of that, the whole basin has an eerie, convalescent quality. The heat and wet and density are so high that it would be overwhelming if it weren’t also so still. The air smells of hot mud, green growth, animal musk, mineral water, and something fruity decaying but not disappearing. The far side of the valley is a dark green wall, and the sky above is a low cream-gray lid. There is no day or night, only dimness. There are no seasons, no storms, no succession, no disturbance. The world has paused here at the very beginning of making a landscape: low land half-emerged from warm water, covered in a saturated forest and a crawling, buzzing, calling mass of animals, held stable by rules that allow no gradient, no net change.

This is a place of abundance without accumulation, activity without progress, sound without variation. It is a hot, wet, green, dense, unfinished country, a few miles wide, never eroding, never darkening, never becoming anything other than exactly what it is.
