The high pass opens onto a place that first seems like a flat grey sea. It is not a sea. It is a high, newly made plateau covered entirely in close-fitted stone, water, and the glitter of reflected daylight. The air is cool and thin; the light is so bright that the eye cannot rest anywhere without being cut by glare. There is no vegetation anywhere. There are no birds, no insects, no animals. What fills the wide basin below is not wilderness but a single uninterrupted construction: ten thousand basins, channels, walls, ramps, sills, and domes, all wet, all stone, all hand-worked.

From the rim the scale is open—several kilometres of built ground falling in broad tiers toward a central sump—but as you descend, the openness closes. The basins are packed against one another with no empty ground between them. Each one is a different shape and depth: some narrow as a footstep, some broad as a house, some only a shallow depression a finger deep. They are cut into a dark blue-grey stone that rings faintly underfoot. The rims are sharp. The channels are crisp. Nothing has been softened by weather. Water runs through the system but does not erode it; the water is clear, cold, and slightly alkaline, and it moves too slowly to do more than polish.

The water does not come from rain. The sky is usually bare and bright. It comes from a high dome of porous cream-coloured stone at the centre of the plateau. The dome sweats. Moisture beads along its surface, gathers into tiny runnels, and spills outward into the network. The whole place is a condenser and a reservoir at once. In the cool high air, water stays at the surface, filling the basins to different levels, standing in sheets over the stone. Some basins are full to the brim and absolutely still; others hold only a wet membrane of water. The water moves by slow gradients, a thread-wide flow, a seep, a drop every few seconds. In places it climbs a few inches up a groove lined with pale mineral fibre, drawn by capillary pull, so that the water does not merely drain. It circulates.

The constructedness is total. There is no exposed soil, no scree, no natural bedrock. Even the far walls of the valley are dressed and stepped. The ground is a pavement. The hills are terraces. The horizon is a retaining wall. Everywhere there are tool marks: overlapping peck-marks from stone hammers, long parallel striations from grinding, the thumb-smoothed coils of fired clay pipes. The technology is primitive but exact. No metal, no glass, no wheels. Only stone, clay, mineral paste, and water. The joints between blocks are so tight they need no mortar; where a seal is needed, the builders used a pale mineral grout. It has cracked a little, but only at the surface.

The place is dense to a degree that feels deliberate. Every square metre is occupied. If it is not a basin, it is a channel. If not a channel, a sluice. If not a sluice, a stair. There are low walls whose only function is to shade a strip of water from the noon glare. There are fields of thumb-sized dimples that hold individual dew-drops. There are baffles that slow the water into braided loops. There are vents, grates, overflows, siphons. The whole plateau is a single object, but it is also a maze. You could not walk ten paces in a straight line. The only open spaces are the water surfaces, and they are not open; they are held in stone frames.

The atmosphere is quiet. The dominant sound is a low, continuous seeping—water moving through narrow grooves, the faint click of a drop falling from a saturated wick into a full basin. Every few minutes, somewhere deep in the stone, a chamber seems to gulp and reset; the note is so low and so isolated that it is felt more than heard. There is no rustle of leaves, no birdcall, no insect sound. The quiet is not empty. It is full of the almost-inaudible work of water.

Nothing alive is here. No moss, no lichen, no roots. The only green is a thin copper stain on some of the channel walls, where mineral-laden water has evaporated over many years. Pale salt rinds grow at the edges of the shallow basins, and black manganese dendrites spread across the wet dome like branching ferns, but they are mineral, not life. The ecology is a slow chemistry: water wets the stone, the sun lifts a little of it back into the air, minerals stay behind, and over years a rind thickens by a flake. This is the only growth. The place has no metabolism, no food chain, no decay.

The light is bright but not warm. The sun is a white glare in a pale blue-white sky. The stone stays cool. The water stays cool. The air has the smell of a well: wet stone, cold mineral dust, a faint metallic edge. In the morning the whole plateau is covered in dew, and the water beads on the stone like a skin. By mid-morning the dew is gone from the high spots but still wet in the shadows. By evening the surfaces are dry enough to show the tool marks, but still damp in the centre. The cycle is slow and small. The water level in a given basin might change by the width of a finger over a month. The salt grows. The dome sweats. Nothing else happens.

The rules of this place are simple and strange. The ground is not a surface over a water table; the water table is the ground. The stone is a vessel, and the whole plateau is held at saturation by its own shape. Gravity is obeyed, but only just. Water runs downhill, except where it is drawn sideways or upward through wicks and siphons. Erosion is prevented because the water is not allowed to fall freely; it is passed from stone to stone, lip to lip, as if the place were not a landscape but a set of cupped hands. And nothing lives here because nothing has ever been allowed to live. The place is not dead. It is a mineral thing with a pulse, but the pulse is water, and the only breath is condensation.

From the far rim, the whole plateau looks like a woven map: a huge, bright, grey and silver surface, full of water and absolutely still. Step into it, and the map becomes a world. Every basin has a different note when you pass it, every channel a different wetness. The place does not explain itself. It only holds water, and catches light, and waits.
