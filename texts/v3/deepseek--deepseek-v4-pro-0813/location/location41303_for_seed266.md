The Salt Bench is a place so high that the clouds lie below it. To stand at its rim is to stand on an island in the air. The land falls away in long, worn terraces—salt cliffs and ironstone ledges, fluted by wind—down into a sea of white cloud that stretches to the limits of the world. The air at this altitude should be thin and cold, but it is not. The atmosphere is deep and heavy, a blanket of nitrogen, carbon dioxide, and sulfur dioxide that holds the sun’s warmth against the ground. It is warm, dry, and faintly sharp to smell, like hot stone and chlorine.

The Bench itself is vast—a tableland of compacted evaporites and iron oxides, six hundred kilometers from rim to rim, so large that it has its own weather. There is no water. What water exists is only a trace, locked in the crystal lattices of old gypsum and clays. Rain has not fallen in the memory of the rock. The only liquids are dense anhydrous leachates—sulfur-rich mineral fluids that rise from deep beds, hot and sluggish—and they do not trickle so much as ooze, darkening the salt to brown and green. The ground is a hard pavement of halite and anhydrite, cracked into polygons and dusted with powder as fine as talcum. The dust stirs underfoot and never turns to mud. Wind is the only river. It has carved yardangs—whale-backed ridges aligned to the prevailing breeze—and polished every boulder into an egg. The erosion here is old; the land has settled into its own shape. Nothing is raw or jagged. The cliffs are terraced, the slopes are stable, the dust is fine enough to pass through cloth.

The light is ordinary daylight, but the air is full of dust, so the sky is a pale brass at noon and a strange green at the horizon. Shadows are sharp but softened at their edges. The heat is not a blaze, only a steady warmth that rises from the ground and the thick air, and by mid-afternoon the salt plain shimmers. Sound is a distant thing in this heavy air. The wind is a low continuous pressure more than a noise; a hammer blow a kilometer away might arrive as a soft click. The loudest sounds are the creak of rail lines and the hum of power wires.

Into this plain a city has been built. It is called the Works, and it is a city in the way a railway is a city—linear, sparse, industrial. Along a ninety-kilometer front of the Bench, the Works runs in clusters: refinery halls of corrugated steel and sintered salt-brick, gantries, elevated pipelines, electrical substations, housing blocks with small sealed courtyards. Between the clusters are kilometers of graded lots, old spoil heaps, and the foundations of earlier Works that have been dismantled and rebuilt. The buildings are colored by the local minerals: pale tan from halite, red from iron oxides, green from copper stains. Nothing is painted. The streets are not paved but bladed flat; the rails are the real streets. Power lines run on pylons the size of ships, humming faintly. The place is not dense. It has the density of a fan, each node of machinery a finger, each gap a desert of salt. Even in the oldest core, buildings rise only three or four stories, their lower floors half-buried in windblown dust. There are shops and mess halls near the rail stops, but they are far apart, and people move between them on slow flatbed crawlers.

The technology is industrial but quiet. Engines are electric, fed by wind turbines turning lazily on the rim and by geothermal plants that tap the hot mineral fluids. The factories crush and leach and electrolyze the salts, extracting metals and ceramics. The machines are enclosed in sound-baffled sheds; their noise is a low thrum. Cranes move at a walking pace. Conveyor belts run at a crawl. The stacks emit only heat and a colorless solvent vapor that is not water. Near the refineries the air smells of hot metal and chlorine; elsewhere it is almost without smell.

There are no plants. Nothing green, nothing leafy, nothing that flowers. But the place is not lifeless. The primary life is a film—rust-mats and sulfur-mats, chemolithotrophic colonies that grow as branching, lichen-like crusts of black, maroon, and pale gold. They derive energy from oxidizing iron and sulfur in the dust, and they grow with a terrible slowness: a millimeter in a good year. Some form low mounds called slow-fires, which may be thousands of years old, their surfaces convoluted like old brains. They are the meadows of this world.

The fauna is abundant. The dust is full of small animals. Grit-mites, six-legged and shelled in fused silica, feed on the rust-mats. They are so many that when one walks across a dark patch, the ground seems to shiver; a hard step sends a wave of them outward with a sound like fine sand sliding. Above them, wind-fish ride the thermals—flat, translucent creatures shaped like rhomboid kites, their bodies rippling along the edges to steer. They are silent, and their shadows move over the salt like the shadows of leaves that do not exist. There are larger grazers: salt-oxen, low-slung animals with backs of fused salt plates and mouths like ceramic scoops. They scrape the rust-mats with a grinding motion, taking in mineral and microbe together, and excrete small spent-iron pellets that collect in windrows. They move a few meters an hour; a herd of forty might take a week to cross a single pan. From a distance they look like boulders. Only the slow change in their arrangement reveals them as animals. Predators are rarer but present: the lure-talker, long-legged, with a head like a cluster of glass rods, stands motionless over a rust-mat and clicks its rods to imitate the sound of grit-mites. When a curious grazer comes close, the lure-talker strikes—still slowly, but with a suddenness that seems impossible from such a patient animal. At dusk, clouds of copper-gnats rise from the spoil heaps, their wings of mica catching the low light like sparks. They do not buzz; their wings click with a faint chime.

The strangeness of this place is that none of these animals contain water. Their bodies run on ionic liquids and sulfur-based solvents; their tissues are fibrous and mineralized, their blood a translucent oil that does not evaporate. They do not pant or sweat. Their eyes, when they have them, are lenses of salt crystal or silica. The whole ecology is inverted: instead of water and sunlight and leaves, there is brine and wind and rust. It is a living desert that has never heard of water.

The city follows the same slow rhythm. Nothing here changes quickly. The wind shifts a little between day and night; the mineral fluids rise faster at dawn; the rust-mats grow a little, die a little. Dust storms, when they come, build over days, not hours, and may last a week. The Works simply waits inside its thick walls, the rail lines buried, the turbines feathered. Time is measured in decades and in the slow advance of the rust-mats. A new factory wing may take a generation to build. The rules here are not quite the rules of earth. Water is a mineral, not a solvent. Life runs on oxidation and reduction, not photosynthesis. The wind is the only weather. And everything—the land, the animals, the machines—moves at the pace of the slow-fires.
