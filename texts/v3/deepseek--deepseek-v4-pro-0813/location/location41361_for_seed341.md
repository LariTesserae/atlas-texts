The town sits in a low depression, below the surrounding country, as if a bucket had been pressed into the earth and never pulled out. It is small enough that a healthy shout could cross it, though no one would hear the shout over the pumps. The air is hot, the temperature of a drawn bath, and so wet that the difference between breathing and drinking becomes uncertain. From the rim, the whole place looks like a bowl of steam with a few industrial shapes rising through it, colored the brown-orange of old iron and dirty sodium light.

The first thing is the water. It does not so much fall here as stand in the air. It lies in black pools, slides across mineral crust, beads on every pipe and leaf. It rises from the ground warm, not boiling, except where it finds a vent and coughs up a cloud. It runs in channels with established banks of pale, layered stone—sinter, the locals call it, though they have other names for the different colors. The ground is low and flat, but not young. Erosion and deposition have reached a truce. The water knows its channels. The mineral crust builds almost as fast as the flow scours it, so the terraces do not slump, the canals do not widen, the little dams hold. The landscape has been in its own state long enough to look inevitable rather than made.

Vegetation is everywhere but strange. It is lush without being crowded. Huge, glossy leaves—elephant ears, some larger than a door—grow in scattered stands, each plant given space by the heat and steam. There is no continuous green mat. Instead there are rooms of one or two great plants, then open plank boardwalk, then a mineral pool, then a cluster of pale vines looped over a pipe rack. The plants are fed by the warm water and the carbon-rich exhaust of the machinery, and they grow visibly. Ferns uncoil overnight. Mosses upholster the damp sides of the tanks. Algae mats in the cooling channels are the bright green of polished copper. But nothing eats the leaves. No chew marks, no droppings, no insect galls. That is the first impossible thing: a hot, wet, green basin with no midges over the water, no frogs in the reeds, no birds crossing the steam. The absence of animal life is exact. The longer you look, the more it seems like a prohibition rather than a scarcity.

The town itself is urban in its bones, though not in its spread. It is organized like a settlement: there are public steps, switchboard boxes, painted labels on cisterns, a single narrow tram line that runs along the boardwalk to the loading spur, a covered market stall that sells salt, lamp oil, rubber boots, and replacement valve seals. But the structures are few, and between them there is a great deal of steam, water, and shadow. That is the sparse density—more infrastructure than crowd, more space than building. Six or seven major buildings do the town’s work. A pump house of riveted iron. A boiler shed with a stack that disappears into mist. A condenser gallery, long and glass-roofed, where steam is coaxed back into liquid. A transformer yard that hums under its own private weather. Dwellings and workshops are bolted to the sides of these buildings or set on stilts above hot pools. Light leaks from their round windows. The streets are slatted catwalks over moving water. The blocks are compartments in a machine.

The materials are all in the middle of a slow argument with the climate. Iron rusts, but not ruinously—it is scraped and painted and allowed to rust again. Copper has gone green. Concrete is stained black with algae. Wood is so wet it never rots exactly; it softens, then hardens with mineral salts, then becomes something halfway between timber and stone. Nothing is new. Nothing has collapsed. The place is weathered the way a working animal is weathered: its scars are maintained, not mourned.

Light is dim, and it is dim even at noon. The steam clings to the basin like a lid. The sun rarely reaches the boardwalk as sunlight; it arrives diffused through vapor, brown at the edges, amber over the open water. What light exists comes from below and within: the red slit of a furnace door, the greenish glow through a row of condenser-glass panels, the orange halos of electric lamps wrapped in mist. Shadows are soft and multiple. A person on the boardwalk may cast three shadows at once, each a different color, none of them dark. Night is not much darker than day; day is not much brighter than night. The place has learned to operate in a permanent twilight, and the lamps stay on through both.

The sound is not an addition to the place. It is the place’s atmosphere, as constant as the wetness. Under everything is the deep rotary thud of the main pumps, felt through the soles of your boots even before you name it as sound. Over that, the hiss of pressure-release stacks, tearing the air open in long white exhalations. At street level, water: a bubbling, gurgling, dripping, sluicing, sucking, spattering chorus. There is the clank of an expansion joint, the groan of hot metal settling, the repeated piston-stroke of something in the pump house that never quite falls into sync with the rest. Somewhere a steam whistle is wound down but not quite off, so it contributes a thin continuous edge, almost a note. No birdsong. No frogs. No crickets. The absence of animal sound is not silence; it is a gap in the spectrum that the machines have filled. Even in the empty yards between buildings, the air is loud. The emptiness has a hum.

The place is active, not as a metaphor but as a process. Water rises from the ground, is gathered in weirs, is pumped through settling pans, is sprayed over condenser coils, drips back into the levees, returns through the earth, mineralized again. Steam forms and collapses into rain several times a day; the rain is warm and tastes faintly of iron. The plants transpire, adding their own water to the air. The mineral crust whitens a pipe overnight. Rust blooms, is painted over, blooms again. A leaf half-open in the morning may be fully open by evening. The racks expand and contract. The sluice gates are always being adjusted, not because they are broken but because the water is always changing its mind. The whole basin is a continuous event, a weather system tethered to its own machinery.

And there is the weirdness, which is less a single shock than a slow wrongness that accumulates.

The most obvious part is the absence of animals. A wet hot lowland should be raucous with them, but there are none. No fish in the warm pools, no larvae in the standing water, no moths battering the lamp globes, no rats in the grain store. Dogs, they say, will not pass the rim of the mineral terraces. Moths veer off. People who keep canaries find them dead by morning, not of heat or poison but as if some frequency had simply canceled them. The town does not keep animals. The local rule is simple: do not bring a live animal inside the ring.

Then there are the shapes in the sinter. The mineral water deposits around pipes, roots, old boards, and forgotten tools, and some of those deposits look exactly like animals—a heron, a hound, a sprawl of frogs. They are not alive. But steam curls from their mouths when the vents beneath them open, and in the dim brown light it is possible to watch them for a long time before being certain.

Some of the plants have learned to move with the machinery. A cluster of hollow reeds near the pressure stack sways not in the wind—there is no wind—but in the puff of each valve, their joints hissing in sequence like a slow conversation. A broad fern near the condenser drip curls its fronds every twenty minutes, not with light, but with the rhythm of the pump stroke. It is hard to say whether the plants are responding to the machines, or the machines were built to accommodate the plants, or the two have been improvising together for so long that the difference no longer matters.

The rules of the place, as far as anyone will state them, are these: keep the water moving, keep the condensers cold, never let a pump run dry, and do not bring an animal inside the ring. The machinery is not there to tame the water; it is there to negotiate with it. The water is the town’s product and its enemy. If the pumps slow, the hot pools rise into the lower galleries. If they stop, the mineral terraces begin to grow over the boardwalks, and in a season the streets would be rimed in pale stone. The plants would survive. They would probably do better. But the town keeps the pumps running, and the water keeps the town half-drowned, and the plants keep the edges from falling apart, and all of it goes on in the loud, hot, green half-light, no animal breathing anywhere, the only hearts in the place made of iron.
