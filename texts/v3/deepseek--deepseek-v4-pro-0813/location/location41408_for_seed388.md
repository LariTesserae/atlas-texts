The place is not visible until you are almost inside it. The land around it is flat and green, full of hidden water; then the ground drops gently into a bowl, and the bowl is filled with a town.

It is a small town, but not small in density. You could walk the rim in ten minutes. The bowl itself is perhaps three or four hundred paces across, its sides terraced with stone and brick. But within that edge the town is stacked a dozen levels deep, and some of its rooms go under the black water at the bottom. It has the grammar of a city—a gate, a pump station, a workshop street, a stair-street, a sluice gate, a boiler hall—but all of it is folded into the hollow.

Water is everywhere. Not just rain, though rain comes often, warm and fine; the place itself seems to exude water. The walls are wet all year. The lower terraces are drowned. Water runs down the stone in sheets and threads, drips from every leaf and pipe and railing, pools on every landing, and then moves on, always downward, through drains and sluices and slow cascades to the bottom. The ground is not ground; it is a mat of wet roots and mud and submerged paving. At the bottom, the town is partly underwater, and the water there is not clear but green-black, with slow bubbles and an occasional blue electric glow.

It is warm. The air is heavy, saturated, close. It smells of wet brick, live moss, hot iron, old oil, black mud, and a sharp mineral note, like copper or iodine. The warmth comes from the air and from the machines and from the ferment of plant matter. The whole town is a hothouse with engines in it.

The vegetation is lush to the point of blurring every line. Moss covers stone and iron. Ferns grow from walls and gutters. Aerial roots as thick as wrists hang from the upper levels and root in the water below. Broad leaves, some as large as doors, lean over the walkways and shed drips. Reeds and sedges fill the slack pools. Vines wrap the pipes until the pipes look like living stems. This is not a town with gardens; it is a town being slowly drawn into a garden. Some walkways are woven from live wood; some walls are held by roots as much as by mortar. The leaves make the air green even before the light fails.

And the town is dense, layered, saturated. Buildings stand shoulder to shoulder, stacked, brick and iron and timber and tile. New roofs have been built over old roofs. Pipes run in bundles along walls and across gaps. Cables sag between insulators. Walkways cross at three or four levels. Stairs descend into fog. There are small signs, stenciled on tin and cracked enamel. There are windows, most of them dark or lit from within by a dim filament left burning for no one. The construction is not raw; it has been worn smooth. Corners are round. Steps are polished in the middle. Rust is not bright orange but a dark brown skin. The place has had time to settle into itself, like a stone in mud.

It is a working place, though no worker is visible. There are engines. A flywheel in the lower chamber turns once a minute, its rim furred with moss. Pumps stroke slowly in the drowned levels, moving water upward and sideways and downward again. Boilers make steam that leaks from joints and adds to the mist. There are looms or presses in the workshop street, their frames green with age, still ticking. Electric light is made somewhere and carried along the walls; it glows dimly through the wet air. The industry is not the bright, fast industry of a factory district. It is the slow industry of a machine that has been running long enough to become part of the weather.

Light is dim. The sun is not absent, but it arrives through steam and leaf. At noon the town is a green-gray aquarium. The real light is small: caged lamps, red indicator lights, blue-green glow from the lower water, a boiler’s orange mouth, a string of weak sparks along a far line. There is no single sun; there are many tiny ones, all soft. The upper levels fade into mist; the lower levels fade into water; the middle is a band of dusky green.

Sound is loud, and it never stops. The pumps thump. Steam hisses. Water drips everywhere, a thousand separate notes. The flywheel grinds softly, like a boulder turning in a wet well. Valves clank. Pipes tick and shudder. The walkways creak as the town breathes. There is a slow hammering somewhere below, as regular as a pulse. The sound is not random; it is layered and rhythmic, a long mechanical chord. But there are no animal sounds. No bird, no insect, no frog, no fish jumping, no call. The water and the warm air should be full of animal noise, but they are not. That absence is the strangest thing, and it makes every machine sound too deliberate, as if the town is speaking to itself.

There are no animals at all. The water is empty of fish and larvae. The leaves are empty of insects. The air is empty of birds. The buildings are empty of people. The only living things are plants and the microbial mats that stain the water, if those count as alive in the usual way. The only moving things are water, steam, leaf, and iron. This is not a dead place; it is almost too alive, but its life has bypassed animals entirely.

Because of that, the plants and the machines have begun to blur into each other. Roots grow into pipes and are not cut. A vine has wrapped a valve wheel, and when the vine’s sap pressure changes, the valve turns a little. A fern has grown through a gauge, and the gauge reads in green. The moss on the flywheel is not scraped away; it is trimmed by a wiper so the wheel can still turn. The plants seem to respond to the machines as if the machines were weather: ferns close when a pump changes pitch, and the broad leaves lean toward the warm steam pipes. The machines, in turn, are colonized by leaves but continue to run, their rhythm set partly by roots and water weight. They are not fighting. They have reached an accommodation.

Time here is slow. The flywheel turns once a minute. The pumps make one stroke every few seconds. The water level rises or falls so slowly that its mark is recorded as a line of mineral salts on the stairs. The plants grow a little each year. The buildings settle a little each decade. The lower levels have already become underwater rooms, green and drowned. The middle levels are becoming leaves. The upper levels are becoming steam. The town is not falling into ruin; it is descending through its own weight, and being grown over, and being washed through, all at a speed too slow to see. Change is measured in the thickening of rust, the leaning of a wall, the widening of a crack in a roof.

The rules of the place are legible if you stay long enough. Water must keep moving, even slowly; standing water becomes too thick and too warm. Nothing is torn down; new boards and new pipes are laid over old, and the old are left to grow moss. The plants are not cleared, only trained. The machines are not stopped. The boundary is kept; outside the rim there is ordinary rain and ordinary air, but inside, the bowl holds its own climate. No animals come, and if any did, the place would not know them.

At the center of the bowl, in a room the size of a cathedral nave—not enormous, but tall enough to disappear into its own haze—is the main engine. Its flywheel is twelve or fourteen feet across, iron with spokes, green with moss on the upper rim. A root has found its way through the wall and passes between two spokes. The wheel turns once a minute. Each time the root is carried down and then up again, a little water is squeezed from it. The root has worn a groove in the stone. The flywheel has a polished stripe where a wiper cleans it. This is the heart of the town, or the lungs, or the pump of a single slow circulatory system. From this chamber, water rises through pipes and roots to the upper terraces, then drips and runs and seeps back down. The whole town is arranged around this one motion.

The place is small enough to be taken in at a glance, but dense enough that you could wander its stair-streets and never learn all its rooms. It is wet, warm, dim, loud, green, industrial, waterlogged, and entirely without animals. It is not a ruin, not a wilderness, not a city in the ordinary sense. It is a sump that became a town; a town that became a machine; a machine that became a garden. It does not change quickly, but it has been changing for a long time. It is still working. It is still wet. It is still breathing, in its own slow iron-and-root way.
