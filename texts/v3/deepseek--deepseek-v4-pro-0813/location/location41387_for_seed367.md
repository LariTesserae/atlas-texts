The plateau outside is old beyond easy measure. It is high country—four thousand meters and more—but the land has been worn down by wind and time until it is almost level, a peneplain of dark volcanic stone. There is no surface water anywhere. The air is warm and very dry; it stings the nose and leaves a fine mineral grit on the teeth. The rock is not jagged. Every edge has been rounded, fluted, polished by ages of windborne dust and the slow expansion and contraction of hot days and cold nights. The ground is covered in a thin, stony soil, and what erosion has happened here has not been by water but by wind, gravity, and the patient work of heat.

Seen from a distance, the plateau looks empty. The sky is dim with a permanent haze of fine dust, and the horizon is the color of tarnished bronze. There are no rivers, no springs, no salt-flats glinting. A traveler could cross a hundred leagues and find only rock and wind. But near the center of this high plain, there is a hidden feature—so low-lipped and so worn that it vanishes into the haze until you are almost upon it. It is a volcanic collapse, a caldera or a pit crater, no more than a few hundred paces across. The rim has been ground down nearly to the level of the surrounding land. It is a pocket, a single contained place, hidden from all but those who walk directly to its edge.

Stand at the lip, and the world changes.

The first thing that rises out of the bowl is sound: a dense, woven noise of animal calls, insect chittering, voices, hammering, and the creak of rope and wood. The second is smell—warm vegetation, dry rot, animal musk, hot stone, and something sweet like fermenting pollen. The third is the green.

Inside the crater, the light is dim even at noon. The sun has to pass through the plateau’s dust haze, then over the rim, then through a layered canopy of leaves. What reaches the floor is greenish and diffuse, like light under water. The air is warm and close and full of movement. The bowl is not empty; it is packed.

The walls of the caldera are terraced, but not only by geology. Ledge after ledge steps down the inner slope, some natural and widened, others built out from the rock with dark volcanic blocks and clay. The stone is basalt, gas-bubbled and umber-black, but it has been handled and weathered for so long that its surfaces are polished and its edges soft. The terraces bear a town, or a city in vertical form. Dwellings are built against one another, cut back into the rock, cantilevered out on beams of dense fibrous wood. There are roofs of woven mat and stretched hide, windows small and shuttered, doorways low and rounded. Ladders and stairways of notched wood connect one level to the next; ropes hang down the walls, some with baskets tied to their ends. The settlement is continuous, a fabric of rooms and passages and shared landings, layered four or five deep against the crater wall. It is not a scattering of houses. It is a hive, a vertical market, a town compressed into a pocket.

The vegetation is lush, but it is not a rainforest. There is no standing water anywhere. The floor of the crater is dry, covered in a deep litter of old leaves and mineral dust that puffs up underfoot. The lushness comes from a different strategy. The plants here are water-catchers. Their leaves are thick, waxy, cup-shaped, or fringed with fine hairs; they catch the fog that rolls over the rim every evening and condense it into beads. The beads run along channels in the bark or down the leaf grooves into swollen stems and roots. The trees are succulents and fibrous oddities: some have trunks like swollen bladders, others have bark that peels in papery scrolls, others hold their leaves in tight rosettes that collect dew. Vines loop between them, hung with small round leaves that feel cool to the touch. Moss-like mats cover the rock where any moisture lingers. There are no ferns or reeds, no plants that need wet soil. The greenery is gray-green, russet, blue-green, and a hundred shades of dusty sage, but it is dense—dense enough that from above the crater floor looks like a single rumpled pelt.

The animals are overwhelming. In one glance you can see a dozen species without searching. Small furred things chase each other along the branches, their bodies long and quick. Birds burst from the leaves in flocks of twenty, fifty, a hundred, their wings clattering. Insects rise in columns from the litter, their wings catching the dim light like sparks. There are larger browsers, broad-backed and slow, tearing at the succulent leaves with flat grinding mouths. Predators follow them: lean, long-limbed, pale-eyed, moving through the undergrowth as ripples in the leaves. The people keep their own animals on the terraces—small woolly grazers, fat flightless birds, cages of insects for food or fiber. Every ledge has nests—mud cups, woven pouches, holes in the rock stuffed with shed fur. The air is never still. There is always a wing, a tail, an antenna moving.

The sound is not a background hum; it is a pressure. The piping and trilling of the small animals rises and falls in waves. The hooting of the larger ones booms from the deeper green. Insects make a constant high drone under the other calls. The people shout to one another across the terraces, their voices echoing off the stone. Hammers strike wood, baskets scrape against rock, and the wind over the rim makes a low continuous note. At dawn and dusk the volume peaks as the day animals and the night animals overlap, and for an hour the crater is a wall of noise. At night it does not stop; it reorganizes into the high-pitched calls of nocturnal insects, the hoot of hunting birds, and the occasional scream of a predator.

The settlement is built from the same materials the crater provides. The walls are of fieldstone set with mud, sometimes without mortar, the stones fitted so tightly by long practice that a knife blade cannot slip between them. The mud plaster is mixed with crushed pumice and dried leaves, giving it a speckled, organic look. Wood is used sparingly, because the trees are more valuable as fog-catchers than as timber; the beams that exist are old, dense, and polished by generations of hands. Roofs are made of hide furred side out, and of woven mats that furl and unfurl with the sun. There is no glass. Windows are small and shuttered. The people use fire for heat and light, but no engines. Their machines are ropes, levers, and wooden bearings. They weave baskets so tightly they hold water; they fire clay pots and cover them with wooden lids; they knap obsidian into blades and use bone needles. Metal is rare, traded from beyond the plateau, and is not used for everyday work.

Light inside is dim. The sun is a pale disk behind the haze and the canopy, and the shadows are deep. The lower rooms of the terraces are lit by small lamps of animal fat or oil pressed from seeds, their flames steady in the still air. The temperature is warm, warmer than the plateau above. The old caldera still breathes heat from deep below—not enough to boil water, but enough to keep the stone warm to the touch. Warm air rises through cracks in the rock, lifts the moisture of leaves and breath, and drives the fog upward; the cool rim condenses it and sends it back down as a pale wave. This heat is the engine of the place, quiet and ancient.

The whole crater is in motion. Every evening the fog comes over the rim, rolling down the terraces like a slow wave, and the plants lift their leaves to meet it. By morning every surface is beaded. The people go out with covered pots to collect the dew from the stone gutters and the leaves of the planted fog-trees. They carry it to buried cisterns, sealed with clay and wood. The animals are released from their pens and move down through the terraces to forage; birds follow them, snapping up the insects they flush. By midday the fog has been drunk or burned off, and the crater settles into a hot, busy stillness. Then the evening fog comes again, and the cycle repeats.

The rules of the crater are simple and absolute. Nothing that can hold water is left open, because the sun will steal it. No tree that catches fog may be cut without planting another. Every animal, wild or tame, is part of the water cycle; to kill wastefully is to spill water. The people have no word for rain, but they have many words for the fog—morning-fog, evening-fog, dry-fog, hungry-fog—and they greet it as a living thing. The crater is not a paradise. It is a tight, loud, crowded arrangement of rock, leaf, and breath that has been holding itself together for a very long time.

The place is ancient not as ruins are ancient, but as a mountain is ancient. It has settled into its final form. The steps are worn into troughs by generations of feet. The thresholds are polished to a shine. The walls have been re-plastered so many times they are smooth as river stone. The crater has no sharp edges, no new rock, no raw construction. It is finished, or nearly so, and it lives on because everything inside it has found its place. There is nothing supernatural here. There is only a very old, very tight arrangement of sun, stone, leaf, and breath, hidden in a pocket of a high dry world.
