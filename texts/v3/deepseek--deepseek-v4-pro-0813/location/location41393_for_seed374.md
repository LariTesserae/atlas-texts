The name of the place is lost in the noise.

It is not a place where sound is an event. It is a place where sound is the element, like water or stone. The sound has the quality of a roar, but a roar made of a thousand interlocked pieces: the hiss of warm water moving through soft mineral, the scrape and chime of insect legs, the bubbling of gas from the earth, the high wire of frogs, the clatter of wings, and the rhythmic knock of stone on stone from the settlement. The noise does not stop. It does not fade at night. It sits over the basin like a lid.

The basin is low, nearly flat, a broad hollow between raw hills. The hills are not old; they have no plant cover, no soil, no smoothness. They are made of pale travertine, sinter, tufa, and some darker volcanic stone that still gives off its warmth. The ground has not been carved by rivers; it has been built up by water from below. Warm mineral springs rise everywhere, not in discrete vents but as a general seepage, so that the whole country is wet. The water is milky white or faintly blue at the source, then clears as it pools, leaving behind a skin of new stone. The terraces, rims, dams, and bowls of this landscape are not eroded forms; they are depositional. They grow.

Every edge is sharp, each pool rim as delicate as a curl of wax. The land is young, still emerging, still being laid down by the springs. But the shape does not change. The deposits come so evenly, and are so quickly cut away by the town-builders or incorporated by the animals, that the basin remains exactly as it has always been. It is an embryonic place in stasis: continually being born, never growing older.

There is no vegetation. None.

The eye searches for green and finds only the false green of mineral stain, the sulfur-yellow of bacterial mats, the orange of iron, the black of manganese. The living film is not plant but something simpler and older: heat-loving bacteria, archaea, and other film-like forms that feed on dissolved minerals and the warmth of the earth. They form mats in the channels and on the wet walls, giving the stone a slick, colored gloss. This is the base of the food web.

The water is thick with tiny shrimp, pale and translucent, that graze on the mats. Snails in layered beds cover the pool edges, their shells packed like cobblestones. Small fish, blind and pinkish-white, move in slow clouds through the warm pools. Every pool, every runnel, every damp surface is dense with animals. The air is full of insects—midges, flies, small translucent things—that never seem to sleep. They rise in columns from the water and are snapped up by birds.

The birds are the loudest part of the din. They are small, pale gray, with short wings and gaping mouths, something between a swift and a swallow, though they rarely leave the basin because the air outside is too cool and too bright. They nest in horizontal bands along the undersides of the travertine rims, hundreds to a ledge. When a flock rises, it is like a splash of ash against white steam; their wingbeats make a sound like heavy rain. They communicate in shrieks that cut through the noise. Their dung is everywhere, white and thick, and it becomes part of the stone. The smell of guano is strong, mixing with sulfur and warm lime. It is not exactly unpleasant, but it is dense, and the warm air holds it close.

Frogs call from every crack. They are the size of a thumb-joint, skin the color of wet clay, and their voices are absurdly loud, a continuous high piping that overlaps into a drone. The pools are so full of fish and tadpoles and small blind eels that one cannot step into them without stirring a dozen bodies. The ground between pools is covered with white crickets and other hopping, crawling things. They are so numerous that the stone itself seems to move when looked at closely. Higher up, in the darker niches and small caves, bats hang in tight rows, their fur wet with condensed steam. They are quiet by day, but at dusk they pour out in a dense, warm stream, adding their clicks and chittering to the noise.

The settlement is built into this mineral basin like a colony of animals. It is a town, or a city by the standards of this world, though its technology is simple: stone, bone, shell, hide, and a little copper. There is almost no wood, because there are no plants, and what wood exists is carried from far away at great cost and used only for small, precious things. The buildings are made of cut tufa blocks, still soft enough to shape with a chisel when freshly quarried, then hardened by air. They are stacked on the terrace rims, carved into the sinter walls, and sometimes hung from the undersides of overhanging ledges. Walls are thick, roofs are stone slabs, doorways are low and narrow. The houses climb over one another, four or five levels deep, connected by notched stone stairs, bone ladders, and narrow walkways that wind between steaming pools. From a distance, the settlement is indistinguishable from the mineral terraces. It is a dense encrustation of rooms and walls, dissolving into steam.

The inhabitants are people, or something close enough to people, and they have adapted to the noise. They do not speak much at a distance. They use signs with their hands, a quick grammar of fingers and eyebrows. Up close, they speak in low voices, but the words are always half-swallowed by the sound. Their houses are noisier than any silence; the floors vibrate with the endless water, the walls echo with frogs. They sleep in rooms where the sound is not lesser but different, a hum rather than a roar. They do not seem to mind it. They are born into it. Silence would feel to them like a kind of death.

The light is dim, though it is not night. The sun is hidden behind a perpetual ceiling of steam and low cloud. There are few shadows. The world appears in flat whites, grays, dull oranges, and the wet gleam of polished stone. At night, some of the pools give off a faint blue-green glow from the bacteria in them, and the town moves by that and by small clay lamps burning animal fat. The lamps are simple, just a hollow stone with a wick of twisted sinew, but they fill the streets with a warm, smoky light that does not reach far. The dark is not empty; it is full of movement and sound.

The rules of the place are simple. Water leaves stone; stone must be scraped away or used. Nothing is wasted. The deposits form overnight on any surface left still, a thin crust like frost made of calcite. The inhabitants scrape their doorsteps, their tools, their water channels, and the bones of their dead. They harvest fresh tufa for building, cut it into blocks, and let it harden in place. The animals do their part: the snails and shelled things take up minerals; the birds and bats add their guano; the bacteria bind the sediment. It is a closed loop. The town does not grow or shrink. The pools do not overflow or drain. The springs do not cool or change.

There are no seasons. The temperature is warm, always warm, day and night, and the humidity is so high that breath and air are barely different. The only events are small: a wall is repaired, a channel is scraped, a child is born, a bird falls into a pool and is eaten by fish. These events happen constantly, but they do not alter the whole. The same sound has been roaring since before memory. The same terraces have held the same pools. The same pale birds have nested on the same ledges. It is a landscape of equilibrium, a place where the earth is still being formed but has decided, for now, to keep its shape.

If one stands at the edge of the basin, looking down through the steam, the impression is of a vast, open field of stone that is also a city, moving with life but not changing, made of water that builds instead of erodes. It is deafening and dim and warm and wet. It is both young and timeless. After a while, the noise ceases to be heard as separate sounds and becomes instead a kind of texture, like the grain of the stone. The place seems less like a landscape and more like a body: a low, wet, densely inhabited body, breathing out warm mineral breath, continuously making itself from water and heat, and never getting any older.
