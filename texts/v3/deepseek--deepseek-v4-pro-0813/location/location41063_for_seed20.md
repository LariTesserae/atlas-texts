It is called, by the few who mark its rim, the Cistern, or the Singing Pan, though the sound does not sing so much as hammer.

The place is small—a broken bowl of black stone high above a cloud sea. The rim is the only standing ground: a collar of freshly fractured basalt, sharp as new flint, no wider than a road in places, falling away on one side into cloud and on the other into warm water. The altitude says high mountain; the air is thin, but it is not cold. The heat comes up through the stone and the water. The water steams. The sun, when it can be found, is a pale smear behind a permanent plume of mineral haze. Even at noon the light is like late dusk.

The true landscape is under the water. The basin is a drowned throat, a vent field of hot springs and black smokers. The water is deep enough to swallow the central vents, but it is not black; it is a thick green-brown, clouded with silica and sulfur. Visibility is poor. You see only a hand’s breadth into it. But there are moments when the water clears for a heartbeat and you see the floor: a cluster of white chimneys, some thin as an arm, some stacked towers of grey sinter, growing from the raw rock like organ pipes. They exhale black water, then clear water, then steam. The rock is new, unweathered, all clean fracture planes and knife edges. There is no soil, no rounded stone. The only softening comes from mineral crust.

The sound is not a background. It is the main occupant of the place. It is a continuous low organ note from the water, overlaid with a high shriek of steam and a rhythmic concussive thud, like a door being slammed far down. It is so loud that you feel it in your skull and chest; your teeth buzz. It changes pitch when a vent opens or a rock falls. It can be so loud that you cannot hear your own voice. The steam above the lake bends into shapes—rings, funnels, standing pillars—that hold for half a second and then collapse. The sound seems to shape the water. The surface is never flat; it is a low dome in the center, then rings of boiling rosettes, then waves that run against the wind.

The place is not dead. But life is rare and sparse. On the rim, in cracks, a pale grey-green lichen grows in small brittle stars. It does not spread far. Underwater, near the hot vents, there are mats of rust-orange and sulfur-yellow slime that pulse very slowly. No leaves, no roots, no shade. The fauna is so rare that most hours pass without a sign. Small blind crustaceans, translucent as glass, dart between vent throats. Occasionally a long pale eel with no eyes rides the upwelling, turns like a strip of cloth, and is gone. Birds do not come here; the noise and the steam keep them away.

There are traces of someone before. On the eastern rim, a line of nine cairns—each no taller than a knee—runs toward a collapsed shelter of flat basalt slabs. The shelter’s doorway faces the water. Inside, there is a flat stone with shallow grooves: not writing, but repeated chevrons and circles, maybe counting marks. Near the waterline, steps descend into the green—six, maybe seven, then they blur under mineral crust. They are deliberate, cut into the rock but not shaped by water. Under the crust, they look recent, as if the stone has not had time to wear. There is no metal, no glass, no cord, no wood; only stacked stone, cut marks, and the remains of a path.

The water does not obey the usual rules. The lake surface is mounded, as if the water is drawn upward from below. In places, droplets run uphill along the rim, slow, then climb a vertical stone. The reflection in the water is wrong: it shows a darker sky with the wrong clouds, or sometimes no sky at all—just a slow spiral of green light. Distance is not reliable. The far rim may seem thirty paces away, then two hundred. The water level rises and falls without cause; the lake may fill to the rim, then show its stone teeth, then fill again. The vents build and collapse. A new chimney grows from the floor, white and fragile, rises to the height of a person, then shudders and falls. A jet of steam screams from the western rim, then closes. A slab of basalt cracks off and slides into the water; the whole basin changes its note.

If the place has rules, they are these: do not trust the water, because it sometimes moves against the slope. Do not trust distance, because the basin is still arguing with itself. The sound is not a byproduct; it is a force, part of the terrain. And nothing here is finished. The stone is new, the water is new, the shape of the basin is still being decided between heat and gravity. The place is a small room, but it contains an ocean’s violence in a cup. It is a high drowned throat, a warm, dim, deafening engine of creation and erasure, with just enough life to notice and just enough ruin to show that someone else once stood at the rim and listened.
