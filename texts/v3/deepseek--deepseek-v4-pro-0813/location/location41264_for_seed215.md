The first thing you notice is that the ground has not yet learned to be old. It is a high, cold tableland, so wide that the weather seems to begin somewhere in the middle of it, and the horizon is not a line but a gradual thickening of fog and gray air. The air is thin, high-altitude, and cold without being violent. The stone is raw. Cliffs and scarps look cut, not worn; their edges are sharp, their surfaces glassy or rough with fresh fractures. There are no rounded boulders, no deep soil, almost no sand. The rock is dark gray and green-black, sometimes veined with quartz or stained with rust. It is a landscape at the very beginning of its relationship with water.

And water is everywhere. Not as rivers—there are almost no permanent rivers yet—but as seepage, condensation, meltwater, and shallow standing water. Every rock face weeps. The air is so often near the dew point that moisture gathers on the stone, on your skin, on the metallic fins of the city. The ground is wet without being saturated in the way of a lowland marsh; it is wet the way a stone is wet after rain, except the rain here never fully leaves. In the low basins between fresh ridges, water sits in broad silver pans. Some basins have turned into thin mires, with dark moss and sparse sedge, but even these are more water than vegetation. The smell is of wet stone and cold air, with a faint iron tang where the rock is rusting and a sour, green edge near the mires.

The cold hovers near freezing. At night, the wet rock glazes with ice; by mid-morning, the ice turns to trickling water. The daily rhythm is freeze, thaw, seep, fog. The landscape breathes water instead of storing it.

The scale is hard to hold in your head. The plateau goes on for so long that it makes its own weather. Clouds form inside the high country and cannot get out; they drift for days, low enough to touch the ground. Fog rises from the seepages and moves in slow, dense herds. Rain comes, but more often it is a fine condensing drizzle that does not fall so much as settle. The light is ordinary, not alien—a pale high-altitude light, often diffused by cloud, giving the whole place a matte silver quality. When the sun appears, it glints off a million wet surfaces.

The city is scattered across this raw land like something sown rather than settled. It is urban in its organization but sparse in its density. There are no continuous streets, no blocks of buildings, no center. Instead, towers rise from the rock in clusters of three or five, or alone at the edge of a basin, or along the long diagonal of a transit line. Each tower is a vertical settlement in itself: stack after stack of habitats, water tanks, machine levels, aeroponic galleries. They are built from a black ceramic-like material that sometimes turns translucent at the edges, grown rather than assembled. The towers repair their own cracks, and they flex slightly in the wind, so that from a distance they seem to be swaying like reeds.

Between the towers, the land is empty for kilometers. The emptiness is not absence; it is part of the city's structure. The ground is left to stone, water, animals, and fog. Infrastructure runs through this emptiness in thin, almost invisible lines: elevated tubes for transit, low light-lines marking routes, mesh collectors strung across the wind. There is little road. Most movement happens above the ground, in capsules that pass with a soft sigh and a pulse you feel in the soles of your feet.

The technology is beyond modern physics, but here it is ordinary engineering. The towers are grown by machines that assemble matter at the molecular scale, pulling carbon and silicon from the raw stone. Power comes from the temperature gradient between the cold air and the warmer rock below, or from the wind, or from the cycling of thaw and freeze. The most visible technology is the water system. Every tower is wrapped in condenser fins and fog mesh—fractal surfaces designed to coax water directly from the air. The water runs down the tower sides in silver channels, into underground cisterns, then out again as warm breath and thin clouds from the habitat levels. The city does not fight the wet; it drinks it.

The vegetation is sparse, but not absent. There are no trees. In sheltered basins and along the seepages, there are low cushions of moss, dark lichen, and a few wiry sedges. The real living skin is microscopic: a film of cyanobacteria and algae on the wet rock, so thin it only looks like a dark sheen. That living film is the foundation of everything else. It makes the rock itself nutritious, and it thrives in the daily water cycle. In some places, the stone is stained green-black and orange from the life growing in the water films. It is not lush, but the land is not barren.

And the fauna is abundant, far more abundant than the vegetation would suggest. The air over every melt-pool is thick with small flying insects in the warmest part of the day. They rise in columns and sheets, too many to count, moving with the wind. Their sound is a fine, high haze, like distant radio static. Birds follow the insects—small, pale gray birds that nest under the tower ledges and move across the plateau in flocks of hundreds. They wheel and fold, their wings making a sound like many soft hands clapping. Above them, falcon-like predators with pale undersides ride the thermals off the towers and the sun-warmed rock. On the ground, small grazers—something like pikas or ptarmigan—hop among the lichen mats, leaving tiny tracks in the frost. There are also pale, stoat-like hunters that slip through the rock clefts. The animals are abundant because the wet film is abundant. The food web is built on water and stone, not on forests.

The place is active, but its action is often quiet. The days move through a cycle. At night, the seeps slow and the ground freezes. Just before dawn, the land is still and crusted with ice. Then the light comes—ordinary light—and the melt begins. Water trickles from every crack. Fog forms from the warming rock and drifts in layers. By mid-afternoon, the pools are full of mirror-light and the insects are up in their millions. The birds arrive in waves. The grazers come out to feed on the damp mats. In the evening, the fog thickens, the temperature drops, and the water on the towers condenses into silver runnels. The city's collectors work hardest at dusk. Then the freeze returns.

The sounds are mostly small. Wind is the largest voice, but even it is soft here, shaped by the towers into low chords. Water ticks and drips everywhere. The rock makes small sounds as it cools and contracts: a click, a pop, occasionally a deep boom far away as a fresh joint opens or a block settles. The birds make a sound that is half whistle, half rustle. The insects are a nearly inaudible sizzle. The city's machinery is not silent, but it is kept below hearing—a low hum at the edge, the sigh of a transit capsule, the faint click of a water valve. You could hear a pebble fall from a cliff a kilometer away.

The rule of this landscape is that it is not finished. Water does not yet have established paths. Any basin can overflow, any scarp can crack. The city has learned to accept this. Its towers are rooted into the raw rock, and they ride the slow settling of the land; their joints flex, their anchors adjust. The infrastructure is designed for impermanence of drainage. The animals, too, are nomadic by necessity. The seeps migrate, the insect hatches shift, and the birds follow the food. The low density of the city is not an accident; the land between the towers is too young, too wet, too raw to build on everywhere. So the city lives in points and lines, and the empty space is not empty at all—it is the place where water, stone, and life are still deciding what they will become.

At dusk, when the fog comes down and the towers begin to gleam with condensation, the plateau looks less like a landscape than like something being poured. The light flattens. The water films catch the last of the sky. A flock of gray birds crosses the gap between two towers, and for a moment the only sound is their wings and the drip of water from a thousand stone tears. Then the cold comes, and the place freezes quiet, waiting for the next thaw.
