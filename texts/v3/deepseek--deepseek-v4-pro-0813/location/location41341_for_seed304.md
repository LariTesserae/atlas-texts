The Hollow of White Stone

It is a small place, low in a fold of gray hills, no more than a stone’s throw from the spring-head at its back to the cut where the overflow leaves it. The land sits wet and cold, the kind of hollow where mist gathers in the evening and the ground never truly dries. The light is ordinary, cloud-filtered, pale. It makes the stone shine without glittering, as if everything has been gone over with a wet brush.

The first thing you notice is the rock. It is pale gray to milky white, pocked and crusted, with edges so sharp they look newly broken. This is a young landscape, unfinished, still being built. There are no rounded boulders or smooth creek stones. The terraces step down through the hollow in shallow bowls, each one cupped behind a raised lip of stone no thicker than a man’s wrist. The lips look almost foamy, like frozen water, and water does pour over them in thin, cold ribbons. The whole place is a staircase of pools, each spilling into the next.

The water comes from the back wall, a low cliff of dark, glistening rock. Springs weep from dozens of small mouths there, not violently, but steadily. It is not a waterfall; it is a constant seeping, dripping, overflowing presence. Water runs in grooves along the lanes, beads on the walls, collects on the undersides of roofs. The air smells of wet lime and cold stone and damp hide.

The water is hard. It carries dissolved stone. Leave a stick in a fast rill for a week and it comes out thickened with white crust. Leave a basket in a pool for a season and it hardens into a brittle stone jar. The hollow is not being worn away by water; it is being added to, layer by layer, day by day. Paths seal themselves if they are not walked. Doorways shrink. Terraces rise slowly. The first rule of the place is that the water makes, and the people unmake just enough to keep living inside its making.

The settlement is not large, but it is dense. It is a town in behavior, packed into a space the size of a cathedral floor. There is no open ground. Buildings share walls, stack over one another, lean across lanes. Some are made of cut blocks of the same pale stone, mortared with lime and crushed crust. Others are partly grown: walls of woven withy now so thick with mineral deposit that they have become stone. Roofs are hide stretched over old timber or bone, or thin slabs of stone laid on beams. The timber is precious, hoarded and reused, brought from beyond the hills. No iron, no bronze. Tools are stone, fire-hardened wood, leather, bone.

The lanes are narrow, sometimes only a shoulder wide. In many places the lane is also a water channel, and you walk on stepping stones while the spring runs between your feet. Paths are scraped daily in the wet season. The tool marks are visible everywhere, fresh chisel cuts beside pale crust. Bridges of plank or stone span the larger pools. Dwellings overhang the water on posts of dark wood, and at the waterline the posts are swollen with white mineral, like legs in thick socks. The reflections make the village look twice as full: a lower town hanging in each pool.

The water is divided by a web of sluices, grooves, leather dams, and little stone gates. Every spring mouth has an owner. People measure water by the width of a cut, the length of a season, the weight of stone it lays down. Some pools are kept clear for drinking. Some are shallow and fast, used to grow stone tiles and vessels. Some are dark and still, for retting reeds or curing hides. Some exist only to carry the overflow away from the lower rooms. The whole hollow is arranged around water, more like an instrument than a place.

Vegetation is sparse. The mineral water is too rich and too constant for most plants. But there are patches of black moss in the spray of the overflow lips, dense as wet fur. Pale gray lichen flowers on old stone and pits it. In a few places where rainwater seeps down from the hills and dilutes the mineral flow, jointed reeds grow in thin stands. They are harvested for thatch and basketry. There are no trees. A stunted shrub or two hangs from cracks in the upper cliff, its roots etching the rock. Mostly the hollow is stone, water, hide, and the green-black stains of things that do not need much.

Animals are rare. The pools are too mineral, the plants too few. Midges hatch in the algal mats and dance over the water at evening in small, silent columns. A pale water-cricket skates on the surface, leaving tiny dimples. A thin gray bird nests under the eaves and eats the midges; its call is one damp note, repeated. In the deepest pool, below the drinking water, a few pale fish flicker, kept by an old woman who feeds them scraps. That is the whole of the predator-prey rhythm here: midges, cricket, bird. No flocks, no beasts of burden, no dogs. The loudest animal in the hollow is human.

The quiet is strange because it is not empty. It is the quiet of many small processes running at once. Water drips. Stone grows. A mallet taps somewhere behind a wall. A child’s feet cross a wet plank. A sluice board is pulled loose, and a pool begins to drain with a low gurgle. Voices are kept close. The stone and the mist swallow sound.

The place is active, though it rarely seems busy. Every day the springs shift slightly. A rim may crack under its own new weight. A channel may clog with fresh crust overnight. People scrape, re-cut, adjust, carry stone, spread hides, tend the stone-farm pools. The landscape is not fixed. It is a settlement inside a process, a village that has to be maintained as much as lived in.

The rules are visible in every surface. Do not leave anything in the water you do not want kept. Do not trust a new rim with your whole weight. Do not walk a path too rarely. What the water takes, it turns to stone. What the stone builds, the people must cut back. The hollow is young, fresh, unfinished. It is not a ruin. It is a place being built and unbuilt at the same time.

At the bottom, the overflow slips through a narrow cut and disappears into lower ground, a flat wet place too fogged to see far. But the hollow itself is the whole world. Its boundaries are close: the low spring-cliff, the rough stone sides, the drop at the overflow. It is small enough to know every inhabitant, every spring, every tile. It is dense enough that no one is alone. You are never out of earshot of water, never more than a step from a wet wall, never far from the smell of lime. It feels less like a town beside water than a town the water has permitted to exist, for now, on the narrow dry edges of its own making.
