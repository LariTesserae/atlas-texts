Call it the Ledges, or the Cradle, or the Unfinished Range—no name sticks because the place is still too new to hold one. At this elevation, the air is cool and thin, yet so saturated with cloud-mist that it feels heavy. You pass into it through a fog that lies not below the city but inside it, threading every steel rail and stone face. The light is a permanent gray-green twilight: cloud decks above and below, smoke from the works, steam from vent pipes. Actual rain is rare. Water does not fall so much as condense—beading on cables, running down rock in thin films, collecting in basins. The entire place is damp without being flooded.

The terrain is the first impossibility. These mountains have not yet been worn down by time. They are raw, jagged, glassy in places, as if the earth’s crust tore open and cooled only last week. There are no rounded hills, no alluvial fans, no soft foothills. The land rises in interlocking saw-tooth ridges, broken amphitheaters, vertical slabs of dark breccia and pale green-gray schist. Some cliffs still bear the ripples of molten flow or the exact angular fracture planes of rock that has never known a season of frost. Erosion is only beginning. Nothing is polished, nothing softened. Boulders at the base of cliffs look cut rather than tumbled. The city has not carved this geology; it has colonized the unfinished edges before the world could decide what the mountain should become.

Because the elevation is high and the air cool, the lushness is strange. Vegetation thrives in the mist and on the mineral-rich weep of the cliffs. Moss grows thick as sponge, in electric greens and rust-orange. Ferns uncurl from every crack. Dwarf pines and twisted willows grow horizontally from the cliff faces, roots threading into fissures. On broader ledges, dense heather-like shrubs and bright lichen form a low cloud-forest understory. Around every steam vent the greenery is so dense it must be cut back with saws. This is not meadow fertility; it is the lushness of a place always dripping, never baking, where growing time is counted in decades.

The city spreads across this vertical landscape in a sparse, multi-level web. Districts are separated by chasms, connected by high bridges, cable tramways, iron staircases, cut paths. Buildings are stacked into the rock: brick tenements with soot-blackened facades, steel-framed factories cantilevered over the abyss, stone blockhouses half-grown out of the cliff. Long roofed galleries run along the ledges, and narrow alleys switchback upward. Because the rock is young and prone to slippage, everything is reinforced, layered, repaired. The architecture is as much engineering as shelter.

Industry dominates the skyline and the sound. There are ore-crushing mills, gas distilleries, processing plants for the mineral-rich seep-water. Smokestacks rise in clusters; their plumes drift horizontally. Steam pipes cross chasms on high trestles, leaking white jets. Electric cables sag between poles, humming at a low constant pitch. In the dimness, the city glows with yellow sodium lamps and blue arc lights, each haloed in fog. Machines run slowly, rhythmically—piston pumps beat once every few seconds, ventilators turn with a creak, a massive stamping press falls like a distant door shutting in bedrock, then falls again only after a long interval.

The fauna is abundant beyond what the dimness should allow. Cinder crows are everywhere, hundreds of them, nesting in chimneys and along steam pipes, their calls a continuous jagged racket. Cable-mites swarm on the guy-wires in rust-colored mats. Bellows-toads in the cisterns inflate pale throat-sacs and give long, mournful notes. Cliff-urchins—six-legged grazers with stone-colored plates—lick mineral blooms off fresh rock faces. At dusk, veil moths as broad as hands cluster around the arc lamps. Higher, above the smog, wind-eaters drift on thermals: pale gas-bag creatures with trailing tendrils, filtering spores and soot. Their calls come down as low, throaty groans, more felt than heard.

The ecosystem depends on the city’s byproducts as much as on the rock. The animals eat fungus from steam-warmed pipes, insects from mineral seep, grazers from lichen. Some have adapted to the half-lit, half-real nature of the place. Shimmer-locusts are visible only when they land, appearing as a heat-shimmer over the cobbles. Ghost-midges bite before they can be seen. Some crows fly through gaps that do not exist until their wings pass through, reassembling on the far side.

This is where the world’s rules fray. The mountains are still forming, not by tectonic uplift over millions of years but as if the landscape has not committed to its final shape. Some cliffs are blurred at the edges, not from fog but from spatial indecision. A stairway may end at a blank wall of rock in the morning; by evening the wall is gone and the stair continues upward to a ledge that had been only a sketch in the mist. Certain districts are always under construction because the world erases them at the same rate they are built. Workers paint beams that deteriorate backward if left unattended. A foundry pours the same foundation for years, and each week the concrete is newly rough. In the Tin Quarter, Stair Nine has an impossible number of steps depending on how many times one counts; the handrails are warm in places where no pipe runs.

The city’s machines are not just for production. They are for insisting—through heat, electricity, repeated motion, and noise—that this version of the mountains is the real one. That is why there is always space between things: a gap of fog, a fall of cliff, an empty terrace where a single crane stands. The built environment feels scattered yet continuous through cables and pipes and repeated sounds.

Time is slow. The fog moves in long, patient waves. Water drips from the rock on a schedule that seems motionless. Cranes turn by degrees. Fauna migrates not with seasons—there are no clear seasons—but along decades-long cycles of mineral bloom and vent warmth. The city changes the way a glacier changes, by slow accumulation and occasional catastrophic rearrangement. But the sound is loud: a continuous layered drone of wind through the cables, clatter of crow colonies, rhythmic thump of engines, hiss of steam, and at intervals the low boom of the quarry on the far rim, which arrives three times because the valley’s acoustics are not entirely linear.

The dimness is never complete. There is always some faint source: a brazier, a window, a patch of bioluminescent lichen, the reflection of an arc lamp in a wet rock face. The cool air clings. The dampness never dries. The vegetation creeps over everything. The animals are so numerous that every surface seems to crawl or call. And the mountains, despite the city’s best efforts, remain half-finished—raw, sharp, unreal at the edges, growing into a world that cannot quite finish them.
