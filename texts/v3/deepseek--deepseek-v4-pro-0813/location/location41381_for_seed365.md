At first the Gape is only a shadow in the high moor, a place where the wind-stunted heath bends into a crack. The plateau here is high enough that clouds walk the ground twice a day, and the crack seems to breathe them in.

The crack is young. Its lips are sharp as a broken pot; flakes of basalt still balance on the edges, and the walls have not yet been rounded by water or time. It is a small place—two hundred paces long, forty wide—but its walls rise several hundred feet, so it feels like a cathedral cut into the plateau. Noon reaches the bottom as a cool, thinned light, strained through mist and flat canopies. The floor lives in a permanent green dusk.

It is damp, not wet. No river runs through it. Water arrives as fog, condenses on every stone and leaf, and gathers in seeps and pans no larger than a basin. The air is cool enough to show breath, but not cold enough to freeze. Everything glistens. The only steady sound is the drip of water moving from one ledge to the next.

Yet the vegetation is lush. Wherever a root can hold, the green is deep and spongy. Moss mats as thick as a hand cover the ledges; ferns unroll from every seam; dwarf alder and cloud-forest shrubs grow sideways from the walls, their roots gripping like fingers. Some leaves are broad and pale underneath, and their flowers open only at night. But the green is not continuous. There are bare slabs of weeping stone between the thickets, open and empty. The lushness is in pockets and seams, not a uniform forest.

Into these walls a town has been built. It is not a scattering of huts; it is a vertical settlement of fifty or so households, organized like a town. There is a common storehouse carved into a dry overhang, a smoke-rack on a ledge, cisterns at the seeps, a hoist at the rim, a council platform, a birthing cave, and a cracked stone bell. But the town is sparse. Dwellings are separated by blank rock and moss, thirty or forty feet apart, connected by ladders, pegged walkways, and rope bridges. There are no streets, only routes. Neighbors see each other’s lamps through the fog but do not call out.

Their technology is primitive and careful. They have no metal; the only iron is a red stain in the walls, too thin to smelt. Their knives are obsidian from a seam near the rim, their needles bone, their pots coiled clay fired on rare dry days. They make rope from inner bark and cloth from the shed skins of animals. Buildings are stone and timber, lashed rather than nailed, built to flex when the young rock creaks. Fire is kept in covered pots because the damp makes it precious, and lamps of animal fat are hooded with scraped horn.

The fauna is overwhelming. The walls and leaves are covered in living bodies. The most common are called wool-snips or lampskins: soft, thumb-sized creatures with many short legs and translucent bodies holding a faint green light. They cluster in the thousands on the undersides of leaves and on wet stone. They are silent. At dawn and dusk they migrate down the walls in sheets to drink from the seep-pools, then crawl back. In spring they shed their skins, and the people gather the skins to felt into cloth. Pale geckos with padded toes hunt the wool-snips, moving soundlessly. Spine-mice live in the moss. And at night a shaggy, long-fingered thing called the Glove comes out of the high caves to eat geckos. It is also silent. You cannot put your hand on a wall without touching a living body, yet the place remains quiet.

The animals do not call, sing, or snarl. There is no birdsong here, no insect chirr. They communicate by light, touch, and tapping on stone with their feet, too faint to hear unless you press your palm to the rock. This is one of the rules of the Gape: sound does not carry. The moss and the bodies of the wool-snips swallow it. The fog muffles it. The stone itself seems to absorb vibration instead of echoing it. A shout from one ledge to another arrives as a whisper. The people long ago stopped fighting this. They speak in low tones, use carved sign-cards, and teach children to walk on the balls of their feet. The stone bell is struck once at evening, not to be heard but to be felt through the rock as a single low thump.

Other rules follow. Do not build on the floor after dark; the migrations pass there, and the Glove hunts there. Never block a seep-pool; the moss and the wool-snips depend on it, and the cisterns are fed by it. Keep lamps shuttered; wool-snips are drawn to warmth and will smother a flame, then fall burning onto the walkways. If a wool-snip is born with two heads, the elders take it as a sign that the crack is about to shift.

The place is active, though it sounds still. Fog rises and falls. Water drips. The wool-snips pulse their green light in slow unison at certain times, and the people read these pulses as a calendar. The geckos follow the wool-snips; the Glove follows the geckos. The moss grows visibly in the wet season, and the young rock sheds flakes into the void with a sound like a dropped pebble. The people re-weave bridges rotted by damp, scrape moss from doorways, haul water, gather shed skins, salt gecko meat, and watch the rim for rockfalls. It is a small place, a single wound in the high moor, but it is never still.

The strangest thing may be this: the Gape is not silent because it is dead. It is silent because it is too full. Every surface is occupied, every drop used, every body moving softly. The silence is not absence; it is the sound of a place that has learned to live without noise, pressing its ear to the stone.
