From the notch in the rim, the place arrives first as sound. A continuous, ribbed roar fills the gap, then separates into many waters: a main sheet falling from the upper lip, a gurgle through runnels, the hiss of mist against leaves, and thousands of small drips from the overhang. The hollow is small enough that the noise has no clear direction. It seems to come from inside the skull and from the stone at once, like standing inside a great shell held close to the ear.

The hollow is not a valley. It is a raw break in the high plateau, a room the mountain is still in the act of making. Its walls are not rounded; they are sharp and blue-black, shed in clean plates. There is no talus, no ancient riverbed, no weathered dome. The floor is a set of pale stone terraces, each pool rim raised like a lip and descending by inches. The whole place might be a hundred and fifty paces across and two hundred high. The floor is nearer to a flooded staircase, a giant’s washboard, a half-formed organ.

Water arrives at the top from a row of seeps in the rim. The water is cold and faintly milky at its source, clearing as it spills. It deposits a pale crust wherever it hangs or runs. The stone is being born. The water is not cutting the hollow; it is plastering it. Where the water has fallen for a long time, blunt piers and draperies of new stone hang like curtains. The terraces are the same process: each pool builds its own rim outward and upward, a little higher each year. Change is slow. A child may see a pool’s edge grow as wide as a hand; a door may close by a thumb. There is no erosion. The place has not yet begun to be worn down. It is still being made.

The air is cool, not biting, close to spring water, a root cellar, the first half hour after cloud leaves a summit. The humidity is total. Stone sweats, leaves hold beads, and the fog comes through the notch in the afternoon until the far wall is only a suggestion. The light is not dim, but opaline. Because the hollow is high and often cloud-filled, the whole place glows without sharp shadows. When the sun breaks, the white terraces become blinding and the sheets of water turn blue. The horizon is often less than a stone’s throw away, white and wet.

Yet the vegetation is dense, unbelievably green, a miniature jungle growing out of what is barely soil. Moss pads every rim and old stone roof; ferns unroll from cracks; mounds of dark cushion plants, tiny-leaved shrubs, saxifrages, and liverworts fill the lower terraces. The watercourses are lined with mats of red-brown algae and fine grass. Dwarf willows grow shoulder-high in the more sheltered hollows, their trunks and lower branches sintered into white stone. Some have died and remain as perfect calcareous shapes, still standing, their twigs like porcelain. The green is so dense that from above the floor seems upholstered; but cross it and you are soaked to the ribs. There is no dry path, only raised stone rims between pools, each a few fingers wide and slick with moisture.

The animals are almost absent. The noise may be why, or the mineral film, or the cool wet that is never still. You will not hear birdsong. You will not see tracks. A few pale frogs no bigger than a fingertip sit at the edges of the least disturbed pools, and there is a kind of white stonefly that breeds only under the fastest drip. In the lower cistern, a thin gray snake is sometimes seen moving like a slow ribbon. But the greenery is empty of grazers and scurrying and song. It is a hush that is not quiet because the water never stops.

Against the southeast wall, rising from the terraces, is the town. It is not large in footprint—perhaps the whole thing could be crossed in a hundred strides by its central stair—but it is built up and into the rock, stacked forty or fifty levels, a city folded into a single wall. From the floor it looks like a honeycomb or a shelf of wet insects, but closer it becomes clearly civic: public stairs, rails, sluice gates, rope bridges, shared drains, lamp niches, a roofed cistern. The buildings are of pale tufa block, cut soft and then cured under the drip until hard, roofed with split stone and sod. Frames are made of willow and pine, fastened with pegs, cord, and a little copper. There are no wide streets, only the one zigzag stair and the galleries branching from it. Some houses are ordinary and warm; others are only armatures, wicker rooms placed under the mineral water on purpose.

The inhabitants are few. The town has far more rooms than it has people. Many windows are dark and open; some doorways have grown too small, sealed by stone, and are now storage. This is not ruin; the place is too new and too wet for ruin. It is more like a hive that has not yet been filled, or a cathedral constructed for a congregation that has not yet arrived. You can stand on a gallery and see no one, only mist and the white stair of pools. The emptiness is not hostile, but it is deep. The sound fills it so completely that there is no loneliness; it is like being inside a great running instrument.

The town exists because the stone grows. The water is the source of building material. The people set willow lattices where the spring water can reach, and over years the water turns them into walls. A newly begun house looks like a basket. A finished one is smooth and pale, with the ghost of the weave pressed inside like a fossil. They call this growing a room. They also cut fresh stone from the edges of pools; it is soft as hard cheese at first and hardens in air. They scrape their channels, not to clear them of silt, but to keep them open against deposition. The gates are arranged so that the water can be moved to wherever it is needed, and any structure left too long under the drip will seal itself shut, becoming a solid lobe of stone.

The rules are simple and strict: Do not stop the main water. Do not cut a channel until you know where the overflow will go. What the water touches, it takes; anything left too long becomes part of the mountain. A house is not owned forever in shape; it must be kept open with chisel and scraper. The dead are wrapped and set on high shelves where the water drips fastest, and over time they vanish into the wall, becoming white forms, then rock. This is not spoken of much. It seems practical rather than holy: the town does not bury, because the ground is a growing thing and would refuse. The stone builds around whatever it is given.

The weirdness lies not in any single impossible thing, but in the order of processes. In most high places, water cuts and frost splits and wind sands. Here, water builds. The hollow is not older than its noise; it is young, raw, unweathered. Its edges are sharp; its stones have no lichen on their broken faces. The place is still emerging from the mountain, from the sill of cloud, from the mineral load of the spring. It feels like a landscape in its first hundred years, barely begun. The town is also under construction, and so is the ground it stands on. Every pool is a wall in waiting. Every plant is half stone, or will be.

The sound does not change much from day to night. It is a loud, deep braided roar, punctuated by the occasional sharp fall of a loosened shard or the creak of a rope bridge. The people do not fight it. They stand close and speak in low voices, or use small hand signs when the water is high. The town has its own rhythm: clearing channels, cutting new blocks, firing copper tools in the small forge under the overhang, moving wicker frames from one drip-line to another. Smoke rises and is folded into the mist. The only time the sound seems to ease is when the cloud is very thick, or when a new flow is diverted for a day, and then the silence is shocking.

By late afternoon the cloud thickens; the light becomes even, the water sounds seem to come from all sides, and the green turns blue-gray. The place is small enough to hold in one sight, but it keeps branching in the memory: the raw black lip, the white stair of pools, the fern-thick cracks, the little dark windows, the empty galleries, the fog moving between them. It is a hollow in the high country, wet and cold and loud, mostly empty but carefully built—a town that is not so much built as being deposited, one wet year at a time, in a bowl that has not yet learned how to erode.
