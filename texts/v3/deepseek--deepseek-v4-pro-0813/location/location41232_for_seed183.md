Call it the Weeping Array, though the name may not stay where you put it.

Above the cloud line, the country opens into a tilted plain of fluted stone. It is not a summit. It is a plateau lifted so high that the lower world disappears under a white sea of vapor. The altitude makes the air thin and the light hard. The sun is white, almost pressing, yet the wind is cool enough that the wet rock feels like chilled metal. Cold comes not as winter but as a permanent edge: mornings leave a skin of ice on the pools, and snow hides in the north-facing cracks, but by midday the light is blinding.

Water is everywhere. It does not so much fall as seep. It leaks from cliff faces, from the bases of pillars, upward through the joints in the bedrock. Every surface is damp. At dawn the cold condenses on the stone and slides down in slow beads. The ground gleams as if freshly lacquered. Pools occupy every hollow. Some are no bigger than a hand; some are broad pans large enough to hold the sky. They overflow into one another in chains, and the sound of their overflow is constant.

Between the pools stands a forest that is not, and never was, vegetation. Thousands of rock pillars, fins, hoodoos, and balanced slabs crowd the plain. They are not scattered monuments; they are dense, near-touching, layered in ranks until the eye cannot resolve them. The rock is pale marble and dolomite, fluted by water and wind. It is cut by seams of black chert, green copper oxide, white quartz. The erosion is old and settled. There is nothing raw about it. These forms have been shaped for so long they look intentional, as if the plateau had grown a city of stone and then forgotten its builders.

Some pillars are tall as towers. Others rise from the ground like a field of broken teeth. Their sides are runneled, pitted, undercut. Where the seep is strongest, travertine has built terraced rims around the pools. These are not alive, but they grow. Mineral spires rise in the water, branching like frost or coral, made of aragonite and calcite. They are the only things here that resemble plants, and they do not resemble plants enough.

Nothing green exists. No moss in the cracks, no lichen on the stone, no grass in the wet soil, no reeds at the water’s edge. The colors are all mineral: bone-white, rust-red, sulfur-yellow, manganese-black, copper-green, gypsum-pink. The absence of vegetation makes the place feel not dead but pre-living, as if life has not yet been invented here, or has been deliberately left out.

Animals are absent too. There are no birds in the bright air, no insects on the water, no tracks in the clay. The mud at the pool edges records only the passage of water and the occasional scrape of a falling stone. Yet the landscape is crowded and loud, so the absence of animals does not feel like emptiness. It feels like a different kind of occupancy.

The sound is not background. It is a physical force. Wind moves through the stone forest and the flutes sing. The hollows whistle; the larger chambers boom. Water pours from ledge to ledge, and every pool overflows with a small clear chime. Rockfall is frequent. Somewhere in the basin, a slab slips, a pillar loses a flake, a wall collapses; the sound arrives like applause, or like distant thunder. The wind crosses the mouths of old stone channels and turns them into low horns. It is loud enough that you would raise your voice, but there is no one to speak.

The place is in motion. The day is cold and bright; by night the wet rock freezes. The ice in the cracks swells and breaks the stone. By morning, meltwater carries the sand and splinters away. Springs shift. A pool full at dusk may be empty by dawn, its water gone into a new fissure, while another pool begins to fill from a new seep. Salt efflorescence blooms across the flats, white and crusted, cracking into polygons. The mineral terraces thicken. The whole landscape is a slow, loud machine for turning rock into water and water back into rock.

Human or near-human things are scattered thinly through this machine. Low walls of dry-stone cross the open ground. They are not enclosures for animals; there were no animals. Some wall around a single pool, or a crack, or nothing at all. Circular cells stand roofless, built of unshaped slabs fitted with great care. Their doorways face away from the wind. Inside, the floors are worn smooth by some repeated kneeling or pacing, but there are no hearths, no ash, no bone. Whatever came here did not burn anything.

Steps are carved into cliff faces, shallow and polished, ending in mid-air. There are wind-harps of slate tied with copper wire that still thrum in the gale. There are hundreds of cairns, each a small pillar of balanced stones, set on ridgelines and in the saddles between spires. From a distance they look like a crowd of standing figures, but they are only stone on stone.

Petroglyphs cover the smooth walls: spirals, rings, branching dendrites, handprints with too many fingers. Some are pecked, some painted in iron-red and gypsum-white. Tools lie half-embedded in the silt: flakes of chert, hammerstones, copper needles, a few tanged blades. The technology is all hand and stone and simple metal. There is no wood, no wheel, no roof timber, no fire-blackened hearth. Whatever culture left these marks lived without burning the world, perhaps because there was nothing here to burn.

Then there is the part the cairns may have been built to mark.

Distance is not dependable. A pillar that looks a mile away may arrive beside you in a few steps. A terrace visible across the valley may be reached more quickly by walking away from it than toward it. There are paths that descend from the plateau and return to it without ever turning. The horizon occasionally doubles: a faint inverted plain hangs above the valley, like a reflection in still water, but there is no water there.

Water here is not always sure which way is down. On some walls, sheets of water cling and ripple vertically, as if the cliff were a floor. Pools stand on the faces of stones, their surfaces perpendicular to the ground. They do not drip. They reflect the sky sideways. In one cleft called the Hinge, a stream runs from the lower side of a pass to the higher, climbing a set of smooth steps without any spray or struggle. It empties into a basin that has no outlet but never overflows. Looking into that basin, you see the bottom clearly, and the bottom is the sky.

Shadows misbehave. In the brightest part of the day, a pillar may cast two shadows in opposite directions, or none at all while the light is strongest. Sometimes the light seems to come from below, throwing the rock’s own shape upward against the cloud ceiling. The wind carries voices, not echoes. They are made of dripping water and clacking stone, and if you listen, they almost speak. They seem to know the words for things before you do. Some of the petroglyphs are not the same when you look back at them.

Cairns repeat. The same cairn can appear on every ridge, and if you scratch a mark on one, the mark appears on all of them, or on none. The wind-harps play in places where there is no wind. The ground sometimes rings like a struck bowl underfoot.

To stand there is to feel that the world’s rules have been worn thin along with the rock. The place is not malevolent; it is simply old and wet and bright and loud, and reality has become local, a habit the stone no longer keeps. The cool air smells of wet limestone and copper and ozone. The sun presses down with too much light and too little heat. The water never stops. The stone never stops. The place is fully itself: an open, crowded, impossible highland where the only living things are mineral, and even they may be something else.
