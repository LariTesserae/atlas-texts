The light is the first thing. It does not arrive the way light should. It has no single source, or else its source is the entire sky, which is not a sky but a high shallow vault of woven reed and stretched membrane, glowing from behind with an even white heat. The brightness is absolute, like noon held too close. Shadows are pale and few, and they do not agree with one another: a pot’s shadow lies north, its neighbor’s shadow lies west, and the water reflects a tree that is not standing anywhere behind you. The air is hot, nearly body-hot, and so wet that breath feels borrowed from water. Condensation runs down every surface—clay, wood, stone, skin.

The land is not land. From where you stand, it slopes away in broad, low terraces toward a sheet of open water, but nothing underfoot is raw. The ground is a mosaic of fired clay tiles, each one slightly uneven from the press of a hand. They are warm and wet, laid edge to edge over a shallow bowl that seems to have been shaped for water to sit in. The bowl is wide enough that its far side is lost in a bright haze, but it is not natural: the tiers descend like steps, and every step is faced with hand-fitted stone or woven wicker. The walkways are planks of adzed wood, pegged and lashed with fiber. There is no soil, no rock outcrop, no sand. If you pry up a tile—though you suspect the tile would not allow it—you would find not earth but another layer of made things: crushed shell, charcoal, packed reed, and the ends of wooden pilings.

Water is the second material of the place, after the human work. It stands in rectilinear channels, square ponds, circular wells, narrow gutters between tiles. It seeps up between the tiles in places, as if the ground is only a lid over water. It beads on every leaf. It runs in slow, silent films down the sides of stone planters. But it does not erode. The edges of the channels are as sharp as if they were formed this morning; the clay has not softened; the stone has not worn. The water is hot, almost like a bath, and mostly flat. Where it moves, it moves without current, more like a muscle flexing than a flow. Some channels tilt uphill, but the water does not seem to notice; it lies level, even when the channel beneath it slopes. The only signs of motion are at the meniscus, where the water curves upward against the tile with a clarity that seems too perfect.

The vegetation is lush in the way a single well-watered plant can be lush, but not in the way a forest is. The place is not crowded. Each plant has its own stage. There are broad elephant-ears standing in huge clay jars, their leaves heavy and dark, each one beaded with drops that never fall. There are reeds rising from stone troughs in straight lines, spaced exactly far enough apart that their leaves do not touch. There are trees—not many—with trunks wrapped in woven sleeves, their roots contained inside wooden boxes or earthen urns, their canopies open enough to let the harsh light through. Vines climb posts, but they do not grow past their trellises. Flowering things open toward the light, but no petal has dropped. The green is deep and healthy, almost artificial in its uniformity. There are no weeds, no dead leaves, no seedlings where they should not be. The growth is abundant but disciplined, as if each plant has been given a place and has agreed to keep it.

The animals are many, and they too are spaced. Frogs sit on the rims of pots, one per pot, their throats moving slowly. Fish hang in the channels and ponds, fins twitching just enough to keep position; they are bright, coppery, or pale, and they do not school. Wading birds stand on one leg in the open water, motionless, their white or gray feathers glaring in the light. Snails rest along the seams of the tiles in long, evenly spaced lines. Insects—beetles, moths, dragonflies—are everywhere but not in swarms; they perch, walk, or hover in place. The air is abundant with them, but it is not busy. The most striking thing is the quiet. Frogs do not call. Birds do not cry. Fish make no splash. The only sounds are very small and seemingly far between: the tick of a wooden joint somewhere, the sigh of your own lungs, the faint creak of fiber as the heat presses down, though the heat never changes. The quiet is not peace exactly; it is more like a held breath, or a noise that has been smoothed out of the air.

The place has the feel of a world that was finished and then stopped. Nothing weathers. Nothing decays. The leaves do not yellow and fall; the water does not carve or stain; the wooden planks do not rot, even where they stand in water. The animals do not age. The plants do not grow. There is no day or night, only the bright ceiling, which does not dim. There is no wind, only the weight of hot, wet air. The horizon seems to repeat itself: a distant walkway, a line of pots, a tall reed, and then the same walkway again, slightly larger or smaller, as if the world is made of repeating sections that do not quite meet. Reflections are wrong. A pool may show a stone tower that is not behind you, or a version of the same terrace with one extra tree. If you look into a bright sheet of water, you might see yourself, but not moving in time with your own hand.

The rules of the world, as near as you can learn, are these:

Everything is made. There is no natural substrate, no raw earth, no unworked stone. The whole visible world is an artifact.

Nothing erodes. Water is everywhere but has no destructive power; it only wets and condenses and reflects. The sharp edges are permanent.

Light is constant and shadowless or multi-shadowed. It does not come from a sun; it comes from the whole constructed sky.

Life is abundant but static. Plants are lush but do not grow or wilt. Animals are many but do not hunt or flee or call. They are placed, or have placed themselves, in a steady arrangement.

Sound is damped. The world is mostly quiet, and what sounds exist are small, isolated, directionless.

Space is not reliable. Distance may fold; paths may return to themselves; reflections may show other arrangements. The same fingerprint appears on many tiles.

Time is not flowing. The place is a tableau, not a process. It is not that it is still; it is that change has been removed from it.

At the center of the basin, the open water is so bright that it looks like a sheet of beaten metal. Around it, the terraces rise in shallow steps, each one with its grid of plantings, its empty tiled plazas, its low platforms and thatched roofs. The roofs are wet, though no rain has fallen; the wood is dark and smooth. Everything looks newly made and incredibly old at once—not old by decay, but old by the immense patience of something that has been the same for too long.

It is a garden, or a cistern, or a city drained of its people but not its upkeep. It is a world built by hands, then left in a single finished moment. The heat and the wetness press in. The light does not let go. The place is not waiting for anything; it is simply held, like a bell that has not yet rung, but never will.
