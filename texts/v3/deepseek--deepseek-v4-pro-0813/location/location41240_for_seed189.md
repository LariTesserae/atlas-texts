Below the forest, hidden under a mat of living roots, there is a hollow that the roots have found but not filled. It is a pocket in the earth, recently opened, its mineral edges still sharp and unweathered. No water has smoothed them, no long age has worn them round. The air is warm as a mouth, damp with condensation, though no standing water gathers. The place is buried, but it is not dark.

The light is the first impossible thing. It is bright and steady, the color of a clouded afternoon, though there is no sky. A single thick root drops through the ceiling, translucent as wax, and glows with daylight gathered from somewhere above. From that root, finer runners spread across the walls and arch over the void, carrying light like veins. The whole chamber is lit from within, as if the plants have swallowed the sun and are leaking it slowly back into the hollow.

Nothing here is built. There are no stones shaped by hands, no cut timbers, no artifacts, no remains of anything made. The chamber is grown, not constructed. Its floor is a mat of interwoven roots, springy and thin, and beneath it the hollow continues. The walls are root and stem pressed into ribs and sheets of leaf. The ceiling is a living canopy growing downward, sending slender tendrils into the open air. All visible surface is plant. Even the rock behind the greenery is only a rumor, a sharp mineral shadow behind the green.

For all that vegetation, the space is strangely empty. The plant matter is stretched into a filigree, a web of hollow tubes and papery leaves. The actual living substance is small compared to the huge central volume of still air. It is less like being inside a tree and more like standing in a lung built of green threads: a few thin walls of life around an almost-void. The emptiness is not absence. It is the main material of the room.

Water moves through the chamber in a cycle that never quite stills. Moisture condenses on root-walls, gathers into beads, runs down. Every few minutes a drop releases from the ceiling and falls through the empty air to strike a broad leaf below with a sound like a knuckle on hide. Sometimes a whole section of roof lets go at once, and a brief rain falls from a clear green ceiling, drumming on the root floor, then stops. The droplets gather again. The warm air drinks them back upward through the living walls.

The sound is loud. A low note comes from air moving through the hollow stems, rising and falling with the chamber’s slow breath. Roots creak as they lengthen, like old ropes under strain. New shoots pop open with wet cracks. The drip is a sharp percussion, and the echo returns a little late, as if the room is larger than it looks. The far wall can seem very close, then very far, without anything moving.

The plants are in constant motion. Tendrils sway with no wind. Pale leaves uncurl from the walls. Old leaves do not fall and rot; they loosen and are drawn back into the root-mass, dissolved directly into the living architecture. One root may wrap around another and tighten slowly. Another may pierce its neighbor and drink. The violence is slow, but it is everywhere. The hollow is an ecosystem made entirely of plants: host, parasite, predator, prey, structure, weather.

Animals are rare. Almost nothing lives here but the vegetation. From time to time, a colorless cricket emerges from a pore in the root-wall, sits motionless in the glow, and then folds back into the tissue as if it were never separate. It may not be an animal at all. It may be a root’s brief idea of an animal, given a few seconds of motion.

At the center of the hollow, the space is not entirely trustworthy. A droplet falls halfway and then stops, hanging in the air as though the idea of down has been misplaced. The sound of its landing arrives before the droplet does. A root grows upward from the floor, narrows, and vanishes before reaching the ceiling; a little later its tip emerges from the ceiling, still growing, as if the middle of the room has been folded out of the world. Shadows from the glowing root do not always lie on the surfaces that cast them. Some shadows rest on the empty air like stains. The plants do not seem surprised. They have grown into the anomaly. Their roots follow lines of space that no longer obey ordinary direction.

From above, the place is nothing: a slight softness under the leaf litter, a spot where the forest floor gives back a hollow sound. Below, it is a green lung buried in the earth—warm, damp, bright, loud, nearly empty, and entirely alive. A hidden pocket where the world’s geometry has loosened, and the roots have woven themselves into the gap.
