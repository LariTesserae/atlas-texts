The Low Works at Brack

You come down into it on a road of crushed slag and brick. The land drops into a wide, low bowl that was once a river bend. The horizon is only just above the rooftops. The air cools as you descend, the way cellar air cools: not sharp, but damp and close. Moisture gathers on every surface—on the guardstones, on the iron rails, on the back of your hands. It isn’t rain. It hangs as a fine, cold condensation, then runs down the cut in slow black lines. The place is never dry.

Sound arrives before the buildings. A low, regular thump, as of a huge piston. A high metallic scrape. An electric hum. Under and over that, a continuous animal noise—thousands of two-note calls, like little flutes tested against one another. The sound is so dense that after a while it stops being separate things and becomes a single loud atmosphere.

The town sits at the bottom of the bowl. It is small enough to cross in twenty minutes, but it is built like a city. Four- and five-storey brick buildings with iron balconies stand along raised walkways of riveted iron and creosoted timber. There is a machine shop, a pump house, a row of terraced cottages, a public bath, a fire bell. Some older stone foundations are sunk into the mud beneath the newer brick, as if the town has been rebuilt in the same place many times. The buildings are widely spaced, separated by open pans of black mud and seep water. Electric lamps on cast-iron poles burn all day, a steady orange-grey. The sky is one white-grey cloud ceiling, the color of unpolished tin, so the light is even and shadowless.

At the center is the engine house, the size of a church. Inside, a compound steam engine with a flywheel as large as a wagon turns at a constant speed. It drives pumps and stamping machinery. Its piston makes a two-part sound: dull clap, high wheeze. Pipes run out from it in every direction, wrapped in cloth, dripping into concrete troughs. The water in the troughs is black and cold but moving. There are power lines, small motors, a foundry with a circular saw. Nothing appears new; nothing appears ruined. The whole town has settled into its own wear, edges rounded, rust stable.

There are no plants. No grass, no leaves, no moss, no lichens. The ground between the buildings is a mineral crust, cracked into plates, overgrown only by a thin, purple-grey chemosynthetic mat that feeds on methane and sulfur rising through the mud. Around some vents, pale mineral spires have grown over centuries—not living, not built, but accreted. The mats smell of wet iron and sulfide. The place is all mineral and metal and animal.

The animals are overwhelming. The mud appears to flow, but it is not mud moving; it is clinkers—small grey isopods with black segmented shells, millions of them, rasping the mat. They make a sound like pebbles rubbing. In the seep pools, blind white whistle-eels lie half-buried. They raise their heads every few seconds and emit the same two-note whistle. There are thousands. Their calls merge into a glassy chorus that comes from the ground itself. Along the banks stand nodders, tall stilt-legged creatures, grey-pink and featherless, eyeless. They probe the mud with long black iron-hard beaks, each in its own fixed place. They do not call. They only nod, once every few seconds, as if listening to the engine.

Larger animals, tunbacks, push through the deeper pans. They are about the size of pigs but slower, with overlapping plates like an armadillo, black and rust-colored. They navigate by sound, following the same circuits around the town. Their shells are crusted with mineral deposits and old iron fittings, and when their plates touch they ring faintly. They make a low hum, almost below hearing.

There are other creatures: pale grubs in the pipe insulation, black flies that do not fly but crawl in spirals, blind eel-like things in the cooling troughs. The built world is sparse; the animal world is dense to the point of saturation. In any direction, something is moving, feeding, calling, listening.

The town’s people are few. They wear grey wool and rubber aprons. They oil the engine, scrape the mats from the walkways, mend the lamps, and watch the animals. There are no children, no dogs, no birds in the usual sense. You can walk for several minutes and see no person, but you will see animals everywhere.

The strangest thing is that the place is finished. It has settled into an equilibrium that does not change. The weather never changes. The puddles never grow. The engine never wears. The animals never multiply beyond their fixed number. The mats regrow exactly as fast as the clinkers graze them. The buildings are weathered but no longer eroding. The rust has reached a stable color and thickness. The sound is loud, but it is the same loudness, the same pattern.

The world here obeys a closed loop. Warm seep gas rises; the mats consume it; the clinkers consume the mats; the eels consume the clinkers; the nodders and tunbacks consume the eels and clinkers; their waste sinks into the mud and feeds the mats. The town’s engine has become part of that loop, a new organ. It removes the water that would otherwise drown the mats and returns it as condensation. The loop is so exact that the place cannot change. No plant grows because the soil is too metallic and the light too grey. No new thing happens.

And yet within that fixed state, the place is ferociously alive. The air is thick with bodies and calls. The ground is covered with movement. The water is full of blind white forms. You cannot tell where the machines end and the animals begin.

If you stay until evening, though it never truly becomes night, the lamps glow a little brighter, and the animal calls rise as if answering. Then they settle. The same day repeats. You leave on the same road, and when you look back, Brack is only a cluster of wet roofs and slow steam above a black pan, ringing with small voices, held in its own steady state.
