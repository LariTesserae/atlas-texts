The heat is the first thing. Not a dry heat, but a close, wet, greenhouse heat that settles in the lungs and makes the dark feel thick. The air smells of wet basalt, warm oil, leaf-rot, and something mineral-sweet, like rain on hot stone. The light is dim and amber, as if the dark has been thinned with old honey. At first the space seems no larger than a cave, but as your eyes adjust, it opens into a tall, narrow chamber—a cathedral in miniature, no more than eighty meters long and fifteen wide, hung with green and iron from floor to ceiling.

You enter from above, through a collapse throat bored into the crater floor. The surface is a black cinder scab; below, the rift opens maybe thirty meters down, not far underground but enough to feel buried. The walls are young stone—sharp, glassy basalt, clinkery and splintered, unsoftened by water or time. There are no rounded pebbles here, only shards. The footpaths are metal grating because the rock would cut through boots. The place feels uncured, like a wound that has not yet healed.

There is no river, no lake except a shallow black sump at the bottom. The wetness is in the air and on every surface. The walls sweat. Water condenses on pipes, leaves, railings, and skin. It drips from the ceiling in uneven rhythms, not rain but a slow, patient exhalation. Gutters of beaten metal run along the galleries, catching the drip and carrying it to cisterns. Every few hours a valve opens with a soft sigh, and a warm mist moves through the chamber like a tide, leaving everything beaded.

Because of the heat and the wet, the green is lush. Not wild jungle, but dense cultivation. Ferns uncurl from cracks in the basalt. Moss pads the tops of old machine casings. Broad-leaved plants with leaves like dark green hands grow in raised beds made from riveted iron and cracked ceramic. Vines are trained along cables and pipes as if the infrastructure is the trellis. Hanging gardens occupy every gallery, their roots hanging down into the mist. The plants are not decoration; they are the place's reason, or one of its reasons. They feed the settlement and are fed by its water.

The settlement is packed into the rift like a lung fills a chest. It is urban in the way a termite colony is urban: every level built upon, every wall given a function. No more than a few hundred people could live here, but in this narrow space that is enough to feel like a city. At the bottom, the sump and the pump house lift black water to the condensers. Above that are workshops and the steam room. Then sleeping galleries with doors of woven fiber. Then terraced gardens. Then the upper galleries, where the light is slightly better. Bridges of iron grating span the gap. Stairs climb steeply. Pipes run overhead in bundles, some wrapped in cloth, others furred with moss. There is no empty wall, no unused corner. Even the air is occupied by vines, cables, and the slow movement of heat.

The technology is industrial but old and muffled. A central steam engine drives a dynamo the size of a cupboard. Banks of acid batteries sit in ceramic jars. A small foundry recasts broken tools. A row of looms turns tough leaf fiber into cloth. The factory is not loud. Flywheels turn at a walking pace. Pistons hiss. Belts run so slowly you can read the cracks in them. The machines are oiled and wrapped in lagging, and moss grows over the lagging. Some of the oldest machines are no longer driven by steam at all, but by the slow push of roots and the contraction of cooling metal. The residents tend them with patience.

The light, what there is of it, comes from a few sources. Amber lamps hang at intervals, shaded and set low, so the upper galleries are dusk and the lower gallery almost dark. The open fissure at the bottom gives a faint red-orange glow, like a banked forge. Fungus panels on the walls give off a weak blue-green phosphorescence, just enough to trace the stairs. Most of the light is absorbed by the leaves, so the place has the quality of a deep forest at night—warm, close, every shape slightly uncertain.

Animals are rare. The heat and sharp rock and dense industry discourage them. There are pale geckos, almost translucent, that live on warm pipes and move only at long intervals. There are blind crickets in the wet leaf litter. Sometimes a single heat-moth, pale and small, drawn to the fissure glow, will appear and circle the upper gallery. The inhabitants count them. A child who sees a gecko on a particular pipe will remember it and tell the others. The lack of fauna makes the place feel strangely quiet, as if it has not yet decided whether to be alive.

Sound obeys the damp and the padding. There is always a low hum from the dynamo, more felt than heard. Water drips. Steam escapes in brief, soft hisses. A valve closes with a click somewhere above. A loom shudders every minute or so as the shuttle passes. Footsteps are muffled by moss and fiber matting. Voices are low, not from secrecy but because the space does not want loudness. The loudest event might be the periodic mist-tide, a long sigh that moves through the whole chamber and then fades.

The strangeness is not in any single thing but in the way categories have blurred. The plants and pipes are intertwined. Some conduits are hollow roots. Some cables have vines braided into them. A turbine, long unpowered, turns very slowly in the updraft from the fissure, and the residents call it the Clock, though it marks no hour—only the breathing of the place. There are rules, but they sound more like cautions. Do not cut a root that has entered a junction box; call a splicer. Do not shout in the lower gallery, because a shout can knock the dew from the leaves and waste a day's water. Do not touch the walls with bare skin after a rain; the glassy rock is sharp and still hot in places. If a gecko looks at you from a pipe, leave it be. Each animal is known.

The rhythm of the place is slow. The condensation cycle sets the only real clock: mist, drip, dry, repeat, over about three days. The plants thicken rather than grow. The rock cools imperceptibly. The machines run at the pace of a resting heartbeat. Change is something you notice when a new root has crossed a walkway or a pipe has grown a collar of mineral scale. There is no urgency. The waiting is not empty; it is full of small adjustments, the settlement tending itself like a body at rest.

Stand long enough, and the place becomes less a settlement and more a single organism: hot, wet, green, dark, almost silent, breathing. You might not notice when the next mist-tide begins—only that the air has thickened and every leaf is holding water again.
