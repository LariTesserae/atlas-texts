The hollow is no bigger than a one-room shrine, and it sits low in the earth like a water-filled palm. The land around it has weathered away or sagged until the place is below the surrounding water table, and so the wetness does not come only from above. It rises through the stone. The walls don’t merely drip; they sweat from the inside, every crack and pore glossy with warm water. There is no dry rock here. Even the air is heavy enough to leave a film on your skin.

The floor is not a floor at all. It is a continuous field of small travertine pools, basin after basin, rim after rim, packed so closely that their edges touch and overlap like the scales of some enormous mineral fish. Each pool is only a few hand-spans across. Some are no larger than bowls. Their raised rims are smooth and scalloped, the color of old bone, cream-grey, rust-streaked, sometimes faintly violet where the stone has taken up some trace mineral. The pools are nested inside each other, terraces within terraces, and there is no empty space between them. To move across the hollow you would have to step from rim to rim, and each step would show you another pool you had not seen before, hidden behind a low stone lip.

The water is warm, blood-warm, and very clear. It fills each pool to the exact edge and then spills over the lowest point in a skin-thin sheet. The movement is so slight that it is more of a continuous brimming than a current. In places the water has left behind minute ripple-stone, little frozen waves and folded curtains of mineral. The main spring does not gush. It comes out of a low slit between two slabs at the far end, not as a spout but as a constant welling, a slow escaping of water from the inside of the world.

Because the hollow is low and mostly roofed by the surrounding stone, the light is dim. A narrow slot opens somewhere above, but the daylight that reaches the floor has already been softened by steam and by repeated bounce against wet walls. It is grey, with a little brown in it, and it has no sharp direction. There are no strong shadows, only degrees of dimness. The mist turns the light into something visible, a haze that hangs just above the warm pools. In the deepest corner the palest mineral growths catch the faint light and seem to hold it.

Nothing green grows here. Not moss, not a single blade, not even the stubborn grey lichen that can survive on bare rock elsewhere. The wetness is too charged with lime and sulfur, and the light never reaches the threshold that green things need. There is no soil, only mineral crust. When a seed somehow blows in, it rests on wet stone for a day or two, then becomes a small dark blot, then becomes part of the stone. The place is not a plant world. It is a mineral world, and the mineral itself is doing the only growing.

But there is life, faint and rare. In the bottoms of some of the deeper pools a rusty film of bacteria spreads in thin mats, feeding not on sunlight but on the warm dissolved minerals. It is not green; it is the color of old rust, nearly black. Rarely, a small white crustacean, no longer than a fingernail, drifts out from under a stone lip and rows itself through the water with fine feathery legs. Once in a long while a pale cave cricket with long antennae climbs the wall, testing the air. There are no birds, no small mammals, no frog calls. The animals that do come are so few that each one feels like a deliberate visitor.

The sound is mostly quiet. The water sheets make a low hush, not a gurgle, more like someone breathing through wet lips. A drop falls somewhere every few seconds, and its click reaches you after the fact. There is no wind, no rustle, no insect chorus. The mist eats sound. When you breathe, your own breath seems louder than the spring.

There are marks of people, but they are old and scattered. Along the southern wall a low bench of stacked stones has been fitted together without mortar. The mineral water has half-grown over it, so the stones are now joined by a smooth crust as much as by their own weight. A shallow channel has been picked into the rock, leading from the spring mouth to a square basin near the center. Its edges are rounded and softened, not by design but by long exposure to water. Above a ledge, someone ground out a row of cup-shaped depressions, each just large enough for a hand. They are polished smooth from use, then sealed by a later film of stone. Against the far rim a single wooden stave lies fused to the travertine, grey and hard as rock. A few flakes of chert, struck by hand, sit in a crevice. There is no metal anywhere, no cut timber, no written sign. These are not ruins of a building. They are the remains of people who came here, used the warm mineral water, shaped a few things to make the place more useful, and then left it to its own quiet system.

The rules of the place are simple and nearly absolute. Water rises warm from below, fills the basins, spills from rim to rim, and leaves only as slowly as it arrived. The mineral precipitates at the edges, but it dissolves again just as slowly at the submerged lips, so the terraces are not growing thicker or thinner in any human lifetime. The temperature does not change from noon to midnight, from dry season to wet. The pools never rise or fall more than a finger’s width. The whole hollow is a closed account, a saturated solution of itself. It is not still in the way a frozen lake is still; it is still the way a held note is still.

If you placed a mark on the wall and returned after ten years, it would be there. After a hundred, perhaps the mark would be blurry, as if a damp cloth had passed over it. Nothing here decays, because there is so little to decay. Nothing here grows fast, because there is no light and no soil. The place is not cursed, not strange, not wrong. It is only a low, warm, water-filled pocket in the mineral crust of the world, full to the point of saturation, packed with stone and water and dim air. It has settled into itself, and it no longer needs to change.
