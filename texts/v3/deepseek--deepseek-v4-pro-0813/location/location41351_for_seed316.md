The first thing is the noise. It is not a background. It has weight, direction, joints. It is the sound of a continent’s worth of warm water moving through soft stone, of gas escaping from ancient mud, of wind crossing a horizon so wide the air itself forgets to stay still. You would not call it separate sounds. It is one continuous roar, like a sea made of weather and earth breathing at once.

Then the light. Dim, brown, greenish at the edges. The sky is low and thick, not with ordinary clouds but with a permanent exhalation. The sun is a pale bruise behind it, sometimes visible only as a lighter smear. There are no clear shadows. Things are lit from all sides and none. The darkness does not come only from night; it comes from saturation—the light is absorbed by damp air before it can reach the ground.

The land is old. Not old in the way of ruins, but old in the way of a face after all expression has been worn away. Whatever mountains existed here were ground down long before any living thing had a name for them. What remains is a low world: whalebacks of black and red stone, ridges of chert and ironstone, all rounded, polished, split by salt. The ground is mostly mud—dense, warm, soft—stretching to every horizon. There are shallow waters everywhere, not one sea but a thousand pools, channels, sheets, all braiding and unbraiding across the flats. The water is warm as a mouth. It weeps from the sides of low hills, seeps up through the mud, runs over stone in films too thin to ripple. It does not always run downhill.

The whole country is a thing at the end of erosion. It has been flattened almost to the level of the sea, but the sea is not stable. The water is a migrating population. A pool lifts out of its bed in the night and walks six kilometers across the flats; by morning it has become a lake, by noon a marsh, by evening a crust of salt. Channels open and close like veins. The ground swells into mud domes that burst with a sound like a slow door slamming, then flatten into new land. This is the strange paradox: the landscape is ancient, but it is not still. It has settled into its final form, and its final form is turbulence.

The mud is not merely brown. It is black with oily rainbows, streaked red from iron, white from salt, yellow from sulfur. In thin films it is the color of strong tea. The air smells of metal, rot, hot stone, and something sweet like overripe fruit left too long in wet heat. You can taste the salt on your teeth. The rain, when it falls, is body-warm and faintly bitter with dissolved minerals. But rain is only one kind of water. There is also water that rises from the ground in slow silver threads, condensing upward toward the warm sky. There is water that hangs in the air as a warm fog so dense it feels like walking through shallow sea. There is water that weeps from the stone itself, not from springs but from the pores of the rock, as if the whole land is sweating.

The vegetation is sparse. No trees. No grass. In places where gas rises from cracks in the earth, there are clumps of black reeds, brittle and hollow, tapping against one another in the wind. They are not green. They are the color of old blood. Around the warm pools grow mats of slime—some rust-red, some sulfur-yellow, some pale as skin—breathing in slow blisters. The mats spread, split, rejoin. They are the only forests here: forests of film, one cell thick, covering every stone and puddle. The air carries spores, but they are small enough to ignore and dense enough to give every breath a grit. There is no bare ground. Every handspan is packed with something: salt, gas, egg, spore, crystal, vibration. The place is not empty. It is full to the point of saturation, but the fullness is mostly invisible.

The fauna is rare. When something does move, it is slow and soft and often half-made. There are eel-like things that travel through the mud, not swimming but pushing through it as if the ground were only a thicker kind of water. They are blind, nearly transparent, and they leave no tracks because the mud closes behind them. There are small crustaceans in the warm pools that only appear when the water is a certain temperature, then sink back into the sediment for years. Sometimes a pale frog-like creature sits on a stone and does not move for a whole season. Its throat pulses with the same rhythm as the steam vents. You might mistake it for a stone. It is a rare event, like seeing a face in the static.

Scattered across the flats are the marks of something else. They are not buildings, not exactly. There are lines of fitted stone, one course high, running for a hundred meters then stopping. There are circles of drilled boulders, each bigger than a person, half-swallowed by mud. There is a single standing pillar of black stone, polished to glass, leaning at an angle that has not changed in centuries though the ground beneath it has. These things are few, and they are very old. They are not shelters. They look more like stitches. They seem to hold certain places together, the way a pin holds a fold of cloth. Near them, the air is slightly quieter. The water runs downhill more often. The dimensions are more reliable. But only near them. Between them, the world frays.

Because reality here is not whole. It is old, and it has been worn thin. The horizon folds. You look out and see a wall of ocean standing upright in the distance, not falling, just hanging there like a curtain. You look again and it is a mudflat. A boulder the size of a house appears on the left, then on the right, then in both places at once. The same storm passes overhead twice, once forward and once backward: lightning first, then the sound arriving before the flash. The rain does not always fall. In some places it rises from the ground in slow silver threads. Water leans against the base of a ridge and holds a tilted surface for hours, ignoring gravity. Sound arrives early. Echoes precede the shout. If you listen long enough, you hear a bell without a source, ringing every thirteen seconds from a place that is not there.

Distance itself is not reliable. A low ridge that seems half a day’s walk away may be reachable in ten steps, or may retreat as you approach. The same stone marker may appear three times along one stretch of mud, then never again. Memory becomes unsure. You see a line of old stone, look away, look back, and it has always been elsewhere. The sky sometimes cracks—a dark seam opens from horizon to zenith, not lightning, more like a tear in wet canvas—and through it comes a sound like the ocean’s memory, vast and salt and full of nothing.

The noise is deafening, but it is not only noise. It shapes the land. The roar of the wind presses ripples into the mud. The thunder cracks open new channels. The hiss of steam scours the stone. The subsonic hum of the earth moving through its own old age keeps the pools trembling. Sound is a physical force here. It packs the air. It makes the ground vibrate like a drumhead. It is impossible to think in straight lines, because thought itself is jostled by the pressure. Silence does not exist, except in the small ring of a stone stitch, and even there it is only a relative thing, a trough between waves.

There are gas vents that never stop. They huff like huge lungs, and around them the air wobbles with heat and something else. Mud pots boil in slow motion, not always with heat but with gas, and their bubbles pop with the sound of wet leather tearing. Salt flowers grow on the flats in the brief intervals between rains—crystals that form in minutes, then dissolve when the next sheet of water slides over them. The stone surfaces are polished to a dark gloss by wind and grit and water, and they are carved with no symbols, only the smoothness of almost infinite patience.

The old stone lines are made of no local rock. They are pale, fine-grained, cold even in the warm air. No tool marks show on them. They appear grown rather than carved, as if they crystallized out of the landscape in some earlier age when the rules were firmer. They do not form rooms or walls high enough to shelter anything. They run in straight lines for a while, then turn at angles that are just slightly impossible. A circle of boulders may have one boulder that is not entirely present; you can see it, but your hand might pass through it, or your mind might refuse to remember its exact shape. These structures are scattered, few, and immensely old. They are the only architecture, and they were probably never meant for creatures with bodies.

The scale of the place is not human. It is large enough to have its own weather, and its own weather is unstable. Storms are born and die within the hour. A wall of rain moves across the flats, and behind it the ground steams. A whirlwind of warm water spirals upward for a few minutes and then collapses, leaving a crater that fills with new mud. The sky changes color from brown to green to a bruised yellow, and each color has its own sound: the brown sky hums, the green sky hisses, the yellow sky rings faintly at the edge of hearing. The pressure changes constantly, and with it the boiling point of water, so that some pools seethe without being hot and others lie still at temperatures that would cook flesh.

At night, which is only a deepening of the dimness, the slime mats glow faintly green. The pools steam. The distant wall of water, if it is visible, turns black and reflects no stars because there are no stars. The standing pillar hums. The mud domes open and close. The eel-things move under the surface, and the rarest thing of all happens: for a few moments, the whole vast plain becomes silent, not because the sound stops, but because it passes beyond hearing, into the body, and becomes something else. Then the storm comes again, and the world starts over.

This is a place that has outlived its own geography. It is a low, warm, wet, nearly empty world, ancient almost beyond time, packed to the brim with invisible life and visible noise, where reality has grown thin from too much age and use. It is not hostile in any deliberate way. It simply no longer remembers all of its own rules. And in the gaps between those rules, the water rises, the ground breathes, and the sound goes on, deafening, endless, older than the stones.
