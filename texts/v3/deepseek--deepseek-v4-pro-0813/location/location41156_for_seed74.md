The approach is along a canal that smells of wet iron and crushed stems. The water is the color of old tea, moving so slowly that the only sign of current is the drift of pollen and the occasional oil sheen breaking into plates. The towpath is a strip of mud and cracked brick, half-swallowed by a mat of small, thick-leaved plants that give underfoot with a wet squelch. It is cool—not cold, but a damp cool that settles into the joints, the kind that makes every metal surface sweat and every leaf drip. The sky is a flat gray, and the light through it is the plain, even light of an overcast afternoon, though here it comes down greenish, strained through the enormous leaves that canopy the street.

The block itself is small: a rectangle of perhaps two hundred paces by a hundred, bounded on one side by the canal, on the others by high walls that are not walls in the usual sense but living barriers—a mass of interwoven trunks, aerial roots, and stems that have grown into a solid, wall-shaped structure, studded with the occasional rusted iron gate or a lantern that still burns a low, yellow flame. The gate stands open, or rather, the vines have grown around it in such a way that they hold it open, the hinges long since fused into the wood. Beyond, the ground is a patchwork of wet stone and open space: there are wide gaps between the buildings, empty areas of cracked concrete where puddles reflect the leaves overhead, and shallow troughs where water trickles continuously from somewhere inside.

The buildings are not buildings. That is the first thing to understand. They are plants that have grown into the shape of buildings, or perhaps buildings that have been so thoroughly colonized that the difference no longer matters. The walls are thick, fibrous, living: the outer surface is a bark-like skin, deeply furrowed, wet with condensation, and from it sprout ferns, small flowering vines, and clusters of pale, waxy leaves. But embedded in this living wall are the bones of the old structure—steel I-beams rusted to a rich brown, brick facades visible in patches where the bark has split, copper pipes emerging from the plant flesh like veins from a wound. The windows are gaps in the growth, some of them still holding panes of glass, now fogged on the inside, others open to the interior darkness, with leaves spilling out over the sills.

The roofs are canopies: broad, overlapping leaves as big as a man's spread arms, supported on a scaffold of iron trusses that the plant has grown around and through. Water drips from the leaf tips in a steady, small sound, like a hundred tiny clocks, and where the leaves overlap there are gutters of bark that channel the water into downspouts of living stem, which carry it down to the ground and into the canal. The whole place is a machine for moving water, among other things.

Inside the first building—if it can be called inside—the air is warmer by a degree or two, and the light comes through the leaf roof in green patches that move slightly with the slow pulse of the plant. The floor is a mat of roots, hard as wood, polished by years of traffic, and in the center stands a machine that is both plant and engine. It is a cylinder of dense, fibrous wood, maybe three meters tall, with a piston that moves up and down with a slow, rhythmic stroke, driven by steam that hisses from a valve made from a seed pod. Around it, pipes of hollow stem run along the walls, carrying water and sap, and every few meters there is a brass fitting, a pressure gauge with a glass face clouded with moisture, a lever made of a thick dried vine. The machine is quiet—a soft thump, a hiss, a creak—but it is working. It is doing something. Perhaps it is pumping water, or crushing seeds, or weaving fiber. The plant is not just covering the machine; it is the machine. The metal parts are inserted into the plant tissue, bolted to living wood, and the plant has grown around them, incorporating them into its body.

There is a rhythm to the place. Water drips, steam hisses, gears turn slowly, leaves rustle. The piston thumps. A conveyor made of woven reeds and iron rollers carries a stream of pale pulp from one machine to another. A series of flowers, each the size of a fist, open and close in sequence, releasing small puffs of fragrant steam. The plant is alive and working, and the machinery is part of its metabolism.

The ground outside is sparse: between the buildings there are stretches of damp stone and mud, with only a few lone plants rising from the cracks—not the dense mass of the walls, but single, solitary stems with a single leaf, or a cluster of moss on a fallen brick. The place is not crowded. There is room to breathe, room for the mist to hang in the air, room for a person to walk without brushing against the walls. The density is low, the spaces open, but the vegetation that is present is so massive, so all-encompassing, that it fills the eye and the mind. The proportion is like a cathedral: the plant masses are huge, but they are spaced out, with empty air between them.

The fauna is rare. In the hours I spent there, I saw perhaps three living creatures that were not plants. A beetle with a shell the color of verdigris, no larger than a thumbnail, crawling along the rim of a pressure gauge. A bird—small, gray, with a sharp black beak—that nested in a vent where the steam was warmest, and flew out only once, low over the canal. And a creature like a pale rat, but with longer, thinner legs, that drank from a sap pipe and disappeared into a crack in the bark. That was all. The place belongs to the plants and the machines, and the machines belong to the plants.

The sound is mostly quiet, but it is not still. The dominant sound is water: the drip from leaves, the trickle through gutters, the lap of the canal against the stone, the occasional soft gurgle from a pipe. Under that is the mechanical sound: a low, continuous hum from somewhere underground, the slow thump of the piston, the hiss of steam, the click of a gear engaging. And under that, or perhaps woven through it, is the sound of the plant itself: the creak of a stem as it grows, the soft snap of a seed pod, the rustle of leaves as they shift to catch the light. It is a working place, but it works quietly, like a sleeping animal breathing.

The light is normal, but everything is tinted. The overcast sky gives a flat, shadowless illumination, and the leaves filter it into a greenish twilight even at midday. The mist from the canal diffuses it further, so that the far end of the block seems to fade into a gray-green haze. There are no strong shadows, only a soft gradation from bright leaf-lit patches near the roof edges to dim, damp darkness under the machines.

The erosion is established. Everything has been here for a long time. The bricks are worn smooth, the iron is rusted to a stable brown, the wood is polished by use, the plant bark is old and thick, with deep furrows. But the place is not decaying. It is in balance. The plant repairs the metal by growing around it, the water wears the stone but the stone is already worn to its final shape, the machines are old but they are maintained by the plant's growth, replacing worn parts with new growth. The cycle is closed. The place has reached a state where it will continue almost indefinitely, barring some great change.

The rules of this world are not spoken, but they are visible. Plants are the dominant life form, and they have absorbed the industrial world. Technology is not opposed to nature; it is grown from it. The engines run on plant oils and sugars, the pipes are stems, the machines are wood and fiber, the electricity comes from the movement of sap and the decay of leaves. The city is a forest that has learned to build, or a factory that has learned to grow. The block is a single organism, a plant-machine that processes air, water, and sunlight into fiber, oil, sugar, and motion. The canal brings water and carries away waste; the mist keeps everything moist; the cool air prevents the machines from overheating. The humans, if there are any, are not here now. Perhaps they are elsewhere, tending other blocks, or perhaps they have become part of the plant, or perhaps they never existed. The place does not seem to need them.

The weirdness is in the details. A vine that reaches out, slowly, and pulls a lever. A flower that opens to release a puff of steam, then closes. A root that has grown through a gear, and the gear still turns, the root flexing with each rotation. The plant seems to be aware, in a way that plants are not supposed to be. It is not a mind, exactly, but a purpose. The place is not a machine that happens to be covered in plants; it is a plant that has built itself into a machine. The boundary between the two is not blurred; it is nonexistent. The plant is the machine, and the machine is the plant. The beetle with the verdigris shell, the bird in the steam vent, the pale rat—they are the only animals, and they seem to know their place. They are tolerated, perhaps even cultivated, like living gears.

As the afternoon wears on, the light begins to fade. The mist thickens over the canal. The piston continues its slow thump. The water keeps dripping. The plant breathes. The place is quiet, active, alive. It is a small, cool, damp, low-lying, sparse, established, urban-industrial block where the vegetation is total and the machine is grown. It is a landscape, a factory, a forest, a church, a body. And it is still there, working, long after I left.
