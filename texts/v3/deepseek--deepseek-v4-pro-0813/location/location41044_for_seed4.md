**The Grafted Hollow**

The Hollow is not entered so much as discovered by accident. From the bog above, there is only a shallow depression where the peat has slumped, its edges raw and black, its center matted with sphagnum. If the day is cold, a thin breath of warmer air rises from it, melting the frost in a small ring. That is the first evidence: the Hollow is hidden, a pocket of wetter, warmer air in a cold landscape. You find it by the absence of wind and the sudden smell of animals.

The descent is short, steep, and newly made. The collapse that opened this place is young—geologically almost yesterday. The walls are sharp, torn, unweathered. Roots hang from the ceiling like snapped cables, their broken ends pale and fibrous, still bleeding sap. There is no stone. The material of the Hollow is plant: a massive, ancient tangle of willow and alder root, sphagnum, liverwort, peat. It is a room dug not into earth but into the root-mat of the bog itself. The floor is a lattice of roots, some as thick as a thigh, with black water between. The Hollow is not large—forty or fifty strides across, twenty or thirty high—a single contained void beneath the moss. It is a town in a room.

Water is the first thing you notice after the dark. It is everywhere. It condenses on every surface and drips from every edge. It runs down the root-walls in thin, shining sheets. It pools in the spaces between roots, so black they look like holes until something moves. The cold is near freezing, but the water rarely hardens; the slow respiration of the root-mass and the warmth of countless animals keep it just liquid. It is a wet cold that settles in the bones, smelling of copper, peat, and something animal-sweet.

The vegetation is not a layer over this landscape; it is the landscape. The walls are living. The floor is living. The ceiling is a canopy of roots and moss. Moss grows over moss; roots grow through older roots; liverworts, ferns, and pale fungi fill every crevice. There is no soil in the usual sense, only the slow accumulation of dead plant matter becoming peat. The people who live here do not build on the plants; they build with them. They prune roots into beams, train willow into walls, weave sphagnum into insulation. The architecture is grown as much as constructed.

The settlement is sparse but unmistakably urban. At first you see only lights: a few small flames and a few pale blue-green glows hanging at different heights in the dark. They reveal platforms of pressed reed, bridges of braided bark, dwellings like woven baskets suspended from the root-ceiling. The town is not crowded; there are perhaps forty structures spread across the hollow, far enough apart that each seems alone in the fog. The spaces between are wide, crossed by single-strand walkways that sag over the water. But it is urban in its layering: five levels in places, connected by ladders, notched stairs, rope hoists. It is a vertical town, a town of empty air and careful knots.

Everything is made by hand. The tools are bone, antler, stone, a little hammered copper. Copper needles and hooks turn green with verdigris in the wet. Rope is made of inner bark; cloth is woven from moss-fiber and animal hair. Nothing is nailed. Joints are lashed with sinew or grafted into living wood. The people know which roots will hold weight, which will bend, which will bleed, and which can be trained over a season into a railing, a gutter, a cradle.

The animals are overwhelming. They are not an addition to the Hollow; they are the main fact. The water is thick with black eel-wyrms that swim backward as easily as forward. Pale newts with feathery gills hang in the pools. Blind fish rise when the fog is thick. The walls and roots crawl with springtails, mites, centipedes, and small shelled things. The air is full of white midges that drift without buzzing. Larger animals move through the lower galleries: shaggy root-grubbers the size of dogs, browsing on soft bark; pale, long-bodied predators that wait motionless for hours, nearly indistinguishable from roots. Nests are tucked into every fork; guano and shed skins streak the walls. Yet the Hollow is mostly quiet. The animals have learned that sound is dangerous. Predators here hunt by vibration and patience. The overwhelming life reveals itself as pressure: many things holding still, watching, waiting.

The light is scarce. The sun never reaches the floor. At noon above, the Hollow is only a blue-gray twilight; at night it is black except for the lamps. Some pools glow faintly when disturbed, as if the dark itself can be stirred. The lamps burn peat-oil in small soapstone or bone cups, or they hold colonies of a fungus called eel-star that gives a cold blue-green light and must be fed with animal milk every few days. The people keep the flames caged in wet reed, because the walls are alive and will burn poorly but can burn.

The Hollow is active, never still. Water cycles constantly. The warm breath of animals rises, meets the cold air, and becomes a fog that sinks down the walls in slow gray waves. This fog-fall happens several times a day. With it comes movement: the eel-wyrms migrate upward through the root channels, the moths drift in sheets, the people open and close sluices of woven reed to drain the new water into lower pools. The plants grow fast in the wet—some roots lengthen by a hand’s width in a single sleep—so the town must be pruned every day or it will close like a wound. The bog above breathes through the entrance, drawing air in and out, so the whole place inhales and exhales.

There is strangeness in the boundaries between plant and animal. The moss will grow through the shells of dead eel-wyrms if the shells are pressed into it, making handholds that are half shell, half plant. Some fungi in the walls are pollinated not by air but by the fur of passing root-grubbers. The people follow rules that sound like superstition but are closer to animal husbandry of the root-mass: keep fire small and caged; never walk the lower sump alone during fog-fall; do not prune the one root at the center that hums faintly on certain nights; give every slain animal’s blood back to the moss, because the moss remembers. The Hollow is not a wilderness with a town in it; it is a single responsive organism, and the town is one of its organs.

From outside, it is nothing. A wet depression in an unnamed bog, a place where the ground gives slightly and the snow melts early. Inside, it is a world: cold, wet, living, listening, full of animals and slow fog, a hidden town grown into the root of the earth, quiet as a held breath.
