**The Unfinished Range**

The first thing is the altitude. Not a view from a peak, but a continuous exposure: the low oxygen, the cold wet pressure in the chest, the way every stone sounds too sharp. The land has not yet been worn down. It rises in black and rust-brown ridgelines that disappear into a ceiling of grey cloud. The light is dim, flat, without a clear source, as if the whole sky has become a frosted pane. It makes the snow-patches on the highest faces look like pale bruises, and the wet rock shine like oiled machinery.

Water is the main presence. It does not fall heavily, but it is always arriving. The cloud brushes the ridges and condenses on them. Every cliff weeps. Every crack has a thin dark line of moisture running down. The ground underfoot is not soil but raw stone with a film of water, and each step sounds like walking across a wet factory floor. Streams run straight along the newest joints, too young to have carved meanders. Pools lie in every depression, some clear and freezing, others warm and milky with minerals, their surfaces stirred by gas.

The terrain is monstrous and unfinished. There are towers of columnar basalt, cliffs with slick, glassy faces, ridges of jagged tephra, and long benches of fresh lava that look as if they cooled only last season. Nothing is rounded. The talus is made of sharp, ringing plates. Fault scarps cut across valleys like fresh cracks in a ceramic glaze. In places, the ground is still hot. Steam plumes rise from vents spaced far apart across the open country, some straight as chimneys, some drifting sideways. The vents hiss, cough, or beat slowly, and their breath smells of sulfur, iron, wet copper, and ozone.

There are no buildings, no walls, no cut paths, no artifacts. The industrial feeling does not come from construction but from process. The mountains are being lifted faster than water can erode them. Heat from below drives columns of steam into the cold air. The steam becomes cloud, condenses on the high rock, runs down as water, meets heat again. It is a closed loop, a natural power plant running without a single made component. In dry moments, the air around the vents carries a static charge; a hand raised near the fumes feels a faint pull, and a blue glimmer sometimes travels along the wet edge of a cliff, lighting the rock like a fault in a wire.

The cold is damp and penetrating. It is rarely far below freezing, and rarely far above. Snow falls as wet granules, melts into the general seep, returns as fog. The cloud rarely breaks, so there is no strong sun, only a shifting of greys. Yet this dimness is not dark; the water films and mineral stains catch what little light there is and return it faintly. The landscape seems to be lit from within, or from the steam.

Life is abundant, but it is not evenly distributed. The open ground is almost empty. Between one vent and the next, or one colony and the next, there may be a kilometer of bare wet stone without a single visible animal. But at the nodes, life is dense. Around the warm springs, bacterial mats grow in orange, sulfur-yellow, and dark green. They are grazed by larvae of midges and brine flies in such numbers that the water surface shivers. The adult flies form a low cloud above each pool. Small pale birds, finch-like and quick, dart through the steam to catch them, and their chittering is constant around the hot ground.

Higher on the cliffs, colonies of larger birds pack the ledges. They are gull-like or petrel-like, grey and white, with black wingtips. Thousands of them rest between flights, so many that their droppings paint the stone. They leave in waves, streaming down into the fog to feed on whatever swarms in the cloud layer or the cold sea beyond, then return in long, orderly lines. Their noise is the loudest sound the land makes, but even that is intermittent, like a factory whistle sounding and fading.

There are small, thick-furred grazers—pika-like creatures that live between the moss patches and the bird colonies, feeding on lichen and the insects that wash up around the pools. They are rarely seen, but their tracks cross the wet silt. At night, a soft-winged predator, owl-like, moves between the steam columns, and its cry is a single low note repeated at long intervals.

The rules of the world are simple. Heat below, water above, rock rising between them. Life does not depend on the sun here so much as on the dark energy of the earth. The food web begins with sulfur and iron dissolved in hot water, passes through bacteria and flies to birds and grazers, and loops back through droppings and minerals. The land is too new for soil, too raw for forests, too exposed for stillness. It is an emerging place, still being assembled. What looks like industry is the planet’s own machinery, running in the open air, unattended and unbuilt.

Mostly, it is quiet. Wind threads the gaps. Water drips from a thousand ledges. A vent hisses far off. Then, without warning, a slab of rock lets go and rides down the slope in a long clatter, and the birds rise in a grey sheet, and the sound rolls across the empty distances. After it fades, there is only the ticking of cooling stone and the faint electric smell of steam, and the sense that somewhere below, the next ridge is already beginning to move.
