The Sump Exchange is not a ruin exactly. A ruin is a place where the built world is losing to time; this is a place where time changed sides and then made peace. It occupies a shallow bowl at the foot of a warm valley, no wider than a village chapel. The ground sinks toward its center, and every slope around it drains inward. It is low, close, and wet all the way through.

Water is the first thing you notice and the last thing you forget. It seeps from the earthen rim, beads on the leaves, runs in slow strings down old brick, and gathers in pools the color of strong tea. There is a permanent indoor rain near the center, spilling from a riveted iron tank lodged high in a fig. The floor gives water back with every step. No surface is dry for long. Even the air is drinkable, warm and heavy, condensing on the glass of old gauges and running down the inside of their faces.

There is no exposed ground. The floor is a platform of interwoven roots, some as broad as thighs, upholstered in moss, liverwort, and tiny round leaves that spring back after you pass. The walls are root curtains over old brick and concrete. The roof is a mat of canopy, wired glass, and steel struts half swallowed by vines. The plants do not occupy the space. They are the space. Ferns grow from brass taps. Moss fills the mouths of pipes. Aerial roots have fused with ladder rungs and handrails. Where the old brick shows through, it appears only in gaps, like teeth in a green jaw.

The light is ordinary, though it arrives strangely. Above the bowl there are a few holes in the canopy, and daylight falls through panels of wired glass in a ruined roof frame, gray-gold and even, the light of a bright overcast afternoon. Caged lamps still hang from the branches, glowing dull orange, though they are not needed. Their light pools in the wet leaves and makes the whole place look like late afternoon inside a machine.

The machinery is scattered through the vegetation the way organs sit inside a body. At the center sits a hot-bulb engine, single-cylinder and heavy, old enough to have hand-painted letters under its rust. It rests on a concrete pad the roots have cracked and then bound back together. Its flywheel turns slowly, one black spoke passing every few seconds. The piston rod slides in and out of a sleeve of polished metal, the only part the moss has not covered. The exhaust stack rises through the canopy, wrapped in fig roots, and with each power stroke it pushes out a soft white puff that smells of burnt swamp gas and wet leaves.

The engine draws from a flooded sump. It sends water up through a pipe screened by roots to the header tank in the strangler fig. The tank leaks in a dozen places, and so over the middle of the Exchange it always rains. The rain falls from leaf to leaf, then onto the root floor, then back into the sump. The water cycle here is not weather. It is the machine’s breath.

Around the engine, the industrial remnants are almost too numerous to list: a brick chimney with vines pouring from its top, the remains of a control shed with three walls and no roof, a rotted catwalk crossing the shallow water, a bank of pressure gauges mounted on a frame that is now half tree. The needles quiver with every pump stroke. A small generator hums under a coat of moss. Wires run through the branches, some spliced by tendrils. A few of the old wires have begun to put out leaves.

There is a strange reciprocity here. Roots have grown into the pipe joints, and pipes have grown through the roots. At one place a root has entered the throttle linkage. When the root swells with water after rain, the engine slows; in a drier spell the root contracts and the engine speeds up a little. It might be coincidence, but it has the feel of a governing mechanism. The machine heats the roots; the roots insulate the machine. The engine’s exhaust is warm, damp, and rich, and the leaves around the stack are darker and broader than anywhere else. The plants filter the sump water, so the pump never clogs. The pump lifts water to the upper plants, so they never go dry. Dead leaves fall into the sump and rot. The rot makes swamp gas. The gas feeds the engine. The engine lifts the water. The water feeds the leaves.

The rule of the Sump Exchange is circulation. Nothing is wasted. The machine produces no goods, only heat, vibration, noise, and movement, but the vegetation has built an entire world around those products.

Sound fills the bowl the way water fills a pond. The engine chuffs once every three seconds, a low, wooden-sounding beat that comes up through the soles of your feet. Between strokes there is no silence. Water drips from a thousand leaves. The header tank runs in thin strings. The generator whines softly under moss. Metal ticks as it cools and warms. The roots creak when the flywheel’s vibration passes through them. At the edge of hearing there is a hiss from a leak in the methane line, a sound like a snake that never moves. The quiet here is never quiet.

Animal life is rare. The usual noise of a warm swamp—frog calls, birdcalls, insect whine—is almost entirely absent. The engine seems to have replaced it. A few small things survive: a pale snail on the warm glass of a pressure gauge, a scattering of water fleas in the sump, and once in a long while a thin white egret that picks its way along the catwalk for an hour and then leaves without a sound. There are no nests, no burrows, no tracks in the wet leaves. The only heartbeat is the pump.

Nothing here happens quickly. The flywheel takes its time. The water level rises or falls only with the slow seepage from the valley walls, a few inches across a season. The roots move too slowly to see, though over years they have shifted the brickwork and bent the catwalk. The place is not in the middle of becoming something else. It is in the middle of staying the same.

There are small, inexplicable details. The leaves near the engine have a faint metallic sheen, as if they have learned to sweat oil. A fern grows out of the mouth of a brass tap and drips into a cast-iron basin. A cluster of roots has formed a perfect handrail along the iron stair, with knuckles of old machinery showing through like bones. In the roofless shed there is a chair, its seat filled with a cushion of moss, facing the gauges as though someone has just stepped away and might come back.

The air smells of hot oil, wet brick, rust, crushed leaves, and a faint sweetness from flowers that bloom inside the exhaust plume. Breathing is like drinking warm tea. The heat from the engine gathers under the canopy and keeps the whole depression a few degrees warmer than the marsh around it. Condensation runs down every vertical surface, old glass and new leaf alike.

The Sump Exchange is small, packed, and saturated. It is an old green machine-room sunk into the earth. It has the feel of a place that was once built, then overgrown, then settled into a long and patient working life that no one intended. It is loud and slow and alive, all at once, and it does not need anyone to keep it going.
