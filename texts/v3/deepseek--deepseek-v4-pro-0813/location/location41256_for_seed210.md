You come through the notch into a high, wet room of the world, and it is far louder than it should be. Not the noise of wind, not the noise of water, though both are here—this is the noise of a factory floor: a deep, regular thud, a metallic hiss, a high whine that sits in the teeth. The sound arrives before the place does, and after a while you understand that the sound is the place.

The basin is small, a few hundred strides across, a hanging hollow bitten into the top of young mountains. The walls are black and glassy, jointed in long clean planes, so sharp they look like they were broken a moment ago and have not yet learned to fall. There is no talus, no scree, no softened edge anywhere. This is a place where erosion has never happened. Water runs down every face in sheets and threads, but the rock is not worn. The cracks are fresh. The slabs sit at angles that pull at your eyes.

It is high. Below the rim, the lower world is gone, replaced by cloud. The air is thin but heavy with moisture, cool enough that breath shows, though the air is already so full of mist that the difference hardly matters. The place is a kind of sky-nest, a sealed upper chamber of terrain.

Water is the main body of the place. It does not come from springs; it seeps out of poreless stone, condenses on the undersides of leaves, beads on every surface, runs in thin films down the walls and across the floor in channels that are too straight to be accidental. Some streams use those channels. Others do not. One stream crosses the basin, turns up a tilted slab, and climbs it in a smooth, silent column, curling over itself without falling. At the far wall, a waterfall drops halfway, turns to mist, and rises back to the lip. Pools fill above their rims and do not spill. The same large drop detaches from a fern tip, falls a hand’s width, and vanishes before it hits the stone. It is already on the leaf again.

The light is dim, sourceless, the color of tarnished silver. It seems to come from the air itself, or from the wet rock, or from nowhere. Shadows are faint and often wrong: a stone casts one shadow toward the valley and another toward the sky. Fog stands in fixed columns between the plants, too dense to be weather, too still to be weather. It does not drift. It has the quality of a structural element, installed.

The vegetation is lush, but not in the way a forest is lush. The plants are widely spaced, each one separate on its own island of bare wet rock, so the overall impression is of sparseness and order. But where a plant occurs, it is saturated, overfull, dripping. Cushion mosses grow the size of oxen, deep green and gleaming like sponge. Tree ferns raise black stems and long serrated fronds, each frond glossy and exactly curved. There are shrubs with leaves in threes, every leaf tipped with a perfect pendant drop that does not fall. Lichens spread in circuit-like patterns, silver-green, faintly warm. Between the plants there is no soil, no duff, no leaf litter. A fallen frond lies as fresh as the moment it fell. It will not brown. Roots run over the rock like insulated cables, and in a few places they pass through solid stone without a crack, as if the stone and the root had agreed not to notice each other.

The air smells of wet stone and ozone. Under that is a faint mineral-oil tang, though there is no oil, no engine, no moving part.

The animals are many, but they do not crowd. They are spaced like the plants, each at the center of a small territory, each performing some fixed action that does not progress. A line of black millipedes with brassy legs moves in a closed loop around the base of a boulder, never leaving the route. Pale newts with red gills gape from separate ledges, their mouths opening in exact time with the deep thud. Above one pool, a cloud of midges hangs in the shape of an upside-down funnel, every insect vibrating in place; if you disturb it, it bends and re-forms like an image correcting itself. Snails with shells of the same black rock make slow circuits, disappear around one side of a stone, and reappear at the start without crossing the space between. A bird with wet dark feathers sits in a pale tree, opens its beak, and lets out a small jet of steam. The call arrives a second later from the wrong direction.

Nothing hunts. Nothing flees. Nothing feeds. The abundance is real, but it has no hunger in it.

The sound is the most insistent fact. The deep pulse comes from a dark cleft in the eastern wall, but it also seems to come from underfoot, and from behind your eyes. The hiss is like many charged wires, the whine like metal on metal. The fronds of the tree ferns produce the high note without wind. There is no machine, but the sounds are phase-locked: the pool surface flexes with each pulse, the midge cloud contracts an instant before, the newts gape in unison on a single silent beat. It is exactly like standing inside an idling engine, except that there is no engine.

The place has no built structures, no artifacts, no walls, no paths, no tools. But its natural forms are machined. The basalt joints run like stamped plate. The water channels have the sharp corners of engineered flumes. The roots are dressed like wire. The shells are finished to a tolerance. Pick up a stone and it is stone through and through, not a casing, but its grain runs in parallel lines, and if you hold it to your ear you hear the thudding, faint and steady.

The strangest thing is that nothing changes. The water moves. The animals move. The sounds continue. But no leaf grows, no rock wears, no pool spills, no insect dies, no bird shifts from its branch. There is no erosion, no accumulation, no rot, no season, no weather. The fog neither thickens nor thins. The light does not change. The temperature does not fall. There is no gradient anywhere: no warmer stone, no cooler pool, no wetter air, no drier leaf. Every process is exactly balanced, and time is a loop that has closed so tightly it has become a solid object.

Motion has been detached from becoming. The waterfall does not arrive. The millipede does not progress. The droplet does not land. The place is a broken-off piece of the world left running, real but impossible—a high, cool, drenched, dim room of sharp rock and separate living things, loud as a factory, sparse as a gallery, and perfectly, unchangeably itself.
