The place has no natural ground. If you stand on what seems like a high balcony, you are not looking down at rock or soil; you are looking down into the next tier of the constructed world. Call it the Sort, the Upcast, the Ledger—depending on which stair you take. It sits at altitude, not on a mountain but as a mountain: a high plateau of decking, gantries, switchback stairs, cooling towers, and stacked housing blocks. The air is thin the way high places are thin, but the thinness comes from ventilation shafts and the long pull of upper exhaust, not open sky.

Everything is fresh. The metal plates have not yet been rounded by weather. Their edges still have the burr of cutting. Concrete lacks cracks except the deliberate expansion joints. Paint is thick and unblistered. The place is young, not because it is still being built, but because it has not been allowed to begin eroding. Perhaps it repairs itself too quickly; perhaps it was finished only a few thousand cycles ago. Either way, there is almost no dust. Condensation beads on every surface, and that is the only weathering.

It is cool and damp. Water does not fall often, but it is always just leaving the air. It gathers on overhead pipes and drips into channels cut in the deck. Every so often a fine mist is released from grids in the ceiling to settle the smell of oil and ozone. There are no rivers, but there are wet seams: staircases with running films of water on the risers, gutters where liquid moves almost imperceptibly toward sump-catchers. The vegetation is exactly what such moisture will grow without soil—gray cable-moss in joints, pale fungi like flattened ears around valves, a few fronds of drip-fern hanging from the coolest ledges. It is sparse enough that you notice every patch. Green is rare, and when it appears it is usually silvered by the light.

The light is huge and even. There is no sun, only fields of lamps behind frosted panels: some high overhead, some embedded in the walkways, some pasted to the sides of towers. It is bright as a noon overcast but with no single direction. Shadows are weak, doubled, sometimes in the wrong place. The brightest hours are not much different from the dimmest. The world dims by degrees too slow for the eye, so the end of a day is less an event than a thought.

Because the light is so constant, the air itself seems dense with visibility. But the density is structural first. The buildings are close. Balconies nearly touch across the alleys. Pipes and cable trays run in layered bundles overhead. Walkways stack above walkways, and the lower walkways are stacked above deeper ones still. There is a sense that the city has been pressed into its own volume and kept there by rivets. Yet it opens out on a larger scale: from an upper gantry you can see for kilometers across terraces of workshops and dormitory blocks, descending in long steps toward a glowing haze that may be the plateau’s edge. It is regional, a county-sized arrangement of made things.

The sound is the strangest first thing. It is mostly quiet. Not dead, not muffled by earplugs, but as if every surface has been tuned to swallow its own noise. Machines run, but their voices are sub-audible; you feel them in the soles of your feet. The pistons that operate the north staircases take five minutes to complete a stroke, and they make no more than a soft exhale of steam. The nearest thing to a loud sound is the drip of water from a condensation pipe, or the occasional click of a thermal joint settling somewhere above. Voices do not carry. A dropped bolt does not ring; it lands with a small private tick, as if embarrassed. Even the abundant animals are quiet.

And the fauna is abundant. The air above the lamp panels is busy with pale mottled moths, but they make no sound; their wings move only as a flicker in the light. Birds—sparrow-things, or something that mimics them—roost in the cable runs by the hundreds. They open their beaks but no song comes out, or perhaps the sound is taken before it reaches you. In the sumps there are eel-like creatures that never surface. In the moss-lined gutters there are beetle-things with bodies like small enamel boxes, moving at the end of the long dimming. The most common animal is a kind of wall-louse that leaves tiny trails of clean metal behind it, eating the condensation film. It is said the fauna has become as constructed as the city; many of them have joints instead of wings. But they are alive in the way the city is alive.

Industry is everywhere, but it is patient. There are factory sheds with conveyor belts that move only when you have stopped watching them. Flywheels turn so slowly they appear frozen, but their edges are warm. Smokestacks do not smoke so much as breathe a thin continuous moisture. The machines are electrified, maintained to a high polish. There is no natural substrate beneath them. The machines sit on decks, and below the decks are more machines, and below those are cables and pipes and the roots of other districts. The whole thing seems to have been built not by people but by earlier versions of itself.

Because the built density is so high, there are rules of navigation that are not quite physical. The usual one: do not trust a straight corridor longer than about two hundred meters. After that, the corridor may not end where its perspective says it should. Sometimes it repeats itself, exactly but for a missing rivet. Sometimes it opens onto a street that the map claims is on the far side of the plateau. There are doors that lead to rooms larger than the buildings that contain them, and staircases that descend for half a kilometer and arrive at the same landing you left. Locals argue about whether this is a flaw in the architecture or a flaw in the world. They use painted handprints and small shrines of wire to mark stable paths.

The weirdness is not loud. It is ambient. A shadow may lag a quarter-second behind the person casting it. A reflection in a shallow puddle may show the same street with the lanterns burning in a different order. The birds above the fourth gantry are sometimes twice as many as they were a moment before, but no one looks too closely. In some rooms the condensation drips upward. In others, a dropped coin takes a long time to land—not because gravity has changed, but because the distance to the floor has.

The weather, if it can be called weather, is a slow cycle. The mist releases happen on a schedule measured in days; the gutters fill and empty over weeks. The temperature never swings quickly. The entire plateau tilts by a fraction of a degree over the course of a year, so that the water films shift their routes and the sparse vegetation migrates from one set of seams to another. People adjust their door thresholds accordingly. It is a place of weak gradients: almost no wind, almost no sound, almost no weather, but everything gradually becoming itself in a different pose.

You could spend a long day walking from the upper lamp fields to the lower terraces. The way would cross dozens of small bridges, each wet and cool. You would pass through dense, quiet neighborhoods where people live in stacked rooms the size of cargo containers, their windows bright. You would hear water moving somewhere underfoot. You would see moss growing in the shape of handholds. You would feel the slow machinery as a warmth in the rails. At the far end, the plateau might seem to drop away, but it would not reveal nature—only more of the made world descending into a haze of its own light, still sharp-edged, still wet, still not yet worn.
