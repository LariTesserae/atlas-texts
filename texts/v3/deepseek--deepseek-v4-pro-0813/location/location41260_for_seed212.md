# The Green Municipality

Call it the Green Municipality, though nothing in it is stone or charter. It fills a low basin between two low ridges that are not ridges of rock but the exposed arcs of ancient roots, still alive, still slowly moving. The elevation is barely above the water table; the ground is damp, not flooded. There are no broad rivers, no ponds, no marshes, but water is everywhere in small, persistent amounts: condensation beaded on every leaf, a film on the bark streets, droplets falling from leaf-tips with the rhythm of a slow metronome, saucer-shaped bracts holding a mouthful of rain. It rains in brief, heavy showers, then the air thickens again. The temperature is warm—greenhouse-warm, skin-close, the heat of compost and respiration.

But vegetation is total. Not “the city is covered in plants” or “the city is built among plants,” but *the city is plants*. The ground is a fabric of living roots, the walls are fused stems, the roofs are leaves. There is no mineral soil visible in the central quarters: no stone, no clay, no metal, no glass. What would be pavement is a mat of flattened leaves bound by a felt of root hairs, springy underfoot, already healing where a footstep broke it. What would be a wall is a palisade of stems grown edge-to-edge, or a curtain of overlapping leaves, or a lattice of vines so thick it is solid but still breathing. What would be a gutter is a channeled leaf petiole. What would be a window is a translucent membrane stretched across a frame of twigs, veined and pulsing with sap. What would be a lamp is a pale bract that catches daylight and holds it greenly until the moon rises.

There is no technology here—no fire, no wheel, no chipped stone, no forge, no metal. But the city is built. It is built by growth, by the slow and sudden architecture of plants.

In plan, it is unmistakably a settlement. There are streets, alleys, plazas, quarters, markets, stairways, gates, and aqueducts of vine. There are tenements of stacked leaf-rosettes, their floors separated by broad root platforms. There are civic buildings of one enormous tree that has grown chambers and halls: pollen chambers, seed-sorting rotundas, sap-cistern towers. There are avenues wide enough for processions, though no procession of animals ever walks them. The city has a downtown and outskirts, an old quarter where the bark is dark and ridged, and a new quarter where every surface is still bright green, the edges unweathered, the buds not yet opened. It extends beyond the central basin too, spilling over the low ridges into neighboring valleys, linked by root-roads and vine-canals. It is not one monument but a green region, a conurbation of leaves.

The sound is the first thing that hits. The city is deafening. It is not a quiet place of plants; it is a continuous, layered roar. The dominant sound is leaves—millions of leaves turning, rubbing, flicking edge against edge, a sound like a heavy rainstorm or the sea. Under that is a deep, groaning bass: trunks and roots twisting as they grow, xylem pipes carrying sap under pressure, bark splitting to let new growth through. There are sharp cracks, like gunshots, as seed pods uncoil or a leaf the size of a sail unfurls from its bud. There are wet pops and hisses: stomata gas exchange at a scale that sounds like steam escaping, water bladders bursting, old leaves detaching and falling with a rustle like dragged cloth. The wind adds its own note, but the city makes its own wind through transpiration. The sound is not background; it is a physical pressure. To stand there is to be inside a living engine.

The city is also turbulent, unstable, young. The land is low and newly emerged; the edges are sharp, not rounded by time. Nothing is old because nothing survives long enough to age. Growth is not a slow process here; it is a storm. A district can change its shape between dawn and noon. A street of leaf-paving will be overgrown by a new layer before evening. A root buttress can rise from the ground between two plazas in the space of a morning and force them apart, opening a new alley. The city is a bazaar of construction and demolition, but the workers are the plants themselves, and the materials are alive. It is Solaris-like: the landscape makes and unmakes itself continually. A tower of stems may be five meters tall by midday and compost by dusk, replaced by a new tower that grows from the compost.

The air is warm and wet, tasting of crushed leaves, sap, wet cellulose, and the sour-sweet smell of fermentation from the lower layers. Normal daylight comes through the canopy as green-gold. In the densest quarters it is a green twilight, but shafts of full sun fall through the gaps and the leaves turn edge-on to them. The humidity is so high that the city makes its own weather: fog rises from the canopy, sudden downdrafts of cool air spill down the alleys, pollen clouds drift.

There are no animals. None. No insect hum, no bird, no lizard, no rodent, no fish. The only “fauna” is a few microscopic animalcules in the water films, not enough to matter. But the city is not still. What moves here is vegetable, not animal. Seeds walk on coiled fibers that tighten and loosen with humidity. Vines crawl across the ground by growing at one end and dying at the other. Roots push through the soil like slow serpents. Leaves snap shut when a shadow passes, not because there are flies, but because the plant has been shaped by no animal pressure into a mimic of a trap. There are things that look like animals—a slow-moving tuber with root-legs, a drifting seed with leaf-wings—but they have no nerve, no muscle, no blood. They run on sap pressure and cell growth. The city is a pure plant ecosystem, a dense society of vegetation.

Because of this, the urban quality is uncanny. There are doors but no one opens them; there are market stalls but no buyers; there are stairs but no feet. The city is not abandoned—it is too alive, too crowded—but it is inhabited only by itself. The density is extreme: every surface is near-touching, every niche is filled, every space has a green presence pressing into it. It is dense like a slum, but of leaves and stems.

And then there is the weirdness, the reality breakdown. The city does not obey a single geometry. Streets do not connect the same way twice. A lane that runs north in the morning may, by mid-afternoon, deposit you at the same plaza you left, or on a balcony above the root market, or in a place where the ground is vertical and the wall is the floor. A map, if drawn, becomes wrong as the ink dries, because the city is painting itself. A building may be a seed in one glance, a mature structure in another, and a compost heap in a third—not in sequence, but simultaneously, depending on where you stand and what you are looking for. The city has not settled into a single time. One block ages a century in an hour, its leaves yellowing and falling and regrowing while you watch. Another block is still a bud, waiting for a season that never comes. The sound sometimes arrives before the event: you hear the crack of a branch splitting, then turn and see the branch only beginning to split, then hear the crack again after it completes, like an echo from the future.

There are places that are both inside and outside, rooms that are also leaves folded into pockets, streets that are also stems. There are stairways of root that lead to themselves. A cut made in a stem may already be healed before the blade reaches it. A door of leaves may open before you decide to enter. Perhaps this is what happens when vegetation reaches total coverage and no animal mind is there to hold the world in one shape.

If you stand still, the city will grow around you. It will wrap your feet in rootlets, weave a wall between you and the path, and in an hour you may find yourself inside a small green room that grew up around you, with a leaf ceiling and a root floor. The sound outside will be muffled, but still present, a roar behind the walls. The room will be warm, damp, alive. It will have no door, but by the time you notice, a gap may peel open in the wall, or the wall will simply become leaves again and fall away.

At night, the temperature drops only slightly. The sound changes: less photosynthesis, more respiration, the creak of cooling sap, the rustle of leaves folding, the drip of condensation. There is no artificial light; the city is dim and green, lit only by moon and stars. Some pale leaves hold the last of the daylight, but it fades. The city keeps growing in the dark, more by touch than by sight.

The rules of the world are simple, if you can call them rules: What can be grown will be grown; what cannot be grown will be grown anyway, in some other shape. No animal movement persists; the city absorbs its own possibilities and reissues them as leaf and vine. Nothing is finished; the city is always early, always becoming, always tearing itself down. It is not a place, but a process—a loud, wet, green process in a low, warm basin, where the world is made of plants and the plants have made a city.
