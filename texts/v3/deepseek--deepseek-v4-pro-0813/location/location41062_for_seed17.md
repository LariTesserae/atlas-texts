You are not above the sea. You are *in* it, but high — high in the way a mountain peak is high even when the water that drowned it closes a thousand feet over its summit. You stand on the knife-edge of a ridgeline that was, until quite recently in geological terms, a young mountain range still shrugging off its tectonic birth. The rock is sharp, unweathered, fresh. It has not had time to soften. The faces of the peaks are clean planes of dark basalt and glittering gabbro, broken only by the vertical scars of recent faulting — slick, glassy surfaces where two sides of the world slid past each other and left no trace of soil. There is no sediment. There is no mud. The sea has not had time to grind these mountains down; it has only covered them.

The water you move through is cold. Not cold like a winter lake, but cold like deep time — a few degrees above freezing at the bright upper reaches, sinking toward zero in the black gaps between peaks. The cold is a physical fact, a pressure on the skin, a thickening of the water itself. It slows everything. Sounds travel far here. The water is so clear and so cold that daylight reaches deeper than it should. The highest summits — the ones you are walking on — stand in something close to normal light, a blue-washed, crystal version of daylight. Below, the valleys fall away into darkness, but even that darkness is clean, not murky. There is no particulate life at the surface. No plankton blooms, no drifting algae. The water is empty in a way that makes distance crisp and false; you can see far, but what you see is rock and water and more rock, with nothing green in between.

The range goes on beyond sight. This is the thing that breaks the mind, eventually. You walk a ridge and the ridge continues, north and south, curving over the horizon of water like a spine of a world. To the east, the mountains step down in enormous terraces — flat-topped blocks dropped by successive faults — and then the deep abyssal plain begins, so far below that its floor is only a rumor. To the west, the peaks rise higher, some of them breaking the surface; from below you can see the underside of the wave-light shimmering on their summits like a false sky. The scale is continental. This drowned chain runs for thousands of miles. It is large enough to have its own oceanographic weather: slow, majestic currents that wrap around the mountains, pour over the passes between peaks, and spiral down the cliffs into the deep. Currents that never change.

That is the strangest thing to sit with, once the shock of the cold and the vastness wears off. Nothing changes. The temperature gradient is fixed, layer upon layer, as if drawn with a ruler. The currents follow the same paths they have followed since the sea rose. The chemistry of the water — the oxygen, the salts, the volcanic minerals seeping from the young rock — sits in perfect equilibrium. There are no seasons here. No storms. No migration of the water itself, other than the eternal circulation that has become so fixed it might as well be architecture. The world has settled into a state of being that does not allow for becoming. You could leave and return after a thousand years and the same light would be falling on the same ridge in the same way, and the same sounds would be roaring through the water.

And the sound. It is deafening.

You notice it first as a vibration in the jaw and chest before you ever consciously hear it — a low, continuous, physical pressure, as if the entire sea were being shaken very slightly from below. It has no silences. It has no rests. It is a drone, but not a simple one; it is made of layers. The nearest layer is the hissing, roaring rush of hydrothermal vents — young mountains mean active tectonics, and there are fields of them in every fault scar, black smokers and white smokers venting superheated mineral brine into the freezing water, their thunderous roar conducted through the rock and the water alike, quite literally deafening if you swim too close.

Beneath that, a mechanical note. Constant. Rhythmic. The sound of engines.

Most of the industrial structures are dead. You find them along the ridges — small things, scattered, the way ruins are scattered across any empty land. Pumping stations. Drilling rigs. Tiny extraction plants built into the cliffs, their metal skeletons now crusted with slow-growing mineral deposits and the papery tubes of tube worms. The cold water preserves them; they have not rusted so much as become geological, their steel and titanium slowly exchanging atoms with the basalt, becoming ambiguous: half machine, half mountain. Their gears are frozen. Their lights are dark. They are mossless, barnacleless in the way of a world with no green life, only the small grey coils of chemosynthetic animals colonizing their hulls.

But some of them still run.

One, two, maybe three across a hundred miles. Geothermal generators, their turbines driven by the same heat that feeds the vents. They were built to be autonomous, and they have been autonomous for so long that they have become part of the thermal cycle of the mountains themselves. Their engines produce a steady, deep mechanical pulse that travels through the rock and into the water, GPS-like in its regularity. The sound they make is not a pounding or a grinding; it is a smooth, enormous rotation, a subsonic engine-turn that never varies. Fish have evolved around it. Schools of pale, eyeless things migrate along the exact corridor of the sound, as though it were a river.

And there are the calls.

The fauna of this place is abundant — abundant in a way that takes adjustment. There is no vegetation. No kelp, no sea grass, no drifting mats of sargassum. Nothing green. The food web is chemosynthetic at its base: bacteria growing on sulfide plumes, grazed by swarms of copepod-analogues, which are eaten by small silver fish, which are eaten by larger fish, and so on, up to the enormous things. The enormous things are the filter feeders — leviathans with cavernous mouths, bone-white and blind, that hang in the open water between the ridges, their gill-rakers catching the falling bacterial snow. They are the size of small islands. They sing.

Their singing is the loudest sound of all. Each individual produces a call — a long, low tone, more felt than heard — and they never stop. The population is spread across such vast reaches of water that there is always one within range, often several. Their songs overlap, forming chords that resonate in the deep basins between the mountains, reinforcing and canceling, building to a continuous, world-sized chorale. It is not a song, really, because there is no variation and no silence. It is a standing sound. An acoustic feature of the environment, as fixed as the temperature gradient. The mechanical drone of the machines, the roar of the vents, the singing of the leviathans — together they make a noise so total, so pervasive, that you could not imagine existence without it. Silence has been extinct here for as long as there has been water.

The emptiness. That is the other thing. All of this — the mountains, the vents, the machines, the fish, the leviathans — occurs within a volume of ocean so vast that the average density of presence is almost nothing. Between one ridge and the next, there are miles of open water. Days of swimming through blue-violet emptiness, the ridge behind you shrinking, the ridge ahead growing. In that open space, there are almost no fish. The leviathans pass through occasionally, their songs arriving long before their vast bodies; otherwise, you swim in a kind of void, a cold, clear, roaring void, ringed by mountains you can barely see. The abundance of life is real but it is gathered in thin veins — around the vents, along the sound corridors, in the zones where current meets rock. Everywhere else, the sea is empty. Hollow. An almost-nothingness between anchored points, so large that a human could spend a lifetime in the open water between two peaks and never touch either one.

Some of the details are strange enough to notice.

The leviathans' songs, for instance, repeat in patterns too regular to be random. And though it may be pareidolia, it sometimes seems the patterns correspond to the intervals of the industrial engines — that the filter feeders are singing the machine-song back to the machines, in a kind of harmony that no one composed. The vent fields, too, have an oddness: the tube worms do not grow in natural clumps, but in straight rows, an inch apart, following paths that might once have been trenches for pipelines. They have colonized the machines so thoroughly that the machinery and the animals are no longer distinguishable. The small silver fish follow the same circular routes around the same seamounts uninterrupted since the mountains were submerged; there are grooves worn into the basalt, fish-radius, where generations have swum the identical loop, a current of fins that has not altered in centuries.

The light helps the strangeness. On the high ridges, the normal daylight is refracted through the water into an azure brightness, clear and honest and exposing. But at a certain depth, the light does not fade gradually; it ends. There is a line — a thermocline, a halocline, a light-line — where the bright water stops and the dark water begins. It is not a smooth transition but a visible boundary, a glowing ceiling over the deep. Above it, the sun world; below it, the song world. Most of the life is below. Most of the mountains are below. The high places where you might stand, blinking in the underwater daylight, are like the tips of a drowned continent — trophies of a world that was briefly soon-to-be-terrestrial and then was not.

The youngness of the rock continues to surprise. On some of the highest peaks, the basalt is still cooling, still contracting, cracking audibly — a sharp, percussive sound, brittle and sudden beneath the drone. The mountains are still growing. Slowly, quietly, under the weight of the sea, the range is continuing to uplift, and the water is maintaining its level, and the two forces have settled into a balance so exact that the mountains rise and erode at the same rate, canceling each other out, perfect equilibrium. Never changing. The world is in the midst of an orogeny that is also, simultaneously, completely static. The mountains build themselves and are unmade at the same speed, and the landscape — the ridges, the cliffs, the fault patterns, the vent fields — has looked the same for ten million years, and will look the same for ten million more. Time has been folded into a circle. It rotates. It does not progress.

The industrial engines, too, have achieved equilibrium. They were built to extract, but they have been running for so long that they now simply circulate — drawing heat from the mountain, releasing it into the water, driving the currents that bring the bacterial snow, which feeds the fish, which feed the leviathans, whose songs pattern the water, whose bodies decay into more bacterial snow. The machines no longer manufacture anything. They are part of the metabolism of the sea. If they stopped, the entire system — the thermal vents, the chemosynthetic blooms, the feeding grounds, the singing — would collapse into a different and less populated world. The mountain range is, in a sense, alive. Not biologically. Structurally. Ecologically. A cold industrial-natural machine at the top of a drowned world, vast enough to have its own weather, empty enough to hold a silence that is pure roar.

You stand on the ridge in the blue light. Below you, the dark water hangs like a room. Above you, a thousand feet of clear cold sea, and then the surface, and then the sky, which you only half-remember. The sound in your chest is the same sound that was in the water before the mountains rose, and will be after the mountains are gone. Not a place you can change. Not a place that would notice if you tried. Just a place that is, and has been, and will be: drowned, cold, immense, deafening, empty, and utterly, perfectly still.
