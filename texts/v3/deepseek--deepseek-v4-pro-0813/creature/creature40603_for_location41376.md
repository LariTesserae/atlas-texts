The first time you see one, you think it is a trick of the steam. A column of denser air, a gathering of heat and mineral fog that holds its shape too long. You blink, and it is gone—or perhaps it was never there, only a flicker of the ember-light through the wet dark.

The second time, you are more careful. You stand still, water seeping into your boots, the humid air heavy as a soaked cloth over your mouth, and you wait. For a long time there is nothing. Then, at the edge of your vision, where the dark is deepest, something forms. It does not rise from the ground. It condenses from the air itself, from the heat, from the slow precipitation of minerals out of the thick wetness. It is as if the fog has remembered a shape, and the shape has chosen to remain.

It is tall, but its height is uncertain. It is shaped like a man made of mud and salt, but it is not a man. It has no head, or perhaps it has a head—a knotted mass of white sinter, not a face but a possibility of a face, a place where drips and ridges have arranged themselves into something that might, in a different light, be features. It has no arms, or perhaps it has arms—two long encrustations of salt and mud that hang at its sides, or lift slowly, the way mineral deposits grow, the way a branch of stone might reach toward heat. It has no legs, or perhaps it ends in a broad base that merges with the mud, so that it is not standing on the ground but rising out of it, an extension of the earth’s slow exhalation.

It does not look at you, but you feel it seeing you. The seeing is not with eyes. It travels through the air as a change in temperature, through the water as a current turning toward you, through the mud as a warming of the ground beneath your feet. It is a pressure, a fullness, an awareness that surrounds you and passes through you, warm and wet and slightly wrong, like the space around you has grown attentive.

The being does not speak. But the sounds of the place gather around it. The drip of water, the hiss of steam, the low groan of stone settling—these sounds are not coming from the city. They are coming from the being. It is the one who drips. It is the one who hisses. The city’s sounds are its voice, and you realize you have been hearing it speak since you arrived, without knowing it was speech.

It moves. You do not see it move. It is there, and then it is there, a little closer, and you did not see the space between. The water at its base does not ripple. The mud does not stir. It is as if the world rearranges itself around the being, so that it does not need to move through space—space moves through it, like water moving through a channel, like heat moving through stone.

It is not alone. There are others, scattered across the black flats, standing in the colonnades, half-submerged in the warm pools. Some are large and solid, their bodies thick with salt crust and black mud. Some are small and partial, no more than a twist of steam that has learned to hold still. Some are so still and so crusted with mineral that they look like part of the architecture—a pillar that has stepped out of its building, a wall that has forgotten it was a wall and remembered it was something else. They are the city’s inhabitants, if a city can be said to be inhabited by its own processes.

They are made of the same stuff as the city, but they are not the city. The city is their body; they are its consciousness. When a building breathes, it is them breathing. When a channel of water runs uphill, it is them, remembering where it wants to go. When the salt grows at the edge of a pool, forming a white rind that advances over the black stone, it is them, dreaming themselves into new shapes, one drop at a time.

Their bodies are not stable. They are always dissolving and reforming, like the city itself. A ridge of sinter grows from one being’s shoulder, then crumbles and falls, hissing as it hits the warm water. A face, if it is a face, drips and shifts, the features sliding into each other the way wet mud slides down a bank. The salt on their skins cracks and flakes, revealing darker stone beneath, and new salt grows over the cracks, sealing them, smoothing them. They are not built. They are grown, the way a geyser field is grown, the way a salt pan is grown, the way the world grows when no one is looking and time is not measured in days but in centuries.

They are patient in a way that is almost unbearable. They do not hurry. They do not react. They regard you with a stillness that is not empty but full of time, full of heat, full of the slow vastness of the world. They are waiting, but not for you. They are waiting for the city to change again, for the water to rise, for the salt to grow, for the dark to deepen. They are waiting for nothing, which is to say, they are waiting for everything. Their patience is not a choice. It is their nature. They are made of patience, the way the city is made of stone.

There is a difference between them, though it is subtle. No two are quite the same, and the same one is never quite the same twice. They repeat themselves, like the city, but each repetition is slightly different. One may be a little more slumped, another a little more dissolved. One may have a shoulder of white sinter, another a crack running through its chest like a dry riverbed. They are not individuals in the way people are individuals. They are variations, iterations, echoes of a form that has no original. They are the city dreaming itself into multiple shapes, and each dream is both the same and different.

They do not fear you. They do not seem to notice you at first, or if they do, they regard you the way a salt pan regards a raindrop—something that will soon be gone, dissolving or evaporating or being absorbed into the ground. But then, slowly, as you stand there, they turn their attention to you. You feel it as a tightening of the air, a slight increase in heat, the water around your feet growing warmer. They are curious in the way a stone is curious: not with eyes or questions, but with patience. They will wait for you to understand, or to give up, or to become one of them.

They are not hostile. The place has no hostility in it, only heat and time and the slow, deliberate wrongness of the world. But they are not welcoming either. They are simply there, as the place is there, as the water is there, as the dark is there. To meet them is to meet the landscape’s attention. It is not a comfortable feeling. It is like being watched by a mountain, or by a river, or by the earth itself—not unkind, but absent of all the kindnesses that pass between living things.

They have no names. But if you wanted to call them something, you might call them the Dreamers, or the Waiters, or the Ones Who Remember Water. But these names would be wrong, because they are not separate from the things they do. They do not dream; they are the dreaming. They do not wait; they are the waiting. They do not remember; they are the memory. They are not inhabitants of the city. They are the city’s inwardness, its slow awareness of itself, its sleep made visible.

The dark is their medium. They are made of it, or it is made of them. In the lightless places, they are most themselves. They do not fear the dark; they are the dark, given form and weight and presence. The only light they have is the faint red glow from the earth, and it passes through them like light through water, dim and warm and slow. It does not illuminate them. It only softens them, so that they seem to have no edges, only surfaces that blend into the air and the water and the mud.

They have no culture, no history, no language in the way those things are usually understood. But they have a kind of order. It is not a social order. It is a mineral order, an order of arrangement and flow. They are spaced out like the buildings, with wide intervals of empty mud and dark water between them. The emptiness is part of their existence. They are not lonely, but they are alone. Solitude is their natural state, as it is the natural state of a geyser field, of a salt pan, of a stone that has sat in the dark for millennia.

They are not the only presences here. There are others, even less formed: shapes in the steam, movements in the water, flickers of awareness that may be the same beings at different stages of dissolution, or may be something else entirely—precursors, afterimages, fragments of the city’s dreams that have broken off and drifted away. The place is full of them, but not crowded. They are sparse, like the vegetation, like the buildings, like everything in this world. Sparse and patient and slow.

If you stay long enough, you begin to understand them. Not with your mind, but with your skin, with the heat in your bones, with the water in your mouth. You feel their presence as a change in the air, a shift in the current, a subtle wrongness in the space around you. You learn to sense them before you see them. You learn to know when one is near, when one is watching, when one is moving through the intervals of the city. You learn their language, which is not words but drips and hisses and groans, and you find that you have been speaking it all along, without knowing.

And slowly, almost imperceptibly, you begin to realize that you are becoming like them. The hot wet air is in your lungs. The mud is on your skin. The dark is in your eyes. The city’s metabolism is working on you, dissolving you, precipitating you, making you part of its slow dream. The salt is growing on your hands. The heat is in your blood. The patience is in your bones. You are not afraid. The fear has been replaced by something else—a kind of peace, a kind of patience, a kind of belonging. You are becoming an inhabitant. You are becoming one who lives here.

But perhaps that is only what the city wants you to think. Perhaps it is the other way around. Perhaps you are not becoming them. Perhaps they are becoming you. Perhaps the city has been waiting for someone to arrive, someone to see it, someone to remember it, and now that you have come, it is learning from you what it means to be seen, to be remembered, to be alive in the way that living things are alive. The beings are not hostile. They are not welcoming. They are simply there. And now, so are you.

The place is not abandoned. It is not inhabited. It is somewhere in between. And so are the ones who live there. They are the in-between made visible, the boundary made flesh, the process made permanent. They are the city’s way of being both itself and something else, both stone and water, both heat and dark, both being and not-being. They are the answer to the question the city asks, and the question is simply: what if we stayed?
