The people who live here are not easy to see at first. They are the same color as the stone, and they move with the same economy. But they are there, and they have been there for a long time.

They are not a large people. Generations of thin air and cool damp have made them compact, with narrow chests and long, careful hands. Their skin is pale, with a gray undertone like the lichen on the north side of the walls, and their hair runs from ash-brown to the color of wet slate. Their eyes are pale too—gray, or a washed-out blue, or the color of the sky just before the light fails. They do not look at you directly for long. It is not rudeness; it is a kind of conservation, as if even a gaze is a thing to be spent carefully.

They dress in wool and hide, in layers that have been mended so many times the original garment is only a memory. The wool is undyed, or dyed with lichen and juniper bark to colors that blend into the rock: gray, brown, a green so dark it is almost black. Their boots are made of goat hide, soft and quiet on the stone. They wear hoods against the damp, and the hoods are shaped to keep the water from running down their necks. When they stand still, which they do often, they are nearly invisible against a wall or a boulder.

They are not many. The town could hold more—there are empty houses, empty doorframes, hearths gone cold—but the number of people has been the same for as long as anyone can remember. Not by law, but by a kind of unspoken agreement with the place. A family has two children, or three, and one of them leaves, or one of them stays, and the number holds. They do not speak of it as a rule. It is simply the way the town breathes.

Their voices are low and clear, with a quality that carries in the thin air without rising. They do not shout. A mother calls a child by name, and the name reaches the child across three lanes without effort. They speak a dialect that is old and slow, with words for kinds of damp and kinds of stone that no other language has. They have a word for the sound of water dripping onto slate from a roof-edge. They have a word for the gray light that comes before the clouds thin. They have a word for the feeling of a house that has been empty for a generation but is still kept clean.

They are a people of maintenance. Their days are shaped by small, necessary tasks: clearing a channel, re-setting a loose stone, patching a roof, checking the cistern, mending a shutter, feeding the goats, carrying water from the spring to a house whose cistern is low. They do these things slowly and well, and they do them over and over. A wall that was built by a great-grandfather is re-pointed by a grandson with the same moss and clay. A path that has been walked for centuries is swept with a broom of bound twigs. Nothing is allowed to fall into ruin, but nothing is added. The town is kept at exactly the level it has always been.

They are not poor, though they have little. They have enough. The goats give milk and hair and, rarely, meat. The high meadows below the pass give a little grazing, and the women and children gather herbs and juniper berries and the small, hard roots that grow in the thin soil. They trade with the lower country, but not often—salt, iron, a little cloth, a few tools. They have no coin. They trade in things, and they remember debts for a long time. A debt of salt might be repaid by a child of the debtor, years later, and the repayment is accepted without surprise.

They are a people of the hearth. Each house has one, a low stone platform with a smoke-hole above, and the fire is kept small and steady. They burn juniper and dwarf willow and the roots of dead shrubs, and they are careful with every stick. The fire is not for warmth—the damp is too deep for that—but for cooking and for light and for the small comfort of a flame. They sit close to it in the evenings, and they talk, or they do not talk, and the silence is not empty. They mend things by firelight. They tell stories, but the stories are short and often end without a moral. They are stories about the town, about the springs, about the time the cistern overflowed, about the winter the pass was closed for a hundred days. The stories are told the same way every time, and no one interrupts.

They are a people of water. They know every spring, every seep, every basin in the rock. They know which pools are safe to drink from and which are not. They know the sound of the cistern when it is full and the sound when it is low. They can tell by the smell of the air whether the seep behind the meeting-house is running clear or has picked up something from the soil. They do not waste water, but they do not hoard it either. The water belongs to the town, and the town belongs to the water. A stranger who asks for water is given it without question, from the best basin, in a cup of polished stone.

They are a people of stone. They work it with patience and skill, but without pride. A wall is a wall; a step is a step. They do not carve decorations, or if they do, the decoration is so subtle it is almost invisible—a slight curve to a lintel, a pattern of chisel marks that is not quite random, a stone set at a deliberate angle. They do not sign their work. The work is the town, and the town is the work of everyone who has ever lived there. A child learns to shape stone by watching, by trying, by being corrected with a quiet word and a hand on the wrist. By the time a person is old, their hands are as hard as the rock they work, and their eyes can see a flaw in a wall from across the lane.

They are a people of the gray light. They rise with it and sleep with it. In the long summer days, they work longer; in the short winter days, they work less. They do not mark time by hours but by the quality of the light and the sound of the water. They have no clocks, no bells. The meeting-house has a small bronze gong, but it is rung only for deaths and for the rare emergency—a fire, a rockfall, a child lost in the cloud. The gong is old and green with patina, and its sound is low and long, and it carries through the damp like a stone dropped into deep water.

They are a people of the meeting-house. It is the highest building, small and square, with a roof of heavy slate and a door that faces the pass. Inside there are no benches, only a floor of smooth stone and a low platform at one end. They gather there when something must be decided—a dispute over a goat, a question of water rights, a decision about whether to repair a house or let it stand empty. The meetings are short. Someone speaks, and then someone else speaks, and then there is a silence, and then the eldest person present says what will be done. No one votes. The decision is made by the weight of the silence. Afterward, they go back to their houses, and the matter is not spoken of again.

They are a people of the dead. The dead are not buried in the town—the rock is too hard, and the soil too thin—but in a small hollow above the quarry, where the ground is a little deeper. The graves are marked with flat stones, uninscribed, laid level with the ground. The living know who lies where, and they tend the graves the way they tend the paths: clearing the moss, re-setting a stone that has shifted, leaving a small offering of juniper or a cup of water. They do not speak to the dead, but they remember them. A child is told the names of the dead the way other children are told the names of the stars. The dead are part of the town, as much as the cistern and the gate.

They are a people of the pass. The pass is their boundary and their connection. In good weather, a few of them will go down to the lower country to trade, and a few strangers will come up. The strangers find the town strange—too quiet, too damp, too gray—and they do not stay long. The people of the town do not mind. They are polite to strangers, and they give them water and a place by the fire, but they are not sorry when the strangers leave. The town is not for everyone. It is for them, and for the water, and for the stone, and for the long gray light.

They are a people of the edge. They know that the town is at the limit of what the mountain can support, and they live accordingly. They do not dream of growth, of expansion, of more. They dream of the same things they have always dreamed of: a clear spring, a sound roof, a warm hearth, a child who will stay. They are not unhappy. They are not happy either, in the way that happiness is usually meant. They are something else—a kind of settledness, a kind of fit. They belong to the place, and the place belongs to them, and neither has ever asked for more.

If you stay long enough, you begin to see them more clearly. The woman who tends the cistern, checking the overflow with a practiced eye. The old man who sits by the gate in the afternoon, not watching, just sitting. The children who play a quiet game with flat stones, stacking them and knocking them down, their laughter small and bright in the thin air. The young man who is learning to shape a lintel, his teacher standing behind him, saying nothing, only watching. The woman who carries water from the spring to a house where an old woman lives alone, and who does it every day without being asked. The man who mends the path, one stone at a time, and who has been mending it for forty years.

They are not a people of heroes or saints or monsters. They are a people of the ordinary, of the necessary, of the long slow work of keeping a place alive. They are the damp and the stone and the gray light made human. They are the town, and the town is them, and neither would exist without the other.

And if you leave, as you will, and go back down to the lower country, you will find that you miss them, though you never spoke to them, though you never learned their names. You will miss the sound of the water, and the color of the light, and the way they moved through the lanes like shadows, like stones, like the slow breath of the mountain itself. And you will understand, a little, why they stay.
