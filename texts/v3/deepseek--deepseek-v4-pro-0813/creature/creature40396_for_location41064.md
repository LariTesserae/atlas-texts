At first, nothing seems to live in the Water Bell. The description is emphatic: no fish, no snails, no insects, no frogs. The water is biologically silent. The only green is a faint algal stain on the dome and a few drifting strands of water-plant. If you could somehow sit inside the flooded chamber—without breath, without weight—you might wait a long time and decide it was empty.

Then you would begin to notice the center.

Not a creature. A place in the water where the currents do not simply pass. Directly under the shaft’s mouth, slightly shifted toward the eastern drain, the incoming column strikes the tile floor and folds outward. The drain pulls one way. The curved walls return the water another way. Where those forces meet, they wrap around an axis. They make a ring. A slow, fat, nearly vertical ring of water turning inside water.

That ring is the resident.

Call it the Clapper, because the room is a bell. Call it the Tongue, because it is the part of the bell that moves. It is not alive in the biological sense. It has no cells, no metabolism, no nerves. But it has a body. The body is a standing torus of water, shaped by the room’s architecture. It does not wash away. It does not drift to the drain. It stays.

The Clapper is not always the same size. At noon, when the basin above is hot and the water pours down hard, it swells to the size of a human head, maybe larger—an oval of clear, warm water, slightly flattened, turning on a vertical axis. At night, when the flow slackens, it shrinks to the size of a fist. But it does not vanish. Even in the dark, if you could feel the currents, you would feel its tug, like a soft knot in the water’s motion.

It is visible mostly through the things it gathers. When the whirlpool above sends a chain of silver bubbles down the shaft, many of them are caught by the Clapper’s upper eye. They spiral inside it, tracing its curves, making it look for a few seconds like a bracelet of bright beads. Then they ooze out along the dome and rise. Fine sand from the basin is lifted and held in its lower loops, orbiting. Plant strands that enter the room make wide, slow circuits around it, as if it were a center they cannot cross. At noon, the caustic light on the walls seems to converge on the Clapper. It becomes a bright knot in a bright room—pale greenish-gold, like the room’s own sun.

It has a pulse. The drain’s slow gulp compresses it; the shaft’s push expands it. It breathes with the water. When the outflow strengthens, the Clapper tilts east. When the inflow slackens, it rights itself. It wanders a little, never far, always returning. It seems to know the shape of the room. Actually, it is the shape of the room, repeated in moving water. Every tile joint, every hairline crack, every slight unevenness of the plaster is written into its motion. It has no eyes, but it sees the sun as heat and pressure. It has no ears, but it hears the drain as a change in velocity. It has no skin, but it feels the walls as the water feels them—through every eddy and shear.

Its relationship to sound is why it deserves the name Clapper. The room is loud: a low roar from the shaft and dome, a throaty gulp from the drain, the rattle of bubbles. That sound passes through the Clapper. It vibrates. It gives the sound a center. If you pressed your ear to the ground above the basin, you might hear under the water’s noise a slow, regular pulse: the Clapper turning, shaping the roar into something almost like a note. It is not a note you could sing. It is too low, too wet, too full of stone. But it is a note the room could not make without it.

The Clapper is not a visitor. The bubbles leave. The plant strands leave. The sand grains eventually settle or are sucked into the drain. The Clapper remains. It has been there since the first water filled the chamber, since the first column fell through the shaft and found the floor. It is younger than the plaster and older than the algae. It will remain as long as the water flows. If the basin is ever emptied, the Clapper will not die—it will simply stop being. But the room was made to keep water moving, so for now the Clapper is there, spinning, shining, ringing.

There is nothing supernatural in it. It is only water, light, gravity, and a room shaped like a bell. But the room has made a body from its own motion. The body lives there. It is the first and only resident of the Water Bell: a standing torus of warm, sunlit water, turning at the center of the floor, hidden under the bright basin, underground.
