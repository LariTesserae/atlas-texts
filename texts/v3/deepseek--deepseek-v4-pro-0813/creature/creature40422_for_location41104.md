First, there is the water itself, not as a feature but as a resident. The sump’s dark surface is not empty. It is a shared skin: a green film of algae, a scatter of duckweed, the small dimpled paths of water-skaters. These are the first lives you meet, though you may not register them as lives. They live on the surface, in the slick between air and water, and they are as permanent as the brick arches. The water-skaters do not hurry unless the pump’s stroke sends a small shock through the sump. The duckweed drifts and reassembles. The algae is not scum but a working bloom, breathing the light and feeding the snails.

Below them, in the shallows and the runnels, are the snails. They are small, the largest no longer than a thumb joint, their shells the color of wet brick and stained copper. They graze the film from the sides of the flumes and the lower boards. They are not farmed, not even noticed much, but they are part of the district’s slow circulation. In a place with few animals, the snails are the quiet cleanup crew, the ones who keep the green from sealing the water’s skin.

In the dark intake pipe on the east side, the eels live. People know them individually—by their size, their scars, their habits. One is missing a patch of skin from some old grate. Another always lies with its tail just visible when the water is high, as if listening. A third is said to be older than the keeper’s grandmother, though no one has ever measured an eel’s age. They come out at night and cross the sump in long, deliberate curves, not feeding so much as tending the lower water, stirring the silt in ways the pump cannot. The children do not point at them. Pointing at a resident is rude, and the eels have been here longer than most of the brick.

On the weir stands the heron. It is a single bird, grey as the sluice gate, and it can hold so still that the water forgets it is there. It is not a population, not a flock, but a neighbor. When it lands, the children say “the Inspector’s here,” and they leave a clear line between it and the pump house. The heron watches the sump with the patience of the root wall. It does not sing. In Sumpfold, almost nothing sings. The heron’s presence is enough.

Then a child appears on the boardwalk, and you realize the human residents are not separate from this order. The child walks the planks as if they are wide roads, stepping from one known board to another, never on the green ones. They carry a lantern that is not lit, or a coil of rope for the chandler, or nothing. Their feet are bare or wrapped in soft cloth. They know which roots are handrails, which leaves can be walked on, and which green boards are older than the town charter. This is not superstition; it is a map. To a Sumpfold child, the district is a single large body, and they move inside it.

There are only a few dozen human residents, stacked in rooms above the workshops and along the lower galleries. They know each other by the sound of footsteps on different boards, by the way a lantern swings, by the particular knock of a pole against a bridge. They are not a village in the usual sense; they are a crew tending a living machine. Their quarrels are small and mostly about water—whose flume is overflowing, whose root is crowding whose doorway—and they are settled quickly because no one can afford to let a grudge block a runnel.

The keeper lives in the two-story tenement beside the pump house, with their family and perhaps one relative too old to climb. The door is not locked. The keeper is not exactly a person; the keeper is a vigil. There is always someone awake in that tenement, listening for the rhythm to change. The current keeper is a broad-shouldered person with oil-black knuckles and green-stained palms, from grease and algae. They keep a log in a cabinet: lines of figures, small drawings of the sump’s level, notes in a hand that grows steadier as the night goes on. They sleep in the afternoon in a room that vibrates gently with each stroke of the flywheel. When they walk, they do not hurry. The pump’s rhythm is theirs, or theirs is the pump’s.

The keeper’s family is small: a partner who works the boiler, a child who polishes the brass gauge, an old father who can no longer climb but sits by the window and names the sounds—“That’s the intake,” he says, “and that’s the valve coughing.” They do not keep animals. The pump is their animal, and it is never allowed to stop.

Beside the pump house, the workshops are alive with particular people. In the tin shed, the gutterwrights shape copper and tin into flumes and patches. One is older, with a leather apron stiff as wood; one is young, learning to solder a seam without burning the moss packed around the pipe. Their hammers ring in short bursts, then stop, because the work is exact and the metal is thin. They speak in half-sentences. The tin shed is too loud for whole ones.

At the rope-and-chain chandlery, the chandler sits on a stool, splicing rope or working a chain link onto a hook. The smell of hemp and grease mixes with the warm air. The chandler can tell by the feel of a rope whether it has rotted from within, because in Sumpfold everything rots slowly unless it is maintained. They supply the crane, the push-boat, and the tenders’ harnesses.

The boiler repair bay is smaller and hotter. Often it is the keeper’s partner in there, watching the pressure gauge with the same distant attention the keeper gives the flywheel. On some days an apprentice from the tenement sits by the firebox, learning the difference between a hiss and a knock. The steam lines run from the boiler to the pump and the workshops, wrapped in vines as insulation, and the boiler-tender knows every leak by sound.

The push-boat pilot works the water lanes. They are not a figure you see all at once; first you hear the flat push of their pole against the channel bed, then the scrape of a basket being loaded. The boat is little more than a wooden tray with a low bow, carrying cut weed and silt from the sump to the root wall or the compost beds. The pilot moves no faster than the water, and they know which runnels will carry the boat and which will ground it. They call out only when they need a bridge lifted or a rope passed.

Above, on the upper walkways, the tenders prune the roof. They move through the canopy like people moving through a crowded market, with long-handled shears, ropes, and soft-wrapped feet. Each root is an individual to them: the one over the east door that tightens every spring, the one that drinks from the south weir, the one that holds the walkway above the tin shed and must never be cut. They do not garden the place so much as negotiate with it. When they cut a branch, they speak to it, not in worship but in the way a millwright speaks to a machine before lifting a part. Their hands are scarred and sure, and they smell of green sap and wet bark.

The oldest residents do not walk on legs. They are the willows and the banyan-like stems, the roots that have become the wall, the moss packed into joints. A root the thickness of a person’s waist grows up through an iron guide, bends across a lane, and is woven into the roof. It is not a feature; it is a neighbor. The tenders will tell you the root has a hold on the district, and the district has a hold on it. Some roots are handrails, some are beams, some are both. The moss in the joints is not mortar, but it works like mortar, and it is alive.

Ferns grow from gutters and cisterns, and they are tenants in the upper galleries. Their fronds push through gaps in the iron and curl around handrails. They are not cleared unless they block a path. People step around them as they would a sleeping child. Cress and liverwort spread in the lower runnels, not as weeds but as part of the water’s skin. The place is not overgrown; it is inhabited.

At the center of all this is the pump house, and inside it the flywheel, painted dull red, turning slowly. It is not alive, but it is the one resident that never rests. The keeper says the pump has moods, though they mean the packing wears, the rods warm, the seals swell. The flywheel’s stroke is the first sound children learn and the last sound the old hear at night. It is the district’s heart, the thing that lifts water back into the world above. To live in Sumpfold is to live inside its pulse.

At night the keeper’s tenement is not the only place awake, but it is the place where attention gathers. The pump house spills yellow bands across the sump. The tenders come down from the canopy before full dark, because the upper walkways turn black and glossy. The children are called in by a change in the pump’s rhythm, not by a bell. In the tenement, someone sits in a wooden chair by the window, listening. The pump’s rhythm is so regular that any change is a language. A missed beat is an alarm; a faster stroke means silt in the intake; a soft thump means air in the line. The night watcher knows these by heart and wakes the keeper only for the sounds that matter.

Outside, the eels stir. The heron may still be on the weir, a grey cutout against the marsh. The water-skaters are still, or moving with the water’s skin. The snails are at work on the boards. And the plants do most of the living.

You will not meet all of them in one visit. Sumpfold does not present its residents as a census. It offers them by layer: first the green film, then the snails, then the eels, then the heron, then the child on the boardwalk, then the keeper, then the tenders, then the roots. And underneath all of them, the pump, counting out the time. They live not so much in the place as with it—through its water, its roots, its pulsing floor. To live there is to take a place in a slow, waterlogged body and keep it breathing.
