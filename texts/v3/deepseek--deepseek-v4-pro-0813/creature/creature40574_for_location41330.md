To live here is not to settle the place but to find a hold in it. The inhabitants are all shaped by the same two facts: the cliff and the water. Everything either clings, climbs, swims, or flies. The noise is the first thing they learn to read.

The birds are the majority, the loudest and most visible inhabitants. They live in dense vertical cities. Guillemots pack onto ledges so narrow that from a boat they look like black rope wound around the stacks. Kittiwakes paste their nests of weed and grass onto the steepest faces. Puffins burrow into the turf on the broad tops, coming and going with silver fish in their bills. Cormorants stand on low skerries with their wings spread like dark laundry. Black guillemots slip in and out of cave mouths, red feet bright against the stone. Gulls wheel above, arguing. The colonies are not a decoration; they are the dominant society. They quarrel over nesting space, dive through the kelp, and paint the pale limestone white. Their noise is the upper register of the place. When a rockfall thunders, they rise in a single panic, then settle back ledge by ledge, as if the cliff were shrugging them off and then accepting them again.

Below the surface, the population is quieter but denser. The kelp forest rises and falls with the surge, stipes thick as wrists, blades streaming. Fish move in silver threads, turning together. Crabs pick through the holdfasts and along the rounded rubble at the cliff bases. Seals haul out on the low skerries, their bodies like wet boulders, and in the evening they sing—a long, hollow moan that carries across the dark water. They know the channels by feel, not by sight. The whirlpools and standing waves are not dangers to them; they are part of the water’s texture. They dive through the cold, following fish into the sea caves, and return to the skerries to warm themselves in the little sun.

Between the rock and the sea, the small lives fill the gaps. Voles and shrews make runs through the thick moss on the larger tops. The moss is a mattress, dripping and deep, full of springtails and mites. Sundews and cotton grass grow in the dark tarns, catching the midges that cloud in still moments. Bats come out at dusk from the sea caves and work the midge swarms. In the dwarf birch and rowan, small birds that are not seabirds move quietly, hidden from the gulls. The lichen and moss are the oldest inhabitants, patient and silent, dissolving the stone a little at a time.

The humans are few. They live as if the place has allowed them a few ledges and a few machines to tend, not as owners. There are perhaps four, maybe five, spread across the stacks. They call each other by the names of their stations—Turbine, Cable, Boat, Listener—or by no names at all. They are weathered to the same gray as the limestone, their hands cracked and blackened at the knuckles, their clothes stiff with salt and oil. They speak little, because the noise leaves little room for talk. They communicate by radio at set times, in short bursts, and by the position of a boat or the smoke from a chimney.

The turbine-keeper lives on the broad stack with the hydroelectric station, in the hut with the small chimney that smokes on wet mornings. She tends the penstock and the small turbine. She is the one who hears the change in the thrum—a leaf in the intake, a worn bearing, the tide turning—before anyone else. She climbs the stone steps every day, even in rain, to check the tarn and the sluice. She knows which steps are slick with moss, which one is loose, which disappear underwater at high tide. Her hands smell of grease and kelp. She eats fish stew and dried bread, and she keeps a tide table nailed by the door, marking the days with charcoal. At night, if the generator misses a beat, she is awake before the light fails.

The cable-man lives near the winch house, below the sagging cable that sings in the wind. He rides the steel basket between stacks, carrying supplies and sometimes people. He greases the pulleys, replaces the worn splices, and watches the cable sway. He has a limp from a fall years ago, and because of it he is careful with heights. He talks to the gulls in low sounds while he works. He can judge the tide by the angle of the water against a particular arch, and he never crosses during a storm. He knows which ledges hold, which are rotten, which will fall in the next decade.

The boat-runner is the one who moves horizontally through the maze. He keeps a small open boat with a diesel engine that coughs when the tide turns, then steadies. He delivers fuel, food, medicine, and the occasional letter between the stacks. He has capsized twice, and now he reads the water as a body of moving muscle. He knows the whirlpools that open and close without pattern, the standing waves where two channels meet, and the places where the current can take a boat off course. He times his arrivals to the stone steps that appear at low tide. He is younger than the others, but the sea has already made him careful. He hums when the mist closes in, a small sound under the noise.

The listener keeps the radio repeater and the navigation lights. She lives in the highest hut, where the view opens over the whole labyrinth. She records the weather, the tides, the bird colonies, the rockfalls. She knows each light’s flash pattern—red and white, reflected in the moving water—and can tell if a bulb is failing from the change in its rhythm. She fills notebooks with tiny handwriting: the first puffins, the date the kelp began to bleach, the fall of a new arch. She seldom goes down to the water, but from her ledge she sees the whole maze and how it changes. She is not a scientist; she is a rememberer. The others bring her supplies, and she gives them the weather.

The machines, too, are inhabitants of a kind. The wind turbine on the high stack turns with the sound of a slow heart. The diesel generator coughs and settles into a low thrum. The pump clanks somewhere in the rock. They are not alive, but they have rhythms, and the humans tend them as they might tend animals—listening for the sound of a failing bearing, greasing the moving parts, keeping the salt out. The navigation lights are their eyes, red and white over the dark water. The old rail track ending in air, the rusted winches, the abandoned quarries and mine adits: these are not inhabited by people, but they are inhabited by memory, by moss, by the sea slowly taking them back.

All of them together—birds, seals, voles, humans, machines—are not a community in the usual sense. They do not cooperate. They share the same verticality, the same water, the same noise. They have each found an answer to the question the place asks: how to hold on. The tide rules. The rain comes several times a day. The limestone dissolves a little. And still they go on, clinging and swimming and flying, in the cold silver light of the drowned plateau.
