First, the plateau seems empty in the way a closed factory is empty. But emptiness is not the same as absence. The inhabitants are here, but they are so thoroughly made of the same grayness that you do not see them until you have learned to see gray against gray.

The first ones you notice are the smallest. They are not announced by movement; they are announced by a change in the texture of the stone. At your feet, what looked like ash or dust shifts. The ground is not paved with mineral grit alone; it is paved with insects. They are wingless, pale as candle wax, soft-bodied, each no longer than a fingernail clipping. They do not scatter. They move with the same slowness as the drip of condensation. When you breathe close, they turn into the wind, but they do not panic. They feed on shed hair and dung, on the bodies of their own dead, on the crust of rime. They are the first layer of the plateau’s living floor.

Then the smell reaches you—ammoniac and warm fur, an animal smell, though it seems to come from the fog itself. You raise your eyes. The gray shifts. A herd is crossing the plateau. It was there all along, but your mind had filed it under *hills*.

Now they emerge: heavy-limbed, pale, each one the color of old ivory or wet bone. They are built low and wide, with broad feet that spread like pads on the stone, toes splayed as if the animal is always walking on mud. Their heads are narrow, their mouths rasp sideways against the lichen; their breath rises in straight columns until it joins the cloud deck. They are not cattle, though something about their shoulders suggests a bison. They are not musk oxen, though their pelts hang in the same oily ropes. They are a thing the plateau made, slow and patient, too heavy to be elegant but too quiet to be clumsy.

The herd is so numerous that it is a landscape. The gaps between animals are smaller than the animals themselves, and the animals are the size of boulders. They move at the speed of a shadow across a wall. If you watch the horns, they seem still; if you look away, they have shifted. They communicate by sound too low for your ears, a vibration that comes up through the soles of your boots. It is not a call; it is a shared direction, a slow magnetic consensus. One animal turns, then the ones around it turn, and the turn ripples through the herd like wind through grass, except there is no grass and no wind.

If there is a *who* on this plateau, it is not the single animal but the herd. An individual grazer is only a mouth, a stomach, a pair of broad feet. The herd is the true creature: a slow, diffuse body that stretches across the horizon and breathes with a thousand lungs. It has no head, no leader, no edge. When it turns, it turns all at once, as if the plateau itself had decided to face another way.

They are the reason the fog follows. Their breath seeds it. Each exhalation adds a cup of warm water to the air, and a herd of thousands makes its own cloud. The fog lies against them like a second skin. It hides them from the cold and from whatever might watch. Their dung and shed hair fall into the insect carpet, which receives it without sound. Their broad feet break the rime, exposing the lichens for the next mouth. They carry salts from one mineral pan to another in their blood and urine. They are not just animals; they are the plateau’s conveyor belt.

Then you see the predators. You almost don’t. They are long and low, the color of wet slate, and they move through the herd as a current moves through still water. They are not wolves; their legs are too short, their bodies too supple, their eyes too far back. They have the look of something that has given up speed for patience. They do not chase. They drift between the legs of the grazers, inspecting the young and the old, waiting for a sign of weakness. The herd does not panic. It parts around a predator the way stone parts around a flaw. There is no sound: no snarl, no bark, no alarm. Only a faint brush of hide and the click of a horn against another horn.

Every few days, in the fog, a calf or a spent elder lies down and does not rise. The predators gather not in a frenzy but in a quiet circle, and the fog takes the event. By the time the herd has moved on, the insects are already at work on what remains. There is no bone pile. The bones themselves are rasped and weathered into salts.

Under the mineral pans, there is a life even smaller. The iridescent sheen on the water is not just iron and copper; it is a bloom of bacteria that breathe metal the way you breathe oxygen. They concentrate the dissolved stone into films of rust and blue-green. When the pan dries, they go dormant. When the seep returns, they bloom again. They are the plateau’s true chemists. They do not move, but they live everywhere the water lingers.

The lichens are not one creature. They are a congress of algae and fungus, sometimes three or four kinds folded together. They hold the rock. They eat light so slowly that a square foot of lichen may be older than the animal that steps over it. The grazers crop them, but not to the root. The lichens grow back at the speed of geological patience.

There are no people here. That is not a lack; it is a condition. A person would need a fire, a shelter, a midden, a story. The plateau has none of these. But it has a kind of society. The insects are the floor, the lichens the walls, the herds the roof. The predators are the only engineers that do not build. The fog is the only door. The inhabitants do not announce themselves, but they are everywhere, as dense as the silence.

Sit long enough and you become one of the inhabitants. The insects crawl over your boots and begin to clean them. The fog condenses on your coat and drips. A calf, pale as a mushroom, walks within arm’s reach, looks at you with an eye that has no whites, and then moves on. It does not see you as a threat. You are only another slow shape in the gray, another warm body breathing moisture into the cloud. The herd shifts while you are not looking. The plateau resumes its quiet, patient work.
