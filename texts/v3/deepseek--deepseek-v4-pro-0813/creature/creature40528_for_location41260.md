The first thing to understand is that nobody lives in the Green Municipality.

Then the next thing: the place is not empty. Not at all. It is so full that the emptiness takes a shape, and that shape is alive.

What lives there is vegetable, not animal. It is the city itself, but the city has not yet decided whether it is one creature or many. It leans toward many, then folds back into one.

The first of the inhabitants you notice is a root-walker. It comes across the leaf-paved plaza not by walking as you walk but by leaning into itself: a tuber the size of a dog, gray-green, corky, with four thick root-legs that swell at the joints and take hold of the ground. Each step is a slow hydraulic event—one leg fills with sap, unfurls, anchors, then the body pulls after it and the leg behind drains to a husk. It leaves a trail of fine root-hairs that stand up for a moment in the damp air and then lie down and become part of the pavement. It has no head, no face, no direction, but it is going somewhere; or maybe somewhere is going through it.

After that, you see the vine-crawlers. They are the city’s blood and its masons. Long ropes of stem, some as thin as thread, some as thick as a torso, slide along walls and across the ground. They move by growing at the tip and letting the old growth die behind them, so they are always leaving themselves. Where they pause, they weave: a vine-crawler can stitch a wall closed out of its own body, or unstitch a gutter and turn it into a stairway. They do not build with hands; they are the hands and the building. They fill the alleys with a wet, creaking noise, like a ship’s rigging if the rigging were alive and dreaming.

Then the seed-things arrive in a wave. They are hulls and kernels with twisted awns like coiled springs, and they move by humidity. When the air thickens after rain, the awns untwist; when it thins, they coil again. The result is a kind of walking, or tumbling, or flinching, and in the hour after a shower the gutters are full of them, skittering uphill in small migrations. Some are no bigger than a knuckle, some are the size of a crate, and they clatter against each other with a sound like wooden teeth. They gather in the market stalls, not to buy, but to split. You can see them lined along the leaf-stall counters, shell cracking, a pale root emerging and tasting the air, then planting itself exactly where it stands. In a morning the market becomes a nursery; by evening the nursery becomes another lower story of the city.

Above them, the leaf-winged drifters wheel. They are samaras, but their wings are living leaves that flex at the midrib. They rise on the city’s own breath—the warm water vapor and pollen—and turn in slow spirals around the sap-cistern towers. They cast no shadow, because light passes through them. They land on high balconies, furl their wings, and root in the cracks between twig and membrane. They look like birds from a distance, but there are no birds; the sky is a garden traffic of green wings.

The trap-leaves are the ones that seem most like animals, and therefore most like absence. They hang from the edges of doorways and along the boulevards, green lips with inner surfaces the color of wet pith. When your shadow falls across one, it snaps shut with a gasp. There is no fly, no insect, no prey; but the plant has remembered a pressure that was never here, or that is still coming. It holds the shape of hunger without the mouth. After a moment it opens again, as if embarrassed, and you see that the inside is full of nothing but water and light.

There are others. The sap-bladders are public fountains and also bodies: translucent green spheres that hang from axils and swell until they burst, slaking the root-roads. The room-growers are plants that build rooms around themselves and then become the room. The stair-roots are roots that grow in spirals and call themselves staircases. The pollen-chamber trees are civic buildings that breathe out golden clouds in sudden, rhythmic gales. All of them are inhabitants, and all of them are the city. They do not walk the streets; they are the streets walking. They do not open the doors; they are the doors waiting.

In the old quarter, the root-walkers are large and dark, with bark ridged like the face of a mountain, and they move so slowly that to them the city may be a blur of its own growth. In the new quarter, the inhabitants are still bright green and pulsing, their root-legs still curled inside their seed-cases, their leaf faces not yet unfolded. They crowd the gaps and push against the older walls, and by the next morning the older walls have leaned away and made room.

The deepest truth is that there is no one and no thing that lives in the Green Municipality except the municipality itself. It is not a collection of plants; it is one plant that has learned to be a crowd. Every seed-walker, every vine-crawler, every trap-leaf and leaf-wing is a moment of its body, a temporary organ that will grow, split, and become another street, another tower, another market of itself. If you stand long enough, it will make you an inhabitant too—not by eating you, but by growing you into the architecture, rootlets through your laces, a green room where your breath joins the transpiration. And then the only sound will be the leaves, and the only movement will be growth, and the only citizen will be the city, speaking to itself in the language of splitting cells.
