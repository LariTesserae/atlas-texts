At first the plateau seems to be the only thing that lives here: bare rock, thin air, a horizon that bends away instead of meeting the sky. Then the smoke resolves. It is not a single plume but a thousand thin blue-gray threads rising from holes in the cliff. Each thread is a household, an animal pen, a fat lamp, a dung fire. The smoke is the first sign of the Kessilim, the people of the Seam.

They do not live on the plateau, and they do not live at the bottom of the rift. They live inside it, across it, up and down it. Their city is a vertical village, a saturation of life in the one place where water, shelter, and warmth meet. From the rim they are almost invisible. From inside they are everywhere.

The Kessilim are not a separate kind of human, but the altitude has shaped them. They are broad in the chest and short in the waist, with wide nostrils and lips that crack in the dry air. Their hands are thick-knuckled from stone and cold. They move slowly, not from weariness but from economy; every step on a narrow ledge is a decision. Their clothing is many layers of wool and hide in the colors of the rock—gray, black, rust, lichen-green—so that at rest they can be mistaken for part of the cliff. They oil their skin with animal fat against the wind. Their names are short, made to be spoken low: Yren, Omal, Sess, Keth, Tov, Ama.

They have no grand facades. Their city is accumulation: a new room set above an older one, a stair cut beneath a granary, an animal pen placed over a dwelling so that its warmth sinks down through the floor. Doorways are low and narrow. The door itself is usually a curtain of heavy felt, because felt cannot slam. Wood is so scarce that a single piece of driftwood may be kept as an heirloom. Ceilings are corbelled or made of small stones fitted with patience. Rooms are cut into the rock and fronted with drystone; roofs are slabs of slate or stretched hide.

A household is arranged around heat. Fire is made from dried dung and fat, and it is too precious to waste. They cook with hot stones dropped into leather pots. The animals live above the people, in pens built over the roof, and their warmth sinks through the stone into the rooms below. The smell of animals, dung smoke, wet wool, and old fat is the smell of continuing. To the Kessilim it is not unpleasant. It is the smell of home.

The long-wooled camelids are the city's wealth. They are herded on the plateau in slow, ragged groups, nibbling cushion plants and lichens. They are tended by children and old people wrapped against the wind. Their wool is the city's main medium: cloth, rope, bridge cables, felt, blankets, bags, slings, inheritance. The animals are not often killed; their life is worth more than their meat. When one dies, nothing is wasted. Sinew for lashing, bone for hooks and needles, hide for water bags, meat for a rare feast.

The rock-pigs are different. They are small, bristly, slate-backed, with clever trotters and a low, percussive grunt. They live in dark stone pens, fed on scraps, moss, and the leavings of the camelids. They are the city's engines of heat. Their bodies warm the stone; their dung is dried on sunny ledges and burned in small braziers. Children turn the dung cakes. It is a task for small hands and still minds.

The birds are not owned, but they are part of the city's life. Black chough-like birds nest in the crevices, descending to pick at scraps. Gray finches and rock doves move among the ledges. Pikas and rock rats rustle in the talus. At dusk, bats pour from the high caves like smoke; at dawn they funnel back. In the river, dark fish are caught in stone weirs on the lower terraces. A bearded vulture may hang over the rim for an hour without moving, its shadow crossing and recrossing the city. The Kessilim do not call it a god, but they honor it. It is the one who carries weight upward.

The city is divided by light and water more than by law. The lower city, near the river, is for leatherworking, dyeing, washing, and fish traps. It lives in blue shadow; breath fogs there even in summer, and water freezes in the basins overnight. The workers there know how to cure hide in cold water, how to keep dye from freezing, how to rebuild a weir after a rockfall. The middle terraces are for living and storage—granaries of wool, dried meat, fat, and salt. The upper terraces and rim, where the sun is strongest, are for animals, drying wool, and the households that can afford the light. But status is not simple. The upper terraces are warm, but they must haul water farther. The lower city has water at its feet but cold in its bones.

Water is assigned by family. Each spring and cistern has a keeper, an office passed down by memory and marriage. The keeper measures the seep with a knotted cord or a row of marked bones. She knows which joint of the cliff is weeping and which has gone dry. The fog-frames are inherited. They are frames of woven wool stretched to catch the dawn fog. The best ones are made by old weavers who know how to angle the fibers to the wind. A good frame can fill a small basin by midmorning. These frames are dowries, gifts, and debts. To steal water from another family's frame is a serious crime, punished by exile to the rim for a season, which can be death.

Weaving is the city's mind. The women and men who weave know the tension of a bridge cable, the give of a felt door, the softness of a child's swaddling. The bridges across the canyon are made of braided wool and leather. They sway underfoot; a single person's weight bends them in the middle. To cross, you move slowly, hand over hand, with the river far below. The bridges are re-woven every few years, and the old cables are unbraided and respun into smaller things. Nothing is wasted. A bridge is not a monument; it is a knot that must be retied.

Bronze is known and treasured, but rare. A bronze needle may pass through four generations, its shaft worn to a sliver. Most tools are stone and bone—scrapers, awls, fishhooks, loom weights, needles of bone. There is no wheeled cart. Goods move on backs—human, animal, or rope.

Silence is both custom and necessity. Sound carries far in thin air and loose stone. Loud noise is thought to disturb the animals and shame the speaker. People speak in low voices, almost in each other's ears. They have a hand-sign for distance, a small language of economical gestures: *water*, *rockfall*, *enough*, *send down*, *come up*, *danger*. Children learn it before they learn to shout. To rush is to waste breath and risk a fall.

The loudest event is rockfall: a single sharp crack, a scatter of echoes, then silence again. When it happens, everyone stops. They count the echoes. If the echoes stop too soon, it means something has landed on a terrace or a bridge, and search parties set out.

Consider the water keeper at the third spring. Her name is Omal, and she is missing two fingers on her left hand from a frostbite winter. She keeps the cistern's tally on a cord of knots, and she can taste the water and know which joint of the basalt it came from. In the morning, she wrings the fog-frames herself, because she trusts no one else with the angle of the wind. She sings to the water, not a song but a low breathing pattern, as if coaxing it down the rock.

Consider the boy on the rim. His name is Tov, and he tends the camelids with his grandmother. He is nine, maybe, or eleven; age is counted by winters and by how many bridges he has helped reweave. He carries a sling of braided wool, but he rarely uses it. He watches for the shadow of the bearded vulture and for cracks in the stone. He knows the names of the pikas in the talus, not because they are pets but because he has watched them for years. At dusk he leads the animals down to the upper terraces, and the city's smoke rises to meet him.

Consider the bridge weaver. Her name is Sess, and she is strong in the hands and forearms. She sits on a ledge with a bundle of wool and leather, braiding a new suspension cable. Around her, the city's sounds are the drip of water and the creak of leather. She works slowly, testing each cord's tension with her teeth and her shoulder. She will not finish this bridge today, or this season. It is enough that the work is going on.

Consider the leatherworker in the lower city. His name is Keth, and his hands are stained dark from tannins and river cold. He works in blue shadow, where his breath fogs. He knows that the river's rising can destroy a winter's work, and he builds his curing vats above the flood line. He trades hides to the upper terraces for wool and dung, and his wife keeps a small garden of bitter greens in a stone trough watered by hand.

Consider the elder who remembers the earthquake. Her name is Ama, and she lives in the middle terraces with three generations of her family. She was a child when the last great shaking came. She remembers the sound of the terraces cracking and the bridges dropping into the rift. She has a row of notches on the door lintel for every year since. She says the city rebuilds because it has no choice. The people are not brave, only attached. "The stone breaks," she says, "but it breaks slowly enough for us to learn its seams."

A day in Kessil is a long, slow breath. At dawn, the fog comes, and the water keepers are first awake. They wring the frames and fill the basins. The camelids are led out to the plateau. The rock-pigs are fed. The smoke begins. By midmorning, the sun reaches the upper terraces, and the weavers and leatherworkers are at their tasks. The lower city remains in shadow, but it is awake: the river's hush is louder there. At noon the sky is a deep blue, almost violet, and the shadows are so cold they feel like a different season. People rest in the sun on the upper ledges, unmoving, like animals. In the afternoon, the wind rises. The plateau shows its weather, a gray curtain of needle-rain that may or may not reach the rift. The herders watch it and decide whether to bring the animals down early. At dusk, the bats pour from the high caves, and the cooking fires are lit. The camelids return, humming. The rock-pigs settle in their pens, their warmth rising through the floors. At night, the city is never fully dark. The fat lamps are tiny, but there are hundreds of them, and the river catches their light. The stars are very bright above the rim, and the cold comes down from the plateau like a slow animal.

The Kessilim do not bury their dead. There is no soil for graves. Instead, they wrap the dead in old wool and carry them to the rim, where the bearded vultures and the cold take them. The bones are later gathered and laid in crevices, where they become part of the cliff. This is not a rite of despair. It is a recognition that the city's dead remain in the stone. To see a vulture hanging over the rim is to see the city's memory lifting.

Change comes slowly, and the Kessilim trust that slowness. The rock is young and still breaking. Frost works into the joints of the basalt and splits off slabs. Each spring, the people clear fresh scree from the lower paths and use it for building. The river rises a little with snowmelt, then falls. Earthquakes, perhaps once in a generation, crack terraces and shake down bridges. The city rebuilds. New rooms are cut a generation at a time. A bridge is re-woven every few years. The fog comes every morning, the ice melts every noon. The people of Kessil are not ambitious; they are persistent. They live in the one place where water, shelter, and warmth meet, and they have learned to be small enough and numerous enough to hold it.

There is nothing uncanny about them. They are a high, cold, crowded people, built into a wound in the earth, full of animals and wool and water. They are the ones who wait for the next rockfall, and when it comes, they will stop, count the echoes, and begin again.
