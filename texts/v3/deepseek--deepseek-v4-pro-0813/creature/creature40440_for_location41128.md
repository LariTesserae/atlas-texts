**The Knotted People**

You ask who lives there. The question is not wrong, but it is incomplete. The first thing you must understand is that Mossgrip does not have inhabitants the way a house has occupants. It has inhabitants the way a reef has inhabitants: uncountable, intertwined, impossible to separate from the structure itself. The moss lives there. The swifts live there, plastering their mud cups to the undersides of the bridges. The frogs live there, thousands of them, their calls a metallic tide at dusk. The moss-squirrels live there, running the ropes like tiny acrobats. The long-tailed mice swim the water channels as easily as they climb. The trees, the ferns, the bitter tubers, the watercress on the terraces—they live there, and they are not subordinate to the people.

And then, yes, the people live there. They are not the rulers of Mossgrip; they are one voice in a dense chorus. They call themselves the Weft, or the Knotted, or sometimes simply the Tied. They are a people of ropes and calloused palms, of ears tuned to creaking tension, of feet that can read a wet knot in the dark. They are not easily seen from the rim. At first, you see only movement—a flicker of a hand through a curtain of fern, a child’s shape on a suspended walkway. Then, gradually, the movement resolves.

Take the girl on the eastern terrace. Her name is Nettle. She is twelve, or near twelve; no one keeps precise count. She wakes in a chamber grown from five dwarf trees, their trunks coaxed into walls, their crowns interlaced overhead. The floor of her room is not a floor but a platform of woven root, lashed to the living branches. Her mattress is a mat of sphagnum, damp but warm. The light comes through the leaves in green patches. The sound of the waterfall is so constant that she no longer hears it; she hears instead the small changes in it, the way a person hears a heartbeat and knows when it quickens.

Nettle’s first task of the day is to whistle away the moss-squirrels from the granary platform. She climbs a rope ladder, her toes finding the knots without looking. The rope is swollen with dew, and she grips it with the ease of long practice. At the granary, she takes up her post with a handful of children. They whistle in high, complex patterns, imitating the hooded swift, the frog’s alarm call, the wind in the sedge. The moss-squirrels scatter, chittering, their fur dark with moisture. One of them pauses on a beam and looks at Nettle with bright, unafraid eyes. She whistles again. It flicks its tail and vanishes. There is no hatred in the gesture, only a negotiation of space. A nest is a room; a granary is a lure; the children are the shepherds of the ropeways.

Later, Nettle is sent down to the water terraces to help the water-wardens. The terraces are stepped pools, each holding water that spills from the one above through a hollowed alder trunk. The water is cloudy with silt and alive with the jelly of frog eggs. The wardens are there, two of them, standing waist-deep in the seep line. One is Silt, a woman with pale skin and wet hair like eelgrass. She is clearing a blocked sluice with her bare fingers, lifting out mats of leaves and egg clusters. She does not throw them aside; she carries them to a safe pool above the channel, still talking to them under her breath. Nettle wades in after her. The water is cold, but not freezing; it is the steady coolness of high altitude and permanent cloud. Nettle’s fingers find the blockage—a knot of twigs, a sheath of shed snake skin, a clump of eggs. She works it free. The water pulses through. Silt gives her a nod. The frogs, disturbed, begin to trill again, louder than before.

The people of Mossgrip know each other by their knots. Not the knots on their clothes, but the knots in the town that they are bound to tend. A knot-holder is not a landowner; land cannot be owned because it is not separate from the living fabric. A knot-holder is a caretaker, a tension-reader, a binder. Runnel, who holds the western rope-ladder system, passes Nettle on the high bridge. She is a young woman with rope-callused palms and a spiral scar on her left hand from a failed splice. She walks her territory every day, gliding her hand along the wet fibers, feeling for weakness. If a knot fails, it is her shame; if a child falls, she will not sleep for a season. The law says you may pull only along the grain of living wood, and Runnel obeys as if the wood were her own sinew. She and Nettle exchange a high-pitched call of greeting. The call is not words; it is a sound that carries over the waterfall’s roar, a rising note that means *I see you, you are on the right rope.*

The old man who tends the longhouse is called Orib. He is as gnarled as the roots he cares for, and his voice is so soft that the frogs seem to answer when he speaks. He carries a copper knife in a sheath of oiled bark, but he uses it rarely, and always at a knot, never against the grain. He knows the name of every tree in the longhouse, and he claims that some of them are older than the settlement itself. When he walks the central floor, the grown trunks creak as if greeting him. He can hear a lashing fail before it breaks; he will stop mid-sentence, tilt his head, and say, “The north beam is tired. Go tighten it.” And he is always right.

At midday, a squall rolls over the rim. The temperature drops. The rain hits hard enough to silence the frogs for a breath. The waterfall doubles, turns the stream brown, and sends a pulse of water through the channels. Nettle hears the call go up—not a cry of alarm, but a complex series of whistles and claps, the town’s alarm system. Children run along the ropeways beating wooden clappers. Adults appear in doorways. Nettle runs to the sluices. She knows her place: the second sluice on the north terrace. She wades in, feeling the water rise against her shins, and works the wooden gate. Beside her, an old man she does not know works another sluice. They do not speak; they move in rhythm. The water has the right of way, and they are not fighting it—only persuading it, giving it a path it already wants to take. A wall of moss shrugs off a sheet of water. Then the squall passes, and the frogs resume louder than before. The whole town steams in the wet afterlight.

Nettle’s grandmother is a net-mender. She sits in the doorway of their grown chamber, her fingers moving with a speed that belies her age. She is reweaving a bird-net, the kind used to gather fledglings from the walls of houses when they fall too early. The net is made of sinew and bark fiber. Nettle watches her for a while, then sits beside her and takes up a coil of wet rope. They work in silence, the only sounds the creak of the house and the endless chitter of birds. The grandmother teaches without words: she adjusts Nettle’s hand, shows her the proper tension, clicks her tongue when a knot is too loose. A small animal—a pika-like creature the size of a fist—sits on the beam above, watching them. It has a bit of dried moss in its mouth, stolen from the bedding. Nettle throws it a piece of dried fish. It catches it, vanishes, then reappears a moment later, still hungry.

At night, when the rain stops for an hour and the clouds part, the moon turns the wet ropes to silver. The whole cirque glitters with the eyes of frogs. Nettle is mending the bird-net by lamp-light. The lamp is a small clay dish of fish oil, its flame trembling in the wet black. The waterfall keeps its deep note. The ropes creak in the wind. The pika sits on the beam above, watching. Nettle’s fingers move automatically, tying the knots that will hold the net against the weight of birds. She is not thinking about the fact that she is inside a room made of roots, in a house that grew from a tree, in a town that is tied together by knots. She is only thinking about the next loop, the next pull, the next breath of the place that holds her.

And that is the answer. The people of Mossgrip are not separate from the moss and the frogs and the creeping wood. They are the town’s hands, its senses, its nervous system. They are the ones who tie the knots, but the knots also tie them. They live inside an animal city that tolerates them, but more than that: they have become animal themselves, woven into the wet excess, too many and too small and too much alive to be anything but a part. To ask who lives there is to ask who the waterfall lives for. The question answers itself: the place lives, and they are its limbs. They are the Weft, the Knotted, the Tied. They are the one thread that ties, and they are tied by the place.
