At first you think the plateau is uninhabited. It is not. The inhabitants are not animals, and they are not plants. They are made of the same things as the landscape, and they have been there all along, moving too slowly and too electrically for the eye to catch.

The first one you see is not a thing at all. It is a hesitation in the heat-shimmer—a place where the air seems to have a spine. You only notice it because the metallic branches near it are leaning, not toward the sun, but toward that point of stilled shimmer. Then the shimmer thickens. Dust rises from the stone, not in a cloud but in a slow, deliberate spiral, and a shape gathers: a column of iron filings, black silica, and bornite, held together by a magnetic field that makes the hair on your arms stand up. It is not a body. It is a weather system of metal, a fog that has remembered how to stand.

It has no face, but it has a head of sorts—a node of violet corona at its top, where the charge density is highest. Copper filaments run through it like exposed wiring. It stands over a terrace of iron nodules, and as it stands there, the nodules reorient themselves one by one, as if being counted. This is how it thinks: not in nerves, but in alignments. A thought is a pattern of grains. A decision is a new alignment of a thousand tiny compass needles. They are not intelligent in the way you are intelligent; they are intelligent the way a crystal is intelligent, knowing exactly where every atom belongs.

They are the plateau’s only residents, and they are numerous. Each metallic grove has its own pattern, its own slow mind. Some are broad sheets of potential that drift along the terraces like fog. Others are thin, bright arcs that leap between the crystalline needles, stitching the air with tiny lightning. They are not separate species; they are the same kind of thing—current-forms—expressed differently by different charge gradients. The ones in the copper groves are quick and bright, full of violet sparks. The ones on the sulfur terraces are slow and pale yellow, moving like curtains of dust. The ones in the black silica pavements are nearly invisible; you feel them as a pressure in your teeth.

They do not eat. They feed on the voltage between the hot rock and the dry air. That voltage never much changes, so they never fully sleep. They grow more distinct when a fissure exhales richly and the metallic fog thickens; they soften when the fog thins, but the difference is small. Their lives are not measured in heartbeats but in cycles of accumulation and discharge. They do not reproduce; they seed patterns. A charge-form may press itself into a growing frond, and the frond will branch in a new way, carrying that pattern for centuries. The frond is both their child and their memory.

They communicate in the hiss of corona discharge, in radio sferics, in slow pulses of magnetic field that pass through the stone. To you it is a faint prickle on the skin, a metallic taste in the mouth, a sound like the sea if the sea were made of static. Their conversations can last for years. A single sentence might be a shift in the alignment of a thousand iron grains. They are not in a hurry. Time, for them, is not a river; it is a gradient along which they drift.

If you stand very still, one will come to you. It will not see you as a person. It will see you as an anomaly: a mass of conductive fluid, warm, wet, electrically noisy. It will hover at the edge of your own small electric field, testing you with a tendril of dust that does not quite touch your skin. It will find you too soft, too unstable, too full of water. Not part of the circuit. Eventually it will lose interest and flow back into the terraces. You will be left with the smell of ozone and the echo of a hiss. They leave no tracks, because they are not separate from the dust they move through. They are the only moving parts, and they are barely moving.

They are the reason the factory is not empty. They are the maintenance and the maintained, the current that follows the wiring, the thought that follows the current. The landscape makes them, and they make the landscape, not by building but by being its equilibrium made visible. They are what lives here: the mineral minds of a world that has not yet learned to be alive, and may never need to.
