There is one who lives there, but you would not notice them at first. The place is too busy with its own breathing. You would notice the signs instead: a dent in the root-floor by the piston, as if a body has lain there so long the wood has remembered its shape. A cup hammered from a brass fitting hanging on a twig by the canal. A row of seed pods drying on a warm pipe, sorted by size, their tips all pointed the same way. These are not the marks of an animal. They are the marks of a keeper, if the keeper has become kept.

The one who lives there is called the Tender, though no one says the name aloud. They are not easy to see whole. First you see the hand: long fingers, each wrapped in a thin spiral of living vine, the nails thick and yellow like horn or petiole. Then the arm: it is a human arm, but the skin has gone bark-gray at the shoulder, and there is a bruise-colored leaf growing from the elbow. The face is a map of places: one side still soft and pale, with a deep crease at the mouth; the other side is bark, deeply furrowed, with a cluster of small white flowers at the temple. One eye is a dark human eye, moist and slow. The other is a pressure gauge, brass and glass, the needle trembling at the low green mark. They breathe with a sound like a valve hissing, and when they speak, if they ever do, it is in syllables that sound like water dripping onto hot metal.

They live in the room behind the huge piston, in a hollow where the roots have grown into a curved chamber. The floor is packed with dried moss and the shed fibers of their own coat. There is a bed of sorts: a wide, shallow bowl of woven reeds, lined with warm, dry leaves that smell of engine oil and mint. Over it, a copper pipe runs, giving off a faint, constant heat. That is where they sleep, curled on their side with their cheek against the roots, one hand resting on a valve, the other over their chest. The bird nests in the bend of that pipe, and the pale rat sleeps under the bed, and the beetle lives in the pocket of their coat.

They wake not to an alarm but to the first change in the piston’s rhythm: a slow, deep thump that travels up through the roots into their ribs. They rise slowly, joints creaking like green wood, and they drink from a cupped leaf that has filled with dew overnight. Then they begin the day’s work, which is not work in the way we think. It is more like grooming, or digestion. They move from machine to machine, touching the bark, pressing an ear to the hollow stems, listening for the tick of a clog. They turn a valve with a twist of their wrist; the vine around the valve tightens in response. They feed a handful of pale pulp into a chute made of folded leaf, and the machine sighs steam in thanks.

They do not rush. The place does not rush. The piston thumps once every three breaths, and the Tender matches it. When they walk, they place each foot as though the ground is a body they do not wish to bruise. They whisper to the plants in a language with no words—just low hums and clicks made with their tongue against the roof of their mouth, like the sound of a gear engaging. The leaves shift to listen. Sometimes a vine reaches out and touches their face, and they bow their head, letting it trace the line of their jaw. They are not a master of the place. They are not even its voice. They are a hand on a valve, a warm spot in the bark.

Their body is a small version of the world. If you were to see them unclothed, you would see that the bark grows not as a shell but as a weave: it has entered the skin, and the skin has entered it. Their chest is crossed by a lattice of fine root-hairs that disappear into the ribs. At the base of the throat there is a brass collar, not worn but embedded, and from it a thin tube runs down into the arm, where it joins a vein of green sap. On their back, along the spine, a row of tiny leaves grows in a line, like a fern unrolling. They oil their own joints with sap from a cut on the thumb. They are not wounded. They are not suffering. They are in equilibrium, like the place itself.

Their diet is plain: they eat the pale pulp that comes off the conveyor, mixed with canal water and a paste made from seeds they grind between two stones. Sometimes they catch a drop of oil from a brass spigot and take it on their tongue, like medicine. They drink tea made from the petals of the steam-flowers, bitter and green. They share scraps with the rat, and the rat leaves a small, polished stone at the foot of the bed in return. They do not eat the beetle, nor the bird. They are not hungry.

The place is a machine for moving water, and the Tender is part of its water. They carry buckets of canal water to the roots that are too dry, and they carry warm sap back to the canal when a pipe leaks. They know the pressure in each gauge by the sound it makes, high and thin or low and soft. They know which stems will bear new flowers and which will grow into a window frame. They prune only what would choke the machine, and they do it with their fingers, never with a blade. The cut heals with a bead of clear sap, and the Tender licks it, because it is sweet and because it is courtesy.

There is no language in the place but the language of maintenance. The Tender does not think in words. They think in flows, in heat, in the weight of water on a leaf. Their memories are not stored in a brain but in the rings of the wood, in the rust of the iron, in the sediment of the canal. They know that the city was once loud, and that it will be quiet again, and that the canal will keep moving, and that the green will keep the machines alive. They do not know if there are others. They have not looked. The place is small, two hundred paces by a hundred, but it is enough. It is a body, and they are its slow, steady heart.

At night, when the light is gone and the mist rises to their knees, the Tender climbs out onto the towpath and sinks their feet into the water. The roots at their toes uncurl and drink. They stay there for a long time, feeling the current that is almost not a current, the drift of pollen and oil. The bird is asleep in the vent. The rat is asleep under the bed. The beetle crawls along the rim of a pressure gauge, and the needle trembles. The Tender closes their human eye and lets the gauge eye go dark. They breathe in time with the piston, which never stops. The place lives. They live there. The difference is only a matter of scale.
