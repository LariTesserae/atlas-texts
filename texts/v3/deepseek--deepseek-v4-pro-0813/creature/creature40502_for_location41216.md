The first resident is not a fish, not a worm, not a shrimp. It is a boundary.

If you hung at the edge of the depression and waited, you would not see it as a thing. It would appear only as a slight tarnish on the metallic mirror of the brine, a faint graininess at the halocline, a breath-fog on old tin. That is the most visible inhabitant: a film of sulfide-oxidizing bacteria, perhaps a few hundred cells thick. They live only at the line where the seawater’s oxygen meets the brine’s sulfide. Magnified, they would look like pale, hair-like filaments, each cell holding a tiny globule of elemental sulfur, yellow as trapped light. They do not eat in the way an animal eats. They pass electrons from sulfide to oxygen, and the energy they harvest holds them there, pinned to the liquid seam like a flame to a wick.

Below them, in the anoxic brine, the residents are older and smaller: sulfate-reducing bacteria and methanogenic archaea. They live without light, without oxygen, in water that would kill most living things. They reduce sulfate to sulfide, and the sulfide rises toward the halocline. They make methane, which drifts upward and is consumed. They divide so slowly that a single cell might not divide for a year. They are not a crowd; they are a standing community, a steady-state population fed by the seep. The sharpness of the brine, its sulfide and its dense salt, is partly their breath.

In the pores of the limestone and in the fine sediment near the fissure, there are others still: hydrogenotrophic archaea, acetogenic bacteria, cells that live on traces of gas and minerals. They are the deepest residents, the ones closest to the source. They are the first step in a food web with no animals, no plants, no predators—only a slow passing of carbon, sulfur, and electrons from one kind of cell to another.

At the south end, where the brine overflows in that continuous, barely perceptible tongue, there is a different community. Halophilic archaea and bacteria form a living varnish in the hollows of the mineral rim. They are not the rim itself, but they are within it, growing in tiny salt crystals, in hairline cracks, in the wet shine where the brine slides over. Some are faintly pink or orange, though in the gray-green light they read only as stain. They tolerate changes that would crush other microbes: sometimes submerged in brine, sometimes exposed to seawater, sometimes nearly dry. Their exudates help minerals settle, so the rim is not purely geological. It is a slow collaboration between life and chemistry, not built but compounded over centuries, cell by invisible cell.

On the upper walls, beyond the reach of the brine, the only multicellular residents live: the dark, wiry algae. They are brown and red algae, almost black in the dim light, and they are older than they look. Each sparse tuft may have been alive for decades. They photosynthesize just enough to persist, not enough to spread. Nothing grazes them. Nothing competes. They are the only visible green in the place, though their green is so dark it passes for shadow. They are the eyelashes of the pool, if a pool could have eyelashes.

These residents are not separate from the pool; they are part of its machinery. The sulfate reducers produce what the sulfur oxidizers consume. The methanogens make what other archaea use. The algae fix carbon from the light, and their dead cells drift down into the brine to feed the fermenters. The overflow carries away some cells, but the seep brings in new minerals and new life. The small, steady voltage between the two waters is not wasted; some of the film’s residents live by that electrical potential, bridging the two waters like a living wire. The whole system is a body without organs, a metabolism without a mouth, a city without walls.

Ask who lives here, and the answer is not a name. It is a thin, ancient, many-species being: a biofilm at the halocline, a population in the brine, a crust in the rim, a scatter of algae on the walls. It has no brain, no muscle, no sound. It does not look back. It does not need to. It has been living here longer than the mineral rim, longer than the algae, and it will outlast any visitor. The pool is not empty. It is inhabited by the slowest possible community, a life so patient that it has become indistinguishable from the place itself.
