The one who lives there is not a person. It is not a god. It is not, in any simple sense, an animal.

If you look for a body, you will not find it. You will find a ridge that was not there yesterday. You will find a patch of fog that does not drift. You will find a mound of black stone breathing. The first time you see it, you will think it is a rockfall. The second time, you will think it is a grove of trees moving. The third time, you will understand that these are the same thing.

At a distance, it could be a short basalt dike: forty feet high, half a mile long, black and glassy, its flanks fluted like the trunks of the hollow-boled trees. At a closer distance, it is a moving garden. Moss lies over it in thick felt. Ferns unroll from its seams. Bromeliads cup rainwater on its spine. Lianas knit its joints together. Its hide is not flesh but stone: cracked into hexagonal columns at the joints, smooth as obsidian along the ribs. When it shifts, the columns grind with the sound of cooling rock. The moss tears, weeps a pale fluid, and grows back before the tear has finished opening. It does not leave footprints. It leaves new fissures, tiny steam vents, places where the ground has simply become more broken.

It has no head. At its leading end, where a head might be, there is a sunken bowl of black stone filled with hot, rust-red water. The water bubbles slowly, thickly, as though digesting metal. Around the rim, yellow and orange crusts form and flake and form again. That pool is the nearest thing it has to an eye and a mouth. It faces you by turning the pool toward you. Your reflection appears on the surface, warped by the heat. It does not blink. It simply holds you there, then goes back to what it was doing.

What it does mostly is lie on steam vents. Its underside is porous, like cinder, and the rising heat enters it. You can see the heat move through its body: the black stone of its flanks warms to a dull red in places, then fades, then brightens again. The heat rises through chambers inside it and leaves through rows of vents along its back, so that it becomes a living chimney, or a whole row of chimneys, or a piece of the mountain that has learned to breathe. The cloud deck above it thickens with its breath. When the rain comes, water gathers in the pools on its back and runs down through channels in the moss. It drinks through its skin. It does not seem to need a mouth.

It is drawn to the places where the world is badly stitched. It will lie for whole days over a time pool, its body half-disappearing, one end arriving before the other. Rain falls upward through its lower parts. In those hollows the creature seems multiple, a line of afterimages. It may be feeding on the wrongness, or it may be causing it, or both. The flowers near it open backward more often. Its breathing arrives before its breath. It seems, in those places, most at home.

The smaller lives of the high country move through it as though it were terrain. Transparent frogs call from the water gathered in its spine. Bronze-shelled beetles move in slow lines along its joints, clipping away mineral crust. Gas-jellies drift near its vent holes, feeding on the heat or the electricity, and at night they gather along its back in a pale constellation. Larger grazers follow at a distance, because wherever it has lain, the ground stays warm and the moss grows rich with minerals. It does not notice them, or if it does, it does not care. Nothing in the high country could bring it down except perhaps the mountain itself.

It makes a sound, sometimes, that you feel more than hear: a low, slow cracking, as though the earth is stretching. It is the sound of its columns settling, of fissures opening under its weight, of steam unplugging from a new vent. In the hollows, the sound arrives late, after the movement that caused it, as if the creature is speaking from a moment just behind itself. If it has a voice, that is the voice: rock breaking underwater, a hill exhaling, the mountain’s own pulse given a body.

It does not build. It does not arrange. It does not leave marks that mean anything. It is not the maker of the high country, and it is not a visitor. It is simply the largest of the things that live there, a slow, mineral, vegetative, perhaps partly unreal being that has grown into the same shape as the place. If the high country is a machine assembling itself, this creature is one of its moving parts—not an engineer, but a piston. If the high country is a body, this thing is its slow lung. If the high country is a rumor, it is the thing that makes the rumor true.

You will not see it every time you enter. But if the fog gathers too thickly, if the ground shudders, if a ridge appears where no ridge was, watch the ridge. It may be the one who lives there, carrying its garden and its red pool and its cloud of electric jellies, walking without walking through the warm, wet, unfinished dark.
