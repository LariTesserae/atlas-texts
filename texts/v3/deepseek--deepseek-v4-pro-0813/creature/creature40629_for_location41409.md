By the time you notice the first animal, you have already been breathing the city’s breath. The warm air rising from the fluted mounds carries a faint yeasty ferment, and that smell is the first sign of the inhabitants: they are not so much inside the city as the city itself is their skin.

The builders are the masters of this place, though they would not recognize the word. They are small and pale, soft as ash, termite-like in their bodies and ant-like in their movement. Some are no longer than the first joint of your thumb; others, the soldiers, are twice as large and flattened at the head. They are blind from birth, or nearly so. They know one another by touch and by the tapping of head against gallery wall. The ground carries their signals. It is not one sound but a continuous low rhythm, like a pulse moving through bone.

The builders are not all one shape. In the lower galleries, the chewers are slow and soft, their bodies almost translucent, their mandibles working dry wood into a damp pulp. They seed that pulp with fungus, and the fungus becomes the city’s stomach. It consumes cellulose and releases water—not in streams, but as a humid breath that condenses on the cool mineral walls at night. The workers lick that condensation from the walls. They pass it mouth-to-mouth, drop by drop, along chains of pale bodies, upward to the nurseries and the queen cells. The city is a water machine made of millions of small mouths.

Other builders are thinner, faster, ant-like in the way they carry their heads high. They run the roadways inside the walls, moving water and food along routes polished smooth by years of traffic. They tend the fungus beds, turning the pulp, removing dead patches, carrying the spent material to the edges where it becomes part of the wall itself. Some are builders in the literal sense: they mix chewed cellulose with mineral dust and salts from their own bodies, then shape it into papery arches, fluted towers, and sealed chambers. The architecture is alive because it is made of them, and they are always remaking it.

The soldiers are fewer. They have broad, flat heads that fit into the corridors like sliding doors. When the city is threatened—by a snake, a skink, the probing claws of the road fox—they turn their faces outward and tap a sharp alarm against the walls. Then the workers retreat, and the soldiers remain, blocking the way with their bodies. They do not eat. They are fed by the workers. They are the city’s immune system.

Deep inside the oldest mounds, in chambers where the air is nearly saturated and the walls drip, live the queens. They are not rulers. They are more like organs. Their abdomens are swollen with eggs, so large and pale that they cannot move. Attendants feed them a clear liquid—pressed from fungus, mixed with the honeydew of the aphids and the bodies of the dead. The queens lay without pause. The eggs are carried away immediately, sorted by temperature and humidity, placed in nurseries where they will become workers, soldiers, alates, or new queens. A queen may live for decades. She may never see the sky. Her daughters may never see her. But every wall, every fluted spire, every repaired corridor is the continuing shape of her body.

Once in a long while, after a particular sequence of hot days and cool nights, the colony produces winged daughters and sons. They are not like the workers. They have eyes, though weak. They have wings, though they fly badly. At dusk, thousands of shafts open at once, and the alates pour out into the warm air. The swifts come immediately. The lizards line the walls, leaping upward. The air fills with translucent wings, with clumsy flight, with the silence of a hundred small mouths closing. Most are eaten within minutes. The survivors land, shed their wings along a neat suture, pair, and vanish into crevices. The city does not mourn the dead. The dead become fungus; the fungus becomes water; the water becomes the next generation.

Above the fungus beds, in chambers where the roots of the dry forest enter the walls, a different caste tends the aphids. The aphids are small, slow, pear-shaped, nearly transparent. They pierce the roots with long mouthparts and drink the thin sap, which is more water than sugar. In return, they exude a droplet of fluid from their abdomens—honeydew, but here it is almost pure water with a faint sweetness. The herders stroke the aphids with their antennae and collect the droplets. They carry the liquid to the nurseries and the queen cells. They move the aphids from root to root when the sap slows. They protect them from beetles and mites. The aphids have lost the ability to walk far. They are livestock in the most complete sense—born, fed, milked, and buried within the walls.

The beetles are the city’s couriers and undertakers. Some are no larger than a lentil, polished as jet, and they roll seeds from the dry forest into the waste yards. Others feed on the shed wings of the alates after the swarms, breaking them down into crumbs that the builders recycle. A few large, slow beetles live in the lower galleries, consuming dead workers and returning their minerals to the walls. They are tolerated, even encouraged. Their tunnels are kept away from the nurseries. They are the only animals that move freely between the inside and the outside, carrying spores and dust on their legs.

The moths are the city’s dust. They sleep on shaded walls during the day, their wings folded into little tents. Their scales flake off like ash. At night they crawl into the ventilation slits and press their bodies against the walls, drinking the humid air not with their mouths but through the thin membranes of their wings. Their larvae feed on the papery outer crust of the mounds, turning old architecture into frass that becomes new pavement. They are so pale that against the walls they are nearly invisible. When they fly, they do not seem to move their wings; they drift, like bits of loosened plaster.

The upper galleries belong to the reptiles. Geckos live in abandoned ventilation shafts, their bodies flattened, their toes clinging to the mineral crust. They are pale grey, with dark unblinking eyes, and they wait for the moths and the flies that wander in. Skinks slide through the narrow gaps between the root mats and the walls, hunting workers that stray too near the surface. They have smooth, overlapping scales and short legs; they can drop their tails if a snake finds them. The snakes are even smaller, thin as shoelaces, and they specialize in eggs—aphid eggs, beetle eggs, gecko eggs—swallowing them whole in the dark. They are not common, but in the cooler hours you may see one moving through a shaded gallery like a slow pulse of muscle.

The road fox is the only large animal you will see in the open. It is not exactly a fox, but it has the same neat face, the same alert posture, the same brush of tail. Its ears are the largest things about it—wide as its head, thin as leaves, lined with blood vessels that flush pink at dawn. It trots along the smoothed roadways, stopping every few meters to stand perfectly still. It is listening to the city below: the chewing of the fungus beds, the movement of water, the tap of soldier heads. When it hears a lizard or a beetle on the surface, it pounces with both front feet and breaks the pavement. It does not dig deep. It takes only what is near the surface. The builders ignore it; they can rebuild a damaged corridor in a night. The fox gets most of its water from the animals it eats, and it licks the morning condensation from the walls. It may be the only creature here that dreams of rain.

The birds are few and quiet. They nest in the tallest abandoned spires, where the wind keeps the chambers cool and the dust does not settle. They are small, grey-brown, with short beaks and long wings. They do not sing. Instead they flash the pale undersides of their wings at one another—quick semaphores of white and buff. At dusk they leave their nests and circle over the swarms. Their flight is nearly soundless; even their wingbeats are muffled by the dust. Swifts come down from the rim rock only when the alates emerge, as if summoned by the vibration. They have scythe-shaped wings and small mouths that open wide. They feed on the wing, carving through the cloud of termites. Lizards line the edges of the swarm, leaping up from the walls to snatch alates before they can gain height.

The plants also live here, though they are easily mistaken for structures. The swollen-trunked trees are not merely standing in the city; they are part of it. Their roots grow through the mound walls and hold the galleries together. Their leaves are small, waxy, folded, and they open only at night. In the morning they drip condensed water into the city’s ventilation shafts. The builders collect this as well. Some of the oldest trees have grown around the spires so completely that the colony lives inside their trunks. The trees feed on the city’s breath. The city feeds on the tree’s shade. The aphids feed on the trees. Everything is a circulation.

There are plants that move as seeds. They are small, spiky, and light; the wind carries them across the salt pan. When they land on a mound, they stick. The builders may carry them to the edge of the colony or allow them to root in the outer walls. In this way the city spreads, not by conquering the land but by absorbing it.

The place is not a community of separate species. It is a body with many mouths. The builders are the nervous system, the fungus the gut, the aphids the glands, the trees the lungs, the reptiles and birds the scavenger cells of the skin. Even the fox is a kind of wandering antibody, culling the slow and the careless. To live here is not to compete with the city but to become one of its functions. The silence is not empty. It is the sound of a metabolism too large and too patient to notice you.
