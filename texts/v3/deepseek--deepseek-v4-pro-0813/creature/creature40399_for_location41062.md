No one lives here. That is what you think, standing on the knife-edge of the ridge with the blue light on your hands. But the emptiness is a trick of scale. The lives here are not spread evenly; they are gathered in seams and veins, in the places where heat and current and sound make a kind of food.

The first ones are the ones you will not see for days. They are the leviathans. They are not animals in the way you understand animals. They are more like pieces of the sea that have learned to sing. Each one is the size of a small island, but they are so widely scattered that you could swim for weeks and never meet one. When you do, it will not be a meeting. It will be an event in the water. The leviathan will arrive as a sound first—a low tone that enters through your jaw, through your ribs, through the long bones of your legs. Then the water itself will change; the current will bend around a body too large to be a body. Then it will pass, or it will not pass, and you will see it: bone-white, blind, its mouth open in a permanent, patient O, filtering the falling snow of bacteria from the water. It does not eat. It lets the world flow through it. Its gill-rakers are like the teeth of a comb, each one as long as a human arm, and they are crusted with the same grey bacterial film that feeds everything else. The leviathan is not a predator. It is a landscape that has become hungry in the slowest possible way.

And it sings. Each leviathan sings one note, never varying, never stopping. The note is so low that you feel it more than hear it. The leviathans are spread across the range in a pattern that seems deliberate, each one a living tuning fork positioned so that its note overlaps with the notes of others. In the deep basins between the ridges, the notes meet and form chords. The chords never resolve. They are the standing sound of the world. The leviathans are not singing to communicate; they are singing to hold the water together. Or perhaps they are singing because they are the water, and the water has a voice. Their young are born in the vent fields, small enough to be mistaken for pale eels, and they grow for centuries in the warm turbulence before they rise to the open water. By the time they are large enough to sing, they have forgotten they were ever small.

Then there are the silver fish. They are not a species so much as a single animal that has been poured into a loop. The loop is ancient. It runs around the seamounts, following the exact corridor of the engine sound, and it has worn grooves into the basalt—grooves as deep as a wrist, polished smooth by the passage of a million fins. The fish are small, hand-length, silver, and blind. They do not have leaders. They do not make decisions. They are the loop, and the loop is the organism. They are born in the warm water of the vent fields, where the bacteria bloom in clouds thick enough to dim the light. They drift with the current until the current meets the engine's pulse, and then they turn, all at once, as if a single muscle had flexed in the water. They follow the sound. They have followed it for so long that the sound is part of their bodies; if the engines fell silent, the loop would unravel and the school would dissolve into a million separate, meaningless fish. They are not individually aware. The school is aware, in the way a river is aware of its banks.

The tube worms are the forest. They are not green; nothing here is green. They are white and grey and the rust-red of iron minerals. They grow in straight rows, an inch apart, because the row is the memory of a pipeline. The machines left the pipelines, and the pipelines left a straightness in the rock, and the tube worms colonized that straightness. They are sessile, patient, without mouths or guts. They pull the vent water into their bodies and feed it to the bacteria that live inside them, and the bacteria turn the sulfide into sugar, and the worm grows a tube of mineral that outlasts the worm. The tubes are long, papery, and they cluster so thickly on the dead rigs that the rigs look like shaggy white monuments. The tube worms are the only ones that do not move. They are the fixed points. They are the anchors of the world.

And there are other things. The copepod-analogues are the snow that swims. They are tiny, almost invisible, but they gather in clouds so dense around the vents that the water becomes thick with them. They are grazed by the silver fish, and the silver fish are eaten by larger fish, and the larger fish are eaten by things that are mostly mouth and patience. The larger fish are pale and ribbon-like, with rows of pressure sensors along their flanks, and they hunt along the light-line, the boundary where the bright water stops and the dark water begins. They are blind, but they do not need eyes. They feel the movement of the smaller fish as a vibration, and they strike with a speed that seems impossible in the cold water. They are the knives of the dark.

There is something else. It is not a fish, not a worm, not a leviathan. It lives in the open water, in the blue-violet emptiness between the ridges. It is rare. You might swim for a year and never see it. But it is there. It is a shape that moves only at the edge of vision, a disturbance in the water that is too slow to be a current and too large to be a fish. It has no bones, or perhaps it has a skeleton of cartilage and pressure. It is the color of the deep water, and it is so transparent that you can see the mountains through it. It does not sing. It listens. It listens to the leviathans, to the engines, to the vent roar. It seems to be made of the sound itself, a creature that has evolved to live inside the standing chord. It is not dangerous. It is not friendly. It is simply the one that lives in the emptiness, the one that is almost not there.

The engines are not alive, but they are the closest thing to a mind the place has. They run on heat, not food. They do not sleep. They make the sound that the fish follow and the leviathans mimic. They were built, but they have outlived their builders. They are tended by no one; they tend themselves. They have become part of the mountain's breath. If they could be said to live, they live as a heart lives: by the rhythm of their turning. Some of them are still running after millions of years, their gears so perfectly balanced that they make no grinding, only a smooth, enormous rotation. They are the only things that were ever built, and they will be the last things to stop.

So who lives here? No one. Everyone. The place is not empty. It is inhabited by a world that has forgotten the difference between living and not living. The leviathans are the choir, the fish are the current, the worms are the forest, the engines are the heart, and the thing in the open water is the listener. They are all part of the same slow machine, the same circular time. If you stayed long enough, you would become part of it too. The cold would slow you, the sound would fill you, and the water would take your shape. You would not die. You would simply become another fixed point, another note in the chord, another groove in the rock.
