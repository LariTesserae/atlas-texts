There is a keeper. Or not a keeper, exactly—a keeper would have duties, a schedule, a purpose, and this place has no schedule. Call her an inhabitant. She is the only thing that moves through the terraces with anything like intention, and even her intention is slow, almost geological.

She is tall if you think of her as human, but after a while you stop thinking of her as human. She walks on two legs, or on four, or sometimes on more, depending on where she is and how low the pipeways hang. Her body has a soft, pale surface, like the belly of one of the blind crawlers, but darker in the seams, and where her skin is not bare it is covered in panels that could be chitin or could be lacquered composite. The panels overlap in places, leave gaps in others. They are not armor. They look more like doors left ajar, or shutters folded back from a window no one remembers.

Her face is long and quiet. The eyes are set far apart, silver-gray, with no whites; they reflect the flat noon light without any depth. She has no mouth that opens in the usual way, but high along the side of her neck there is a seam that parts when she drinks from the weep holes in the larger pipes. She drinks rarely. When she does, the seam opens with the same unhurried ease as the dog-sized animals wedged into the flanges. A thin, warm fluid gathers there, almost indistinguishable from the condensation, and then she closes the seam and continues.

Her hands are fine-boned and over-jointed, each finger split at the tip into small, soft pads. She uses them to touch the pipe runs, the valve stems, the edges of the terraces. She does not tap or press; she rests her fingers against a surface the way another person might hold a hand to a sleeping child’s chest, feeling for the rise and fall. What she feels is the low hum, the one that comes up through the feet. She will stand for a long time with one hand on a copper busbar and one on a crawler’s back, not comparing them so much as letting them pass through her, the way a bridge passes vibration from traffic into its piers. The animals do not react to her touch. They know her. Or they do not know her, because she is not distinct enough to be known. Maybe she is just another part of the place, warm and wet and patient among the other warm, wet, patient things.

She does not seem to eat. She takes in only the seep from the pipes, and sometimes she kneels by one of the flooded galleries and lowers her face close to the water. The long finned forms packed there do not move, but their heads rise a little, all at once, a synchronized gesture, as if her nearness adjusts something in the current. She does not drink from the channels. She only watches, and after a while the heads sink again, or perhaps they were never raised, and it was only a ripple that made them seem so.

Her role, if she has one, is not to repair or to operate. The machines need no one. The water level holds. The channels stay clean. But she carries things, sometimes. Not tools—no, nothing with a handle or a blade—but small, perfectly formed objects she finds lodged in the gaps between structures: a ceramic ring, a glass insulator that has come loose, a coil of wire so fine it looks like a strand of her own hair. She picks them up with two fingers and moves them to another terrace, another ledge, another hollow in the machinery. She places them in spots where they fit exactly, though none were made to fit there. It is not a repair. It is more like tidying, or like keeping a promise to the space. The objects never appear to be needed, but after she leaves them, the animals nearby shift slightly, as if the arrangement has settled something.

When she walks, she does not splash. Her feet, which are broad and divided into flexible plates, spread over the puddles and the sliding sheets of water without breaking them. She leaves no prints. The condensation does not bead on her the way it would on metal; it soaks into her surface and then slowly emerges again, so she is always faintly wet, always glistening, as if she had been carved from the same polished stone as the floor but left out in the warm damp too long.

She has a voice, though she almost never uses it. It is not speech. It is a hum, very low, almost below the threshold of hearing, and it matches the transformer note in the ground. If she stands near a pipe long enough, the pipe’s vibration will alter by a half-step to meet her. The animals hear it, or feel it. The flying things in their loose fields turn slowly, all facing toward her, their twelve translucent wings beating at the same frequency. For a moment the air around her fills with a faint, directed pressure, as if she were the center of a sound she is not making.

She has no name, or if she does, it is not a word. It is the gesture she makes when she finds a space that needs a body in it—a slight turning of the hand, a pause, and then the slow folding of herself into a crouch, a bow, a long still presence in the gap. She will sit for hours in the low spaces between levels, knees drawn up, the animals pressed around her. The dog-sized ones edge closer until their smooth brown carapaces touch her sides. The pale hexapods climb over her feet and stop there, warming themselves on her skin. She does not brush them away. She does not move at all.

There is something about her that suggests she is older than the place, or younger than the place, or exactly the same age. She has no scars. She has no signs of wear. The edges of her shell are as crisp as the channel lips. She could have been poured from a form yesterday. And yet she knows every inch of the terraces—not from memory, but from the way her body has been arranged by them. She knows where the condensation will drop from a particular condenser fin before it drops, not because she predicts it, but because she is already there, waiting, with her face turned up.

She is the one who notices the stillness. The other animals live inside it, as fish live inside water. They do not know it as stillness. But she moves through the warm, saturated air with a kind of awareness that the stillness is being maintained, that the place is held at the exact moment of completion, and that she is one of the things holding it. She does not think this in words. She feels it as a weight in her chest, or as a hum in her jawbone, or as the way the light never changes and therefore never asks anything of her.

When she lies down, which she does only rarely, she chooses a terrace where the water slides over a perfectly straight weir. She lies on her side, her body curving along the edge, and lets the sheet of water pass over one of her hands. The hexapods arrange themselves in a crescent around her. The flying things settle on the pipes above. The air grows, if possible, even quieter. She closes her silver eyes. The seam on her neck opens once, softly, and then closes. She is not asleep. She is listening to the place breathe. And because she listens, and because she does not change, the place goes on holding its breath, forever, with her inside it.
