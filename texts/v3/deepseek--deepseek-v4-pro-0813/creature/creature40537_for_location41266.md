The one who lives here is not easily separated from the place. You might search for tracks, and there are tracks, but they are not exactly footprints: long, three-toed depressions, deep at the heel and shallow at the toe, placed far apart in the silt. They lead from the wayhouse doorway to the dew fence, then along the roadline, then vanish where the stone is too hard to take a mark. The track is made not by weight but by habit, worn into the pink-grey dust over a very long time.

At dawn she is already out. She stands by the dew fence, a little too tall to be a person, and the fog crowds around her as if she were part of the ground. Her body is the color of the old roadline: grey-white with a warm undertone, later patched with the dark varnish of the plateau stones at her shoulders, knees, and heels. She is slim, not starved—pared down, like the stones. Her arms are long, and her hands have four long fingers, each with one more joint than yours, and a thumb that folds back close to the wrist. Her feet are narrow, with three toes that spread on soft ground. She does not wear anything. There is no need. Her skin is smooth and faintly warm, and it sheds the fine dust of the roadline the way a leaf sheds water.

Her face is long and quiet. She has no mane of hair, only a fine pale down that catches the morning fog like a net. Her eyes are set deep under a heavy brow, dark as the water in the oldest basins. In bright light the pupil closes to a slit, not from pain but from the same patience that has worn the stone. She has a mouth, but it is small and closed, and she uses it mostly for water and the pale leafless plants that grow in the shade of the wayhouse. She eats slowly, tasting each leaf as if remembering it.

At dawn she walks the dew fence line. The mirror flies have come, thousands of them, their silver-lined wings still close to the wires. They do not scatter when she reaches out. She runs one long finger down a filament, gathering the beads into her palm, and then lifts the water to her mouth. The flies settle on her arm, her shoulder, the curve of her head. For a few minutes she stands there as still as the stanchions, and the only sound is the faint ticking of their feet on her skin. She is drinking, but she is also listening. The water has a sound, or maybe it is the wire that does.

After the fog burns off, she goes to the seeps. She walks slowly, not with the weakness of age but with the conserved motion of something that has no need to hurry. She kneels in the wet gravel and places her hands flat on the ground. The deep heat below rises to meet her. She clears the seeps with circular movements of her fingertips, moving aside the silt that the wind has carried in. Where the water has grown dense and overfull, she cups it in her hands and carries it to another basin, letting it fall in a thin stream. She knows which seep is ordinary and which holds the heavier water. She can tell by the way her fingers part it, by the way the pebbles settle.

She is the one who keeps the basins clear, though she would not call it keeping. It is more that she agrees with the place. The old roadline knows her weight and changes its hum when she steps onto it. She walks its buckled lengths in the afternoon heat, when the air shimmers. At each stanchion she stops and places a palm against the smooth curve, feeling the inner channel draw moisture from the air. If there is a small obstruction—a grain of sand, a splinter of stone—she taps the stanchion with one knuckle, and the thing loosens. She never breaks the roadline. She only reminds it of its own shape.

The animals know her. The dust hares do not flee from her shadow; they lift their huge ears and then go back to grazing the crusts. The pale harriers see her from high above and circle once, then move on, as if marking her position. The skitter-lizards will run across her bare foot in the heat of the day and vanish into a crack. The foxes that follow the old roadline at night will sometimes lie in the doorway of the wayhouse where she rests. They do not come inside. They lie with their tails over their noses, and the light from the walls touches the tops of their heads.

At dusk she goes to the cairns. These are her slowest task. She circles each one once and then presses her forehead to the warm side. She does not speak. The core inside the cairn gives off a trace of warmth, and her own warmth answers it. If you put your ear to the air nearby, you might feel a change of pressure, a low thrum below hearing. She stays there for a long time, long enough for the sky to turn blue-black at the zenith. The dust hares come out into the swales. The first foxes move in the dark. She listens to the cairn the way you would listen to a sleeping animal, not to wake it but to know that it is still breathing.

When she returns to the wayhouse, the lines of light wake in the walls as if they recognize her. The dust on the floor does not stir. The shelf in the wall, the one that seems to have grown rather than been attached, holds her few things: a shed skin from an iron-colored snake, a mirror fly’s wing with its silver line gone dull, a smooth black pebble from the bottom of a dense basin. They are not offerings. They are just the records she has kept.

She does not sleep, exactly. She folds her long legs and sits against the cool wall. Her eyes dim but do not close. The heat in her skin fades to a low warmth, enough to keep the interior from becoming cold. The wall lights slowly dim. Outside the fog begins to gather again, pouring up the escarpment, and the night animals move around her stillness. The roadline hums its one low note under the floor of hearing. The place settles into itself.

She is not alone. There are others like her, but they are spaced across the plateau the way cushion plants are spaced: so far apart that each one seems like a separate event. Twice a year, when the fog is thick enough to erase the horizon, two of them may meet at a seep. They do not talk, or if they do it is below hearing, a low pressure in the air. They sit on opposite sides of the water and pass a handful from one to the other. Then they stand, and one goes north, and one goes south, and the dust settles over their tracks.

If you ask what she is, there is no easy answer. She is not a builder, not a human, not a ghost. She is what the place produced from its long patience: a body grown out of the same material as the roadline and the wayhouse, given just enough heat and water and time to stand up and walk. She keeps the water clear, the roadline humming, the cairns warm. She does not remember a beginning. She is not waiting for anything. She lives there the way the fog lives there, the way the heat lives under the ground—not as a guest, but as part of the plateau’s slow, continuous life.
