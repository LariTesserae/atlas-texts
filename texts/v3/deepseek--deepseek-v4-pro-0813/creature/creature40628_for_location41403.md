At first the place seems empty of anything you’d call a tenant. The eel is there, the amphipods, the bacterial mats, the machines. But there are others. You will not see them until you learn not to look for faces.

They are called the Tenders, though no one here calls them anything. There are nine. Maybe ten. One is so deep in the rock above the transformer gallery that it may be part of the rock now. Another is only a pair of pale hands you glimpse in the dead water behind the heat exchanger, exactly where the eel likes to rest. They are not the city’s masters. They are not its guests.

The first one you see looks like a shed skin. It is folded into a crook of pipe in the smelter hall, legs drawn up, arms wrapped around a cooled brine line. Its skin is the color of quartz fouled with sulfur, streaked with rust and white anhydrite. It is not thin; it is dense, built for pressure, with a barrel chest and a short neck. Its head is smooth, eyeless, with a mouth like a healed tear. Along its ribs the skin opens in a row of gill slits that flutter too slowly to be breathing. They are not breathing. They are listening.

Its hands are the only part that seems fully awake. They are broad, six-fingered, each finger tipped with a black nail that looks chipped from vent stone. The pads are covered in fine sensory pits, like the lateral line of a fish, but deeper, more ordered. It reads the pipe through those pits. Temperature, pressure, the faint beginning of a new crack, the complaint of a valve three decks down. It knows the city not as a map but as a pressure.

It moves only when the pump misses a stroke. Then it uncoils, not with a start, but with the patience of something that expects every phase to end. It pulls itself along the pipe with its arms. Its legs trail behind, not useless but secondary. In open water it moves like a pale ribbon, slow, every motion borrowed from the current, costing nothing.

Since it has no eyes, it sees the way the water pushes against the pits on its face and wrists. It knows the pump cathedral by the way the roar bends around it. A closed valve makes the water taste sharper, more metallic. An open valve brings a thread of cold from above. It can tell you the tide state by the pressure on its sternum.

It does not eat, not in the way you do. Its gills are full of bacteria that oxidize sulfur. Its blood carries hydrogen sulfide the way yours carries oxygen. It feeds on the city’s waste heat and the vent water. Sometimes it scrapes a handful of white bacterial mat from a vent mouth and pushes it into its gill slits, not to swallow, but to keep the culture alive. It is a walking garden of heat-eaters. Nothing green lives here because green was never enough; this is the older metabolism, the one that does not need light.

It carries no tools. When a valve begins to seize, it places a hand on the housing and waits. The city answers. Sometimes the mineral scale cracks and falls away on its own. Sometimes the Tender takes a flake of its own nail and presses it into the crack, and the hot mineral water welds it there. The machines are maintained by pressure switches and thermal relays. The Tenders are the part of that logic that can choose to touch.

They do not speak. But they signal by tapping the pipes, using the same frequencies the cooling metal uses. A conversation between two Tenders sounds like a building settling: a tick, a groan, a low thud that travels through the structure. They do not need words because they are always talking to the whole city, not just to each other. Every tap is a vote in the city’s argument with itself.

They have no names, no hierarchy. If one is older, it is only because it has accumulated more mineral scale. The oldest one has settled into a basalt terrace above the transformer gallery. Its legs are fused to the rock, its rib cage opened and filled with pipe work. At first you think it is a failed piece of machinery. Then you see its hand, still flexing slowly, still reading the vibrations. The other Tenders come to it, not to feed it, but to lay their hands on the ledge beside it and listen. It is the oldest part of the city, or the city’s oldest part that ever had a choice.

They do not reproduce, or if they do, it is not in any way you would call birth. They precipitate. When one is torn apart by a pressure surge or lost in a vent collapse, the city does not mourn. It grows another from the mineral gardens over a decade: first a pale mass on a hot rock, then a shape that learns to tap. You would call it calcification, not birth.

Most of the time they are still. They hang in the water with their fingers wrapped around a pipe or a cable, letting the current press the world into them. They are so still that the amphipods walk over them. The bone-white eel sometimes coils beside the youngest one, sharing the dead water behind the heat exchanger. They do not startle. They are not startled by anything.

If you stay long enough, one of them will come to you. Not out of curiosity; you are just a new pressure in the water, a new vibration. It will drift close, its eyeless face passing within a hand's breadth. You will see your own light reflected in the mineral film on its skin. Then it will reach out and touch the faceplate of your mask, not to hurt, but to listen to your heartbeat through the glass. It will tap once on your tank. The sound will ring inside your teeth. Then it will let go and move away, satisfied that you are another soft, temporary part of the city's rhythm.

What lives here is not a creature, not a person. It is a function that has grown a body. The city is a standing wave, and the Tenders are the places where the wave curls back on itself and looks, briefly, through a pair of blind hands. They are not the city's masters. They are not its guests. They are the part of the city that can touch.
