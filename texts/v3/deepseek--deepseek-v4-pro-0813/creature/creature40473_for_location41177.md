Nothing human lives in the Cold Chancel, and nothing human could. The place is too thin-aired, too dark, too newly broken. But it is not empty. It is occupied from the lowest film of wet rock to the pale seam of sky, by creatures whose bodies are their only walls.

The first inhabitants are the walls themselves, or rather the living skin on them. Pale, barrel-shaped polyps, each no bigger than a thumb, are packed together into a continuous rubbery crust. They are simple: a tube of gut and flesh, a mouth at one end, a grip at the other. They open when cloud water runs down the rock and carries dissolved mineral dust; they close when the air dries. They do not move except to swell and shrink, but there are millions of them. They turn the sharp gabbro into something almost soft to the eye. They are the floor of the living wall. Above them, the black wall-lice lie overlapping like shingles, flattened and many-legged. They graze the same films, and when one shifts, the whole wall seems to move. When many shift together, the rock ripples as if breathing. They are the muscle of the Chancel.

On ledges and in cracks sit the song-newts. They are pale and blind, their eye sockets grown over with smooth skin. They do not need eyes. They sit nose-downward so the seep-water runs over their backs, and they call in notes that match the exact dimensions of the rift. Their songs stack into standing waves, so some notes seem to come from inside the listener’s chest. They are territory made of pitch: each ledge has its own note or chord, and two males can live side by side if their calls interlock rather than overlap. They eat wall-lice and polyps, snapping quickly in the dark. They are the voice of the place, a continuous chiming under the drips.

The air is crowded with glass-flies, small white midges so numerous that the air feels thick. They feed on slime and on one another. Among them are the lantern-flies, which carry two small blue lights on their abdomens. They do not flash quickly. Once in many hours, the entire population pulses together in a slow wave that starts at the floor and climbs to the sky-slot. The wave takes so long to cross the chamber that a human eye might doubt it. As it passes, the wall-lice ripple, the polyps hesitate, and the whole rift seems to inhale. It is the closest thing this place has to weather, to a shared heartbeat. Some of the lights on the walls are not animals but pyrite crystals, and some of the “crystals” are animals; you cannot know until they move.

In the pools live the pale octopuses, each no bigger than a fist, each wedged into a crevice, watching with one eye. They are the most awake of the Chancel’s residents. They choose sharp flakes of quartz or gabbro from the pool floor and carry them. They do not shape them. They use the flakes to scrape polyps from the rock or to wedge open crevices where wall-lice hide. A flake may be used for a season, then dropped and replaced. Some flakes on the pool floor have been polished by generations of use, but they remain only stones. The octopuses do not build shelters or walls. Their intelligence is not expressed in construction but in memory and patience. They know which pools stay warm, which seeps last through the dry, where the newts lay their eggs, when the lantern wave will come. They may live for decades. They watch the slow pulse of the lantern-flies and the settling of the mountain. In the dark, they are the only animals that seem to look back.

There is one in the deepest pool, near the slab that rings like a struck bell when a footstep shifts it. Its skin is pale gray, textured like old stone, and it has carried the same flake of quartz for three seasons. It rests with one eye half-open. When the lantern wave passes, it shifts; the flake settles in the silt. One arm extends, touches the flake, and draws it back. The octopus has no name. It is not alone. The pool around it is full of smaller lives, and the walls above are full of sound.

Death here is not rot. A dead body, a fallen newt or old octopus, sinks into a pool or lodges in a crevice. There is no soil to bury it. Instead it is encrusted by polyps and grazed by wall-lice; its minerals dissolve back into the water and the stone. The dead become the floor. The food web is mineral: windblown dust and dissolved minerals feed the biofilms; the polyps and wall-lice graze the biofilms; the newts and octopuses eat the grazers; the glass-flies feed on slime and one another. Nothing builds. Nothing hoards. The only technology is a chosen stone flake, carried for a while and then dropped.

The Chancel is not a shelter the creatures made. It is a wound in the mountain that they have filled. It is too dark for a forest and too newly broken for soil, but it is alive. It hums with small lights and smaller sounds. If you stay too long, your heartbeat seems to fall into time with the drips, and the boundary between your body and the place grows thin. The inhabitants are not separate from the rock; they are a living skin over a fresh break, a slow breath in the high cold. This is a place not built for anyone, but occupied completely.
