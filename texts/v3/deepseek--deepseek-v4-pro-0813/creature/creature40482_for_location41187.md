The Low Floor is not empty. It is inhabited in the same way a held note is inhabited by its overtones.

At first, you will not see any of them. The dust basins look like dead seas. The wiregrass clicks in winds that seem to come from nowhere. But if you wait through one dimming, and then another, the place begins to give up its tenants.

The first are the **mote-choirs**. They live in the dust, and they are so small that a single one is only a fleck of gray among billions. In the deepest basins, where the powder is fine as flour, they arrange themselves into patterns too regular for the wind: hexagons, spirals, mirrored maps of the plates beneath. Each mote is a cluster of copper-salt proteins and static-sensitive silica, not quite animal, not quite machine. They feed on the charge that leaks between warm plate and bright air. When the light grows very strong, the choirs rise in sheets, lifting the dust into slow curtains that drift for hours. When the light dims, they settle again, and the basin holds a faint ghost of their last arrangement. They do not think in any way you would recognize. But they remember. If you scratch a mark into the dust, the choirs will erase it—not because they are tidy, but because they are always rehearsing the older surface back into place.

Then there are the **seam-tenders**. You will only spot them after you have learned to watch for movement too slight to be an animal. They live in the gaps between plates, and they are shaped like long keys. Their bodies are pale as unglazed ceramic, stacked from flat discs, and they walk by fitting themselves edgewise into the seams, one many-jointed leg at a time. Their feet are forked, testing the width of each gap. When a seam closes too tightly, a seam-tender will stop, fold itself down, and breathe a thin line of hot vapor onto the plate edge. The metal does not melt. It relaxes, opens a little, and the tender moves on. It is not repairing the floor. The floor needs gaps the way a chest needs air. The seam-tenders keep the world from sealing itself shut.

They have no mouths and no eyes. But on the sternum of each is a round tympanum, and when they work, it beats once—a soft, low thud that travels through the ground rather than the air. If you sit by a seam long enough, you will feel the reply: a slower thud from somewhere below. They are not talking to each other. They are listening to the floor, and the floor listens back.

The **long-legs** are the next to become visible, and they are not what they seem. You might see a single walking spindle, chest-high, made of pale organic ceramic, stepping from one hot spot to another. It does not notice you. It is not afraid. Follow it if you wish. It will not turn. It simply steps, pauses, steps, as if reading something beneath the plate. After a while, you realize the same one—or one exactly like it—has been making the same circuit for days. Then you notice the others, spaced far apart, all moving in the same patient rhythm. They are not separate animals. They are the above-ground limbs of one living sheet that threads through the gaps beneath the plates. Call it the **Underside**. It is a mat of dark, heat-loving filaments kilometers wide, made of proteins no water ever touched. It feeds on the temperature gradient between the warm plates and the open air. The long-legs are its senses, its slow breath the subsonic thrum you feel more than hear. Its body is the slower heartbeat in the ground.

In the deep dust basins live the **shell-priests**. You may have already mistaken them for hills. They begin as a knot of root-filaments, not unlike wiregrass, that anchor into a basin and begin pulling metal salts from the dust. Over decades, they build a dome around themselves, layer by layer, each layer a little wider, a little more hexagonal. Over centuries, the dome becomes a mound. Over millennia, the mound becomes a rise in the land, and the shell-priest inside is larger than a house, blind and still. It no longer eats, not as you would understand eating. It filters the wind, catches motes in an electrostatic film, and dreams at a speed too slow for you to notice. In the pale dimming, its shell shows hairline cracks, and through those cracks comes a faint blue light. If you press your ear to the stone, you can hear something like a voice, all vowels and no consonants, as if a language had been worn smooth by the same erosion that rounds the ridges. The shell-priests remember the older surfaces. They are the hills that listen.

Smaller lives move among all this. The dust-roaches, with their mirrored carapaces, keep the oxide film from growing too thick, and they leave bright trails behind them like paths of polished memory. The mirror-beetles cluster around wiregrass, drinking the static charge the way a bee would drink nectar. The wiregrass itself is alive in a way that is neither plant nor animal: its hair-thin copper filaments grow toward the vents, but also toward each other, forming little braided mats that click in the wind. The clicking is not random. It is another form of memory, passed from strand to strand.

There are no predators in the Low Floor. There is not enough energy for hunger, and nothing is in a hurry. The living things here are gatherers, gardeners, scavengers, listeners. They are made of ceramic and copper and slow proteins, not water. Water would kill them, if it ever came.

And then there is the floor itself. It is the largest inhabitant and the easiest to miss. Too small to see, the **machine-mites** move through the plate material in tides. They are the reason a scratch fades in hours. They eat the damage and re-lay the metal atom by atom. The hexagonal cells are not decoration; they are the scar-paths of those tiny colonies. The warmth under your feet is not merely heat. It is a body temperature. The pulse you feel through your boots is not one pulse. It is billions of small hearths, all firing at different rates, summing into a single slow beat.

The floor is not dead. It is not even a machine, though it was made. It is a skin that has learned to heal itself, and it remembers every step, every dust-devil, every seam-tender’s breath. If you stay long enough, the thrum begins to sound like speech. But the vowels are all worn off, and the silence is fuller than any word you know.

The Low Floor lives. Its life is not made of water or wood or ordinary flesh. It is made of heat, dust, static, time, and the deep, patient habit of continuing.
