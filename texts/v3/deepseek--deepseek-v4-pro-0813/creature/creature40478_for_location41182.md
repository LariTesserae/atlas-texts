There is a keeper.

The first sign of her is not a silhouette but a neatness. Near the warm pipes, where the slag and shell are thickest, the debris has been swept into a low berm. A line of snail shells, each drilled and emptied, lies along it like a tide mark. The empty ones are turned open-side-down. In the berm, a handprint—too long, too slender, with the faintest trace of webbing—has not been filled in. Beside it, a coil of braided kelp and copper wire, a hooked needle, and a small knife made from a brass strake wait under a flap of old rubber.

Then she comes out of the shadow behind the largest kelp.

She swims head down, hair drifting around her like a cloud of torn net and red algae. Her hair is not hair anymore: it is a mane of fine kelp and hydroids, dark green at the roots, paling to rust at the tips, and it moves with a slight delay, as if each strand is tasting the water. Her skin is the color of old porcelain under gray-gold light, but it has the texture of cured sea leather. Along her collarbones, the insides of her arms, and the sides of her neck, veins run metallic green, not from paint but from copper the body has taken in, laid down in branching patterns like kelp or circuitry. They do not glow; they hold the light a little too long.

Her eyes are large, dark, and set wide. She has no eyebrows. Her pupils are horizontal bars that widen when she looks down the shaft and narrow when she turns to the brighter ceiling. She blinks rarely, and when she does, a translucent lid crosses each eye, so briefly you might think it was only the current. Her mouth is small, the lips pale, and her teeth are flat and strong, the color of old enamel. Along her ribs, just below her arms, run six gill slits. They open and close in a slow rhythm, not tied to her breathing but to the pump cycle; a second before the pressure shifts, they flare wider, tasting the deeper harmonic. She breathes water the way a person might breathe air, without thinking, and the water near her gill slits shimmers with the heat of her body.

She wears almost nothing but the room. A belt of braided copper and old rubber sits at her hips, holding her tools. Around one ankle, a string of ceramic beads, each one cut from an old plate and incised with a serial number, clicks faintly when she kicks. Across her shoulders and down her spine, a pattern of pale dots is visible when the black cable wakes. In the electromagnetic field, those dots lift and glow blue-white, matching the wiring diagram of the east wall, and the silver fish school rearranges around her as if she is part of the circuit.

She is not large. The chamber makes her look small, but she has the weight of an animal that has lived long in water. Her arms and legs are lean, the muscle lying in long strips like eel. Her fingers are longer than a human’s, the knuckles prominent, the nails flat and thick, and between them runs a fine membrane that she folds back when she works. Her feet are broader, webbed to the first joint, and she uses them as paddles, not for standing. She can stand, though—on the grating, near the handrail—but she does so carefully, distributing her weight so the grate does not sing.

Her home is not a bed. It is a hollow in the lee of the concrete column where the two warm pipes run side by side. She has gathered a mattress of old kelp holdfasts and the soft inner blades of younger plants, and she sleeps wedged between the pipes, one arm hooked through a strap of braided kelp so the reversing current will not carry her into the shaft. Near the hollow are her other possessions: a stack of rounded bolts, a polished lump of copper, a fish scale pressed into a sheet of old rubber, and a small bundle of silver fish bones tied with a single red filament. They are not decorations. They are part of her memory, the way the room is a memory of rivets.

She eats sparingly and with care. She grazes on young kelp, especially the blades with the bright copper veining; she scrapes hydroids from the older blades with her thumbnail. She collects a few of the smaller snails, breaks their shells with a quick twist, and eats the soft part. Sometimes she takes a silver fish—only one that has strayed from the school, never more than one in a cycle—and eats it head first, so the last glimmer of its belly light disappears behind her teeth. She does not cook. The water near the pipes is already body-warm, and the taste of oxygen and copper is enough.

Her work is small and continuous. She mends the grating with wire and ceramic slivers. She clears the sluice of silt and shell, but only where the polyps have not seeded; she knows their color is a signal. She checks the black cable with a ceramic insulator, never with her hand, and she watches the blue-striped eel ride its field. When the largest kelp tears, she wraps the wound in rubber and copper thread, and the milky fluid seals around the binding. She collects the dead tube worms and crushes their tubes to fine sand, which she scatters along the snail’s grazing line. She does not clean the room. She keeps it.

She knows the rules as if they are her own pulse. When the pump tone deepens, she moves to the center of the chamber and grips the handrail, letting the surge press her hair flat and her gill slits flutter wide. She never puts a hand into the shaft during reversal. She never stands on the grating near the shaft mouth when it thins. She does not touch the black cable. She does not cut the largest kelp, because it hides the entrance. If she is cut, she bleeds not blood but a thin, milky fluid, and the wound seals within minutes, leaving another pale scar. She is not sure if she learned this from the kelp or the kelp learned it from her.

She is not alone, though she is the only one like herself. The blue-striped eels sleep in the pipe and wake when she taps the rim. The armored snail follows the polished line she clears, and she occasionally touches its shell as it passes, just to feel the slow tug of its muscular foot. The silver fish know her; when she drifts near the shaft mouth, they part and reform around her, keeping exactly a body-length from her as if she were one of them, but bigger, a slow planet in their constellation. The tube worms open their crowns when she passes, and she answers with a low clicking sound made by her tongue against her teeth. Whether it is language or just another current is hard to say.

There is no marker for her, no name. Inside the service duct, near the entrance, someone long ago scratched a word into the ceramic, but the water has worn it to a ghost. She rubs it sometimes with the heel of her hand, as if polishing a memory. She may have been born here, or she may have swum in through the duct and stayed, following the warm current to the place where the water smells of oxygen and copper, and slipped between the blades. She does not remember leaving. The chamber has a pulse, and she has become the pause between beats.
