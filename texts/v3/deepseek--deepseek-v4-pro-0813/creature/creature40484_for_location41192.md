What lives here is not a who, not at first. The chamber seems to house only the sound and the water and the green. But if you stay, if you let the roar press into your sternum and the warm air bead on your skin, the room’s residents begin to show themselves—slowly, the way a shape appears in dark water after your eyes have stopped searching for it.

The first is the isopod. It is small, the color of candle wax, and it moves along a wet steel beam with the patience of a drip. It is not in a hurry because there is nowhere to go. Its world is a few meters of corroded metal and a patch of biofilm. It grazes with mouthparts too small to see. It has no predators here, no need to hide. Its shell is pale because there is no sun. You watch it for an hour, and it travels the length of your hand. When it reaches a rivet, it pauses, testing the air with its antennae, then turns and goes back the way it came. It is not lost. It is doing exactly what it has done for ten thousand days. It is the room’s smallest visible clock.

The plants are not decorations. They are the room’s oldest inhabitants. The ferns uncoil from their steel troughs like dark green springs. Each frond is a slow thought; they do not grow so much as persist. Their roots have wrapped around the mineral wool and the cracked ceramic and the rust, and they have made a soil out of the room’s own decay. The elephant ears by the square tank are the room’s hands, broad and veined, each leaf holding a bead of water like a tiny lens. The moss is the room’s skin, thick as upholstery, spreading over the lower walls and the planters. It drinks the weeping walls. The watercress in the channels is the only thing that looks young, bright green, but it is just as old; it has been held in a permanent adolescence by the room’s chemistry.

Below the grating, in the black water, something else lives. You will not see it at first. The water is too dark, too still. But if you lie on the grate with your face close to the steel, and wait until the roar has become a part of your body, you may see a pale shape move. It is not a fish, not exactly. It is an eel, blind and long, the color of weak tea. It has no eyes, only small dark pits where eyes might have been. Its body is as thick as your wrist, and it moves in a slow spiral, close to the bottom. It does not disturb the water; the water parts and closes behind it without a ripple. It is the room’s largest animal, and it is almost never seen because it has no reason to surface. It breathes through its skin, or perhaps it does not breathe at all in the way we understand. It may have been here since the room was young, arriving as a thread of a creature through a pipe, and then growing to fill the basin like a root filling a pot. It eats the isopods and the worms, but it eats slowly, a mouthful a week. Its metabolism is so low it can go a year without food. It is not a predator; it is a composter, turning the small lives into the water’s chemistry. It is the room’s hidden pulse.

In the sump, there are worms. They are thin as horsehair, red-brown, and they sway in the slow current that you cannot see. They are the room’s gut, breaking down the sediment that falls through the grate. They do not bite; they do not see; they only wave and filter.

And beneath all of them, there is the microbial film. It is on the water, on the walls, on the leaves, on the steel. It is the color of rust and tea. It is the reason the air smells of iron and green. It is the oldest thing in the room, older than the plants, older than the eel. It has been here since the first water seeped through the first seam. It is not one species; it is a city of species, so tangled that they have become a single organism. It eats the minerals, the rust, the light; it feeds the moss, the isopod, the eel. It is the room’s true blood.

If you ask who lives here, the answer is not any one of these. It is the room itself. The room is a body. The iron walls are its skin, the grating its ribs, the water its blood, the pipes its veins. The roar is its heartbeat. The plants are its lungs, the moss its fur, the isopod its tongue, the eel its slow gut. The whole chamber is one living thing made of many living things, and it has been alive in this exact shape for so long that it has stopped changing. It is not a creature with a face. But if you need a face, it is the blind eel, surfacing only when the light is just so, its pale body a question mark under the black water, asking nothing, answering nothing, simply being.

When you climb back up the ladder, the roar changes. The light from below follows for a few rungs, gray and even. You replace the panel, and the heat and the smell are gone. But the eel is still down there, moving in its slow spiral, and the isopod is still on its beam, and the moss is still drinking the walls. The chamber does not miss you. It was complete before you came, and it is complete after you leave.
