Beneath the carbonate ledge, in a hollow that is not quite cave and not quite cradle, lives a pale eelpout. She has no name. In the blue-gray light under the ice, names have no use.

At first she is only a smudge of colder cold, a slight thickening in the water, a bone-colored pause in the dark. Her body is long and flattened, eel-like but heavier in the tail. Her skin is nearly transparent, the color of old ice that has melted and refrozen without air. The blue light passes through her, so she seems made of diluted milk and faint shadow. Her belly is paler still, and through it, faintly, you might see the silhouette of her last meal. Her eyes are milky, almost blind, two dull pearls set far back on a blunt head. She does not need them. The city speaks to her in pressure, in taste, in vibration.

Her hollow is her whole country. She has widened it over time with the slow flick of her tail, wearing the young carbonate walls until they fit her shape. The ledge around her is porous and sharp, still growing, still being built by the seep. The floor of her home is littered with small deaths: the shells of amphipods, the crumbled tubes of lesser worms, white pebbles that are not pebbles but the first stages of new stone. She does not keep the hollow clean. The city does not keep clean. It accretes.

She is an ambush predator. She does not hunt. She waits. Her gill covers barely move. Her jaw rests slightly open, showing a row of tiny, inward-curved teeth. When a shrimp or a young fish drifts too close, she does not lunge. She opens her mouth a little wider, and the water does the rest. The prey is gone in a movement too quick for the dim light. Then she settles again, and the only sign of the act is a small cloud of sediment that sinks back to the floor.

She rarely leaves the ledge. To one side, the brine pool sits like a black mirror laid into the floor of the world. Its surface is denser than the water above, and when she comes too close, she feels it as a cold boundary that wants to pull her down. The brine is older than the sea around it, sharper, almost oxygenless. It would slow her blood. She keeps to the higher ground, to the living water. Her hollow is just above that dark mirror, and from its entrance she can taste the difference between the two waters. The brine is a heavy, ancient taste. The seep water is sulfurous and warm with chemistry. The incoming tide is fresher, full of oxygen from far away. She reads the world as a map of tastes.

She has lived here long enough to become part of the architecture. A thin white film of bacteria has begun to grow along her dorsal ridge. It is not an infection. It is the same bacterial mat that covers the rocks and shells, and on her it has found another surface. A scale worm lives in her gill slit, not quite a parasite, not quite a guest, feeding on the scraps of her feeding. A small snail once crossed her back and left a trail of mucus that became a path for other tiny grazers. She is a landscape. She is a body and a place at once.

Her world is not made of sight. It is made of lines. The lateral line along her body reads every movement in the water: the clack of a crab’s claw on mussel shell, the sudden flinch of a tube worm plume, the slow pulse of the brine pool shifting as the tide turns. She feels the ice creak overhead as a pressure in her skull, a kind of deep, low music she has always known. When methane bubbles pop from the mud, she feels them as small sharp notes. When a limpet rasps its teeth over carbonate, she feels it as a high fine scratch at the edge of her attention. The city is not silent to her. It is a slow symphony of pressures, most of it too patient to be called sound.

When the tide shifts, she adjusts her body slightly, turning her blunt head toward the current. The water moves through her gills with the evenness of a machine. She is cold, but she has never been warm, so she does not know cold as an absence. She knows it as her own body, as the pace of her blood, as the reason she can wait for hours without hunger. Cold is not suffering here. Cold is the rate at which the city lives.

In the back of her hollow, pressed against the mineral wall, there is a clutch of amber eggs, each no bigger than a pea. She does not guard them the way a bird guards a nest. She simply remains with them. Her body blocks the entrance. Her slow tail-flicks keep oxygen moving over them. She does not know why she stays. The behavior is older than her, older than the ice. But she stays, and the cavity is not truly occupied until she is in it.

She is not the only resident. The mussels are residents. The tube worms are residents. The crabs, the brittle stars, the sea spiders, the ribbon worm, the blind fish—all of them live there. But the eelpout is the one who seems to have been made by the place itself. She is the color of its light. She is the speed of its cold. She is the patience of its slow construction.

When she dies, she will not be wasted. The scale worm will leave her gill. The amphipods will come. The bacteria will spread over her skin and begin to turn her into something else. Her bones will be covered in carbonate. Her hollow will fill, then hollow again. And somewhere else in the city, under the same blue-gray light, another pale eelpout will wait.
