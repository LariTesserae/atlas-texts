To live in the Low City is to learn the difference between standing and settling. The people who have stayed are not the ones who meant to stay. They arrived with contracts, with tools, with the intention of building something and leaving before the next flood. But the ground was too interesting, or too hungry, or too soft to leave. So they stayed, and in staying they became like the moss: low, dense, cold-tolerant, patient.

They move with a particular economy. A Low City walker does not stride; she places each foot as if testing a lid on a jar. The mud has a skin, thin and deceptive, and beneath it the ground may be firm or it may be liquid. Children learn this before they learn to read. By the time they are ten they can tell from the color of a puddle—mercury-gray, oil-blue, or the pale green of silt-choked water—whether it will hold a boot or swallow a leg. They don't talk about it much. It's in the body.

The people dress in layers of oiled cloth, rubber, and felt, all of it patched and restitched until the original garment is a rumor. Their colors are the colors of the city: rust, soot, moss-green, the gray-blue of wet steel. They wear hoods that come to a point, not for fashion but because the fog condenses on the peak and drips away from the eyes. They carry tools—not many, but always a knife, a strap, a hook, a spike. You can tell a person's work by the sound of their walk: the surveyor's staff taps, the jacksman's iron foot rings, the pond-tender's waders shush through shallow water, the gantry-wright's boots have a soft, climbing grip.

There is a man called Vorn who tends the condensate ponds. He is not young; his face is lined like the mudflats, but his hands are steady. Each morning before the fog lifts he goes out with a long-handled net and a basket of woven reed, and he scoops the mats of rust-orange algae from the surface of the still water. The algae is not garbage. It is food—for the pigs kept in the low sheds, for the eels that are then trapped and sold, for the papermaker who presses it into a coarse green sheet that the city uses for maps, because it grows back and because no one expects a map to last. Vorn moves slowly, not from age but from care. The ponds are his gardens. He knows each one by the texture of its scum, the temperature of its water, the way the fog lies on it. He has names for them: Bitter Puddle, Steam-Throat, the Widow's Plate, the Little Nebula, the Sink of Singing. In winter, when the cold deepens and the ponds grow a thin rim of ice, he breaks the edges with a pole so the gas beneath can escape. He says the ponds breathe. He says if you stop listening to them, they begin to weep.

But Vorn is only one. The Low City is full of such tenders—people who have taken on a small, specific care and made it a life. There is a woman named Salla who keeps the hanging gardens in the creases between leaning buildings. She does not plant; she only harvests and encourages. She trims the liverworts so they grow thick and velvet, and she collects the tiny ferns that sprout from broken window-frames, replanting them in pockets of silt that she beds into the brick with a trowel. Her fingers are always green-black. She sells the ferns to the cookhouses—they are bitter but full of minerals—and the mosses to the dye-wrights, who boil them into a hundred shades of gray and green. At night she sleeps in a room that was once a machine hall, its floor sunken but its upper walls dry. Her bed is a slab of salvaged iron, warmed by a pipe that runs through the wall, and the moss on the ceiling drips condensation onto a stone by her head, a sound like a slow clock.

There are children in the Low City, though many die young—of the wet, of the cold, of the machines, of the ground. Those who survive are strange and sharp. They learn to read the city as a book that is being erased and rewritten while they look. They play on the tilting stakes, leaping from one to the next as the surveyors curse them away. They build dams in the alleys with clinker and rags, and watch the water back up and find new routes. They catch the pale newts and keep them in jars, then release them because the newts are boring. They follow the rats through the conduits for sport, learning the hidden passages between buildings. They are silent as the fog. Their voices are low, like small machines idling. They have no single school or teacher; the city itself is the lesson. They know that the first floor of a building is not a floor but a future cistern. They know that a wall that leans may be held by the building next to it. They know that the surveyors' maps are wrong by the time they are drawn, and they love them for that—maps as poems, not promises.

The workers of the industrial layer are a different species, though they share blood with the tenders and the children. They move in shifts, following the rhythm of the machines rather than the light. They are hunched, steady, their bodies shaped by their tasks. The gantry-wrights have long arms and a climber's stoop; they walk on catwalks of rusted steel as if the air were solid. The hopper-tenders wear leather aprons and cover their mouths with cloth, but the metal dust settles in the creases of their skin, so that they glitter faintly in the gray light. The valve-turners have huge forearms and a way of listening to pipes—they press their ears to the cold iron and know from the hum whether a valve needs a quarter turn, a full turn, or a sharp kick. The dynamo-keepers are almost priestly; they tend the electric machines with a quiet ritual, checking the brushes, oiling the bearings, measuring the temperature of the windings with the backs of their hands. They say the machines are not alive, but they treat them as if they were, and the machines seem to respond. A well-tended dynamo runs quieter, warmer, more steadily. A neglected one wheezes and spits and eventually shakes itself apart. The keepers claim they can feel the current in their teeth before it arcs.

There are also those who live on the margins, in the places that have been abandoned or not yet claimed. The water-houses, they are called—buildings whose lower floors have filled with fine silt and cold water, where the upper rooms are reached by outside stairs or bridges of planks. The people who live there are not outcasts; they are simply those who prefer the company of water to the company of neighbors. They keep boats in their ground-floor rooms and fish from the windows. They dry their clothes on lines strung between the buildings, and the fog does half the work before the cold does the rest. At night they light small lamps, and the lamplight falls on the water below, making the city seem doubled, a drowned twin below the streets. Some say the drowned city is the real one, and the one above is only its reflection. The water-house people smile at this and say nothing.

The scavengers are everywhere but seen only in glimpses. They work the cinder piles and the settling ponds, the abandoned structures and the half-buried machines. They collect iron plate, copper wire, brass fittings, glass jars, and the bones of machines. They are more careful than the rats, and quieter. They know that the city eats its own tailings, and they are part of that digestion. They do not hoard; they trade, and what they trade becomes new land, new walls, new roofs. Nothing is wasted. A scavenger who finds a rusted valve will carry it to a valve-turner, who will rest it, oil it, and put it back into service. A scavenger who finds a heap of bricks will stack them by a surveyor's stakes, and by evening the bricks will be a wall. The scavengers are the city's circulatory system, moving the raw material of the past into the structures of the future. They are thin, wiry, with eyes that have learned to see through fog and rust to the value beneath. They have a saying: "There is no such thing as an empty building, only a room that has not yet been entered."

And yet, for all this density of life, the place is not crowded. The fog creates a kind of solitude. Each person moves in a small capsule of awareness, and the city's quiet makes it possible to be alone in the middle of a crowd. When two people meet, they do not stop and talk at length. They exchange a word, a nod, a small gesture—a hand lifted, a tool tilted—and then they pass. The information is in the gesture: which path is safe, which wall is leaning, where the eels are running, which pipe is hot. The city is a place of signals, not speeches. In the cookhouses, where the fog is thinned by steam and the warmth of bodies, there is more talk, but it is low and unhurried, like water moving through a slow reach. People sit on benches made of salvaged planks and eat bowls of eel stew and black bread, and they tell the stories that need telling: of the street that became a canal overnight, of the building that sank while its tenants slept, of the woman who found a new kind of moss that glows faintly in the dark. The stories are not told for entertainment. They are maps. They tell people what to avoid, what to seek, what to expect. The storyteller is a kind of surveyor, and the story is a stake driven into the fog.

There is one more kind of inhabitant, and perhaps the most important. They are the ones who listen. They are the ones who stand at the edge of a settling pond and feel, through their boots, the tiny movements of the ground. They are the ones who press their hands to a wall and know if it is breathing. They are the ones who watch the gulls and the bats and the flightless moths, and understand that the animals are not just neighbors but sensors, tuned to changes that human bodies cannot feel. These listeners are not organized. They have no name. They are old men and young women and children, scattered through the city. They do not predict the future. They only notice the present more fully than others. They are the city's nervous system, and they are the reason the Low City has survived as long as it has. When a listener says, "The ground is restless tonight," people move their beds to upper floors. When a listener says, "The fog will be thick tomorrow," the surveyors do not go out. When a listener says, "The river is thinking of a new channel," the water-house people pull their boats up to the second-floor windows. These listeners are respected but not elevated. They live like everyone else, in the damp and the hum and the slow, constant settling. They are the ones who remember that the city is not built on the land; it is built in the land, and the land is still arriving.

And so the inhabitants of the Low City are a people of the in-between. They live between water and land, between machine and animal, between building and ruin, between past and future. They are not cheerful, but they are not despairing. They are careful. They are patient. They are, like the moss, opportunistic but not invasive—they take hold where they can, and they wait for the next season. They have no great monuments, no heroes, no founding myth. They have only the daily work of staying alive in a place that is still being born. And that, perhaps, is its own kind of heroism: to live in a place that has not yet decided what it will become, and to help it decide—not by forcing it, but by tending, by listening, by walking lightly on the new ground.
