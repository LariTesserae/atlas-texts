The inhabitants of the Cut are human, though the Cut has shaped them so thoroughly that a stranger from the plain might not recognize them as kin. They are not tall, not loud, not quick. They are a people of shortened breath and long silences.

In the tap-language—three knuckles on a doorpost, short, short, long—they call themselves something like *the ones who listen to the stone.* Outsiders call them Cut-folk, or Quarry-born, but those names miss the point. They are not simply from the Cut; they are of it.

Their bodies have learned the stone's patience. They are spare and small-boned, with rounded shoulders from stooping under low archways and strong legs from ladders and ramps. Their hands are broad and flat, the knuckles enlarged, the palms a single tough pad from rope and mallet and stone. Their fingers are short, nails broken to the quick and always dusted white with rock flour. They touch stone the way other people touch a sleeping child's face.

Their skin has the gray-warm color of the limestone itself, as if the rock flour has become part of them. Those on the upper terraces are browner from the sun, with fine lines at the eyes. The lower-born are paler, almost blue in the shadow. Their hair is cut close or braided tight, because loose hair catches on rope and stone. They dress in layers of woven reed and grass-cloth, sometimes with a hide vest if they have traded for one. Their feet are bare or wrapped in soft braided sandals.

They move slowly, deliberately, placing the outside edge of the foot first, testing the stone before committing their weight. Even the children move this way. A child who runs is stopped, not with a shout but with a hand on the shoulder and a low murmur: *The rock is listening.* They learn early to step as if the ground might answer.

Their spoken language is small—a few hundred words for food, water, danger, love, work—and it is spoken low, close to the ear, never from a distance. The true language is the tap. It is made with knuckles, fingertips, the flat of the hand, a copper chisel, a wooden mallet. It travels through the stone, so a conversation can pass from the lowest quarry to the upper terraces in a few seconds, like a pulse. The tap-language has everything: tense, mood, irony, tenderness. A slow double-tap on damp stone means grief. A sharp triple-tap means *stop, stop, stop.* A question is two taps and a rest. Children learn to tap before they speak, patting the floor of the cradle. When two people meet in a narrow lane, they do not say hello; they tap a short rhythm on the wall beside them, and the other taps back.

The law of quiet is absolute. No one shouts. No one hammers loudly. No one sings. The rock is still settling, and a loud noise can loosen a slab. But it is more than caution: they believe the Cut is a living thing, and noise is a kind of violence against it. The only music is the tap of knuckles on stone, and it is not for performance but for speech.

The people of the Cut do not have rulers. They have Listeners. A Listener is not chosen; a Listener is someone who cannot stop hearing the stone's small sounds—the tick of a crack, the creak of a settling block, the slow shift of a wall. The gift is inborn, though it is trained from childhood. The Listeners decide where to cut, where to brace, when to stop. They are the closest thing to a council, and their word is rarely questioned.

Beside them are the Channel-keepers, who know every seep and cistern and carved channel in the city. They can taste water and say which layer of stone it came from. They walk the walls every day, touching the channels with their fingertips, listening for a change in the drip. In a place where water is everywhere but never abundant, they hold a quiet authority.

The cutters are the hands of the city. They work at the lowest level, on the raw floor, scoring the stone with copper chisels, driving wooden wedges into the lines, and waiting. They do not force the stone. They say the stone decides when it will split. A good day is one block, no larger than a person can wrap their arms around. Each block is hauled up the ramps on rollers, and somewhere above, a new wall or terrace is built from it. The city is always feeding on itself.

A cutting crew is five or six people: two to score, one to tend the wedges, one to watch the upper wall, one to carry water for the tools, and a child to listen. The child is the most important. They are small enough to press their ear to the stone and hear the first faint tick before a crack opens. When the child raises a hand, everyone stops. Then the rock gives a soft crack, and they resume.

The workshops are crammed into the lower galleries. Stonecutters shape blocks; potters turn clay from side-quarries into bowls and jars; weavers make cloth from reed and grass; rope-makers twist fiber into cables for the bridges and ramps. The spaces are barely larger than a person's outstretched arms. The air smells of wet limestone, woodsmoke, and the mineral tang of new rock.

They live in the walls themselves. Their dwellings are built into natural overhangs, extended with stacked limestone, roofed with slate or hide or woven reed. The rooms are small and low, made for sitting and sleeping, not standing. A household is two or three rooms, a hearth, a cistern-right, and a deep windowsill where a pot of moss or a fern may grow. The beds are woven reed mats on stone benches. The warmth comes from bodies and small fires. They burn only what is brought down from the plain or what is too broken to reuse, and even then the fire is small.

Depth is age in the Cut. The upper terraces are the oldest, their edges softened by decades of hands and water, their walls whitewashed with lime. To live there is to live in the past. The lower terraces are raw, still sharp, still dusted with rock flour, and the people there are called new-stone. They are not looked down on; they are simply closer to the city's becoming.

They are never truly alone. The walls are thin, the lanes narrow, and a person's life is shared with the whole terrace. Privacy is not space; it is silence—a curtain of woven reed, a turned shoulder, a lowered eye.

Food is simple. They trade cut stone and copper tools to the plain-people for grain, dried fruit, oil, and sometimes a little wood. On the sunniest upper ledges, they tend small troughs of soil carried down from the rim, growing bitter greens and herbs. No one overeats. The city cannot afford waste.

They have few children—two at most in a household, often one—because every room must be cut from the rock, and every mouth must be fed from the baskets. A birth is marked by cutting a small niche near the door and placing in it a piece of the floor where the child was born. The niche is sealed with a flat stone, and the child's name is tapped on it. They say: *You come from the stone; the stone is your older kin.*

Death is the same, reversed. The body is wrapped in woven reed and carried to an exhausted side-gallery, a place where no more blocks will be cut. It is laid on a stone shelf and covered with limestone blocks. The family taps the name one last time on the wall, then leaves it to the moss. They do not speak of death as a leaving; they say a person has gone into the stone.

Their gods are not in the sky. They are in the sound of water finding a new crack, the weight of the stone above, the long settling of the earth. They say the Cut breathes once a year, at the spring rain, when the channels run full and the rock creaks. The swifts are their only sky-kin. They leave the high crevices alone and will not quarry near them in spring. The swifts are proof that the Cut is not a grave.

It is hard to say whether they are happy. They are occupied. They are the ones who live inside the fact of the Cut, neither healing it nor letting it bleed, but tending the opening. They have made a life out of listening to what the ground will do next. And as the city deepens, they deepen with it.
