At first the place looks as if it is crowded with separate animals. But after a while you begin to feel that the animals are not living in the basin; they are the basin living through many small bodies. The dragonflies hover at eye height on wings that never change pitch. They are not hunting. They are not mating. They are holding their place in the air, as if the air would sag without them. The tree frogs sit in dense rows on every wet leaf, their throats pulsing in a cycle that never varies. They do not sing to one another. The note they make is continuous, and if one misses a beat, the next frog catches it, and the chord does not break. They are the basin’s voice, but the voice does not speak.

The water is opaque with tadpoles, small fish, freshwater shrimp, and pale worms. They turn in the same water, their movements paired and canceling. The surface dimples and smooths, dimples and smooths. The snakes drape over branches in braided tangles, not to fight or mate but because the space between them has become too warm and wet to remain empty. Lizards grip the trunks the way moss does. Birds sit shoulder to shoulder, feathers nearly touching, eyes half-closed. Bats hang beneath leaves in soft, breathing clusters. They are not separate colonies; they are a single blanket of warm, breathing tissue. They build nothing, because they are already part of something built.

The larger shapes are the most patient. The tapir-like animal stands knee-deep in a warm pool, motionless except for the slow blink of an eye. The long-necked turtle is half sunk in the mud, its shell furred with algae. They do not rest; they are being kept. The place has no night, no season, no predator, no hunger, so their bodies have slowed to the speed of mineral deposit. A thin crust of tufa is forming on their lower parts, and they do not mind, because they are not going anywhere.

But the true inhabitant is not any one of these. It is beneath and through them. In the deepest part of the basin, where the pools refuse to separate, there is a membrane. At first it looks like water lying over the mineral paste, but it is not water. It is a flat, living sheet, pale and faintly veined, stretched over the warm ground. Its edges are ragged and merge with the terraces. The tapir stands on it. The turtle has sunk into it, and the membrane holds them without breaking. It is not a pool, though it shimmers like one. It is a body—the largest single body in the valley, as wide as the lowest places, but no thicker than a hand.

This membrane is what lives here. It has no head, no mouth, no limbs, no eyes. It breathes through its entire surface. The moisture that beads on its upper side is its exhalation; the seep that rises from the ground below is its inhalation. The two waters are the same water, passed back and forth without gain or loss. It does not eat, because the mineral paste dissolves into its underside at the same rate that its underside dissolves back into the paste. It does not grow, because the chemistry is exactly balanced. It does not age, because there is no season to mark age. It does not think in sequences; if it thinks at all, it is a single sustained sensation—warm, wet, dim, full.

The frogs are its voice. The insects are its breath. The birds and bats are its blinking. The snakes are its tendons, holding the branches together. The roots and mycelia are its nerves, not firing but resting in the same pattern. The whole basin is one animal that has not yet decided to become many, and may never decide. It is not a god or a spirit. It is the place’s life, gathered into a skin.

If you stood there long enough, the roar would stop sounding like many small voices. It would become the sound of a body holding one note. The warmth would feel not like weather but like the body’s own heat. The water on your skin would seem less like condensation and more like sweat, or tears, or saliva—not hostile, just alive. You would understand that you are not standing in the place. You are standing on it, or in it, and the thing that lives there is the place itself: unfinished, uneroded, unbegun, and utterly present.

If it needed a name, you might call it the Unbegun. But it would not answer. It does not know its name, because it has never needed to be separate from the warm, wet air, or the leaves, or the frogs, or you.
