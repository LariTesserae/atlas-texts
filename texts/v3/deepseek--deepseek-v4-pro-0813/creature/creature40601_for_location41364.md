The place is not empty. It only seems that way because its residents are shaped like their materials: low, brown, water-logged, deliberate. They are not in the open. They are in the water, or just behind the alder, or inside the mounds that look like hills but are not hills.

The first sign is a wake. It crosses a pool without sound, a V-shaped fold in the grey surface, and then a head appears—sleek, blunt, dark-eyed—and vanishes again. It is not a fish. It is too aware. It has a nose, long whiskers, small rounded ears. It makes no noise.

The ones who live here are beavers. They are the dam-makers, the canal-diggers, the ones who cannot leave running water alone. The dams are theirs, every stick and stone. The lodges are theirs, each one a fort of mud and branches rising out of the water. The canals are theirs, dug by repeated swimming and carrying until a beaver can move from one pool to another without dragging itself overland. Even the felled willows are theirs, cut in a circle of pale splinters at the base and floated to where they are needed.

In the water they are all competence. A beaver swims with its front paws tucked against its chest or paddling, its hind feet webbed and powerful, its tail trailing behind like a leather rudder. The tail is broad, flat, dark, scaled. It is not a trowel. It is a warning drum, a swimming aid, a prop when the animal sits upright to gnaw, a fat reserve in winter. When an adult slaps the water, the sound is a flat crack that carries down the valley, and every other beaver disappears.

On land they are slower. They waddle with a rolling gait, rump high, belly low. A beaver carrying mud will walk on its hind legs, holding a load of mud and stones against its chest with its front paws, stepping carefully down a bank and into the water. Its footprints are distinctive: broad hind feet, small hand-like front feet, and sometimes a smooth drag mark where the tail touched. The mud paths are packed by use, the same routes over and over from pond to dam to woodlot.

Their teeth are the only tools they use. They are orange, thick, chisel-edged, and they grow continuously, worn down by wood and stone. With them a beaver can cut a willow as thick as a wrist in a few minutes. It bites out a wedge, then another, working around the trunk until the tree creaks and leans and falls. The sound is not loud—a splintering, a hiss of leaves, a soft thud in the moss. Then the beaver strips the bark in long curls, eating the cambium, and cuts the branches into lengths. Some branches go to the food raft. Some go to the dam. Some are woven into the lodge. The peeled sticks are left pale and neat.

They are not solitary. A lodge holds a family: a mated pair, the kits born in spring, and sometimes the yearlings from the previous year. The parents are patient architects. The kits are small, dark, buoyant, still learning to dive. They stay close to the lodge, nursing inside, then follow the adults on short trips. Their tails are small and dark, not yet the wide paddles of adults. The yearlings carry sticks and mud with less skill than the adults but with the same compulsion. They learn by doing, and the valley is full of their practice: a spillway patched too loosely, a canal that dead-ends, a dam section that slumps and must be rebuilt.

Inside the lodge, it is dark and warm enough to smell of musk, wet wood, and the grass used for bedding. The chamber is above the waterline, reached by underwater tunnels. A beaver enters without a ripple and climbs up through a narrow passage, shakes itself, and settles among the others. The walls are thick, shaggy with mud and sticks, and in winter the outer layers freeze but the inner chamber stays just above the cold. The family sleeps in a pile, breathing slowly, tails and bodies tangled. Sometimes one will groom another with a split claw on its hind foot, drawing the fur through the claw to pick out debris.

They are driven by water. More precisely, they are driven by the sound of water running where it should be still. If a spillway wears a notch, a beaver will find it in the dark. It will bring mud from the canal bottom, stones from the bank, sticks cut to length, and pack them into the breach. It will pat the mud smooth with its forepaws, then sniff, then pat again. The work is constant because the water is constant. The valley does not let them rest. A dam is never finished; it is only held. The water is always finding a way through, and the beavers are always finding it first.

But they are not the only ones living here. The valley is a tenement. Muskrats use the same water, smaller and rounder, with thin tails and less interest in engineering. They build small huts of cattails and mud, or dig bank burrows, and they move through the canals leaving finer tracks. The beavers tolerate them, mostly. An otter is different. It comes through the pools like a long, low ribbon of muscle, curious and quick, and it will take a kit if it can. The beavers know this. When an otter appears, the tail slaps come fast, and the water is suddenly empty. Mink follow the dam tops, dark and sinuous, hunting frogs, fish, and young muskrats. A heron stands at the spillway for an hour, grey as the light, and then stabs the water and lifts a fish. Small brown birds work the willows, quiet.

The fish are always there, dark shapes under the polished surface. They wait in the cold pools, under the ice in winter, in the slow places behind the dams. The beavers do not hunt them; the fish are simply part of the water, moving through the maze the beavers built. The dams slow the streams, make deep water, and the fish find their way up through gaps. The valley is as much theirs as the builders’, though they leave no mark except an occasional ripple.

The place is a collaboration. The beavers cut willow; the willow sends up new shoots from the stump, and the new shoots become food and building material again. The dams trap silt; the silt becomes mud; the mud grows moss; the moss covers the dams until they look like natural hills. The beavers are the hands of the valley, but the valley is also shaping them. They are not an outside force. They are born in the lodges, raised in the pools, and they die in the water. Their bones sink into the silt and become part of the next dam.

At dusk, the valley becomes loud with small sounds. A beaver surfaces, breathes, dives. Another gnaws a willow stem, the sound rhythmic and rough, like a saw made of bone. In a backwater, two kits wrestle, pushing each other under and climbing out again. A yearling drags a branch down a canal, swimming with its head just above the water, the branch trailing behind. An adult slaps its tail once, and the wrestling stops. The only sound is water moving over the dams, soft as exhalation.

By morning, the notch in the lower dam will be patched. The food raft will be larger. A new willow will be down. The tracks will be fresh around the spillway, and the mud will be smoothed by bodies sliding back into the water. The place is not empty. It is full of architects, all of them brown and quiet, working in the cold and the grey, building the valley deeper into itself.
