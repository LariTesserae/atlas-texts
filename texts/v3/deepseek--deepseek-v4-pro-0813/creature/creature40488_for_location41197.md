The Undersalt does not have inhabitants in the way a forest has inhabitants. It has a single slow body of life, pressed into the dark and divided only by appetite. But to describe it, I must separate that body into its parts.

Start with the vents. The chimneys look like mineral, and they are, but they are also animals. Each spire is a colony of tube-worms, blind and mouthless at the crown, opening only as a dense fringe of gray-red tendrils when the warm gas rises. The worms do not eat in any way a surface creature would understand. Their bodies host a dry chemistry of sulfur and copper, a slow burning that turns the vent’s exhalation into flesh. They add aragonite to their tubes as they grow, layer upon layer, so the oldest chimneys are like ancient cypress trunks, sharp and fluted, rising from floor to ceiling. When a worm dies, it dries without rotting. Its body flakes away in paper-thin sheets, and those flakes are the first food of the Undersalt.

Then comes the pavement. The flat, gray, hand-sized animals are called salt-lice in some records, though they are not lice. They are living cobblestones. They lie edge to edge in dense, tessellated sheets, their margins fringed with gas-exchanging gills that open and close too slowly to be seen as movement. Their backs are crusted with tiny halite crystals, so they look like pieces of the floor that have grown muscle and learned to feed. When a flake from the worm forest settles onto them, one of them opens a narrow mouth along its underside and takes the flake in. The others do not stir. They are not competitors; they are more like a single tissue spread over the salt, a skin that covers the chamber floor. Sometimes a slow ripple passes through ten thousand of them, a wave of muscle memory, and then they settle again.

Then there are the ticks. They are small, blind, armored, and they tap. Their shells are salt-white, almost translucent, and they strike them against the hard salt in a constant, delicate percussion. The sound is the only regular voice of the place, a fine rain of clicks in the absolute quiet. They eat what the pavement sheds: fragments of gill-fringe, dust from old shells, the crumbs of mummified flesh. They are the gravediggers, the cleaners, the ones who map the dark by listening to how their own shells ring against the salt.

Then the crawlers. They are dog-sized, six-legged, their armor fused with salt crystals so that they look like walking slabs of geology. They move so slowly that a single step can take an hour, and the sound of that step arrives a second after the movement, as if the dark itself is translating it. They lower themselves over a patch of salt-lice and take one or two, never more. Their mouths are soft, hidden beneath the hard shell, and the pavement closes over the wound immediately. The crawler then stands still for days, digesting, adding a new growth ring to its shell. They die standing, most of them. Then they become part of the shell-drifts, sharp-edged and uneroded, which may never decay.

But above all these, or woven through them, are the ones who left the marks.

I do not know their name. They made the shaft with walls polished smooth by old hands, and that alone tells you they have been here longer than any surface record. They are shorter than a human, or at least their doorways are too low to walk through upright. They stoop, or crawl, or perhaps bent walking is simply the shape of their reverence. They have hands with too many fingers. The halite figures they left show this clearly: the hands spread into fans of seven or eight long fingers, each with too many joints. They have no eyes. The figures have smooth heads, no eye sockets, only a faint spiral where an eye might be. Whether they were always blind or became blind in the dark, the dark now fits them exactly.

They build low walls of salt bricks across passes between vents—too low to be walls against anything living, too deliberate to be anything but meaning. Each brick is carved with a spiral. The same spiral repeats on the black glass blades, on the copper needles, on the doorposts, on the shells of the animals outside. It may be a name, a map, a calendar, a prayer, or the shape of warm gas rising in still air. I have looked at it for a long time, and I still do not know.

The shelters are half-domed, made of salt blocks and shed carapace. Their doorways are too low to enter standing. Inside, the floor is swept clean, and the sweep marks are still visible. That is the most disturbing detail: the broom marks have not worn away, because nothing in the Undersalt wears away. Dust does not blow. Water does not drip. No footstep erases what came before. The marks may be a thousand years old, or ten thousand, and they look as if the sweeper just left the room. The tools—stone basins, copper needles, black glass blades, braided sinew now mineralized—are set down, not abandoned. The copper has the green-black patina of sulfur in a waterless world, which takes a very long time. The sinew has turned to brittle stone. But the blades are still sharp.

So the builders are not gone. They are still here. I am certain of it, even if I have never clearly seen one. They stand among the piles of salt-lice, indistinguishable by design. Their skin is the same gray as the floor, their stillness the same stillness. They do not hide from you. They simply do not move in a place where movement is scarce. If you pass one, you may mistake it for a tilted slab of salt, or a low wall, or a mineral chimney that has fallen and caught itself.

When you turn off your lamp, the green points emerge slowly, like a sky turned inside out. Those points belong to the animals—small lights on shells, on gill-fringes, on the joints of crawlers, dim as old stars. The darkness is not empty. It is packed close with life, with sharp mineral, with warm gas, with old walls, with silence. And among the green points, if you watch long enough, there are shapes that are not green. They are only a deeper blackness, a body-shaped absence, standing exactly where no one is supposed to be.

The Undersalt does not breathe. It waits. Its citizens—the tube-worms, the salt-lice, the ticks, the crawlers, the spiral-makers—wait with it. They are not lorded over by time the way we are. They are held at the same warm temperature, in the same black air, moving at the speed of salt becoming crystal. If you want to see one of the builders, do not turn on your lamp. Do not call out. Stand still until your own breathing stops being vandalism. Then, after a very long time, one of them might turn its head. And you will understand that the place was never empty. It was only too patient to be seen.
