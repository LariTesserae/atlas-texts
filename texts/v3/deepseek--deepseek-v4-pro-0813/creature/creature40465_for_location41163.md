The first sign is not a body. It is a path through the plant mat.

At first the floor looks unbroken, a continuous felt of tiny rosettes and cold stems from wall to wall. But if you stand far enough back, you see a slight settling in the wool, a route where the leaves have been pressed flat and the roots have learned not to spring back. It runs from the control kiosk to the running pump, out toward the nearest sump pool, then back again. A closed loop, no wider than one person. The rest of the chamber is untrodden.

Beside the kiosk, a tin cup sits on the concrete plinth. The tea in it is old, skinned over, but the rim is not rusted; it is polished from use. A pair of rubber boots is set neatly by the step, toes pointed inward. Moss has begun to grow in the treads, not because the boots are abandoned but because they are left there long enough between rounds. Above them, on a hook, hangs a waxed canvas coat. In its seams, small gray leaves have taken root, feeding on condensation and the dust of the place. The coat is not worn by a person so much as colonized by the same slow life that covers the floor.

You do not see him at first. You see a shadow against the hot casing of the running pump, a figure folded into a crouch so still that the red indicator light blinks on his back like a second heart. He is not a large man. The gallery makes him small. His shoulders are rounded, not with age but with the habit of listening. One bare hand is pressed flat against the pump housing. The other rests on his knee, fingers slightly curled. His eyes are open, pale gray in the green lamplight, and they blink slowly, as if he is not watching but measuring.

He does not hear you approach. The noise here is too total for that. It comes up through the concrete and along his jaw, through the soles of his boots and into the long bones of his legs. When he stands, he does so without pushing with his hands, unfolding like a plant straightening toward light. He wears a wool sweater under the waxed coat, unraveling at the cuffs. His trousers are patched with duct tape. His boots are old but oiled, and the left sole has a split filled with black rubber compound. He is not dirty. He is stained, the way the walls are stained, by years of oil and condensation and the green breath of the mat.

His face is gaunt, lined, the skin pale and rough. There is a small scar across his left eyebrow, and the backs of his hands are cross-hatched with fine white marks from the sharp stone. His fingernails are short and rimmed with oil. When he moves, he does not hurry. Every gesture seems to cost him a little heat, and he spends it carefully.

He walks past you without surprise. He goes to the sump, steps onto a board laid across the dampest patch, and crouches again. He takes a small flashlight from his pocket, not to see by, but to shine into the black water. In the beam, blind amphipods drift and scatter. He watches them with the patience of a man for whom time has become a physical property, like the cold.

Behind the kiosk, in the angle of the concrete plinth and the wall, is his bed. It is a cot with a rubber mattress and two wool blankets. A clear plastic sheet is tacked above it, carrying the condensation into a tin gutter. A small gas stove on a steel shelf holds a kettle. There are tins of beans, tomatoes, peaches, sardines. A loaf of bread in a plastic bag. A block of soap wrapped in cloth. A mirror pitted with moisture. A paperback novel swollen to twice its size. It is not a home. It is a campsite that has been kept for years.

He sits on an upturned bucket, pours water from a blue jug into the kettle, lights the gas. The flame is blue and steady, the only thing in the chamber that does not lean toward the lamps. He sets two cups on the concrete, handles turned the same way. Then he writes in a notebook. Not the official logbook—that one is locked in the kiosk, months old—but a green cloth book he keeps in an inside pocket. He writes with a pencil, in small block capitals, and when he finishes he blows on the page though there is no dust.

If you ask how long he has been here, he taps the side of his head. “Eleven years,” he says. His voice is high and flat, pitched to carry over the noise, or maybe just worn into that register. “But the pumps have been here longer.”

He does not give his name, or he gives it as if it belongs to someone else. “Nobody uses it,” he says. “The pumps have better names.” He points to them one by one. “The Heart. The Sleeper. The Dead Man.” The Heart is the one still running. The Sleeper could be brought back if the water rose. The Dead Man will not run again. He says this without sentiment, as if naming the dead is only practical.

He does not hate the plant mat. He calls it the wool. He says it holds the floor together. “Without it, the dust would rise,” he says. “And the dust is not dirt. It is rock that has not learned to be soil.” He clears it from the pump controls with a soft brush, not a knife. When he walks, he follows the path he has worn, so the wool can grow. He has even transplanted a few of the paler rosettes into a shallow tin near his cot, under a flickering lamp. He waters them with a dropper. They are not for food. They are for company, or maybe for the same reason he keeps the cups turned the same way.

He talks about the sound. “At first I wore earplugs,” he says. “Then I stopped. Not because I wanted to go deaf. Because the noise is information. The pump has a pulse. The transformer has a note. The compressor has a schedule. The loose panel—” he points into the dark, “—it taps only when the air pressure changes. If you block it all out, you are walking through the place blind.” He taps his sternum. “Now I hear with this.”

You ask about the surface. He looks up at the ventilation shafts, where the gray columns of daylight are just visible. “I go up when I need to,” he says. “Not often. Up there, the air is too thin. Too quiet. The sky moves too much.” He says it without bitterness. It is simply the difference between two stations.

He pours the tea. It is strong and black, and he drinks it without sugar. The two of you sit in the noise, which after a while becomes less like sound and more like silence, a silence with a shape. He looks at the pool where the lamps are reflected as scattered orange stars. “Some nights,” he says, “I think the whole place is breathing. The pumps are the lungs. The ventilation shafts are the throat. The wool is the skin.” He pauses. “But maybe I have been down too long.”

When the compressor stops, he stops talking. For a few seconds, the chamber is not silent but smaller. You can hear the individual drips, distant and distinct. You can hear his breath, slow and even. He closes his eyes. Then the compressor starts again, and the sound closes over you both.

He does not ask you to stay. He does not ask you to leave. He rinses the cups in a bucket and sets them back on the kiosk step. Then he takes a shovel, the handle worn smooth, and walks toward a sump that has begun to silt. His figure grows small in the lamplight, then dissolves into the dark beyond the pump. The path he has worn is the last thing you see, a faint depression in the wool. It is not a path to anywhere. It is a loop, a closed circuit, as if he is not going somewhere but keeping time.

By the time you leave, he has become part of the place again, another shape among the shapes. The red light on the Heart blinks. The plant mat leans toward the lamp. The water in the pools holds its slow constellation. And the sound, the long patient noise, goes on as if it had never been interrupted, as if it has been waiting for you to leave.
