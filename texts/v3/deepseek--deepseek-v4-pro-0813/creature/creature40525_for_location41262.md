At first, the hollow seems to have no single inhabitant. The frogs are too regular, the tadpoles too numerous, the beetles too methodical—but none of them is the tenant. They are more like the ticks of a clock, not the clock.

Then you begin to notice the floor.

The mat is not simply a plant. Under the pale green skin there is another skin, darker, with a grain like muscle. Where your boot pressed, the imprint does not fill with rainwater but with a clear, warm fluid that smells of iron and peat. The mat is not growing on soil. It is growing on something that is itself growing.

The stacks are the first clue you can touch. Their horny ridges are not annual rings. They are stacked valves, the old breathing tubes of a body that has outlived them. Each stack is hollow all the way down, not just at the top. If you put your ear to one, you hear not only the chuff but a deeper sound—a wet, rhythmic rush, like blood moving through a great artery. The stack is warm, and not from the sun. It is warm from inside.

The pools are not scattered randomly. They spiral inward, or they radiate from a center. The largest pool, the one you did not see at first because it lies exactly in the middle, is almost perfectly round. Its surface is not water but a membrane, the oil film thickened to a skin. It domes slightly, rising and falling with the thudding. That is the dweller breathing.

If you wait until the light begins to change—though it never changes much—you may see the membrane part. It does not tear. It unzips along a seam that is not a seam but a mouth. Two pale lips, thick and soft, slide apart. Inside is not air but water darker than the rest, and something moves in it: a row of translucent fingers, or gills, or the cilia of a gut. The lips open, and the hollow exhales. A bubble of gas rises, not from a stack but from the mouth itself. It smells of methane and warm mud and something sweet, like overripe fruit.

The inhabitant is the hollow. Or rather, the hollow is its body.

What you have been walking on is its back. The mat is a garden it grows on its own skin—a pelt of liverwort that feeds on its sweat. The stacks are its breathing siphons, thickened over centuries until they are hard as horn. The pools are the openings of its filter chambers, where water from the old sea, trapped underground, is cycled through its body and expelled. The frogs are its commensals, sitting on its skin like oxpeckers on a rhino, eating the small flies that would otherwise lay eggs in its membranes. Their click is not a call to one another. It is a response to the electrical pulse of its heart, which they feel through their feet.

It has no limbs, no head, no eyes. It may once have had a shell, but the shell has dissolved or been absorbed, leaving only the leathery mantle. It is shaped like a huge, flattened worm or a giant flattened clam, as wide as the hollow and as deep as the water table. Its mouth is the central pool. Its vent is the stack you first mistook for a dead stalk. Its body is segmented, each segment bearing a pair of siphons, though some have fused or become clogged, so the stacks are irregular. The thudding is not a machine. It is the pulse of a muscular piston that drives water through its body. The hiss is the sound of that water passing over its gills. The chuff is gas being pressed out of solution.

It is not aware of you in the way a mammal would be. It has no brain, only a rope of nerves along its belly. But it knows you are there. When you stepped over the rim, you stepped onto its skin. It felt your weight as a change in pressure. It slowed its heart for a moment, testing you. Then it decided you were not a predator, or not a threat, and resumed its rhythm. The frogs, in turn, resumed their clicking.

This is what lives here: not a beast you can see whole, but a body so large and so still that you can only perceive it as a place. It has been here since the sea pulled back, since the plain was a marsh, since the first frog called. It will be here until the water below runs dry or the sun changes. It does not need to move because the world moves through it.

When you leave, you step off its body. The soft rim is the edge of its mantle, thickened with moss. The dry plain is its old world. The faint thudding underfoot is its heartbeat, still going—the only machine that was never built.
