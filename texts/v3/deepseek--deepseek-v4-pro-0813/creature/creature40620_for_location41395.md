The ones who live here are not hidden; they are the walls.

A binder is a soft, translucent sac no larger than a human hand, filled with ammonia and a cold, slow metabolism. It lives inside the mineral, in a chamber it has grown around itself. From its surface extend ciliated filaments—each one a thread of living glass, no thicker than a hair—through pores in the wall. The cilia wave in the thin air, not quickly, but with a patient, combing motion. They catch dust, sulfur compounds, and the occasional spore of a silica fern. The binder draws these into its body, dissolves the useful parts in ammonia, and uses the rest to build more wall.

The wall is not stone that the binder lives in. It is the binder’s own accretion, layer upon layer of sulfate and anhydrite, glued with an organic resin that darkens with age. The living part is only the thin inner skin, the soft body; the rest is mineral, deposited and abandoned. A tower is a cairn of dead binders and living ones, growing upward by the width of a cilium each year.

Each binder is not alone. It is connected to its colony by thin channels of fluid and chemical signals. The colony is a lineage, a single genetic line spread through dozens of towers and galleries. It does not think, but it responds. If a wall is damaged, the nearby binders seal the breach with resin. If a spire leans, they thicken the base. If a neighboring colony grows too close, they build a facing wall and fill the gap with a bitter mineral the others will not touch.

The streets are the gaps between colonies, the places where two territories press against each other without overgrowing. They are not for walking. They are the city’s boundaries, narrow and winding, sometimes only a hand’s width. Where colonies meet, the walls are dense and scarred. Sometimes a gallery of one colony will cross the gap, a blind tube of mineral and cilia. The other colony grows over it, sealing it shut. The first colony abandons the gallery, leaving a sealed tunnel, a fossil of a border dispute. Over centuries the city accumulates these scars, and the streets become a labyrinth of old rivalries.

The towers are not buildings. They are ventilation organs. The pale mineral catches the small sun’s heat, and the air inside rises, drawing fresh air in at the base. The hollow interior is lined with ciliated galleries, each one a throat of living tissue. The binders’ cilia comb dust from the rising air. At the top, the air exits through a ring of small pores, carrying waste gases. The whole settlement is a slow machine, powered by the temperature difference between the ground and the dim sky. It takes a year for a particle of air to move from one end of a tower to the other.

Inside, the galleries are not empty. They are lined with cilia and the soft glow of the binders’ bodies. The walls filter the dim light to an amber dusk. A few kilograms of living tissue are spread through a tower, so thin that a single human hand could crush a colony. But the colony has no single point of failure. It is a web.

A new spire begins as a single binder that lands as a speck of resin and mineral dust. It secretes a ring of anhydrite around itself. The ring grows upward, and the binder follows, adding material at the rim. Other binders join, settling on the inner surface. They connect their cilia into a common net. The spire grows tall and hollow. The first binder dies and becomes a resin stain in the mineral, but its descendants continue. Over decades, the spire thickens and branches. It may fuse with neighboring spires to form arches. It may split at the top into multiple points. The colony inside is not a single animal but a continuous sheet of living tissue, a skin of cilia and ammonia-filled sacs. Where the sheet is thickest, the wall grows fastest. Where it is thinnest, the wall is translucent and amber.

Around the mouths of the ventilation pores, the air is richer in sulfur dioxide. That is where the glassy ferns grow. They are not green. They are the color of tarnished glass, grey-violet, with fronds that branch like miniature antlers. Each frond is made of silica, deposited cell by cell. The plant is a chemical garden, growing where the city exhales. If a frond breaks, it oozes a blue fluid—ammonia and dissolved sulfur—which evaporates in minutes, leaving a white salt bloom that glitters like hoarfrost. The ferns have no roots. They attach to the mineral with a crust of silica and absorb gases directly through their skin. They grow a few millimeters a year. They can survive cold and dark, but not dryness. The air is almost always dry, so they grow only in the microclimates where the city’s breath keeps the humidity just high enough.

The animals are rare and slow. The commonest is the dust grazer, a creature the size of a thumb. It is armored with faceted plates of pyrite, each one a perfect crystal. It looks like a walking piece of the city, a fallen chip of spire that has learned to move. It has six pairs of short legs, each ending in a chisel-like claw. It moves over the mineral plates and scrapes the film of dust and sulfur compounds from the surface. Its mouth is a soft, rasping disk. As it feeds, it leaves a clean trail behind it, a pale line in the darker dust. The grazer is slow because its food is poor. It must scrape for hours to get enough. It moves mostly at midday, when the temperature is high enough for its muscles to work. At night it stops, and its armor freezes to the ground.

The grazer’s only predator is the sulfur skulk. The skulk is a pale, jointed worm, longer than a hand, with a body so translucent you can see the bluish fluid inside. Its head is hidden under a hood of flexible mineral, like a helmet of milky quartz. Inside the hood is a single feeding arm, folded like a pocket knife. The arm is serrated on one edge, and its tip is a sharp point. The skulk lives in deep cracks, where it waits. It can wait for a year, two years, its metabolism slowed to almost nothing. It feels the world through the stone, sensing the tiny vibrations of a grazer’s feet. When the grazer is close enough, the skulk unfolds the arm. It does not strike like a snake. It extends the arm with a slow hydraulic pressure, the serrated edge pressing against the grazer’s shell. The grazer’s armor is strong, but the skulk’s arm is patient. It saws back and forth, a hundredth of a millimeter at a time. The sound, if it could be heard, would be a faint ticking, like a stone cracking in the cold. After a while, the shell gives way. The skulk pulls out the soft tissue and retreats, leaving the hollow pyrite plates scattered in the dust. It will not need to eat again for months.

These lives are not separate. The city changes the air, and the air changes the dust, and the dust feeds the grazers, and the grazers feed the skulks. The binders grow, and the ferns grow in their breath. The skulks hide in the cracks made by the daily temperature cycle. The grazers follow the streets between colonies because the dust collects there. When a tower collapses, the fall is almost silent. The air is too thin to carry the crash far. You feel a low tremor through the ground. Dust rises in a pale cloud and hangs for minutes. The binders in the lower part of the spire may survive, sealed behind mineral scabs. They begin to grow a new tower from the stump. The fallen upper part becomes a long, sharp ridge on the basin floor. Within a year, ferns grow in its cracks, grazers feed on the dust it sheds, and a skulk may move into the hollow core.

The binders do not see the sky. They feel the heat of the sun on their mineral walls. They know the day not by light but by the expansion of stone. At dawn, the walls warm and the cilia extend. At dusk, they contract. When a dust devil passes, the cilia flatten, the grazers anchor to the rock, the ferns shiver and shed a few glassy needles. The settling dust feeds the binders.

The binders are not people. But they are not mere things. They are the closest thing this place has to an inner life—not thought, but a slow, chemical awareness of pressure, heat, and the presence of other colonies. The grazers know the taste of the dust. The skulks know the weight of a footstep. The ferns know the breath of the city. And the city itself is not a thing but a relationship, a slow negotiation between air and mineral, ammonia and sulfur, growth and collapse. It is alive, but not conscious. It keeps its own time.

If you stood there long enough, you would stop seeing a town at all. You would see a body of many bodies, breathing once a year, thinking in stone.
