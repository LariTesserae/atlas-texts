No one lives in the Condensate Works the way a person lives anywhere. The sign is gone, and whatever human presence once held the place has long since drained out. But the place is not empty. It is tenanted by things that have learned to live on rust and seepage, and some of them have been there long enough to become part of the floor.

The first resident is the film. It is not plant, not animal, not quite fungus, but a living patina that eats iron and sulfur out of the water. It spreads in tide-lines across the concrete and the weeping wall, brown-black and faintly iridescent, like old gasoline. It does not need light. It needs only water and whatever the old Works still gives up. Without it, none of the others would have a foothold. It is the base of everything; it is the skin of the place.

On that skin lives the shell-carpet. At first you mistake them for cobbles, for old rivets, for parts of the floor. But they are animals. Small conical shells, something between limpets and barnacles, cluster so thickly near the waterline that they form a continuous paved skin. Some are no bigger than a lentil, others the size of a thumbnail. Their shells are not plain calcium. They have taken on the Works. The oldest carry rims of iron and rust scale, as if each animal has learned to plate its own roof with the place. They are slow, grazing the film with a rasp too fine to hear. When one shifts, there is a faint wet click, and the shells around it resettle like teeth in a jaw. They do not move far. Their whole territory is the distance between one waterline and the next, but they have held it for generations. Their shells build up over older shells, over rust, over concrete, over older shells again.

Above the shell-carpet, on the damp walls and around the sump, the tube-dwellers make their own kind of city. Their tubes are small chimneys of sand, crumbled concrete, and flecks of old brass, some of them green with copper salts. From each tube, a crown of pale tentacles opens and closes, not a flower but a net. They filter the water for whatever the mineral film releases, whatever the limpets loosen, whatever the pump spits back. They are numerous in a way that only becomes visible when your shadow falls on them. Then the crowns close in a wave, not fast but synchronized, and the wall becomes a field of shut mouths. They have no faces, but they know the difference between the fog-light and a passing shape. They do not need to know more.

Between the fixed residents move the first of the wanderers. Amphipods the size of rice grains pick their way on thread-legs, pale as waterlogged starch, bodies curled like commas. They are scavengers and opportunists. They gather where the pump spits water, testing the new film that forms there. They work the edges of the tube-dwellers’ crowns, stealing whatever the current brings. They climb over the limpets without disturbing them much. If you stand still too long, they will climb your boots. It is not aggression. To them you are not a threat; you are new terrain, wet and still and possibly edible, and they are only testing the surface.

Then come the flatworms. They are black and glossy, shaped like spilled ink that has learned direction. They move over the concrete without legs, gliding on a layer of slime so thin it is only visible as a faint shine. Their bodies are simple, but they hunt with a slow patience. They eat the amphipods, the larvae of the tube-dwellers, the weak and the dead. They do not hurry. Their hunting is a soft constriction, a blanket of muscle laid over whatever they find. When one crosses a patch of iridescent film, the film parts around it, then closes behind it like water. They are not malevolent. They are only hunger given a shape that moves.

In the troughs and low places, the heaviest visible residents lie half-buried. They are blunt-headed eel-things, the color of wet clay, with small eyes that seem to do nothing and skin that breathes water. They wait with only the tops of their heads showing, sometimes for hours, sometimes for longer. When a flatworm passes too near, they stir. When the water moves, they move with it, writhing upstream into pipes too dark to see. They are not fierce, but they are patient in a way the smaller animals are not. They may be the oldest things here, older than the pump cycle, older than the last human footprints. They know the paths between the sump and the pipe-mouths by feel, by the taste of the water, by the faint electric charge in the wet.

There is one resident you will not see until you look at the pump itself. A soft gray thing the size of a hand is pressed against the warm brass housing. It is not a dead rag or a smear of mineral paste. It is alive. Its back is studded with copper-colored granules, as if it has been slowly encrusted by the pump’s breath. It expands and contracts with the pump’s cycle—swelling as the machinery draws in, settling as it clunks and spits. It has no face, but there is a small opening at one end that opens when the water flows. It seems to live off the warmth, the vibration, the mineral sweat of the brass. It does not flinch at the little electric pop, the one that makes the hair on your arms stand up. It may be the only resident that treats the pump as a host rather than a hazard. It may have been there longest of all, though it looks too soft to be old.

In the corner crate, the white tube animals have made a different kind of architecture. The wooden slats have not rotted in the green way; they have been pickled by water and sulfides, turned dark and leathery. Inside, the copper wire has gone green, and through the coils the white tubes have grown, pale and almost pearly, as if they have used no concrete at all. Their crowns are nearly transparent, smaller than the wall-dwellers, more hidden. They look like a nervous system built in reverse, a tangle of wire and tube and pale feeding mouths. They are not a separate species, maybe, but a separate culture: the same animals changed by the close dark, by the taste of copper, by the shape of the crate.

And there are more you will not see. The pipes, the side channel, the dark sump itself hold residents that only announce themselves by movement—a blunt head at a pipe’s rim, a fringe of tentacles at the edge of light, a scatter of silt where something pulled itself back. The place is denser than it seems. The floor is not floor; it is a layer of shells over older shells over rust over concrete. The walls are not walls; they are a slowly growing reef compressed into a room.

These residents are not a collection of individuals. They are a stack of appetites. The film eats the seepage. The limpets eat the film. The tube-dwellers filter what the film and the limpets release. The amphipods eat the scraps. The flatworms eat the amphipods. The eel-things eat the flatworms. The pump creature takes something less clear—heat, electricity, the pulse of the machine—and turns it into flesh. When the black water gives a gas bubble and the feathery crowns close all at once, the whole room flinches like a single animal, and you understand that the Condensate Works is not a place where living things happen to occur. It is a body, built of rust and water and small hungers.

If you ask who lives here, the answer is not one thing. It is the film and the shell-carpet and the hidden pipe-dwellers and the soft thing at the pump. It is the slow machinery of eating and being eaten. No one has arranged them. They have arranged themselves. They are the place’s answer to its own ruin: not a dying, but a new wet order. They have taken the industrial world into their own bodies, and in doing so they have made the Works theirs.

You are not a guest. You are a surface. If you stay too long, they will accept you the way they accept a new pipe or a fallen rafter: as structure, as terrain, as another place for the film to grow. That is not malice. It is just how the place lives.
