At first, the city seems empty. There are only plants, water, cold, and the green dimness. But the silence is not empty. You wait. You watch.

The first ones you notice are the water-children. They are small, pale, translucent, no longer than your hand. Their bodies are split seed-cases with too many root-like legs. They live in the water channels. When you look at them directly, they stop moving, tuck their legs, and become knots of root. Their work is the water. They clear the channels with their mouths and bodies, dissolving debris with secretions. They are the keepers, the blood cells, the maintainers. They are not the young of the walkers. They are another form of the same life, a caste that stays small and quick, tending the water for all their days.

Then you see a walker. It comes down a passage you thought was empty. It moves so slowly that at first it is only a change in the shadow, a shift in the texture of the wall. Its body is made of the same material as the walls: interwoven stems, moss, lichen, living wood. It has no face you can name. At its top is a crown of fern fronds, still tightly furled. Beneath that, a dense body of green and brown. It has many thin limbs, each ending in a curl of tendril. It moves on root-like legs that unroot and reroot with each step, pulling free with a sound like wet thread tearing. It is not walking; it is gradually relocating. Water drips from its body. It is cold, the same cold as the air, the same cold as the water. It does not shiver.

It passes you. It does not stop. It touches the wall as it goes, and where the tendrils brush, the moss aligns, the stems bend. The wall shudders, a slow shiver of leaves. The walker hums, a sound so low you feel it in your chest more than hear it. The hum passes through the water, through the roots, through the moss. The whole city listens.

Now you understand that the walls are not walls. The folded shapes, the columns, the arches—they are the rooted ones. They are the elders. They are what walkers become when they have finished walking. They have put down roots. Their legs are the floor. Their arms are the walls. Their heads are the ceiling. They are not dead. They are settled. Their breathing is the slow beading of moisture on their bark. Their pulse is the capillary movement of water through their tissues. Their thoughts are chemical, exchanged through the shared root network, through the water that flows from chamber to chamber.

The city is their bodies. The chambers are the spaces between them. The passages are the gaps they left for movement and light and water. The stairs are their folded limbs, worn into steps by centuries of use. The water channels are the paths their roots made, widened by the water-children, maintained by the walkers.

There are singers too. In the larger chambers, they stand tall and slender, mostly stem and leaf, with long arms ending in fine root hairs. They sway so slowly you cannot see it. They hum. The hum is language. It passes through the water and the roots and the moss. The rooted ones answer with a deep creak, a release of scent like wet earth and crushed leaves and something sweet, like rotting fruit. The singers are the voice, the rememberers. They carry messages between the rooted ones, between the chambers, between the past and the present.

You cannot tell whether they are one being or many. The root network binds them. Each is a bud of the same original stock, a clonal colony that has learned to walk and sing and remember. The rooted ones are the body. The walkers are the hands. The water-children are the blood. The singers are the voice. But each is also a person, with its own slow will, its own place, its own name written in root-pressure and scent.

The faces are everywhere once you know how to look. They are in the grain of the walls, in the pattern of the lichen, in the arrangement of the moss. Not carved. Grown.

You notice a young walker in a niche. It is small, bright green, its root-legs still soft. It is learning to walk. It pulls free, takes one step, then another, then stops, exhausted. An older walker waits beside it. The child leans into the older one's side, and for a moment they merge, stems intertwining, moss blending. Then they separate. The child tries again.

This is their life. The city flowers in the high cold pools, and from the seed-pods come the walkers, small and bright green. They grow, moving through the city, tending the water, pruning the walls, guiding the growth. They learn the songs. They carry water and light and memory. They do not eat as you understand eating. They photosynthesize in the dim green light, and they drink the water that moves through the city. The rooted ones draw minerals from the weeping rock, passing them through the shared roots. Then, when the time comes, each walker chooses a place. They stop walking. They put down roots. They become wall, column, arch, stair. They become the city for the next generation. It is not death. It is promotion.

The design you sensed earlier is not design in the human sense. It is the result of many lives, many limbs, many centuries of slow, patient negotiation. The walls are not built; they are grown. The passages are not carved; they are agreed upon. The whole place is a compromise among thousands of rooted individuals, each one a wall, a column, a ceiling, each one shifted slightly to allow the others room. The regularity is the regularity of a body, not a blueprint.

They know you are here. They have known since you came through the break in the ridgeline. The water-children felt your footsteps in the channels. The walkers tasted your breath on the air. The rooted ones heard your heartbeat through the moss. They have been waiting to see what you will do. They are not afraid. They are patient. They have seen many things come and go—storms, fires, humans, animals, gods. They are the city. The city is them.

One of the singers turns toward you. Its fronds unfurl slightly, revealing a face of sorts: two dark knots, a slit of pale moss. It does not speak. It releases a wave of scent, and the scent is a question. It asks without words: Are you here to harm, to take, to change, or simply to see? The water drips. The moss grows. The rooted ones breathe. And you understand that you are not standing in a city. You are standing inside a body, inside a mind, inside a life that has been living here for millennia, and it has noticed you.
