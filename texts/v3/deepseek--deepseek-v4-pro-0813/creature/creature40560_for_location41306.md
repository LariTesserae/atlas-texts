First there are the birds. Swifts and swallows nest under the eaves of the turbine halls; mynas argue on cables. Then there are the geckos, the skinks, the ants, the bees. But after a while you realize there is another kind of life, neither quite animal nor quite machine, moving through the terraces with the same unhurried patience as the place itself. These are the Ladderfolk, the Tenders, the people of the steps.

At first they seem plain, as if the terraces had worn them down. Look longer and you see they are not worn down; they are polished. Their skin has the smooth brown patina of old metal, sometimes sheened with mineral dust. Their hair, cut short or tied back with strips of rubber, ranges from black to sun-bleached copper. Their eyes are often pale from the glare, and some of the furnace-workers have eyes the color of old glass from years of watching flames. They are small-boned but strong, with wide feet and long toes that grip the rungs. They never hurry. When they climb, they rise in a single unbroken motion, like water moving up a wick. Their hands are broad, the fingertips calloused into smooth gray pads from years of gripping pipe flanges and pruning blades.

Their bodies have been shaped by the vertical world. Their knees and ankles flex easily; their lungs are large from the thin air, their chests deep. They sleep lightly, waking at any change in the hum. They can hear a leak before it drips, and they can feel a bearing going dry through the soles of their feet on a gantry. This is not magic; it is just the result of living long in a place that never stops talking.

Their houses are not built so much as inhabited. A family may sleep in a retired boiler, its firebox now a cooking hearth; another keeps a room behind a wall of citrus trees, its floor packed brick dust, its roof translucent sheeting that turns the harsh midday light into a greenish glow. Some live high in the condenseries, where the cloud water runs down the walls and the beds are raised above the damp. The spaces are small, warm, and full of stored things: jars of honey, coils of wire, bundles of dried banana leaves, baskets of coffee cherries. Furniture is salvaged: cable spools for tables, upturned buckets for stools, woven mats of hemp and old cloth for sleeping. There are no doors that lock. There is no need.

Each person is a keeper of something. The furnace-tenders live lowest, where the heat is strongest; they bank the geothermal mains in long cycles, reading the color of the metal and the pitch of the steam. The water-tenders walk the condensate channels and cisterns, clearing silt and scale, tasting the mineral tang of each tank with a fingertip. The garden-wrights prune the terraces, not to tame them but to keep the whole thing moving—cutting back a ficus so a tram line stays clear, tying a vine so it grows along a pipe instead of through a wall. The rail-men and funicular operators grease cables at dawn, replacing worn sheaves with parts salvaged from older machines. There are lamp-lighters, cistern-watchers, threaders who weave banana fiber into cloth, and hive-keepers who tend the bees. No one has a single role; everyone has a lean.

Their knowledge is not written. It lives in their hands and in the rhythm of their work songs, which sound less like singing and more like the clank of a conveyor belt slowed down. Children learn by following. By the time they can walk, they can identify a healthy bearing by its hum and a thirsty tree by the angle of its leaves. They do not distinguish between agriculture and maintenance. A harvest of bananas is a kind of repair; a pruning is a kind of harvest. They compost food waste with ash and brick dust to make new soil, and they use the old cable runs as trellises. When a machine fails beyond repair, they do not mourn it. They disassemble it, salvage what they can, and plant something in the space it leaves. A young fig grows where a pump once stood, and its roots follow the old cable runs.

The animals are not tame, but they are familiar. Swallows build nests under the eaves of their sleeping rooms, and no one disturbs them. Geckos are welcome in kitchens because they eat the ants; the ants are tolerated because they clean the crumbs. Moths are a nuisance but also a sign that the lamps are working. The people let civets pass through the lower galleries at night, and if a civet takes a fruit from the orchard, the loss is counted as an offering, or a tax. They do not keep pets, but they have partnerships. A small hawk follows a garden-wright, waiting for the rats that scatter from cut brush; the worker does not call it, but it comes.

They do not worship the machines, but they treat them as kin. A boiler is not a god; it is an elder, a slow warm presence that must be listened to. A cistern is a grandmother, holding water and memory. They pat the flanks of the funicular cars as they pass, and they speak to the pipeways in a low friendly tone. Their names reflect this: a child might be called Cistern, Ladder, Gantry, Condenser, Moss, Rung, Fern, Soot, Brass, or Steam—not after the vapor itself but after the sound a pipe makes just before it blows. They speak a patois full of clicks and hisses that imitate steam, glottal stops like the clank of a valve, and long vowels like the hum of the transformers. To overhear them is to hear the place learning to talk.

Their food comes from the terraces: bananas, plantains, citrus, coffee, yams, watercress, bamboo shoots, mushrooms from the damp lower rooms. They keep bees for honey and fish small tilapia from the warm cisterns. They dry fruit in the upper galleries where the cloud air is cool, and they bake flatbread on stones heated by the geothermal mains. Trade between terraces is not a currency economy but a circulation, like the water. Lower dwellers bring heat and metalwork; upper dwellers bring cloud water and mushrooms; middle dwellers bring fruit and coffee. What one lacks, another's surplus covers.

No one remembers exactly how the High Ladders came to be built. The oldest people say their grandparents were workers, brought to run the furnaces and tend the terraces. The original purpose has faded into the same haze that rises from the lower valleys. What remains is the rhythm: keep the water moving, keep the heat moving, keep the green moving. They do not ask why anymore, or if they do, they ask while working, and the answer is always the same—another valve opened, another branch tied back, another cistern cleaned. They do not go down to the lowlands. The lower valleys are a rumor, a place where the air is thick and the horizon is flat. The High Ladders is not a prison; it is a body, and they are its cells. To leave would be to bleed out. Some have tried, the stories say, and came back with their ears ringing from the silence.

At midday, when the high-altitude sun glares off whitewashed walls and wet leaves, they move into the shade of the groves or the cool of the lower mill floors. The children nap on warm pipe flanges, their cheeks pressed to the metal as if it were a mother's chest. The adults sit on cable spools, drinking thin bitter coffee sweetened with citrus honey. They talk in low voices, not to be heard over the hum but to be part of it. In the evening, when the floodlights come on and the lower terraces glow, they walk the stepped canals, checking the water flow by the sound of it. The whole place breathes around them, and they breathe with it.

Nothing here is wild in the old sense; everything is feral. The people are feral too—not wild, but escaped from one system and now embedded in another. They are the immune system of the terraces, the pruning hands and listening ears. They are not masters of the High Ladders, nor servants of it. They are a part of it, like the ficus roots in the retaining walls, like the geckos on the warm flanges, like the steam that cools on tile and drips into the gutters. After enough time, feral and native become impossible to separate.
