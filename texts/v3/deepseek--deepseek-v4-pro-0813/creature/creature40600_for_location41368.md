The place is inhabited, though at first you do not see it. The feeling of being watched is there, but it is indistinguishable from the damp. You mistake them for the way a wall leans, for a coat of black moss breathing on a north-facing stone, for the afterimage of a window seen through wet air. Then one of them moves, and you understand that the city has a population after all.

They are few. If you count them as living, there are perhaps thirty or forty. If you do not, the quarter is empty. Both are true.

They are tall and narrow, because the streets are tall and narrow. They turn slightly even in open spaces, as if every room still has a doorway closing somewhere. Their clothing is not exactly clothing but an accrued surface: mineral damp, pale lichen scabbing at the elbows, gray-green ceramic scales at the cuffs and collars. Black moss grows along their seams, not from neglect but from belonging. It beads with condensation. When they stand very still, they look like old stone that has been standing in the rain for a long time.

Their faces are the hardest to keep. A face may be smooth as polished pavement, with features appearing only when you are not looking directly. Some have eyes too far apart, as if the face has not yet decided how many rooms it contains. Some have mouths that move before their words, or after them. A greeting may reach you while their lips are still, and then the lips move a little, as if catching up to the sound. Their voices lack overtones, like the bell in the tower that has not decided to be a bell tower. It is not unpleasant. It is simply unfinished.

Each resident carries two shadows. They are soft and doubled and do not agree. One may lag behind by half a step. The other may pool at the feet like a puddle, or lift partway up a wall, or point the wrong way down an alley. The residents are careful never to step on either shadow. If the two shadows cross, the resident pauses until they separate again. This is a courtesy, not a fear.

They move with the city’s patience. They do not walk so much as let the street pass beneath them. Their feet leave clean prints in the soft mineral paste, and the prints remain for hours, but they do not fade by drying. They fade the way a thought fades when you stop thinking of it. Sometimes a resident will look back at their own prints and nod, as if making sure they are being forgotten properly.

Their hands are long, with too many small joints, or too few; it is impolite to count. They use them for adjustments. You may see one press a palm against a wall that has thickened too quickly, and the wall pauses, apologetic. Another will stand in a doorway that has forgotten it was ever an opening, humming at a frequency just under hearing, until the door remembers and rises back into the wall like skin healing. They carry no tools. When a correction is needed, they pluck a moth-sized drone from the air and whisper to it, or they simply gesture, and the air agrees.

Their work is not work. It is the opposite of completion. They keep the quarter from finishing, not because they fear completion but because they are completion’s inverse. A building that could be called finished is reabsorbed into its own blueprint and grows again differently. A resident who could be called finished is reabsorbed into the city’s memory. So they remain slightly unfinished in everything. They leave every task one step short. They never close a door all the way. They never speak a sentence that ends in a hard stop.

They have no market, no plaza, no place of assembly that could be mistaken for a finished courtyard. They gather at the central trough when the water is very cold, when the eel-like things become visible—faintly pink cartilage and glass, seeming to be elsewhere at the same time. The residents stand shoulder to shoulder on the wet stones, saying nothing, watching. Perhaps they are watching themselves. The long-legged insect that walks across the wall without casting a shadow is treated as a colleague. They nod to it. It does not nod back, but that is considered polite.

They eat little. Some press their lips to a north-facing wall where the black moss is thickest and drink the condensation. Some nibble the colorless ferns that grow from buried heat, careful never to take the last frond. They do not need much, but they need the moisture. The cold is not a discomfort to them. It is a medium. It settles in their hands and behind their ears, and they let it.

They speak rarely. Their language is a set of open sentences. A conversation can be left hanging for days, resumed by another voice as if no time has passed. They do not use the street names. They refer to a street as “the one that answers if you speak it too often,” or “the one with fourteen steps descending and eleven ascending.” They call each other by a word that changes each meeting—sometimes a color, sometimes the sound of water, sometimes a numeral left unfinished. If you ask a resident their name, they will look at you as if you have asked a puddle where it keeps its map.

They know the rules not as laws but as the true shape of the place. They do not close a door all the way. If a door latches, they sit with it, humming, until it remembers it was an opening. They do not speak a street’s name more than once. If a street answers, they answer back politely and then change the subject. They pour water onto the pavement to read the map of the quarter, and they know the map is always one day out of date. They walk it anyway, because yesterday’s map is still true for someone, and they are not the kind to leave a true thing unwalked.

It is unclear if they are born. There are no children, or perhaps the children are the alleys that exist only when seen from one end. If a new resident appears, it is usually after a building is reabsorbed. Something is left over: a fold of intention, a half-finished thought, a stair that no longer counts correctly. That leftover stands up, brushes the mineral damp from its shoulders, and joins the others. Sometimes a resident disappears, and a wall thickens by the width of a thumb, or a puddle appears in five places at once, all reflecting the same unbuilt tower. No one remarks on it. The city is dense, and so is its memory.

They rest in upper rooms where gravity is managed in patches. They prefer the rooms where your inner ear insists you are falling. They lie down on the air, or on floors that are not quite horizontal, and they dream of streets that connect both ends. Those dreams are never finished. They wake before the connection is complete, and they are careful to leave the dream exactly one step short.

Their music is the city’s hum. Sometimes, at the bottom of the bowl, they open their mouths and sing a tone that is missing its overtone, joining the bell. The water vibrates. The eel-like things grow visible. The black moss drips a little faster. It is not singing exactly. It is the sound of a place remembering not to finish.

They are not ghosts. They are more like the space between a word and its echo, the hesitation between a runnel flowing uphill and remembering itself. They are damp, patient, half-built, and almost silent. They are the quarter’s inner weather, its open sentence, its open doors.

If you stand long enough in one of those alleys that exist only when seen from one end, you may feel one of them pass behind you. You will not hear footsteps. You will feel the air thicken, then hesitate, then go on. A puddle at your feet will ripple, though no stone has fallen. When you look down, the reflection will show the unbuilt tower and two soft shadows standing where yours should be. By the time you turn, they will be gone, or they will not have been there at all. Both are true.
