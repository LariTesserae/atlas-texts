The one who lives here is not found by looking at the water, but by looking at what is attended to. The pools are too clear, the terraces too exact, the moss curtains not torn but drawn aside. Follow the newest marks on the wall, the ones that still hold a slight glitter of powdered stone, and they will lead downward to the deepest basin, where the broken arch makes a shadow like a mouth.

At first you will see only a long dark shape on the marl. You will take it for a fallen lintel or a slow shadow. Then it shifts slightly, and the water above it dimples, and you notice the shape has the regular rise and fall of a thing breathing. That is the first true recognition: the bottom of the pool is not bottom. It is a body, holding still.

When it rises, it does not burst or splash. It grows more solid. The water sheets off a back as smooth as polished graphite, with the same hairline seams as the old posts. The head is broad and flat, set low on a short, muscular neck. Its eyes sit wide on top of the skull, and they are the color of the tea-brown pools, with horizontal pupils that are not really pupils but long slits, like cracks that open slightly as the light changes. A second lid closes sideways, not blinking but wiping.

Its front limbs are long and folded. It has hands, or something close to hands: seven fingers, each with an extra joint, ending in soft rounded pads that grip wet stone without slipping. The thumbs are set low and are the strongest. The hands are the most human part of it, though they are no longer exactly human. The hind legs are shorter, webbed to the tips, and on land it uses them poorly, dragging them behind like a seal. In water they unfold into broad translucent planes, and the tail, long and laterally flattened, waves with a slow, finned motion. It swims without hurry, but with great precision, by moving only enough water to pass between two pools without breaking the surface.

Its skin is the color of the hollow: black-grey on the back, pale mushroom-grey on the belly, with moss-green film along the lower edges. Over the ribs the skin is patterned with fine seams that glint like the seams on the posts, and when it breathes a row of small triangular frills opens along its back, red-brown like the water, each one vibrating slightly with the sub-bass tone. It smells of wet limestone and fern root, with a faint sharpness of ozone, especially after it has been out of the water for a while.

It is not an animal that evolved here by accident. It is too fitted to the place. It may have been born here, or built, or simply left behind by whatever built the arch and the posts. It has the look of something that was once designed and has since become wild. Its hands, in particular, are not the hands of a pond animal; they are the hands of a caretaker, and they still remember their work.

It keeps a schedule, though the schedule is long and many parts of it are invisible. At dawn it hauls itself onto the stone shelf near the trough and waits there, one hand resting on the trough’s rim. The trough fills and empties at a regular interval, and if you stay long enough you notice the interval is exactly the same as the creature’s breathing. It may be that the trough runs on the same old field as the creature’s body, or that the creature has learned to breathe in time with it; from outside, there is no way to tell.

After the mist burns off, it slides into the water and begins its rounds. It does not disturb the surface. It moves along the undersides of ledges, where the current already moves, flattening its body to pass through gaps no wider than a hand. It checks the terraced pools one by one, feeling the sinter lips with its soft fingertips. If a lip has become rough or clogged, it cleans it with a slow licking motion, and a clear, gluey secretion from the corner of its mouth sets the mineral smooth. It is the reason the pools spill so cleanly. It is also the reason the plants are spaced the way they are. It does not let them crowd. With its long fingers it folds old leaves under, presses them into the marl, and clears small channels so each plant has its own light and water. It is not gardening as a human would do it. It is more like breathing: an old, continuous maintenance that has become part of the place.

It pays little attention to the smaller life, but the smaller life pays attention to it. The glass minnows school around its gill frills, feeding on what the frills release. The stilt-birds stand on its back while it rests, as if it were a warm stone. The frogs sit in the hollows of its spine. Even the dragonflies land on the ridge between its eyes to drink the film of water there. It tolerates them all. It rarely eats, and when it does, it opens its mouth underwater and lets the current bring tiny shrimp and larvae into the comb of its gill arches. It does not hunt. It invites.

The wall-marks are its doing. Some mornings it climbs the rock face, clings there with its padded hands, and reads the old grooves with its fingertips and tongue. It may stay there for an hour, perfectly still, while the water drips from its tail. Then, with a single curved claw on its second finger, it scrapes a new line or deepens an old pit. It works slowly, stopping often to listen to the stone. The marks are not decoration. They are a record—of water levels, migrations, the intervals of the trough, the positions of light grids at night. The creature is the one who keeps the record current. When the marks change between visits, it is because the creature has changed them.

At night it returns to the deepest pool, under the broken arch. The grids of light rise from the water, not reflections but old patterns too fine to read, and they move over the creature’s back as it lies on the bottom. Its eyes remain half-open. The sub-bass tone strengthens, though only slightly. It is singing, or dreaming, or perhaps just breathing in a way that makes the rock around it vibrate. The water in the other pools rises a fraction. The convex meniscus on the oldest pool tightens. The trough empties once, exactly.

It is not the only resident of the hollow. There are fish, birds, larvae, frogs, insects, worms, all going about their own lives. But it is the one that keeps the place from becoming merely an environment. It is the difference between a wet hole and a kept garden, between a machine and a machine that is still looked after. Without it, the filters would still run and the water would still cycle. But the marks would fade, the terraces would clog, and the migrations would gradually lose their timing. The creature is not the owner. It is more like a memory that has taken on flesh, or a function that has grown a body. It lives there the way a word lives in a mouth: quietly, patiently, at the edge of hearing.
