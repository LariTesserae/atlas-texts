No one lives on the Vent Shelf, if by “lives” you mean an animal, a human, a creature with muscles and a face. But it is not empty. It is inhabited by things that do not need to move, and by things that move without being alive.

Start with the water. The water looks empty, but it is not sterile. In every gutter, sump, and condensation film there are bacteria and single-celled algae. They are the first residents. They are so small they do not disturb the surface. A drop of water on a fern frond is a lake to them. They eat what the larger plants leak and shed. They are the bottom of a food web that has no top. They break down what the plants drop. They release heat and nutrients. They are the shelf’s hidden digestion.

Then come the mosses. They are not one species but many, and they cover the rock and concrete like a pelt. There is copper moss, thin and crusty, bright as oxidized metal, growing on the bare basalt. There is grey moss, plush and soft, growing on concrete. There is acid-green moss, hanging in curtains where water drips from pipe seams. There is dark-red moss, growing in warm cracks of old brick. Up close, each is a miniature forest: stems like threads, leaves like tiny tongues. They hold water in the gaps between their leaves. They grow in straight lines along pipe seams because the condensation is more reliable there. They grow in rosettes where a steam valve exhales. They grow on the sills of empty windows, on the lips of gutters, on the backs of old brick buildings until the brick is only a shape under a living skin.

Under the green surface is another city. The lower stems are dead or dying, packed into peat. This peat is not waste; it is a sponge, a filter, a fuel. The city’s automatic harvesters cut it at the same rate it forms. The moss does not experience the harvest. It simply grows upward as the bottom sinks into brown water. The peat is inhabited by fungi: pale threads that spread through the dead material, dissolving it. They are the decomposers, the residents that eat the dead. Some of those threads connect living moss plants to one another, sharing water and nutrients. The moss is not a collection of individuals; it is a single, slow body stretched across the plateau.

Near the warm vents, where steam leaks and the air is a few degrees above freezing, ferns unroll. They are not tall; the tallest comes to the knee. Their fronds are leathery, dark, waxy. They do not flower. They release spores into the wet air. The spores drift until they catch on a warm, wet surface. The ferns form groves around the vents, each grove a small green room in the roar. They are the closest thing the shelf has to a forest, but they are silent and still. Their fronds collect condensation and send it down their stems to the ground. They are the only larger plants that can tolerate the constant heat and steam, and they grow only where the balance is right.

In sheltered hollows, dwarf willows and birches grow in dense thickets. They are old and gnarled, though never high. Their leaves are shaped to shed water, each one a drip-tip. In the wind they rattle. They are pollinated by the wind, not by insects. Their catkins are small and brown. They produce tiny seeds that are carried by the fans and gutters. The thickets are not browsed. They grow and die and fall into the moss. They have no bright flowers, no scent, no fruit. Nothing would come if they did; there are no animals to attract. So they have never evolved color or sweetness. Their beauty is in their patience.

On the steam pipes there is a plant that has no leaves. It is a spongy, rubbery growth, pale grey or yellowish, sometimes blushed with pink where the pipe is hottest. It grows directly on the insulation. It drinks the leaked heat and the condensed water. It is warm to the touch. It is not a moss and not a fungus; it may be a partnership between an alga and a fungus, or something older. It forms sleeves around the pipes, thickening where the insulation is cracked. It is the only resident that seems to prefer the machine to the rock. Where the pipe passes through a wall, the pipe-skin grows through the gap and joins itself on the other side. It can be peeled away in sheets, but it grows back. The city’s maintenance arms scrape it from valves and joints, but only where it would clog the moving parts. Elsewhere it is left alone, because it insulates and filters. It is the city’s living gasket.

In the cracks of new concrete and fresh basalt, liverworts and hornworts grow. They are even simpler than mosses. They are flat, green, lobed, like spilled paint. They need almost nothing: a film of water, a little light, a little heat. They are the first to colonize a new gutter or a newly laid slab. They are followed by moss, then by ferns. They are the pioneers of the Vent Shelf’s slow succession, though succession here is mostly paused. Some reproduce by tiny cups of gemmae, which splash out in raindrops and start new plants where they land. The city’s water system is their seed-distribution network.

The real decomposers are invisible. Fungi thread through the peat and the living moss, connecting plant to plant. Bacteria live in the canals, in the sumps, in the water films on leaves. They are the only residents that can be said to swim. The water is clear because nothing larger eats them or stirs them. They are the bottom of a food web that has no top. They break down what the plants drop. They release heat and nutrients. They are the shelf’s metabolism.

Then there are the machines. They are not alive, but they are residents in the way that weather is a resident. They move. The trams run empty, open their doors at empty stations, wait, close them, and continue. They are the closest thing to large animals. They have schedules, they follow paths, they pause at the same places. Their wheels are rustless. Their windows are washed by condensation. Some carry moss on their roofs. The automatic loaders move along rail yards, carrying peat and ash. The sweeping arms clear leaves and moss from the tram tracks. They move with a slow, blind patience, like wading birds. The fans rotate slowly in the condenser arrays. The pistons rock. The pressure gauges tremble. These machines are not self-aware, but they are self-regulating. They are the muscles of the city.

Finally, the largest resident is the city itself. It is not a creature. It is a system. But it breathes. It takes in cold air and water, burns peat, releases steam. It drinks through gutters and canals. It sheds heat through condensers. It repairs itself before failure. It is a body without a mind, or with a mind distributed across pipes and valves and relays. It is not dead; it is idling, holding itself at a steady state. It does not notice the moss or the ferns or the trams. It simply keeps them, and they keep it.

The only living beings are plants, fungi, and bacteria. The rest is metal, stone, steam, and electricity. Yet the whole place has the feel of a single body, one loop turning without end. The moss does not need to be eaten. The ferns do not need to be pollinated. The trams do not need passengers. The city does not need people. It is a garden-city without animals, preserved at the moment of its birth, and it is inhabited by everything that can live in that stillness.
