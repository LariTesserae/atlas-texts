The first answer is the sound. It lives there more fully than anything with flesh. It is the main occupant, the one that never leaves. But if you stand on the rim long enough—if the hammering does not drive you off—you may begin to notice a second tenant.

At first they are easy to mistake for a stack of basalt. Near the nine cairns on the eastern rim, a figure stands so still that the steam has grown a second skin over them. They are not tall. The mineral haze has softened their outline. In the permanent dusk they look like one more vent, a man-shaped smoker.

They have no spoken name. If they ever had one, the sound swallowed it long ago. The few who mark the rim call them the Listener, or the One Who Counts. They have lived there long enough that the place has begun to claim them. Their skin is pale as the blind eel, with a faint green-grey bloom like the lichen in the cracks. Their eyes are milky with steam, kept half-closed, not because they cannot see but because the light is a liar and the water is worse. Their hair has been replaced by a thin thatch of mineral scale, white and sulfur-yellow, so that from a distance their head resembles a young chimney. They wear no cloth; cloth would rot in the hot acid breath of the place. Instead they are wrapped in their own accretion—layers of sinter and lichen, cracked at the elbows and knees, so that when they move they flake like old stone.

But they do move. They move slowly, each step placed on the off-beat of the thud. They know the rim the way a tongue knows teeth. Their feet are bare and broad, the soles hardened to something like hoof or stone. They do not trust the rock, but they know its moods. They have heard a slab crack before it falls and have stepped back without looking. They have never fallen.

Their life is built around listening. The place is so loud that speech is useless, but the Listener does not need to speak. The whole body is an ear. They feel the low organ note in their sternum, the shriek in their jaw, the concussive thud in the soles of their feet. They can tell the difference between a vent opening and a rock falling, between water rising from below and wind pushing the surface. They know the water’s moods: when it will clear for a heartbeat, when it will run uphill, when it will show the wrong sky. They do not look for long when the reflection goes wrong. They turn their back to the water and press their palms to the basalt, letting the stone’s vibration tell them where they are.

They live in the ruin of the shelter, or rather beside it. The flat slabs have collapsed, but they have not repaired them. They use the fallen doorway as a windbreak and sleep with their face toward the water. Inside, the flat stone with its grooves is their ledger. The old marks are deep and worn—chevrons and circles, made by someone before. The Listener has added new ones. A chevron for a chimney born, a circle for a rising and falling of the lake, a crossed circle for the days when the far rim refuses to hold its distance. They carve with a flake of basalt, not writing. Writing would be too fragile. The place erases language. It cannot easily erase a groove.

They tend the nine cairns. When the rim shifts and a cairn topples, they rebuild it, knee-high. The cairns are not markers for anyone but themselves. They are how the Listener keeps distance honest when the basin argues with itself. They count them by touch when the steam is thick. Each cairn is a knot in the path, holding the way to the steps.

The steps are older than the Listener. They descend into the green water—six, maybe seven—then blur under mineral crust. The Listener goes down only so far. They stand ankle-deep on the last visible step and let the hot water lap at their feet. The translucent crustaceans gather there, darting between the Listener’s ankles. Sometimes the Listener scoops one into a palm and holds it until it stills. They do not eat much. They scrape the sulfur-yellow slime from the rocks with a basalt flake and chew it slowly. It tastes of metal and heat and the place’s unfinished heart.

The pale eel comes sometimes, riding the upwelling. It has no eyes, but the Listener feels it coming through the water’s push. The eel turns like a strip of cloth and is gone. The Listener does not reach for it. They have an understanding: the eel belongs to the deep vents; the Listener belongs to the rim. The water is not for bodies.

No one knows how long the Listener has been there. They may be the one who built the shelter, or the child of that one, or what the place has made from that one. The distinction has stopped mattering. The place is still being decided, and so are they. They are not exactly human anymore, but they are not exactly a creature either. They are an organ the basin has grown to hear itself.

They do not sleep as others sleep. They rest in short intervals, always on the rim, always facing the water. The sound never stops, and they do not want it to. In the rare moments when the water clears and the chimneys are visible—white and fragile, growing and falling—they watch with an expression that is not awe and not fear. It is recognition. They know that they, too, are a shape the heat is building and the gravity will eventually pull down.

If you saw them from the far rim, you might not believe they were real. The distance lies. The steam bends them into a pillar, a cairn, a figure of spray. Some who have seen them say the place has a hermit. Others say it has a ghost. The few who mark the rim do not correct them. They know the truth is stranger: the Cistern has a listener. It has someone to hear its hammering. And the listener has stayed long enough to become part of the noise.

Ask what lives there, and the answer is not singular. The sound lives there. The blind eel lives there. The lichen and the crustaceans and the sulfur slime live there. But the one who watches over all of it, the one who keeps the count and rebuilds the cairns and sleeps with the water’s roar in their chest—that one is the Listener. They are the place’s witness, its keeper, its second voice. They are what happens when a person stops asking the noise to make sense and instead becomes another instrument for it to play.
