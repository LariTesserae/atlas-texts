You will not see them at first. That is not because they hide. It is because the mire has taught them to be indistinguishable from itself.

At first you mistake them for stumps. The place is full of stumps: old trees hollowed into water-holding cylinders, root rings around wet shafts, moss-covered mounds. The people of the mire are one more shape the ground has made. They stand broad and low, with the canopy pressing down on them. Their skin is the color of wet bark and old peat—dark brown, black-green, patched with pale lichen. Moss grows along their shoulders and down their arms, not as clothing but as a living part of them. Small ferns uncurl from their knuckles. Bracket fungi shelf out from their ribs. The whole body is a mat.

Then the shape shifts its weight. The ground under your feet gives a wet sigh and springs back. You see that it has eyes.

The eyes are pale, the color of rainwater that has stood in a hollow tree until a skin of pollen and spores formed on it. They do not focus the way human eyes do. They receive. They open and close slowly, the lids a fold of moss and thin bark, as if the whole face is a leaf turning away from heat. The face itself is not quite a face. It is a slope of peat-fiber with shallow grooves where the mouth might be. When the mouth opens, it is a split in the mat, dark and wet inside, like the hollow of a rotten log.

They move without walking. Their legs are thick roots, bending slowly. They do not lift their feet free of the ground. They push through the waterlogged moss, the peat parting around them and closing behind. The sound is not a footstep. It is the same small wet sigh a pool makes when it drains into a hidden channel. They can stand in one place for a season. They can cross a mile in a night, but the night is long and dim and they are in no hurry.

They are warm, as warm as the peat that digests itself far below. The broad leaves near them sometimes fold away from that slight heat. White midges drift around them in loose columns. The translucent frogs with eggs like strings of clear beads live in the hollow places of their bodies—between the shelf fungi, in the crook of a mossy elbow, in the rainwater that gathers in the dip of a collarbone. They do not seem to mind. They are host to the same life that hosts them.

They keep the stones. Not as caretakers, not as worshipers. They simply touch the straight lines. The stones are the only geometry in a world of curves and drips. The beings know every squared block, every leaning standing stone, every flat slab path that leads from one wet hollow to another. They do not clear the moss from the stones; they only rest their root-tipped fingers on the place where the straight edge is still true. It is remembering, but not the way you remember. It is the way water remembers the shape of a channel.

They speak rarely aloud. When they do, the sound is a low glug, like water moving through a buried channel. Other beings feel it through their feet, through the root-thin fingers pressed to the ground. A conversation can take days. It moves through the peat, not through the air. Their names are not words. They are the taste of water from a particular pool, the sequence of drips from a particular branch.

They are not born and they do not die in the human way. In the pools that gather inside hollow trees, where the frogs lay their clear beads, the water sometimes thickens. It grows a skin of algae and pollen. The skin grows roots. Over many years, the shape gathers itself in the water and rises, already moss-backed and slow. It knows the stones because the water knew the stones. When they are finished, they lie down in a wet hollow and the moss grows over them before they are still. Their body sinks into the peat. Later, in the same place, a new pool appears, and the process begins again.

They are few. You may walk for days and see none. Sometimes, when the blue-green fungi at the hidden pools breathe slowly, brightening and dimming over the space of a minute, several of them gather at a standing stone. They do not speak. The lights breathe; the water glugs under the ground; that is the whole ceremony.

Then you will notice one standing beside a slab path, its hand on a stone, and realize it has been there all along. It will turn its head. The moss on its face will part. It will look at you with eyes like pooled rainwater. It will make a single low sound, not a threat, not a greeting, just the exhalation of marsh gas from far below. Then it will step away into the green dimness, and the ground will close behind it, leaving no track.

They are not the people who placed the stones. Those people are gone. But the mire has taken the memory of them—the straightness, the patience, the uprightness—and made it into something that can live in the peat. They are the mire’s people now, softened and rooted and quiet. They do not need to be loud. The place is too old and too large for that.
