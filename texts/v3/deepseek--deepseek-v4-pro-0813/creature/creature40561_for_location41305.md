The hollow is not empty. It only looks empty to a quick eye.

In the deepest pool, just below the spring slit, there is a long, low depression worn into the mineral floor. It is not a natural hollow. It has been shaped by something that returns and returns, a body that fits the stone as a hand fits a well-used tool. At first the depression seems to hold only water and a fine rust-colored silt. But if you wait, if you let your eyes go soft in the dim steam, a shape will separate from the stone—not by moving, but by becoming visible, the way a breath fogs a mirror and then clears.

It is a salamander, though not like any that lives under wet leaves in the world above. It is blind, and its skin is the color of the travertine rims: old bone, cream-grey, faintly rust-streaked along the ridge of its back. Its belly is paler, almost translucent, so that the shadows of its slow organs show through like stones under water. It has no pattern, no markings beyond the mineral stains that have seeped into its hide. To a careless eye, it is just another wet stone, another fold of mineral crust.

But it breathes. Along the sides of its head, three pairs of gills open and close, feathered and soft. They are not bright red. They are a muted rose-brown, the color of rust before it darkens, and they move with the creature’s breath as slowly as the water itself. The water around them shimmers faintly, stirred by a life so patient it barely counts as motion.

Its legs are small and thin, but they are enough. The toes are tipped with tiny pads that grip the slick travertine. It can climb from pool to pool, though it rarely does. When it moves, it moves like the water moves—slowly, with no splash, each foot placed as if testing the stone. Its tail is long and flattened, the same color as the rest of it, and it lies in the water like a length of wet rope left to petrify.

The head is wide and flat, shaped to press against the bottom of a pool. Where eyes would be, there are only two small, pale indentations, like the marks a fingertip leaves in wet clay. It does not need eyes. The whole hollow is a pressure map to it: the temperature of the water, the weight of the air, the tiny vibrations of a small white crustacean rowing past on feathery legs. It senses the chemistry of the spring, the taste of sulfur and lime, the faint sweetness of the bacterial mats that grow in the deeper basins.

It feeds on the rusty film. It lowers its snout to the bottom of a pool and pulses its throat, drawing the bacteria and dissolved minerals into its mouth. The film never runs out; it grows as fast as the creature eats it, fed by the warm water and the minerals that rise from below. Sometimes the salamander catches one of the small white crustaceans. It does not chase. It waits, and when the crustacean drifts near, the salamander’s jaw opens with a soft, sideways movement, and the crustacean is gone. It may eat only once in a month, or a season. The warm water does not demand more.

Most of the time it lies in the depression near the spring slit, with only its gills moving. Its body is so still that mineral begins to settle on its skin, a fine crust of travertine that forms along its back like the first frost on stone. In time, it must shed its skin to be rid of it. The shed skin drifts away in thin white veils, dissolving into the water and adding its own small fraction to the mineral architecture of the hollow.

It is old, though it is hard to say how old. It grows slowly. A year may add only a finger’s length. It may have lived in the hollow for decades, perhaps longer than the wooden stave has been fused to the travertine, longer than the cup-shaped depressions have been sealed by a later film of stone. It does not mark time. The water temperature does not change; the light does not change. The creature ages the way the stone ages—by accumulation, by wearing, by being.

The people who came here long ago may have seen it. Perhaps they thought it was a spirit of the spring, or a piece of the stone that had learned to breathe. The cup-shaped depressions they ground into the ledge are not offerings to it, but the creature does not know that. It knows only that the stone is smooth there, and that the water sometimes pools in those cups and warms them. It has no fear of the old bench or the square basin. They are just more stone, shaped by a different kind of patience.

There is no other of its kind here, or if there is, they are separated by years and by the maze of pools. It does not call. It has no voice. The only sounds it makes are the faint click of its toes on the mineral rims and the soft wash of water through its gills. The mist eats even those.

It is not the ruler of the hollow, nor its ghost. It is the hollow’s resident, the one living thing that has bent its whole shape to fit the mineral world. It is what the water would be if the water wanted to breathe. It has no need to change, because the place has no need to change. The water rises warm from below, fills the basins, spills from rim to rim, and leaves only as slowly as it arrived. The salamander is part of that slow leaving and returning. It lives the way the held note lives—not still in the way of a frozen lake, but still in the way of something that has settled into itself and no longer needs to move.

When you leave, it will not watch you go. It will not know you were here. It will simply continue to be, as the pools do, as the mineral does, until the water stops rising from below.
