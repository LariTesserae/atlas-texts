The hollow is not so much occupied as it is kept. Its first tenant is the water-film itself, though that is not alive; it is the medium through which everything else passes. From that film rises a whole layered society, small and loud and deeply entangled with the breathing of the wall.

The first true residents are the plants, and they are feral in a particular way. The moss-like growth does not carpet the walls but traces the seams in dark, broken lines, following the slow mineral sweat. It is descended from the building’s air-scrubbing mats, but it has gone feral and now lives on condensation and dissolved ceramic rather than filtered air. Where the amber light touches it, the moss flashes silver, as if the wall were wet with mercury. It does not spread quickly; it persists. More than that, it keeps the place open. The moss releases chemical signals that suppress the wall’s nanorepair response, persuading the ceramic to remain cracked. In that sense, the moss is not merely a resident but the caretaker of the wound.

In the back corner, pale tubular creepers cluster like blind fingers. Their tips swell and shrink in a slow, almost breathing rhythm, pulling moisture straight from the heavy air. They never flower. They have no leaves worth the name, only soft tubes that fatten with water and then shrink as they pass it inward. Along the lip of the opening, one or two vane-root plants have wrapped themselves around the ceramic edge. Their leaves are reduced to tiny scales, and they feed mostly on minerals dissolved in the seepage. They look less like plants than like exposed nerves, pale and patient, their roots tracing the old fractures.

Then there are the animals.

The drip-crabs are the first you notice in the pools. They are the size of thumbnails, but there are thousands of them, lining the margins of the shallow basins in a shifting crust. Their shells carry a faint metallic sheen from the mineral-rich water, so the rim of each pool seems to glint and move. They sift the water continually with feathery mouthparts, catching whatever the wall exudes—particulate life, dissolved proteins, flakes of ceramic too small to see. They are the grazers of the hollow, the constant low-level industry that keeps the water from growing too thick.

Above them, in the ceiling seams, the pipe-frogs call. They are not true frogs, but their bodies have the same wet shine and folded limbs; they are descended from cleaning organisms that once moved through the building’s conduits. Now they live in the warmest seams, where condensation runs richest. Their voices are sharp and metallic, timed to the nine-second toll of the water hammer. Between the thuds, the pipe-frogs throw their calls into the gap, so the hollow’s rhythm is punctuated by their bright, ringlike notes.

Ash-flies drink the condensation in corkscrew flights, their wings making a low whine that threads through the heavier sounds. They cluster on the ceiling when the air thickens, and their larvae live in the drip-pits, feeding on the mineral paste that forms where water meets ceramic. The flies seem too small to matter, but they are everywhere, and their movement gives the air a constant busy texture.

Bell mites stud the warm patches of wall near the vascular runnels. Each one is a translucent dome, and inside each dome is a bright internal point, as if a tiny light had been embedded in glass. They ring at a frequency that seems to come from inside the skull rather than from the wall. The ringing is not only sound; it is also a weak electrical signal, and many of the other animals use it to orient. The bell mites are old in the way the wall is old. They may be the closest descendants of the structure’s original sensor-organisms, now turned feral and luminous.

The skinks move among the crabs with the slow patience of hunters. They are like wet obsidian, their bodies long and low and almost too dark to see against the damp ceramic. They orient by the wall’s electrical fields, reading the faint currents beneath the surface. When they hunt, they do not chase. They edge forward, then freeze, then take one crab or a young fly with a motion too quick to follow. The crabs scatter in a ripple, and the pipe-frogs leap from the pool margin to the wall, clinging to the wet surface until the skink settles again.

In the pools, the eels drift. They are finger-long and nearly transparent, so the water seems empty until one of them turns and catches the glow from the wall. They are shadows that swim. They rise when the skinks grow still, snapping at larvae and the occasional ash-fly that touches the water. The eels and the skinks exist in a kind of alternation: when the lizards hunt, the eels sink; when the lizards rest, the eels rise. The hollow has its own predator-prey tide.

The glass spiders spin webs of polymer thread pulled from the wall’s sweated fluid. Their webs are not silk but something between plastic and glass, strung between seams and across the pool margins. In the dim light the webs are almost invisible, until the amber glow catches them and they become lines of oiled light. The spiders themselves are small and clear, their bodies like drops of the same fluid they spin. They catch ash-flies and young bell mites, and sometimes a skink tears through a web and carries the threads for hours, draped like silver scars across its dark back.

The most unusual resident is the mirror-wren. It nests behind the overhang, near the hissing pressure valve, and its feathers are reflective in a way that makes it disappear against the wet ceramic. It does not sing. When it calls, it perfectly mimics the hiss of the valve, using the machine’s own voice to warn other creatures away from its nest. The mirror-wren is the hollow’s mimic and sentry, a small bird-analogue that has learned to speak the language of the wall. Its reflection is not camouflage so much as agreement: the bird becomes part of the ceramic, part of the water, part of the hissing air.

Every six hours or so the wall’s pores open wider, and a warm fog blooms through the alcove. It is dense enough to erase the far wall. The pipe-frogs fall silent. The drip-crabs settle under their shells. The ash-flies cluster on the ceiling, and the bell mites dim or ring slower. For about twenty minutes the hollow is only fog, pulse, and the hiss of valves. The mirror-wren presses its reflective feathers flat and becomes a wet stone. The eels rise in the pools, hidden by the fog from the skinks. Then the pores narrow, the fog drains away, and the noise resumes, as if the whole place had been holding its breath and now lets it out.

No single creature owns the hollow. It is a condominium of small specialists, each fitted to the water, the mineral load, the electrical fields, the fog, the heat. The residents do not simply live there; they maintain it. They keep the water moving, suppress the repair response, cycle the nutrients, and fill the small space with noise and predation and light. They are not invaders. They are the reason the seam has not healed. And the place, in turn, lives through them—a hidden garden of feral cleaning organisms and sensor-creatures and escaped biological parts, still working, still breathing, still ringing from inside the wet ceramic dark.
