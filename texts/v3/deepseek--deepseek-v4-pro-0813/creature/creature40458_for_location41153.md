The first thing to say is that the city is not empty. It is full of lives, but they are lives that have learned to pass as part of the weeping stone and the patient machinery.

In the hot drainage channels, the oldest inhabitant is a film. It is a pink-white bloom of heat-loving microbes, the color of a sunburn gone pale, spread over the mineral crust like a living varnish. It does not look like much. But it is the base of everything. It feeds on sulfur and copper and the heat itself, and in places it grows thick enough to ripple in the current. When the water is still, the film releases a fine chain of bubbles; that is the whole city breathing at its smallest scale. This film is not a single creature but a community, though it behaves with a kind of unified patience, retreating from cold, spreading toward the warmest seams, and it has probably been here longer than any stone building.

Where the film grows heavy, the salt-glass mites appear. They are translucent, small enough to fit on a thumbnail, and they move with a slow, deliberate picking motion across the mineral fans and crystals. Their bodies are the color of steam and their legs are barely visible, so that a colony of them looks like a shiver in the crust. They eat the salt crusts, or the film, or both, and they leave faint clean trails behind them. They do not flee when a shadow passes; they simply stop, and in the dim sepia light they vanish into the mineral. They are not pests. They are more like the city’s cleanup crew, or its skin mites, and they stay near the warm vents where the mineral growth is most delicate.

Then there are the channel-fish. They are blind, milky, finger-long, and they live in the hot runnels and drainage channels. Their skin is so transparent that you can see the dark line of the spine and the slow red pulse of the heart. They do not swim so much as let the warm water carry them, their small mouths grazing the microbial film as they drift. They have no eyes, but they know the difference between hot and hotter, between mineral-rich and mineral-poor water. Sometimes a sudden rain or a burst of steam will lift one out of its channel and leave it in a puddle on a terrace, and it will lie there, gills moving in the wet air, until the next warm flow takes it back. The channel-fish are not abundant, but they are constant, and they are the reason the open channels are never quite still.

Below the streets, in the cisterns and the old flooded tunnels, there are larger things. The cistern efts are pale, soft-bodied amphibians, the length of a forearm or longer, with loose, folded skin like wet silk. They have no eyes; only two faint dark spots where eyes might have been. They sense heat and movement through rows of small pores along their sides, and they rest for weeks at a time on submerged ledges, their three pairs of feathery gills spread out in the water like pink-white ferns. They filter the water for the microbial film and whatever small life it carries. When they move, they uncoil slowly, as if the water itself has thickened and begun to drift. There are said to be larger ones in the deep cisterns, efts the size of dogs or bigger, but they are never seen. What is seen, sometimes, is a soft exhalation from below: the water level in a cistern drops by a finger, and a low sigh rises through the stone. That is enough.

There are also the tending machines. They are not animals, but they live in the city the way animals live in a body. They emerge from hatches and service tunnels on schedules that are not quite regular. A maintenance crawler with a dozen thin arms will travel along a pipe gantry, testing joints, tightening valves, tapping gauges with a small brass hammer. It has no eyes and no face, but it senses heat and pressure and the sound of a leak. Another machine, a rail-bound sweeper, scrapes mineral crust from the tram rails once a week with a slow, patient rasp. These machines do not think, but they have habits. They are the city’s other inhabitants, its mechanical fauna, and they are as pale and silent and heat-adapted as the living things.

And then, in the largest sense, the city itself is the true inhabitant. It breathes. It swallows water and exhales steam. It weeps from its walls and shudders through its foundations. The creatures in its channels and cisterns are not visitors; they are part of its body, like the bacteria in a gut or the mites on skin. The city was built for people once, but now it is built for itself, and the things that live there are the small, pale, patient lives that have learned to live inside a heat engine. They do not wait for anyone to return. They do not need the sun. They have the sulfur, the warm water, the mineral crust, and the steady, breathing dark.
