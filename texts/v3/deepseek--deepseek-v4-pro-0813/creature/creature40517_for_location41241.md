At first there is no one. The center stays empty; the air holds it open. Then you look at the wall, down near the floor where two great roots meet in a low arch, and the wall changes. It is not a root. It is a long body, folded into the arch, the same color as the wood, the same wet sheen, the same ancient stillness.

It is not bark. It is skin—smooth, cool-looking, with fine wrinkles where the body bends. A film of water glistens on it, the same condensation that beads on every surface of the hollow. Along its flanks, tiny pores open and close in no hurry, like small mouths tasting the air. It has four short legs, pale and soft, each with three toes. They are not built for walking. They are built for holding still.

Its head is broad and flat, shaped like a water-worn stone. Where the eyes should be, there are only two dark patches beneath the skin, old buds that never opened. Behind the head, three pairs of gills trail into the damp air. They are deep red, almost crimson, and they stir when the pulse of the plant rises, then settle again. It is a kind of salamander—or something older than a salamander, a siren that has grown into the place.

From its blunt snout to the tip of its tail, it is as long as a tall man. Its body is as thick as a thigh, but it seems smaller because it lies so well fitted to the root’s curve. Its tail, flattened and soft-finned, wraps once around a root and disappears into a crevice. The wood has grown over part of its left hip. It is not trapped. It could free itself, if it ever wanted to, with a slow peeling sound and a wet slide into another hollow. It does not want to.

The drip from the root above falls onto its back every few seconds. The skin there is a little paler, polished by the same drop for longer than memory. When the water lands, it spreads into the fine channels between the creature’s ribs and runs toward the floor. The creature does not shake it off. The water is its air. It breathes through its skin, through its gills, through the soft lining of its mouth. When the deep mechanical thrum of the living pipes rises, its flanks rise a half-second later, then fall. It is not the source of the sound. It rides the sound the way a bellows rides the air, a soft bellows the chamber has grown around.

Now and then a frog drops from a bromeliad and lands near its head. The creature does not turn. Its gill tufts fan out, as if tasting the small disturbance. The mouth opens a little—not fast, not wide—and the floor of the mouth drops. There is a soft sip, a movement of air and water. The frog is inside. The mouth closes. For a moment the gills darken, then loosen. It may eat once a week, or once a month, or less. The rest of the time it takes its nourishment from the water itself, from the dissolved dust of bat guano and leaf mold that the hollow pump washes through the air and over the roots.

Nothing hunts it. It is too large, too still, too much a part of the wall. The green snake has slid across its back more than once, taking it for floodwood. The bats scramble over its tail on their way out at dusk, and it does not stir. Even the centipedes move around it as though it were a fallen trunk. It is not the ruler of the hollow pump. It is the soft, slow thing that keeps the edge clear—a living filter, a valve of flesh. Its droppings, a fine grey silt, collect beneath its belly, and the roots there grow thicker and darker than anywhere else in the chamber. The moss there is rich. The plant takes back what the creature does not need.

It has lived here for a very long time, probably since before the opening above was as wide as it is now. It may have arrived as a larva, small enough to swim in a hollow stem or a bromeliad cup. It grew as the roots grew, and the roots grew around it. Now it is old, older than the bats, older than the snake, older than the worn basin in the floor. It does not know its age. It does not know the hollow as a place. Its world is pressure and wetness and the taste of the air. It knows the difference between a frog and a falling leaf by the way the air bends. It knows the difference between a root and a snake by the small heat of muscle, or by something older and less nameable.

Stay until the light changes, and you may see it more clearly: the pale belly under the shadow, the red gills trembling, the slow bellows of its throat. Then the cloud closes, and it is gone again into the wall. It is not hiding. It is simply the right shape for the roots, and the roots are the right shape for it. It lives there the way water lives in a cistern, the way air lives in a bell.

It is the oldest animal in the hollow pump, and it is not waiting for anything. It is only continuing, exactly as the room continues, on the same slow water and green light.
