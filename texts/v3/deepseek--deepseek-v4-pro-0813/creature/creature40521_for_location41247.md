The one who lives there is not a figure you would see from the col. Even when the light is full, she is the same color as the pale quartzite: hair the grey-white of old firn, skin the color of stone dust, a patched smock of goat hide and woven saxifrage fiber rubbed with powdered rock until it vanishes against the talus. She is small, narrow-shouldered, and moves with the slow economy of something that has learned not to waste heat. Her hands are the texture of dry lichen; her eyes are black as the water cups, and they do not reflect the sky so much as hold it, still and cold.

She lives in the crevice beneath the stone slab pinned between two boulders, not because it is a shelter but because it is a good place to listen. She sleeps there in the coldest hours, breathing so shallowly that the frost on the moss does not melt. When the sun clears the rim and the first drips begin, she rises and moves out along the low slabs, following the same loops the animals use—never straight across the bowl, never between certain stones. Her footfalls do not crack the loose flakes; they settle them, as if the stone has been waiting for exactly that pressure.

She tends the water. Each morning she visits the stone cups in order, from the highest to the lowest, clearing tiny obstructions with a sliver of bone, testing the temperature with one finger, drinking only from the cup that has been warmed by the cairns. When overflow spills from one cup to another, she watches it with the patience of someone counting slow breaths. In the afternoon she sits in the sun-trap of a saxifrage hummock, twisting cord from hand-spun plant fiber, adding strands of goat wool and pika fur gathered from the talus. The rope wedged in the crack is hers, unfinished, waiting for the next hard season. She uses the carved bone needle to stitch her smock and to repair the windbreak at the northern edge, but only on days when the wind carries valley mist. She adds flat stones to the low semicircular wall, never making it higher than a sitting height, and weaves dwarf willow stems into the gaps until the wind no longer finds them.

The white stones on the pika hay piles are her doing. Each morning she touches each one once, leaving a faint warmth, and then sets it back on the same side, always the same side. The pikas have learned to accept this; they do not scatter when she passes. The snow finches follow her because her slow circuit stirs midges from the melt cups. The stoat gives her a wide, looping berth, as if she were a patch of dangerous shadow. The wild goats pause on the ledges above and watch, but they do not startle; sometimes she leaves a small handful of dried saxifrage on a ledge where they cross.

She does not speak. When she needs to call an animal or warn something away, she taps two pieces of quartzite together with a sound like a tiny glass bell, the same note as ice calving into the farthest cup. The place has shaped her voice out of her; all that remains is listening. When a single thread of condensation rises from a crack in the floor on a still morning, it is not the mountain breathing. It is her breath, held through the cold hours and released only when the sun clears the rim—she has been lying beneath the stone slab, breathing shallowly through the night, and for one moment the air shows it.

The cairns are warm because she sits on them. She places her hands flat on the top stone until the warmth soaks down, not for comfort but as a kind of attention. The stones remember longer than stone should. The lichen on the large striped boulder smells of damp earth and honey because she rests against it at midday and the heat of her body draws something out of the rock, slow and sweet. The animals loop around certain stones because those are the stones she has touched with warmth, and they have learned that warmth can mean danger or safety, and either way it is better to go around.

She may have been a woman once, high in a pass, stranded or choosing to stay. Or she may be the cirque itself, condensed into a shape that can walk its own bowl, a small and patient knot of the place’s own attention. It does not matter. The place has worn her down to the same sharp edges. She has no name, or if she does, it is a word for the pause between drips.

If you were to sit at the windbreak until evening, you might see her. She will not acknowledge you. She will tend the cups, touch the white stones, rest her hand on the warm cairn, and then step into a crack you had mistaken for shadow. The last light will hold the shape of her for a moment—a grey-white blur against the quartzite—and then the cold will close over it like water over a stone. The cirque will be quiet again, but not empty.
