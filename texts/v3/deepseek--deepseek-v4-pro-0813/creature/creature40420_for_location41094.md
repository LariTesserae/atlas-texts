What lives in the Unfinished Range is not a single thing. It is a set of lives pressed into the seams between heat and water, gathered at the nodes where the earth’s deep energy reaches the surface.

The first inhabitants are the mats. Around each warm spring, where the water is milky with minerals or clear and freezing only a finger’s length away, the surface carries a film of color: rust-orange, sulfur-yellow, dark green, white. The mats are not plants. They are communities of bacteria and archaea, layered so tightly that they become a skin. They eat the dissolved iron and sulfur that rise with the hot water. They do not need the sun. They are the base of everything, and they are everywhere the heat is, though they are easy to overlook because they look like stain rather than life.

Beneath them and across them live the first animals. The larvae of midges and brine flies are tiny and translucent, like shards of wet glass. They graze the bacterial film with a collective motion that makes the water shiver. In the warmer pools, where gas rises and breaks the surface in slow blinks, they gather in such numbers that the edge of the pool looks dusted with black soot. But the soot is alive. They absorb the minerals and the heat. When they pupate and rise, the air above each pool fills with a low cloud of adult flies. They do not buzz so much as tick, a sound like rain on hot stone. They stay close to the water, riding the steam, so the columns appear to have a second, finer grain.

Where the flies are thickest, the vent finches come. They are small and pale, grey as ash, with a dark line through the eye and a quick, nervous flick of the tail. They do not sing. They chitter, a sound like pebbles tapping together, and their constant sound is the voice of the hot ground. They nest in cracks near the vents, building small cups of moss and the down of larger birds. The earth warms their eggs. They fly directly through the steam, and when they come out their feathers are beaded with moisture, and they shake themselves in a puff of mist. They are not afraid of the heat. They are the only birds here that belong entirely to the warm ground; they may leave a single vent but they never leave the range.

Above them, on the high ledges, the fog gulls live in numbers so large that the colony seems less like a gathering of individuals than a single body stretched across the rock. They are grey and white, with black wingtips, and their droppings paint the stone in long white streaks that become, over years, a kind of soil. The smell is ammoniacal and sharp, but it is the smell of abundance. They rest between flights in packed rows, preening, turning their heads. When one rises, a few follow, and then a whole section of the ledge peels away in a grey sheet. They stream down into the fog in long, orderly lines, and their wings make a sound like heavy canvas being shaken. They feed on whatever swarms in the cloud layer or the cold sea beyond, and when they return each bird finds its exact ledge, its exact place, as though the colony is a loom being rethreaded. The eggs are laid on bare stone, mottled grey so they look like stones. The chicks are pale down and very still. When the adults leave, the ledge falls silent; the chicks do not call. It is only when the first adults return that the noise starts again, building like an engine.

Between the vents and the bird cliffs, the tephra pikas make their careful living. They are small, no bigger than a hand, with round ears and fur so dense it holds a layer of air against the damp. Their fur is the color of wet basalt, with rust-red under the chin and belly. They move in quick, darting runs between the moss patches and the bird colonies, and their tracks make little chains in the silt. They eat lichen and the insects that wash up around the pools, and they nibble the moss that grows below the ledges where the guano has enriched the rock. They have a habit of sitting up on their hind legs, front paws tucked, staring into the steam as if listening. Their trails always loop around the hottest ground, as if they carry a map of the heat in their bodies. In crevices near the warm ground they make small piles of dried moss and dead flies, stored with a neatness that is almost deliberate. Their call is a single sharp note, like a stone hitting another stone, and it carries farther than it should in the wet air.

At night, or in the dimmer hours, the steam owl moves between the columns. Its wings are soft-edged, and it flies without sound. Its eyes are large and dark, and they reflect the blue glimmer that sometimes travels along the wet rock. It is grey-brown, mottled like wet stone, with a pale face that appears and disappears in the fog. It hunts the pikas and the young gulls on the lower ledges. Its cry is a single low note, repeated at long intervals, and it seems to come from everywhere at once. The note is not loud, but it travels, and it is the only sound that seems to belong to the whole range rather than to any one part of it. It nests alone in a crack where two fault scarps meet, its nest a scrape in the mineral crust lined with the soft fur of pikas and the down of birds. There may be no other of its kind visible, only the note answering itself.

There are smaller lives too, easy to miss. In the water films that run down every cliff, rotifers and tardigrades live in the moisture itself. In the warm pools, tiny crustaceans and worms graze the same mats as the fly larvae, hidden under the milky surface. They are not separate from the mineral world; they are a kind of living moisture, a middle layer between water and stone. They survive in places that look sterile, and their presence explains why the mats do not grow unchecked.

Between these points, the land is empty. A kilometer of bare wet stone can pass without a single visible animal. But the emptiness is not absence. It is the distance between households, the unbuilt lots of a city that assembles itself around heat.

No person lives there. Nothing builds a wall or cuts a path. But the place is inhabited. The mats, the larvae, the flies, the finches, the fog gulls, the pikas, the owl are not visitors. They are the shapes the planet’s machinery takes when it touches the surface. And above or beneath them all is the range itself, which is not alive in the way a bird is alive but is not entirely dead either. It moves. It exhales. The steam is its breath, the water its blood, the heat its slow heartbeat. The creatures of the Unfinished Range are not separate from that process. They are the process, briefly given fur, feather, chitin, and call.
