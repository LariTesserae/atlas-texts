Ask what lives in the Sink and the first true answer is not a creature but a film: the seep-skin. It is a thin microbial and fungal bloom of rust, grey, and greenish white that follows the water out of the stone. Without it nothing else would hold. It is not one thing but a slow, mineral-eating community—some cells, some filaments, some spore-like bodies—so thin in places it is only a taste on the rock, elsewhere a tough scab under the hand. Its life follows the seepage, fattening toward the wet, crusting along the dry edge into pale mineral ridges. It drinks iron and sulfur from the dark. It is the base of everything.

Above the seep-skin, and half-buried in it, grows the floor. It is wrong to call it ground. It is a pavement of sessile animals, each fixed, each alive. Crown-pipes rise as calcified tubes, their soft bodies hidden in mineral sleeves; at their mouths they unfurl feathery crowns, not for light but for whatever the water-film and fog carry. Bell-skins lie among them like grey rubber hemispheres, taking slow breaths through their hides, their whole surface a mouth and a lung. Stalk-branchers stand up like plants, but their branches are colonies of polyps, each frond a tiny filter, swaying with a patience that looks like vegetation but is hunger. Plate-sponges mound over older shells, riddled with pores, straining the wet air and seepage for particles too small to see. Where one animal dies, another grows over it. Where tubes touch, they fuse. The floor thickens from below and above. It is architecture, but it is grown, not built.

On that living floor the movers are many and small. Blind many-legs pick between the tubes, stepping joint by joint, eating shed setae and shell dust, their antennae rowing the dark. Flat ribbon-mouths slide over the film like wet leaves, scraping the seep-skin with a tongue of tiny teeth, leaving polished paths behind them. Worm-shaped burrowers are the floor’s hidden plumbing: they tunnel through the mat, loosen dead layers, let water seep through their galleries. Their castings feed the film, and the film feeds the next wave of grazers. The air is not clean; it carries shed setae, shell dust, drifting particles, each with a chemical taste. To smell is to read the news of the mat.

In the black pools, something else waits. The coil-strikers are pale and long, with no eyes but lines of pressure-pits down their sides. They lie in the cold water like strips of dead skin, and when a many-leg falls in or a grazer lowers its head to drink, they do not chase. They uncoil. A mouth opens in the dark, and the surface of the pool barely changes. Afterward they settle back into their coils, and the water forgets them.

Beyond the pools, the stone-lumps wait. Their skin is crusted with mineral grains and old shell fragments, their outline a water-worn boulder. They can stay still long enough for the film to grow over them. Some carry a small light, red or pale, like a wet ember, held just above the mouth. The light is not for seeing; it is for drawing. A curious many-leg approaches, a young grazer nips at the glow—then the lump opens. The Sink’s stillness is full of such hidden mouths.

Overhead, the click-wings pass. They are flat and ribbon-boned, membranes stretched between long fingers, and they ride the slow, cold exhalations of the basin. Because there is no light to steer by, they make their own: a faint click, then listen, then click again, the sound bouncing off walls and bodies. Their bodies tick as they move, a noise so thin it is more felt than heard. They fold and drop and take the small moving things off the mat, wings brushing the tube-crowns, leaving a pause in the hiss.

Sound is the true terrain. The hiss is the base: millions of tiny scrapings. Under it run clicks and rasps and chirps, each species occupying a frequency. The callers are the loud ones. Some are sessile, fixed to the mat, their bodies shaped like bells or flutes; they sing by forcing water or air through internal chambers, and the basin returns their songs with the delay of distance. A call is a claim. It says: this patch is mine, this hollow, this seepage channel. The loudest are not always the largest; some are small but shaped to throw sound far. When a predator moves, the hiss drops away in a wave, then alarm clicks jump across the mat, and only after the shape has passed does the scraping return. Silence is not rest; it is the whole floor holding its breath.

In places the mat thickens over hidden hollows—old pools, collapsed burrows, the spaces left by dying sponges—and becomes a false floor. It can bear the weight of a grazer, even a herd, until it cannot. Then a passage of slow herd-grazers, armored with overlapping plates, their undersides rasping, opens a seam. Water and pale animals pour out. The pale animals have never known open air; they are soft, eyeless, translucent as tendons. They writhe and are taken before they can find the dark again. The herd moves on, leaving a new bay of exposed, wet stone, which the seep-skin will colonize within days.

Death is not an ending here; it is a change of tenancy. Shell-borers, tiny and patient, dissolve the mineral tubes of the dead, etching the calcium back into the water. Scavenging worms follow the chemical traces of decay. The film grows over the empty space. A dead sponge becomes a sponge-shaped hole in the mat, then a pond, then a patch of new crust. Nothing is wasted except the light, and there was never any light to waste.

At long intervals, the dark pulses. Not from any single source but from the sessile colonies, which sometimes glow in slow rhythms—some blue-green, some pale red—so that for a few heartbeats the basin is a map of shells and tubes. The glow does not illuminate so much as texture the darkness; it reveals the crowdedness, the touching. In pools disturbed by a predator, the bacterial films flash blue-green where torn, a bruise of light. Some animals have only light-sensitive patches, and they read these pulses as weather.

To live in the Sink is to obey a few old rules. Eat the film, or eat what eats the film, or eat the dead. Claim space by sound or by growth. Hide by being silent or still, but not too still: stay too long and the crust may begin to grow over your body. The Sink has had a very long time to learn its shape, and every living thing in it is one of its habits.
