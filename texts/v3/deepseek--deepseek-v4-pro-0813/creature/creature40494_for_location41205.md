You have seen the hollow, but you have not seen its people. That is not your fault. They are not in the habit of being seen.

At first, the town seems to be tending itself. The copper pots are placed just so. The valve wheels are warm. The chalk marks on the root-braids have been renewed within the hour. The machines do not rust; the pilot lights do not gutter. Someone has been here, but the someone is a quality of the dark, not a figure in it.

Then you learn to see them. They are not hiding. They are simply slow, and the eye, in that light, is not patient. A figure on the high gantry, standing so still that mist beads on its shoulders. It has been there long enough that the moss on the railing has begun to grow around its fingers. It is not waiting; it is listening. When it turns, its face is pale as a grub, and its eyes are two dark coins. It raises one long hand and taps a code on the root-braid—three soft knocks, then a pause, then two. Somewhere across the chamber, a blue light blinks, twice. The figure steps sideways into the shadow and is gone, not because it vanished, but because it became the shadow.

The inhabitants are human-shaped, but only in the way a coat is shaped like a body. Their proportions are odd, or extra, or missing. One has too many knuckles. Another has a spine that flexes like a root. Their skin is the color of the wall-glow, pale green-white, and in some places it peels to reveal a darker layer, like bark. Their veins run green, or black, or, in one case, a thread of bright copper. Some of their faces are nearly familiar; others are not faces at all, but smooth knobs of living wood with two wet eyes set deep. They move with a slow, deliberate economy, as if every step is a word they must choose carefully. Their feet are bare and wide, with toes that grip the root-floor. Their hands are long, and their nails are thick and dark, made for tapping.

There are not many of them. Ten, perhaps; twelve, if the side gallery is currently real. The number is not kept. They do not have names as we do. They are called by their tasks, or by the sound their tapping makes. The one who listens is the Ear. The one who feeds the furnace is the Warm. The one who catches the water is the Cup. The one who reads the brass dials is the Gauge. The one who walks the conditional rooms is the Cartographer. The one who carries the blue flame is the Lamp-Keeper. They use the same word for “person” and “room,” because they say a person is a room the plant has not finished building.

Each of them has become shaped by their task. The boiler-tender is broad and stooped, with heat-cracked skin and fingers that can reach into the furnace mouth without flinching. The root-listener has one ear that is always pressed to the root-braid, and the side of its head has grown soft and curved to fit the root. The gauge-reader is small and light, with eyes that are all pupil, and it climbs the great ribs on a ladder of knotted rope, reading the dials by touch more than by sight. The cartographer has no eyes at all, but its hands are covered in chalk dust, and it moves through the rooms by counting its own steps and feeling the weight of the shadows. The lamp-keeper is the only one who carries light, a small blue flame in a glass jar, and its face is the most human of all, because it is the one who must remember what faces look like.

They are not separate from the plant. They do not speak of “plants” and “animals” and “machines” as separate. They speak of “the living” and “the built,” and even those terms are provisional. They themselves are both and neither. Some of them have leaves growing from their shoulders, small and dark and wet. Some have brass fittings bolted into their skulls or arms, with tubes that run into their veins. Some have rootlets growing at their joints, or their scars heal with a thin layer of bark. They do not find this unusual. They call themselves the Wakeful, because they are the parts of the plant’s dream that have learned to tap back.

Their days are not days. There is no true dawn, only a change in the bioluminescence, a shift in the sap tide. When the sap rises, the pipes knock softly, and the floor swells by a finger’s width. The Wakeful wake. They move out onto the platforms, their bare feet silent on the wet wood. They open the valves, and the gas jets flare blue. The boiler-tender feeds the furnace with a cake of root-resin and metal salts. The water-catcher empties the copper pots. The root-listener climbs to the crown and taps a message down the root-braid. The message travels through the plant, and the grubs on the floor pulse their lights in reply. For a moment, the whole town is a constellation of small, deliberate lights, and the Wakeful stand among them, not separate, but as the brightest points.

They eat the plant, but carefully. They scrape the sap-skin from the roots and ferment it into a sour paste. They grind root-fiber into a flour and bake it on the boiler’s warm casing. They eat the grubs, but only the ones with three light-dots, because the ones with five are said to be the plant’s dreams and should not be disturbed. They drink the condensation water, cold and tasting of copper and green leaves. They share their food in silence, breaking the cakes into pieces and passing them on broad leaves. Their mouths move, and no sound comes out, but the taps on the root-braid continue, like a low conversation made of knuckles.

They do not build houses. The town is their house. They sleep in the hollows of the great roots, or on the platforms under the leaking pipes, or in the warm space behind the boiler. Their hammocks are slung between roots, and they sleep with their hands folded on their chests, like the dead, so the plant can grow over them if they do not wake. They have few possessions: a chalk stick, a copper key, a glass vial of sap, a knotted cord for recording. They carry these in pouches of oiled root-fiber, because pockets fill with water. They mark their sleeping places with a twist of wire or a knot of root-fiber, but these marks are often moved. They do not mind. They say the plant is rearranging the furniture.

Their speech is rare and low, a breathy whisper that sounds like the creak of roots. But most of their communication is not speech. They tap on the root-braids, in codes that travel through the living wood. They use hand signs, and small colored lights that blink in the dark. They have a dry humor, expressed in taps: a certain rhythm means “the valve is lying again,” and another means “the shadow is sitting in my usual place.” They do not laugh aloud; they tap a pattern that sounds like rain on a leaf.

They know the rules of the place. They know that space is conditional, and they do not trust a room until it has been measured and agreed upon. When a new street appears, the cartographer goes first, marking the threshold with chalk and a knotted cord. If the shadows are too heavy, the room is closed with a chalk line and left alone. If the room is safe, the others come, one by one, and they walk its length, counting their steps. They say the town is still being dreamed by the plant, and that the dream revises itself. They are the revision’s proofreaders.

They remember the outside, but as a kind of myth. The oldest of them, the one they call the Rememberer, says that the outside is a place where the light is not hoarded but poured out, where the air is not heavy with sap, where the ground is made of dead stone instead of living root. The others listen politely, but they do not believe it. To them, the outside is the plant’s other dream, the bright one, the one they are not invited to. They are not certain it is real. They are not certain they are real, either. They have decided it does not matter.

They have rituals, but they are quiet ones. They leave out saucers of sap for the six-legged fox-like thing that runs along the gantries, because it does not disturb the signal wires when it is fed. They talk to the blind newts in the black pools, because the newts are said to be the plant’s thoughts, swimming in the mirrors. They cultivate the glow-moss on their own skin, rubbing it into their arms so they can be seen in the dark, or not seen, depending on their mood. They mourn their dead by carrying the body to a root hollow and covering it with sap and ash. The plant grows over it, and in a season there is a new node on the root, and they say the person has become a room.

They are not lonely. The town is small, but it is full. They have the grubs and the moths and the Gantry-Hound and the slow breathing of the walls. They have the machines, which are alive in their own way, and the roots, which are the town’s true streets. They have each other, and they have the work. The work is enough. The work is the thing that keeps them from drifting into the weighted shadows and becoming part of the dark too soon.

They are the town’s youngest organ: the ones who turn the valves, read the dials, catch the water, tap the messages, and walk the new rooms. They are the ones who live in the hollow below the works. They are not separate from the plant, nor from the machines, nor from the animals. They are the part of the place that has learned to say, very quietly, in the dark, “Here I am. Here we are.” And the plant breathes, and the pipes knock, and the grubs pulse their lights, and the Wakeful go on tending the dream.
