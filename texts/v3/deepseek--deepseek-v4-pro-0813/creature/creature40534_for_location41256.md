The thing that lives there is not hidden. You have been hearing it since you came through the notch. You have been standing inside it.

At first the eastern cleft looks like a slit of shadow. Then you see that the shadow has edges, and the edges are moving, very slightly, in time with the thud. The cleft is not empty. It is occupied. A long body lies wedged in it, headless, segmented, each segment a plate of black glassy stone, the same stone as the walls. The plates are jointed so cleanly they look broken a moment ago, but they have been fitted to each other for longer than the mountain has had weather. Between the plates runs a membrane the color of tarnished silver, and through it a slow light moves, not like a lamp but like the underside of a wave.

The body fills the cleft like a piston in a cylinder. Its upper end disappears into the rock, not into a hole but into the stone itself, without a seam. Its lower end is hidden by mist and the pool that never spills. It does not move the way an animal moves. Its stillness is the stillness of an idling engine: full of small, exact, repeated tensions. The plates do not grind; they rest against each other with the faintest metallic hiss, and that hiss is the same hiss that fills the whole basin.

The newts are its gills. Or rather, the newts are not really newts. They are gill-fronds that have budded from the body’s slits and settled on the ledges, still attached to the parent by something too fine to see, still opening their mouths in exact time with the thud. They are breathing for a body too large to breathe for itself. When the deep pulse comes, they gape. When it passes, they close. The red of their gills is the only bright color the body has allowed.

The thud is its heartbeat. You do not see the heart, but you feel it in the soles of your feet and behind your eyes. The pool surfaces flex with each beat. The midge cloud contracts an instant before. If you pick up a stone and hold it to your ear, you hear the same thud, faint and steady, because the stone is part of the body. The water is its blood. The straight channels are its vessels. The stream that climbs the tilted slab is not climbing; it is being pumped back up by a pulse too slow to see. The waterfall that drops halfway, turns to mist, and rises is not falling; it is inhaling. The droplet that detaches from the fern tip and vanishes before it hits the stone is one blood cell completing a circuit too fast to follow. It is already back on the leaf, because the circulatory system of this body is closed, and time in it has been folded into a loop.

The roots are its nerves. They run over the rock like insulated cables, and where they pass through solid stone without a crack, that is not a trick. The stone and the root have agreed not to notice each other because they are both the body of the same thing. The lichens are its sensory skin, silver-green and faintly warm, laid out in circuit-like patterns that match the paths of the roots. The fog columns are its fingers, or its held breath, standing between the plants like structural supports. The shadows are its attention. One shadow points toward the valley and another toward the sky because it is looking at you with two separate organs, from two different directions, and it has not yet decided whether you are inside it or outside it.

The other animals are its reflexes. The black millipedes with brassy legs are the contractions of its deeper gut, moving in closed loops around the boulders that are its joints. The snails with shells of the same black rock are scales or armored corpuscles, polishing the stone skin in slow, unending circuits. The bird in the pale tree is a valve. It opens its beak and lets out a small jet of steam, not as a call but as a release of pressure from the larger body’s respiratory system. The sound that arrives a second later from the wrong direction is the same note traveling through a nerve pathway that does not match the bird’s position. The midge cloud is not a swarm; it is an aerosol of the body’s exhalation, held in a standing wave by the high whine.

The high whine is its voice, or its thought. It sits in your teeth like metal on metal, though there is no metal. It is the sound of a mind that is not thinking but idling, a dream with no images, a song with no sequence. The mineral-oil tang is its breath. The smell of ozone is its electrical field.

It does not hunt. It does not flee. It does not feed. It has no need to, because the whole basin is its body, and the body is already complete. The plants are its lungs and skin. The animals are its cells. The water is its blood. The stone is its bone. The fog is its connective tissue. The sound is its pulse. It is not a creature that lives in the place; it is the place, one living thing that has been cut off from the rest of the world and left running.

You came through the notch into a high, wet room of the world, but the room is not a room. It is a chest cavity. The inhabitant is the idling heart of the place, and it has been living there so long that living and being have become the same steady thud. If you need a name for it, call it the Idling One. But names are for things that can be separated from their homes. This thing cannot be separated from its home, because its home is its body. It is the only thing that truly lives there, and it is the place itself.
