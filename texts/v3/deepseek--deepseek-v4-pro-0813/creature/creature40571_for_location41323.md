They are not the builders. The builders are gone so long that the city no longer remembers their faces, only the way they dressed the stone. What lives there now is something smaller, softer, and far more patient.

They are the color of the dust and the lichen. Their skin is pale gray-brown, sometimes olive where the light falls, and it has a fine powdery bloom like the surface of a dry leaf. They are shaped like people, but stretched and quieted. They stand a little shorter than a tall man, with long arms and long hands, fingers thin and jointed like the twigs of the gray shrubs. Their legs are lean, their feet broad and flat for walking on dust and cracked paving. They move slowly, not from weakness but from the same economy as the herds. Their breath is shallow and cool; it does not fog. Their eyes are large, dark, and set deep, with a second inner lid that comes down when the wind drives the dust. Their ears are the most striking thing: not pointed, but cupped and broad, with fine muscles that can turn them independently, catching the long echoes that bounce around the basin. They hear the city the way others see it.

They have no fire, no metal, no tools. Their hands are enough. They pick insects from cracks, pull soft lichen from stones, and lift the thin films of dew from leaves with their fingertips. They get their water by licking the dew from stone in the early morning, or by pressing their skin against wet walls and letting the moisture soak in. They eat the small pale flies, the beetles, the moths, and the young shoots of the waxy shrubs. They never hunt the large herbivores; they follow them, because the herds know where the fog settles thickest, and where the vegetation is richest. They wear nothing, or at most a loose mat of dried lichen and shed fur gathered from the branches, not woven, held in place by its own roughness.

Their lives are built around the fog. They sleep through the brightest part of the day, curled in the hollows of the old cisterns, in the mouths of doorways, under overhangs where the stone stays cool. At dusk, when the first thin mist rolls over the rim, they wake. They come out of their hiding places one by one, like shadows thickening. They move to the high terraces where the fog is combed by the hanging plants, and they stand with their arms spread, letting the dew settle on their skin and hair. They lick their own forearms and the backs of their hands, slow and methodical, as if the night itself is a meal. They do not speak during this time. The only sounds are the tick of insects and the low, distant calls of the herds.

Their language is not like ours. It is a language of low notes, placed carefully into the city's echo. They do not have many words; they have phrases that are meant to be bounced off a particular wall or through a particular arch. A single call, pitched to the right frequency, can travel for kilometers, returning to the speaker as a chord. They use this to know where others are, to warn of predators, to find mates, to mark the edges of territories. They also use it to listen. They have a practice, which might be called prayer or conversation, of sitting in the amphitheater at the center of the city and letting the wind play through the fluted stone. They do not sing then; they listen. They say that the city speaks in its own voice, and that if you listen long enough, you can hear the builders' footsteps still echoing, too faint for the ears, but not for the bones.

They live in small groups, never more than twelve or fifteen, scattered across the city. Each group claims a district: a cluster of rooms, a colonnade, a series of cisterns. They do not build new walls or mend old ones; they only clear away the dust from certain doorways with their hands, and they mark the boundaries with scent and with the pattern of their calls. They sleep in the same places for years, and their bodies wear the stone smooth in the shape of their sleeping. The groups are not isolated; they meet at certain times of the year, when the fog is thickest, at the big central plazas. There they exchange news, tell long stories in overlapping echoes, and match the young ones who are ready to leave their birth group. These gatherings are quiet by our standards, but to them they are deafening, a festival of resonance. The flyers rise in clouds at the disturbance, and the herds move to the edges of the city.

Their children are born slowly, one at a time, after a long gestation. They are born covered in a fine, pale down, their ears already open. They do not cry; they make a small, questioning note that the mother answers with a soft echo from her throat. The child learns to listen before it learns to walk. It is carried against the mother's chest, where it can feel the vibrations of her voice through her ribs. Later, it is taught to find dew, to pick the right lichen, to avoid the places where the predators wait. It is also taught the history of the city, not in words but in sound. Each important place has a note, and the sequence of notes is a story. The child learns that the city was not made by hands but by a great silence that shaped the stone, and that the people are the listeners who keep the silence from being empty. They do not know the real builders. They do not wonder about them. The city is simply there, like the basin, like the fog, like the sky.

They are not many. The city could hold thousands, but there are only a few hundred of them in the whole basin. They are spread thin, like the lichen, appearing only where the conditions are right. In some districts, there are none at all, only the wind and the dust and the slow collapse of walls. But where they live, they are part of the place, as much as the gnarled trees and the nesting flyers. They have no name for themselves that can be written. They are simply the ones who stay, the ones who listen, the ones who lick the dew from the old stones and wait for the fog to return.
