The first sign is not the animal itself. It is a place where the mud has been turned in broad, shallow folds, as if the ground has been kneaded by something too slow to be a storm and too heavy to be a bird. The tracks are not sharp. They are wide, five-toed depressions, each as broad as a lily pad, with a smooth channel where a tail or a belly has passed. The water in these tracks has already filled from below, so each print is a small dark mirror.

If you wait there, in the late afternoon when the light begins to slant, you may see the mound at the edge of the far pool. At first it is only a low shape of wet stone and silt, and the water lies over it. Then the water moves, not with a splash but with a long, slow peeling away, and the stone rises. The eyes come first, set wide on a flat head, gold and still. Then the back, broad as a table, pitted and folded, the color of old limestone with patches of the same dark green crust that covers the mud. Then the legs, short and thick, and the wide splayed feet. The whole body is a thing the land might have made from itself.

It stands in the shallows, water running from it in thin sheets, and breathes once, deeply, through its skin. The throat pulses. The sides expand and settle. It is not a bird or a frog or a crab, though it shares something with all of them. It is the kind of animal that belongs to this place the way the water table does: always present, always just under the surface.

When it moves, it does not hurry. The front foot lifts, spreads, and comes down with the toes open, and the mud receives it without a sound. The back foot follows. The low tail drags behind, leaving a smooth furrow that will fill with water by morning. It walks this way from one pool to the next, along the faint runnels that the land already knows, and the water barely breaks around its legs. The sound of its movement is not a step but a slow settling, as if the ground is simply accepting it.

At the edge of a pool, it lowers its head. The mouth is not a beak or a jaw; it is a wide, soft line that seems to have no expression except patience. It touches the living mat of algae and cyanobacteria and begins to graze, peeling the dark crust away in long, clean strips. The mud beneath is pale and wet. The walker moves in an arc, leaving a trail like a snail’s path but wide enough to lie down in. It feeds for a long time, and the sound is a soft wet rasp, almost lost under the drone of insects.

When the light is almost gone, it sinks into deeper water. Its legs fold, and the pool closes over its back, leaving only the eyes and the two low ridges of its spine above the surface. Minnows return. Tadpoles cloud the water near its sides. The walker waits, still as a sunken log, until a small fish passes too close. Then the mouth opens under the surface and the water rushes in, carrying the fish and a few tadpoles with it. The head lifts, the water drains out through the corners of the mouth, and the throat moves once. Then it sinks again, and the pool becomes a pool again.

It is not the only one, but the others are far away. A walker holds a territory of many pools, a day’s slow walking across the flats. It does not call often. When it does, the sound does not begin in the air. It begins in the chest of the animal, passes through the water and the stone, and rises into the ground under your feet. It is a low hollow note, a single pulse, like the sound of a deep hole breathing out. The frog chorus pauses for a moment and then resumes. Far off, another walker answers, not with the same note but with a shorter, softer one, like a stone dropped into a well. In the wet season, when the whole plain becomes one shallow lake, the calls come more often. The walkers move toward each other through the wide water, not in a herd but in a loose gathering of dark islands. They breed then, in the season when the horizon disappears and a bird can seem to walk across the sky.

The young are not like the adults. They are small, long-tailed, with feathery gills and bright eyes, and they live among the tadpoles and the minnows. Most are eaten. The ones that survive grow slowly, shedding their gills only after many months, learning the work of the land. By their tenth year they are the length of a forearm, and they have begun to dig. They clear the silt from small depressions, pressing it aside with their front feet. Water seeps in from below. Frogs find these little pools. Crabs burrow at their edges. The young walker does not know it is making a place for others; it only knows the motion.

In a dry year, the sheet water pulls back and the plain becomes a cracked mosaic. The pools shrink until they are crowded and loud. Then the walkers become the keepers of the last water. They dig down into the basins, deeper than the birds can probe, deeper than the roots of the sparse grass. The water rises around them from below, and they rest there, half-submerged, for weeks. Frogs and wading birds gather at the shrinking margins. A heron may stand on a walker’s back, picking at the snails that grow there, and the walker does not shake it off. The sound of the crowded pool is a chorus of many small lives, but beneath it, if you listen, there is the deep, slow pulse of the animal waiting.

The old ones look less like animals than like places. Their backs are crusted with silt and colonized by algae, studded with tiny snails. They are so still that heat shimmer moves over them without a ripple. At midday, when the distant shrubs seem to float, a walker at the far edge of a pool is only another low mound, another stone. But if you watch long enough, the mound will shift, or the eyes will open, and the whole landscape will adjust itself around the fact of its presence.

It is old. There is no way to know how old, because it leaves no record except the land. The pools it tends are arranged in a loose line along the faint slope, and each one has been dug and re-dug, year after year, by the same broad feet. The land has not been touched by the human will to make or leave something behind, but it has been touched by this other will, which is not a will to build but a will to keep the water open. The walker does not leave a wall or a path. It leaves a basin. The basin fills with rain and seepage, and the frogs come, and the birds come, and the crabs come, and the sound comes. In this way the walker is not the center of the land, but it is one of the reasons the land has so many centers.

At dawn, when the wide sheets of water reflect the sky so completely that the horizon disappears, the walker may stand far out in the shallows, and the water around its legs is full of clouds. It appears to be walking across the sky, a low dark shape moving through the reflected light, and the birds that step near it appear to be doing the same. It does not call at dawn. It only stands, letting the light come over it, and breathes slowly through its skin.

If you came upon it in the night, you would not know it at first. You would see a low shape on the open flat, perhaps a rock or a mound of mud, and then the shape would shift, and the wide golden eyes would open, and you would understand that the ground itself was alive and watching. It would not move away. It would not move toward you. It would only wait, as the water waits under the stone, as the land waits for the next wet season or the next dry one. And then you would pass, and it would remain, part of the land’s patience, part of the water’s memory, until the next slow step, the next pool, the next long breath.
