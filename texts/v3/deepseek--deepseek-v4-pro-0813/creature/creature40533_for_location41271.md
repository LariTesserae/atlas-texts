Corbel Sink is not empty, but its emptiness is the first thing a stranger notices. The city was built for thousands; now it holds perhaps forty, and those forty are quiet and widely spaced. If you stand on the old market ledge, you may see a hundred doorways and no face. But the place is not empty. The place is full of lives that have become small, slow, and perfectly shaped to the dark.

The first lives are in the walls. Sweat-thread grows in the dampest seams, a pale lace of mineral and living tissue, not plant, not fungus. It pulls water out of the humid air and holds it in tiny beads. The Keepers press cloth to the wall to gather it. They call it “the city’s hair,” and they speak to it politely when they must take more. Here and there, from the oldest masonry, pale hair-like stalks reach toward the vents, swaying in air currents, eating heat and sulfur. In a few table-sized gardens, red sump-grass bends toward heat-lamps, and grey rootless sump-vines hang from balconies, putting out small water-filled berries that taste of salt and iron. A gardener named Cinder tends them. She is thin and pale, with a blue stain on her left cheek from a dust-manta. She calls the vines “the long sisters” and waters them with condensation saved in jars. When she picks the berries, she leaves three for the geckos, because the geckos keep the crickets from eating the grass.

The animals are not many but they are everywhere. Translucent blind crickets tick near the warm pools; their sound is part of the city’s weather, a dry second hand under the drip of water. Pearl-white geckos, nearly transparent, hunt them. Their eyes are black dots under pale skin, and when they move across a lit wall, their bodies glow like small moons. High in the dome, bat-like things roost. The Keepers call them “the surface’s fingers” because they leave at night through a small vent and return before dawn, carrying the cold taste of outside on their wings. Their droppings feed the sump-grass; their wing-scratch is like sand falling. Then there are the dust-mantas, flat, soft-bodied creatures the size of a hand, which ride the thermal currents and eat metal flakes and salt. They are not tame, but they are curious. To have one land on you is good luck, and it leaves a faint blue stain that never quite fades. Several of the Keepers carry these stains like medals.

In the central sump lives the Long Resident. It is not a fish, not a snake, not any animal the Keepers have a name for. It is old, pale as a scar, and almost never seen whole. Maro, the sump-tender, is the only one who goes fully into the water. He hums a low note that matches the pump’s thrum, and the Long Resident surfaces as a change in pressure rather than a shape—a long, slow, cooler presence, maybe two metres below, maybe twelve. It sheds a white film twice a year, which the Keepers gather from the water and use as bandage-cloth. Maro says the Long Resident is the city’s digestion. Others say it is the city’s dream. The child, Fenn, says it is a door that forgot to close.

The people themselves are small in number and large in silence. They call themselves the Keepers, or sometimes the Downstairs. They are pale as the salt pan above, with wide pupils and long, thin hands. Their voices are low, and they use them rarely. For distance, they tap messages on stone or pipes with knuckles or a small brass wand. The tapping is a language of rhythm and pause: a message can travel from the sump to the upper galleries faster than a person can climb. They know the stairs by the count and by the sound of each step; they know the doorways by the warmth of the lintels; they know the bridges by the hum of their cables. A lit lamp, among them, is a claim or a warning. Strangers descending from the surface carry bright lamps and cannot hear anything for the noise. The Keepers find them by the light, and they do not call it an arrival. The child knows the whole city grid by the feel of the floor under her feet. She has never seen the sun, and she does not miss it.

The Keepers are organized by work, not by family, though blood matters somewhat. Old Oud is the Listener. She sits near the tram station, where the brass bell rings once every eleven minutes. The bell has rung for so long that it is a heartbeat. Oud does not count it with numbers; she counts it in the bones of her ear, the hinge of her jaw, the pulse at her wrist. When the bell is late, she knows before the silence becomes loud, and she taps a warning into the stone: pause, pause, pause. Everyone stops. The city’s sudden quiet is the one sound they fear, because a stopped thing in Corbel is either dead or about to become dangerous. Oud is old and nearly blind, but her ears are so attuned to the city that she can tell the health of the sump by the pitch of the pumps, the mood of the air by the gate’s exhale. She wears a brass key on a cord around her neck, but no one knows what it opens.

Maro, the sump-tender, is broader and warmer than the others, because he spends his days near the geothermal vents. His arms are scarred with fine white lines from the heat. He maintains the water recyclers—the glass vats of glowing bacteria that eat salt and heavy metals—and he owns no water himself, only measures it. He knows the sump’s four fountains, which flow for exactly six minutes each cycle, and he can smell the water quality by the sharpness of the ozone in the air. When the sump breathes every fourteen hours, he walks the lower channels and listens to the water rise and drain. He taught Fenn to throw a pebble into the dark and hear the city answer.

Aunt Silt is the Threader. She is small, grey, and her fingers are permanently red from tying knots. She maintains the wrong places. There are rooms in Corbel that cannot fit inside the wall, corridors longer in one direction than the other, a lift that sometimes arrives with a passenger who was not there before. These are not stories; they are obstacles. Aunt Silt marks them with red thread, white stones, small careful knots. She walks the anchor points on the outer edge, never the inner curve, and she does not enter a room that is too quiet. If a new wrongness appears, she unspools thread and ties it around the nearest pipe or knob, leaving enough slack for the thing to breathe. The Keepers need her. She is their negotiation with the city’s habits.

Kel of the North Gate keeps the three-times-daily exhale. The gate opens and shuts on a timer no one can reach, and Kel stands inside the grinding breath and listens. He can tell if the gate is opening a fraction too slow, if the air moving through it tastes of surface dust or of something else. He cleans the gate’s track with a brush of wire and oil, and he never turns his back to it. When the gate stops, even for an hour, he gathers the others at the market ledge.

Cinder, the gardener, is also the keeper of the heat-lamps. She understands how much light the plants need without blinding the city. She grows the sump-grass and the vines, and she cooks a dark, salty bread in the community oven on the east wall. Her kitchen is one of the few places with a heat-ribbon always glowing, a soft orange line around the doorway. It is the warmest room in Corbel, and the child spends many hours there.

Fenn, the child, is the youngest. She may be the last. She counts stairs not by number but by sound: the seventh step whines, the thirty-second rings, the fortieth swallows her footfall. She can walk from the east market to the granary in perfect dark, never touching a wall, and she knows the red knots by the way the wall’s warmth changes where the thread pulls. She feeds the blind crickets by spitting on her palm and holding it to the stone. She believes the cleaning constructs are the city’s children. She waves to the empty tram when it passes, and sometimes—though she will not say it to the others—she sees something wave back. She carries a small jar of glow-mold at her belt, but she never lights it unless she is hurt or afraid. That is the rule.

The cleaning constructs are almost alive. They are eight-legged machines of old metal and glass, with a green flicker behind their lenses. They come out at irregular intervals to polish mosaics, clear salt blooms, remove debris. They wait if you stand in their path, then continue. They have no names, but some of the Keepers name them anyway: One-of-Four, Slow Glass, Left-Ankle, Mender. The child says they dream when they stand still. The adults do not argue.

Then there is the last resident, which is not human and not animal and not one of the named things. It is the city itself. Corbel has a kind of attention, not conscious but present. The locals do not pray to it, exactly, but they speak to it as a caretaker. They greet a stair when it has repaired itself. They apologize to the lift when it hesitates. They leave a shallow bowl of water on the empty market ledge on the day the fountains run. Twice a day, the air circulation reverses and the dust hangs in slow spirals; the Keepers say the city is turning in its sleep. The city answers in its own ways: a door opens a little easier, a pipe grows warm, a loose ventilation vane taps a different rhythm. The city is not empty. It is inhabited—by people, by animals, by walls that drink the air, by machines that remember to clean, and by a kind of slow, warm attention that holds them all inside its hollow heart.

This is what lives in Corbel Sink: not many, but enough. Not abandoned, but kept. Not silent, but full of sound. In the dark below the salt-pan, a new bell rings every eleven minutes, and old Oud tilts her head, and the city goes on living.
