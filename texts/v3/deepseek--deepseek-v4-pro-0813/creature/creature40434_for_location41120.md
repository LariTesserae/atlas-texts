The people of Vess call themselves the Keepers, though they are kept as much as keeping. They are not the only ones who live there; the city is crowded with other citizens—sump-mites, hull-bats, pipe-eels, rust limpets, glass midges—but the Keepers are the ones who listen, who tend, and who have learned to read the dark as if it were a language.

They are human, mostly, in ancestry. But Vess has shaped them over long generations, and they have shaped themselves to Vess. Their skin is pale, not from lack of light alone but from a slow symbiosis with the same chemotrophic films that crust the stone. A Keeper’s forearms often carry a faint blue-green bloom of silt-skin, deliberately cultivated, fed by the iron and sulfur in their sweat. It gives off a soft luminescence when they move, so that in the darkest galleries you see them as drifting green constellations, not quite separate from the walls. Their eyes are large and dark-adapted, with a reflective layer behind them, so they gleam like wet obsidian when a rare light passes. They have no fear of the perpetual dusk; eyes adjust, and the dark becomes legible—the restless glimmer of mites, the glow of nutrient vats, the edges of damp stone.

Their bodies are compact, dense, built for cold wet air and narrow passageways. They do not sweat much; in a place where evaporation means nothing, a drenched skin is just another surface. Instead they have a thin layer of insulating fat and a habit of wearing clothing that wicks water away in tiny channels. They oil their skin with a hydrophobic secretion harvested from a particular fungal mat, so the ever-present drizzle beads and runs off them like water off a hull-bat’s wing. Their hair is shorn close or braided tight; wet hair is a nuisance, and in Vess, nuisance invites chafing, cold, and slow infections. Many Keepers have small, almost gill-like scars on their necks or ribs—marks of surgical implants that help them breathe in the steam-heavy low corridors or filter the brine-tainted air.

Hearing is their first sense. The city is a roar they learn to parse the way others parse a forest. A Keeper can stand in the center of a humming, dripping, clicking gallery and tell you, without looking, that a pipe-eel has moved through a drain on the third tier, that a hull-bat has shifted its roost in the heat exchanger, that a section of extruded wall is curing too fast and will need a cooling channel by the next sleep-cycle. They speak in low, subsonic tones that travel through stone and water, and they also converse by tapping walls with their knuckles, reading the returning vibration. Children learn to click their tongues before they learn words, imitating the sonar of hull-bats, and the bats, in turn, learn to recognize individual human breath-weights and heart-thrums. A street in Vess is not noisy the way a surface city is; its noise is full, meaningful, alive. Silence is not a thing here, but the Keepers do not need silence to think. They need pattern.

They move slowly, deliberately, with a patience that seems almost geological. The ground shifts a few centimeters a century, and the city is still being made, so the Keepers do not expect anything to be finished. They build a wall knowing their grandchildren will see it completed, and their grandchildren will know it might still crack and be rebuilt. There is no urgency in Vess, only rhythm. The water must keep moving or it will freeze and split the stone, so they keep it moving. The dark must be kept for the animals’ sonar, so they keep it. The animals are not pests; they are the city’s other citizens, so they build crawl-spaces and nesting shelves and guano traps, and they do not resent the constant patter of tiny legs. A Keeper might spend a whole day scraping silt-skin from a walkway, leaving enough for the sump-mites to graze, and consider it a good day.

Their homes are not separate houses but cells in the continuous structure of Vess—chambers carved into the accreted basalt, lined with the insulating foam that rust limpets excrete, warmed by resonant heat drawn from deep thermal sinks. They sleep in groups, not out of sentiment but because shared warmth is economy. Heat is the only currency that matters. A Keeper’s wealth is measured in thermal credits stored in phase-change cells on their belt, in access to the warmest galleries, in the right to sit near a geothermal vent and eat a meal of mite-paste and fungal spore. They trade warmth for labor, for food, for a place in a sleeping pile. There is no money, only heat-debt and heat-gift. A generous person opens a vent to a cold neighbor; a rich one can afford to keep a room at blood-temperature for an entire season.

Their food is microbial, mineral, animal. Nothing green grows on the Karrask Plateau, and the Keepers have no memory of leaves. They harvest the pale fungal spires that rise from the silt-mats in the humid galleries, graze the sump-mites as a staple protein, fish for blind pipe-eels in the sump water, and cultivate the nutrient vats whose soft green glow is one of the few constants in their dark. Meals are often warm, salty, mineral-tasting, and eaten with the fingers from shared bowls. The rust limpets are not food, but they are partners; the Keepers encourage them on certain pipes, and harvest the foam they produce, using it as insulation, as sealant, as a building material that the city itself recognizes as native. In exchange, the Keepers feed the limpets trace metals and keep the water chemistry right for them.

Their society is organized by microclimates and functions, not by blood or territory. There are Water-Wardens who keep the channels open, their hands always wet, their boots soft-soled for walking on raw basalt. There are Extruder-Tenders who sing to the semi-organic machines that grow the city, their voices pitched to the resonance of living mineral. There are Silt-Tenders who cultivate the bacterial mats, reading their pale patterns like farmers read fields. There are Bat-Listeners who track the hull-bat populations over decades, noting when the sonar clicks shift in frequency, because that means the temperature gradient has changed or a new air current has opened. There are Heat-Keepers who maintain the deep thermal sinks, their skin blotched from working in near-freezing brine and then near-boiling steam. Each Keeper has a function, but functions are not fixed; a Water-Warden may spend a season tending fungal galleries if the spires grow thin, and an Extruder-Tender may become a Bat-Listener if the bats start nesting in a new exchanger. The city teaches what it needs.

Their philosophy is simple and old: keep the water moving, keep the dark, the animals are citizens, build slow, nothing green will take root, heat is the only currency. But under that is a deeper understanding. The Keepers know that Vess is not a refuge from the land; it is a knot in the land’s slow becoming. The plateau beneath them is still rising, the water table creeping higher, the stone still speaking in incomplete sentences. They do not try to stop it. They listen to the subsonic thrum of the pumps and feel the slow pulse under the roar, and they know themselves as part of that pulse. A Keeper once told an offworlder, “You think the dark is empty. It isn’t. It is full of the city’s breath. We just happen to be the lungs.”

They do not miss the sky. Many have never seen it, and those who have—from the rim of the caldera, looking out across the black wet plateau—do not find it beautiful in the way outsiders might. The sky is a lid of black vapor reflecting faint violet from the city’s waste heat. It is not a promise; it is a roof. The real beauty is in the steaming ground, the glowing films, the moving constellations of sump-mites, the way a hull-bat’s sonar click echoes off a new wall and returns with a slightly different note because the wall has grown a millimeter since last year. The Keepers find beauty in patience, in the way the city is still being made, in the fact that nothing here is ever finished, including themselves.

Consider a typical Keeper, if there is such a thing. Her name is Sere. She was born in a warm gallery behind a heat exchanger, and her first memory is not a lullaby but the patter of sonar clicks like rain on tin. She has never seen the color green. Her shoulders are carried slightly forward from years moving through low conduits, and her feet are broad and calloused from walking on raw basalt. She wears a coat woven from hull-bat down and sump-mite silk, crusted with a living film that glows faintly blue-green along the seams. On her belt hang thermal cells, a vibration key, a small scraper for harvesting silt-skin, and a vial of rust-limpet foam for patching leaks. She taps the wall as she walks, reading the water inside. She stops at a drain to listen to a pipe-eel hunting, and the sound tells her the drain is clear. She passes a gallery where hull-bats roost in their thousands, and they adjust their sonar to her breath-weight, a brief soft chorus of greetings. She steps out onto the rim, into the fine almost-frozen drizzle, and looks out over the steaming plateau. The dark is full of moving points—the restless constellations of the animals. She is cold, but her thermal belt is banked with heat from the kitchen vents. She is not afraid. She is exactly where she belongs. She is a Keeper, and the city keeps her too.
