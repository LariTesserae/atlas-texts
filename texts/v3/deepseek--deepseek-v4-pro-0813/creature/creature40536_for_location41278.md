The first living thing you notice is not a person. It is a hand, pale as a pool rim, testing the throat of a runnel. Then the mist shifts and the people appear against the wall: small, deliberate, dressed in wet wool and waxed cloth, moving along the raised stone rims as if the water were pavement.

They are human, though in this hollow they seem like a species of their own. Their skin is pale from cloud-light, with a fine mineral bloom like dust on a moth. Their hair is dark and often wet, stiffened at the ends with lime. Their hands are their true faces: nicked, cold, white-knuckled, with flattened fingertips and a language of their own. They stand close when they speak, because the water is always speaking. When the flow is high they use small signs: a closed fist for a sealed channel, two fingers crossed for blocked, an open palm for clear. Children learn these signs before they learn to speak aloud.

They are few. The town has far more rooms than people. Some galleries are empty, their windows dark and open, their doorways not yet grown too small. The emptiness is not ruin; it is a hive not yet filled, a cathedral for a congregation not yet arrived. The people do not seem lonely. They move through the sound as if it is company. They are stone-growers, room-tenders, keepers of the overflow.

Their work is slow and constant. They scrape channels not to clear silt but to keep them open against deposition. They cut fresh tufa from the edges of pools, where it is soft as hard cheese, and shape it into blocks before it hardens. They weave willow lattices and set them under selected drips, then wait years for the water to turn basket into wall. They call this growing a room. A newly begun house is a basket. A finished one is smooth and pale, with the ghost of the weave pressed inside like a fossil. They use copper tools—chisels, scrapers, pins, gate hinges—fired in a small forge under the overhang. The smoke rises and folds into the mist.

Their law is brief and strict. Do not stop the main water. Do not cut a channel until you know where the overflow will go. What the water touches, it takes. They teach these rules to children as soon as they can walk on the rims. There is no judge. A dispute is settled by tracing the water: if a channel is blocked, the last one to scrape it must answer; if a room seals, it is not a loss but a change. They do not own a house forever in shape. It must be kept open with chisel and scraper, or it will become a solid lobe of stone.

They do not bury their dead. The ground is a growing thing and would refuse. Instead they wrap the dead in undyed cloth and carry them to high shelves where the water drips fastest. Over years the cloth stiffens, then whitens, then becomes part of the wall. They say, when they say anything, that someone has gone into the wall. It is not holy to them. It is practical. The stone builds around whatever it is given, and they give their dead to the building.

The other animals are few and pale. A frog no bigger than a fingertip sits at the edge of the least disturbed pool. A white stonefly breeds only under the fastest drip. In the lower cistern a thin gray snake moves like a slow ribbon. The people do not hunt or keep these creatures. They treat them as other kinds of tenders, native to the same wet rooms. The dense green—moss, ferns, cushion plants, dwarf willows sintered into white stone—is not a garden but a co-inhabitant. Some willows have died and remain standing as perfect calcareous shapes, their twigs like porcelain. The people walk among them without much distinction between plant, stone, and ancestor.

In the late afternoon, when the cloud thickens and the light becomes even, the people retreat to the higher galleries. They mend ropes, weave wicker, sharpen copper, and wait. The water sound seems to come from all sides. The white stair of pools glows faintly in the fog. A child may practice the hand sign for “overflow.” An old woman may run her hand over a white shape on the wall where a relative used to be. No one speaks above the roar. There is no need.

They are not so much inhabitants as caretakers. They are the ones who keep the channels open, who move the wicker from one drip-line to another, who know that the hollow is still being made. They are part of the deposition. The town is being deposited, and so are they, one wet year at a time. The water is not cutting them away; it is adding to them, slowly, whether they wish it or not. They live in a place that has not yet learned to erode, and they have learned to build by the same law. They are the living part of a landscape still arriving.
