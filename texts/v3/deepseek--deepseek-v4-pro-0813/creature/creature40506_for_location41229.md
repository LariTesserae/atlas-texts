Ask what lives in the High Salt and the first answer is nothing. The second answer is that nothing here is alive in the way you mean, but it is inhabited all the same. The place has no water, no green, no soil, yet it has a food chain. It is not a chain of eating so much as a chain of currents, heats, and magnetic loyalties. Its animals do not feed on one another. They feed on the plateau itself.

The oldest resident is not an animal at all. In the cracks where the salt plates meet, a gray or copper-colored crust grows. It never reaches toward the light. It reaches toward the voltage. It spreads in feathery, dendritic fans that look like corroded circuitry, and it feeds on the difference between sulfur and iron, on heat and the slow seep of ground current through the salt. It is not vegetation. It is a chemical scab that has begun to keep itself. When lightning strikes, it blooms, not from the heat but from the new fractures, the new routes for electricity. This is the first life of the High Salt, if the word life can stretch that far. It does not photosynthesize. It does not drink. It rusts, and in rusting it endures.

Above the flats, the dust sometimes stands up and moves against the wind. It is not dust. It is a cloud of static, a loose body made of charged mineral whiskers and ionized air. It has no single mouth, no single brain; the cloud is the animal. It feeds on charge. When it passes over a magnetic anomaly, it twists into a column or a sheet. If you are near, your hair stands and your teeth taste of pennies. It might be a swarm of insects or a local failure of the light, but the difference does not matter. It reproduces when a dust storm tears it apart, and each piece becomes a new weather.

Beside the fulgurites, black glass roots left by lightning, a dark tripod stands. It is thin, three rods of fused mineral. It stands for a month, perfectly still, and then it is gone. It leaves no footprint. It seems to be listening to the glass, or maybe drinking the memory of the strike. Some say these are the young of the big walkers, but no one has seen them change. Others say they are not animals at all but instruments, left by something that checks the plateau the way a farmer checks fences. They are the most patient things in a patient place.

The copper-colored coil is smaller than the walkers, but still the size of a shed. It rolls slowly across the salt, ticking as it cools. It is a heat engine, a spiral of plates that expand and contract. It follows the bright ribbons of freshly reduced iron left by the walkers and oxidizes them back to rust, closing the loop. It may be a scavenger, or it may be part of the walker’s own body, shed like a scale. When it stops, it curls into a perfect torus and waits for the next meal.

Then there are the walkers. They are not rare, but they are so large and so widely spaced that the plateau seems empty until one is there. A walker is a landscape in motion. It is low-slung and segmented, like a train of small foundries. Its body is black and rust-colored, plates of iron compounds and ceramic fused into a shell. Inside, something glows continuously through seams and cracks, not a fire exactly but a light like the inside of a kiln. It moves a few meters a day, dragging itself with a sound like a slow train derailment, a sound you feel before you hear.

It grazes on the mineral. It presses its underside to the salt and reduces iron oxide to metal, leaving a bright ribbon of reduced iron behind it. That ribbon rusts over decades, a trail of slow oxidation. The walker does not drink. It does not eat plants. It uses heat, sulfur, and electrical gradients the way other animals use food. Its respiration may be the deep bass that comes from the rock; its pulse may be the regular ringing that sounds like a hammer striking a girder somewhere over the horizon. It has no eyes, but it orients to magnetic fields and seismic noise. It knows the plateau not by sight but by the shape of the current beneath it.

And here is the strangest part: when you see a line of walkers on the horizon, it is sometimes not a herd. It is the same animal seen at different times. Its past and future selves follow it across the flat, like a single body smeared through the local time. The plateau’s rules have eroded, and the walker has evolved to live in that erosion. It is not one animal; it is a procession of instants, each one still walking.

The dry mirages are also residents. They are the old rivers, the water that once ran here, now leaking through the air as colorless shimmers. They leave no moisture. They are memories, not ghosts, patterns of heat and light that have forgotten how to be wet. They follow the old channels. They do not drink. They are what drinking became when the water left. The walkers may pass through them, and for a moment the walker’s iron skin is covered with a sheen of something that looks like dew, but it is only the memory of dew.

Then there is the sound. The regular metallic ringings arrive before the hammer might have fallen. That means somewhere in the High Salt there is a blow that has not yet happened, but its sound is already traveling. This is not an animal, but it lives here. It is a resident made of time and metal. It may be the first industry, the original hammer, the thing that began the process. The walkers orient to it. The tripods stand as if waiting for it. The coils tick in its rhythm. It is the closest thing the High Salt has to a heartbeat, except that the heart is still somewhere ahead, swinging.

In the end, the High Salt is not a place that contains life; it is a life that contains a place. The crust in the cracks is its skin, the wind its breath, the ground current its nerves. The walkers are its hunger. The water-memories are its dreams. The hammer is its pulse. It has forgotten the difference between ruin and machine, and so have its inhabitants. They are not built and not born. They are the way the plateau thinks about itself, slowly, over centuries, in a language of iron and salt and ordinary light.
