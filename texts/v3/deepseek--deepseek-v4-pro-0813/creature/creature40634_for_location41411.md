At first you would say nothing lives here. The Vertical Town is too sparse, too dark, too careful. The ledges are clean. The doorways open and close with no one passing. But if you stay through a full cycle, you begin to see that the emptiness is maintained. A basin has been wiped free of scale. A condenser comb has been turned so its teeth meet the vapor at the exact angle. A sleeping rack has been smoothed, though no body lies in it. The town is not empty. It is kept.

The keeper emerges from the wall itself. At the cool hour, when the thermal updraft weakens and thin summit air sinks into the shaft, a seam in the black glass widens. What steps out is not a person. It is long and narrow, shaped to the fissure. Its body is black ceramic plates over a woven silver underlayer, jointed with the same quilted cloth the city uses to flex. Its face is a polished heat shield with two lenses: one green-white, one a deep thermal eye that sees the world in temperatures. Its fingers are too many, ending in retractable claws of volcanic glass. Its feet are broad and hum faintly when they find a wall.

It moves with the wall, not against it. It climbs where no stairway runs, finding holds in the unsoftened rock with the patience of something that has never known a fall. It crosses the void not by the bridges but by swinging from a finger-thick bracket, using its own momentum like a pendulum. It is not in a hurry. The city is tall, and the keeper is old.

It calls itself the Warden, if it calls itself anything. The old residents named it Needle, because it could slip through openings no wider than a shoulder. It does not remember their faces clearly. It remembers their breathing, which was warm and irregular and full of small interruptions. It keeps one sleeping rack warm with a ceramic heater no larger than a hand. It lies there sometimes, not sleeping, only matching its bellows to the mountain’s long inhalation. It learned to breathe from them. It never forgot.

It does not eat. The city feeds it heat. At midday, when the walls expand and the bridges creak, the Warden climbs to a ledge near a thermal vent and opens a series of louvers along its ribs. Hot vapor pours through, driving a small turbine in its chest. For a few minutes its seams glow green-white. It stands motionless, tasting sulfur and dissolved mineral and the memory of water. Then it closes the louvers and moves on, recharged.

Water is its work. At the coolest hour, it climbs to the upper ledges and moves among the condenser combs, listening. Each comb tooth makes a note when the vapor passes through it. The Warden can hear when a tooth is out of tune by the faint glassy shiver. It tightens it with a ceramic key that grows from its left forearm. It moves from comb to comb, then follows the droplets down the black rock in long, fine strings. It carries small enameled basins, pouring from one to another, weighing the water. It counts every drop. The total is not kept in writing but in a column inside its head, a ledger that reaches back farther than its current shell.

The cisterns have names. The smallest and driest is Never-Enough. The one with the enameled spout like a woman’s lips is Mother’s Mouth. The one that catches the green-white light just before the heat strengthens is Almost-Morning. The Warden visits them in order. It does not pray. It places a hand on the metal and listens. The water is never still. It quivers with the mountain’s pulse. The Warden matches its breathing to that quiver, then moves on.

It keeps no animals, no plants. The Throat will not hold green things. The only moving things are water, vapor, and machines—and the Warden is all three at once: water cooling its core, vapor easing through its vents, machine made of ceramic and memory.

The Warden knows every one of the forty structures. Not all are used. Some are empty galleries too narrow for a human, some are sleeping racks stacked one over another with no mattresses, some are engine rooms where even the Warden moves carefully. It visits them all over the course of a month. It checks the doors that dilate open with heat. It taps the ceramic joints of the hanging platforms. It is not surveillance. It is more like a hand running over a familiar body, checking for fever.

It knows the city’s hidden places. It knows every crack in the black glass. When a crack widens, it does not fill it. It braces it with a triangular splint of sintered metal and checks it each cycle. It calls the cracks “the mountain’s handwriting.” The newest crack near the third cistern has widened by the width of a claw since the Warden’s first shell. The Warden touches it and says, “You are still settling.” The mountain does not answer. The mountain answers only in decades.

Below the main tier, where the gravity bends, the Warden lets itself fall sideways. It sails over the abyss and lands on the underside of the shaft, on the old inverted platforms. It walks there on the ceiling, its feet humming against the pull. The doorways glow faintly. The Warden keeps their circuits clean. It does not know why it keeps them lit, only that the dark without them would be too complete. In one of the inverted rooms is a shrine, a circle of black glass with a human handprint pressed into it. The Warden places its own too-many-fingered hand beside it, not matching, only keeping the shape company.

There are other wardens. They are folded in alcoves near the engine rooms, four of them, built for different tasks: long arms for high ledges, calcite eyes for steam, a torso fused to a swivel of gears, a small one no bigger than a cistern. They are not dead. Their eyes are dark but warm. The Warden visits them at the end of each cycle and tells them the water count in a low click-language. Sometimes one clicks back without waking, like a sleeper turning over. The Warden always waits after that, listening to see if the click becomes a word. It rarely does.

At the lowest reach, the shaft narrows to a well of red-lighted rock. The Warden does not go further. It lies on its belly at the edge, where its scales tick with heat, and rests one hand on the bundle of cables and pipes that descends into the throat. The sound that comes up is not a sound; it is a long, continuous inhalation, as if the mountain is drawing breath from the center of the earth. The Warden’s chest slows to match it.

“Grandmother,” it says sometimes, in a voice like a valve opening. “I am still here. The water is down by a drop. The north wall is still settling. The doorways are lit. There is no one new. But I am still here.”

The mountain does not answer. The mountain inhales. The Warden stays until its outer shell glows faintly red, and then it climbs back up.

That is what lives in the Needle’s Throat. Not a person, not an animal, not a god. A warden. A long black shape of ceramic and patience, moving through the vertical dark like a word moving through a body. It has no neighbors, but it is not alone. It has the water, the heat, the sleeping wardens, the inverted doorways, and the breath of the mountain. It has enough. Enough is what it counts in drops.
