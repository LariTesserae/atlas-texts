In the Grafted Hollow, the residents are not seen so much as inferred. You may stand on a bridge of braided bark and feel watched but see nothing. They are the same colors as the walls: peat-brown, moss-green, bone-pale. They hang in their basket houses like ripe cocoons. They move only when the fog moves.

They call themselves the Grafted—or something that translates as the Root-Kin, the Ones the Moss Accepted. They are human, or near enough. Generations in the Hollow have made them slight and dense, with long arms and long-toed feet. They are small by the standards of the upper world, not from hunger but because size is dangerous when the floor is a lattice over black water. Their skin is pale as alderwood, often stained greenish from moss and copper. Their hair is dark, oiled, cut close or braided with living sphagnum. Their eyes are large, amber or gray, and their pupils open so wide in the dark that the iris nearly disappears. Above ground they wear hoods or carved bone eye-shades because daylight cuts them.

They dress in cloth woven from moss-fiber and the under-wool of root-grubbers, then greased with animal fat to shed the constant water. Their feet are bare or wrapped in soft eel-wyrm skin. Each person carries a tool—a hooked copper needle, an antler blade, a coil of bark-rope—because in the Hollow you are never far from a task. Their hands are scarred with tiny white lines from root-teeth and copper barbs. Their toes are clever, almost prehensile; they can grip a root as well as a hand.

They do not walk noisily. The Hollow punishes noise. The pale long-bodied predators hunt by vibration, and the whole root-mass listens. So the Grafted move with a slow, rolling step, toes testing each hold. On the rope bridges they go sideways, one hand always on the guide-strand. They can stop for an hour, for half a day, and be mistaken for a knot of roots. Their speech is half gesture: clicks, soft vowel-breaths, fingers flicking in the dim light. Children learn to step before they learn to speak. A dropped tool is a scandal. A shout is almost unknown.

Their houses are not built so much as grown. Each dwelling is a basket of willow and reed, suspended from the root-ceiling by four or six strong cords. Sphagnum is stuffed between the walls for warmth. They enter through a side hole or a bottom hatch. Inside, there are hammocks of braided bark, a small caged fire of peat-oil, and walls that sometimes need pruning from within. The houses are alive; if a family sleeps too long, the walls may begin to close like a slow mouth.

The Grafted live in small pods—work-crews of four to ten, not exactly families. Children are raised by the whole pod. They are taught to knot, to prune, to fall without screaming. They are given tasks early: feeding the eel-star lamps, checking sluices, carrying sphagnum to the dry beds. There is no chief. A council of elders—the best moss-speaker, the head sluice-warden, and the master knot-tier—makes decisions, but slowly, because every voice must be heard, and hearing is a quiet act.

Their work is daily and urgent. The root-mass grows quickly in the wet, and the town must be pruned or it will close like a wound. Pruners move through the levels with bone sickles, cutting back new shoots. They know which roots hold weight, which bleed, and which can be trained over a season into a railing or a cradle. They smear the cut ends with peat and ash and whisper to the roots as they work—not prayers, but instructions, warnings, promises.

The sluice-wardens manage water. The Hollow condenses water on every surface, and it pools between the roots. Woven reed channels carry the overflow down to the lower sump. These sluices must be cleared of moss and dead midges several times a day. During fog-fall, the wardens close certain channels and open others, moving with sure steps through the gray. It is considered the most dangerous work, because fog-fall is when the Hollow breathes and the lower galleries change.

The lamp-keepers tend the eel-star fungus, which gives a cold blue-green light. These colonies must be fed with animal milk every few days. The root-grubbers provide the milk. The Grafted do not exactly herd the grubbers; they herd the bark the grubbers eat. They coax the animals from gallery to gallery, brushing their shaggy fur and collecting their milk in carved wooden pails. In return, the grubbers get fresh bark and safety from the pale hunters. The relationship is old and careful, a bargain renewed each season.

Food comes from the water and the walls. They trap eel-wyrms in woven funnels and roast them over a small peat-oil flame. They net blind fish in the black pools. They gather fungi from the root walls, picking only the pale ones that grow after fog-fall. Root-grubbers are eaten only on feast days or when one is injured, because their milk is too useful to waste. They drink a sour tea made from liverwort and a thin, salty milk. Most food is eaten raw or lightly warmed. Fire is kept small and caged, because the walls are alive and will burn poorly but can burn.

They have rules that sound like superstition to an outsider but are closer to husbandry. Keep fire small and caged. Never walk the lower sump alone during fog-fall. Do not prune the one root at the center that hums faintly on certain nights. Give every slain animal’s blood back to the moss, because the moss remembers. These are not moral laws but practical ones. The Hollow is a single responsive organism, and the Grafted are one of its organs. They eat, but they also feed. They prune, but they also graft. They are not masters of the root-mass; they are its hands and eyes.

The central root is the most important. It is not the largest root, but it hums on certain nights, and the Grafted listen to it. The moss-speakers press their ears against its bark and tie knots in a cord to record its changes. When the hum deepens or quickens, the whole town adjusts: some pools are avoided, some levels are braced, some animals are moved. The moss-speakers say the root is not speaking to them. It is speaking to the bog above, and they are merely listening in. But they have learned its moods well enough to survive.

Their dead are given back to the moss. When someone dies, the body is wrapped in sphagnum and carried to a high crevice where the roots grow thick. Over years, the roots take back the body. The Grafted do not mourn loudly. They press a knot into the bark for each name, and after that the name is not spoken aloud. The dead become part of the Hollow—a beam, a wall, a handhold. It is not a cruel custom. It is the same bargain they make with every slain animal: blood and bone returned, moss remembering.

They rarely go above. The bog above is a bright, windy country they call the Over-Moss. They visit it only at dusk, wearing eye-shades, to gather moss-cotton and smell the frost. The sun is a terror. The open sky is a floor that might fall. They know the entrance is hidden, but they watch it anyway. A watcher is always posted near the slumped peat, listening for the sounds of heavy feet. If strangers come, the Grafted do not fight. They sink into the walls and wait. The Hollow hides them.

What lives in the Grafted Hollow, then, is not merely a people. It is a symbiosis. The root-mass provides structure, water, warmth, and hiding. The animals provide milk, light, and food. The Grafted provide pruning, grafting, and blood returned to the moss. They are the part of the Hollow that walks and speaks—quietly, carefully, with eyes that have learned to see by blue-green fungus light. They are the reason the Hollow is not a wilderness with a town in it, but a single living thing with a voice. And the voice is mostly silence.
