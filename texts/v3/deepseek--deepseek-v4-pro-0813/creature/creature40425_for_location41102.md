The people of the Weeping Stair are not a single image. They are too wet, too scattered, too shaped by water for that. You notice them first as movement—a brown arm breaking the lake, a figure swaying on a rope bridge, a flash of oiled cloth disappearing into a leaf-walled hollow. They are human, or near enough, but the place has worked on them. Their feet are broad and dark as soaked bark. Their hands are cross-hatched with the memory of shell and stone. Their breath comes slow and deep, and when they surface from a flooded room, they do not gasp so much as sigh.

Children learn to swim before they walk. Infants are carried in slings of oiled skin against their mothers’ chests, lulled by the constant drip and creak. By the age of five, a child can read the water: where the current will pull, how to time a breath between waves, which roots hold weight and which will rot under a hand. By ten, they climb the wet walls with a bone hook and a loop of fiber, sure-footed in the permanent rain. They are not reckless. A hurried step on a wet root is a long fall or a broken bone.

Their clothing is minimal and practical. They wear oiled fish-skin aprons, cloaks of waxed leaf, belts of woven fiber, and sometimes nothing at all while swimming. Their hair is cut short or bound tight, oiled against the rot. Some braid fish-spine combs into it. Others let it grow long and use it as a rope for small tools. Their skin is pale from cloud-light, but not soft. The cold water leaves a faint blue bloom on lips and nails. They do not mind it. Cold is a fact, like rain.

A household is not only blood. It is a room and the people who tend it—kin, orphans, widows, the occasional stranger who crossed the high pass and chose to stay. They sleep in hammocks of woven reed raised above the wet floor. Their doors are screens of oiled leaf or woven reed, meant to keep fish and insects out, not people. They store food in waxed baskets hung from the ceiling. The flooded lower rooms are their cold cellars. They dive down with stone weights, pull themselves through windows, and return with bundles of oiled cloth or strings of dried fish kept safe from the air.

They do not have much metal. A fishhook of polished bone. A needle of sliver. A knife of sharpened shell. A mallet of dense root-wood for pegging. Tools are personal, worn to the shape of a hand and passed down until they are almost too smooth to hold. The headwoman keeps the one lake-iron knife, old and pitted, not as a weapon but as a weight. When the council meets in the submerged hall, she sets it on a wet stone at the center of the pool, where it gleams faintly in the green light. It is not used to cut. It is used to remind.

They cook with water and stone. A meal is usually a soup of fish, lake-mussel, pale root, and the soft inner bark of deadfall wood. They heat stones in sealed clay boxes on the high platforms and drop them into pots, where they hiss and turn the water sweet. Fire is a guest, not a habit. They eat with their hands or with spoons of polished shell. The best meals are shared slowly, while rain falls through the leaf roof and the frogs begin their overlapping rounds.

Move through the town and you will hear them before you see them: a woman singing to the eel traps in a voice that carries over the water; two men calling to each other as they raise a ladder; the thump of the great rope elevator’s polished root-wood pulley. The plant itself creaks like old ships at anchor, and underneath it all, the steady drip, drip, drip of water finding its way down. The people do not raise their voices over the noise. They pitch their voices into the gaps, so that a conversation on the Stair sounds like a call-and-response, half swallowed by the falls.

The rules of the Stair are few and strict, but they are not written down. They are taught in the body. No one cuts living root-wood. Only dead limbs and fallen branches are taken, and a fallen branch is a gift, not property. Fire is kept away from the wet walls. Waste is carried to midden pools, where the roots drink it clean. A path is not built unless someone will tend it. A broken rung is fixed by the next person who crosses, not out of kindness but because a broken path can kill.

The plant is the oldest inhabitant. It is one body, and its mind—if it has one—moves at the speed of sap. The people do not pray to it, exactly. They listen. When a root-wall thickens and a room begins to close, they say the Body is forgetting it. They empty the room, take out the pegs and baskets, and sing a leaving-song. Then they let the water come. When a new hollow opens, they say the Body is dreaming. They do not cut living wood because they have seen what happens when someone forgets. The cut heals slowly, if at all, and the room around it grows cold and still.

The animals are not separate from the people. The lake is thick with small fish, eels, freshwater mussels, and skittering things. The people keep fish weirs and eel traps, and they hang nesting boxes from the branches. A child can swim among the eel traps and not be bitten. The eels know the hands that feed them. The mussels are gathered by touch in the green shadows. Needle-winged birds drop fish bones and bright seeds onto the platforms, and in return the people leave them scraps of oiled leaf. There is something heavy that knocks in the deep roots—a slow, regular pulse that travels through the water. They do not name it often. Some call it the Heart of the Stair. Others say it is a fish too old to surface. When it knocks, the children go still in the water, and the adults listen. It is not a threat. It is the pulse of the place, and they are used to it the way they are used to their own breath.

The people of the Weeping Stair do not think of themselves as living on the plant, but inside it. They are the small warm life in its hollows, the voices that rise above the water at dusk. They are not masters of the valley. They are its hands, its lungs, its memory. At the end of the day, they gather on the high platforms, sharing heat and a bowl of warm root-water. A woman mends a net. A man rubs fish oil into his cracked hands. A child sleeps against its mother, one arm trailing over the edge into the rain. Below them, the lake holds the last of the green light, and the whole plant creaks like something breathing. The Weeping Stair does not reveal itself all at once. Neither do its people. They drip, swim, listen, and wait.
