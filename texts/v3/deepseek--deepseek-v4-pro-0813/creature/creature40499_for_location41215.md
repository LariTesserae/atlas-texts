The first inhabitant of Nooth is not a person. It is the Vivus, the old lady, the dead plant that is not dead. She is the only one who was there before the city, and she will be there after. Her body is the city. Her breath is the condensation on the walls. Her dreams are the knots where space forgets itself. The people do not say they live in Nooth; they say they live inside her. They mean it literally, biologically. They are not masters of the house; they are at best a kind of lichen, or a small warm thought in a very old skull.

The human population is sparse. The city is built for many more than it holds, so every footstep sounds twice. The people are miners, engineers, chemists, sap-refiners, and a small order of botanists who study the old plant’s residual behaviour. There are mechanics who run the pumps, and rail-gangs who maintain the funicular lines down the escarpment. There is a church. There is a school. There is a market that holds its own weather. But numbers do not describe the inhabitants. What describes them is their relation to the old lady.

The dew-wives are the first to wake. They wake before dawn, when the old lady begins her exhalation. They carry copper vessels and folded cloths. Their trade is water. They know the walls of the upper terraces the way a nurse knows a feverish skin. A dew-wife named Morna can tell by the taste of the breath whether the old lady’s cycle is early or late, whether the deep halls will be slick by evening. She walks with her head cocked, listening. She is not old, but her hands are, and her voice is low from years of talking to stone. The dew-wives are not many: a dozen women and a few men. They are considered the city’s most essential workers, because without the breath there is no water, and without water there is no steam, no factories, no milk.

Below the dew-wives, in the eastern amphitheatre, live the sap-cutters and heartwood miners. They know the old lady’s body as material. They go into the deep galleries with ceramic tools, because iron rings too loudly and the old lady does not like it. They have amber dust in their hair and under their fingernails. They smell of burnt sugar and cold earth. They say the resin is her blood, and they do not waste it. At the end of each shift they leave a small offering of salt in the cracks, something the old lady can taste, something to say: *we are still here, we are careful.* Their work is dangerous, but not only because of collapse. It is dangerous because sometimes a wall forgets to be a wall.

The engineers and mechanics are a louder kind. They live in the machine layer, where the city smells of steam and hot brass. They call the old lady “the substrate” or “the old girl.” They are deaf to the wind in the way the dew-wives are not, but they feel it through their boots and through the pipes. When the sap-joints crack, they pause, but only long enough to mark the time. They are not superstitious, they say. But each pump-room has a small niche with a coil of dried vine, and they touch it before starting a big shift.

The botanists, the knot-mappers, are part scientist, part cartographer, part priest. They study the old lady’s residual behaviour. They map the city’s folds. There are not many of them: perhaps twenty. They wear grey clothes and carry string, chalk, tuning forks, and compasses that do not always point north. One of them, Olin, has an eye that sees double. He spent too long inside the Hollow Meridian, and now he cannot look at a straight corridor without seeing the corridor that should not be there. The botanists do not consider this a curse. They consider it a qualification. When a new knot opens, they are the first to tie a red cord across the entrance. They seal the worst ones with brick and resin, but they always leave a small gap, because you do not close a dream entirely.

The Church of the Vivus is the smallest formal order. They are not numerous: forty men and women, maybe fewer. They live in the old crown, near the Hollow Meridian. They wear undyed wool and keep their hair cut short. They believe the old lady is not dead but remembering herself, and that one day the city will be folded back into her body. They are not preachers. They are listeners. They sit in the deep halls and hum at the same pitch as the wind. They say the old lady hums back. Their leader, Hedda, has a face like cracked bark and a voice like water moving through a very old pipe. She can tell when a knot is about to open by the taste of the air. The city tolerates the church, and even consults it when a knot is too large to seal.

The rail-gangs are the only ones who regularly leave. They run the funicular down the escarpment to the warmer valleys, and they come back with valley fruit, cloth, lime, letters, sometimes a new goat. They are the doors of the city, the ones who see it from outside. They say Nooth from below looks like a grey swirl, a fossil skull, a sleeping animal. They are practical and a little rough. They have no patience for the old lady’s whispers. But every one of them, before the descent, taps the brake drum three times. It is not a prayer, they say. It is a rhythm. The old lady likes it.

The children of Nooth are few, and they grow up in a city that is larger than its population by a factor of ten. They learn to be quiet. They learn to count rooms. They play games of finding edges: a corridor that looks forty paces but takes a hundred; a ceiling that recedes; a stair that leads back to where you started. They are taught to read the breath. They carry small pieces of chalk to mark their way. Some of them are knot-born, born near or inside a knot. A knot-born child named Liri can draw maps of rooms she has never entered. She sleeps with her head against the wall, and her mother, a sap-refiner, says the wall whispers to her. The botanists visit Liri sometimes. They do not test her. They just ask her to draw what she sees. Her drawings look like the inside of a lung.

Then there are the animals. The goats of Nooth are few but important. They have the nervous, alert look of animals in a place that is not entirely safe. They refuse certain corridors. They will not enter a room that has shifted in the night. The goat-keepers follow them, and more than once a goat has found a new knot before the botanists. The goats’ bells are small iron bells, and they sound like rain on stone. The chickens live in a converted sap-chamber, and their eggs have a faint taste of resin and smoke. The pale orb-spiders are the city’s smallest visible inhabitants. They live in condensation channels and spin webs that catch the breath. In the morning, the webs are beaded with what looks like glass. No one clears them. They are part of the city’s water system, and children call them dew-keepers.

Below the inhabited terraces, in the dark, wet halls, the true majority lives: mats of pale bacteria and slow fungi. They bloom and recede with the old lady’s breath. They are the reason the walls are slick. In some chambers they form rosettes and rings, and after rain they give off a faint green light. There are fungi that fruit from the heartwood over years. One, called the old lady’s ear, grows flat and brown and takes on the shape of the wall. The botanists say it might be part of the plant’s nervous system, still listening. The mushroom warden, a quiet man named Bram, is the only one who knows which ears are safe to eat and which will dream back.

The wind, too, is a kind of inhabitant. It is not alive, but it behaves like a resident. It knows every hall and chimney. It moves through the high vaults and broken pipes, making the city into a wind instrument. The sound is constant, a low flute-note that rises to a howl. The people have learned to ignore it, but they also miss it when they go down to the valleys. Some say the old lady’s voice is in the wind, but the dew-wives say no, the old lady’s voice is under the wind, in the walls.

And then there is the listener. Her name is Ansa. She is not old, but she seems old. She lives in a room shaped like the inside of a conch, near the old crown. Her job is to hear. She sits in a chair of mineralised fibre, and she presses a brass cup to the wall. The city pays her to distinguish sap-joint from speech. She listens for the sound the old lady makes when she is about to dream a knot. When she hears it, she sends a bell down to the botanists, and they come with chalk and resin. She has been doing this for thirty years. Her left ear is dead; her right ear misses nothing. She says the old lady’s voice is not a voice but a rope of air pulled through a thousand small holes. She calls the children who follow her *little echoes*.

The dead are not separate from the living. They are laid in the deep, cool heartwood, where the fibres slowly close over them. In time, the body becomes part of the wall. People say the old lady remembers her dead.

At the centre of the city, in a repaired sap-chamber so high it holds its own weather, the market is the only place that feels like a crowd. It is not a noisy place; it is a place where people come to see other people. The ceiling sweats its own small cloud, and the vendors of cress and root vegetables arrange their goods under little umbrellas made of waxed cloth. Children chase each other through the aisles. Their footsteps echo in the plant-stone. It is the only place where the wind is not the loudest thing.

What lives in Nooth, then, is not a population so much as a set of relations to a body. The old lady is the only true resident. The rest are her symbionts, her parasites, her keepers, her interpreters. They are not there to conquer the place. They are there to tend it, to listen to it, to avoid being digested by it. They are sparse, but they are enough. They have built a small, warm brain of industry inside a fossil skull. And every night, when the factories glow and the wind blows and the walls sweat, they sleep inside a dream that is not their own, and they call it home.
