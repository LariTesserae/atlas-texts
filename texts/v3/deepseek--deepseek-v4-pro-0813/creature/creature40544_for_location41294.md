They are the makers. The word is mine, not theirs. They have no name, or none I can hear. They live in the gaps between the tiles, in the heat that follows the fissures, in the steam that rises after rain. To say they live here is not quite true. They are not here. They are what the place does when the ground cannot hold its shape.

The first one I saw was not a body but a hand. It lay in a depression near a newly repaired crack, a print pressed into the mud, three long grooves radiating from a central pad. The grooves were as thick as my wrist. The clay around the print had fired hard, as if the hand had carried enough heat to bake it. I put my own hand into the print, and it was like a child’s hand laid in a grown man’s tool.

Then the tools began to appear. A wooden paddle with the grip worn smooth by fingers that could not be mine. A coil of rope spun from a fiber I did not know, with strands that bled a pale sap when wet. A shallow basket that held water so still it reflected the brown sky. And everywhere the tiles—new tiles, still warm, laid in long curves that vanished into the shimmer. I followed one curve for an hour. It led to another, and another. The floor was mending itself in a spiral.

I saw the first one after rain, when the steam stood in thick towers. I had learned to look sideways at the steam, not directly, because direct looking made my eyes ache with a heat that had no source. Far off, where three steam columns leaned together, a shape detached itself from the haze. At first it was only a vertical line, darker than the steam. Then it took on weight.

It was taller than a man, but not by much—perhaps nine feet, perhaps eleven. Its legs were long in the way a shadow is long when the light is low. Its arms hung below its knees, and its hands were three long clay-colored branches, not jointed but cracked, bending slowly like willow. Its head was the wrong shape: narrow, thrown up to a blunt peak, like a chimney pot put on backward. Where a face should have been there was a shallow basin, and in the basin rainwater had gathered. It tilted its head as it walked, and the water spilled down its front.

It walked with a slow, careful gait, not lifting its feet high. Each foot was a flat paddle of clay, spreading over the tiles. When it stepped, the tile beneath its foot seemed to rise to meet it, and when it lifted, the tile remained, still hot. I watched it for a long time. It did not notice me, or if it did, it did not stop.

The body was made of the same material as the floor. One shoulder was dark brick-red, fired to a hard glaze. The other was wet, soft, glistening from the rain. Its chest was narrow, and on its back was a high curved plate like the side of a kiln jar. Between its ribs—if ribs they were—the cracks glowed a dull orange, as if the fire inside had banked but not gone out. When it breathed, steam curled from the glowing seams.

Its face—I will call it a face, though it had no eyes and no mouth—held three pits in a horizontal line. The pits were filled with a black, glassy mineral that gleamed like tiny pools. They were not eyes but mouths for heat. They opened and closed, tasting the air. When it turned its head toward me, the pits widened, and I felt a wave of warmth pass over my skin, like standing too near a stove.

It did not speak. The only sounds it made were not from its throat but from its whole body: a low clack-clack of fired clay contracting, and beneath that a deeper sound I felt in my teeth. It was the same rumble I had heard under the plain, but now it was localized, as if the creature were a small opening into that deeper fire.

It knelt by a hairline crack. With two of its long fingers it smoothed something into the fissure. At first I thought it was pulling clay from a pouch or satchel, but there was no pouch. It drew the clay from its own forearm, peeling it off in a long ribbon like a snake of olive-gray mud. It pressed the ribbon into the crack, then laid its hand flat over the place. The hand glowed faintly. The clay in the crack dried, darkened, fired. When the creature lifted its hand, the crack was gone. The floor was whole.

I saw others over the following days. Some were far off, their long arms swinging low. Some stood as still as chimney stacks for hours, and steam from the ground gathered around them. One passed close enough that I could see the exact joints of its fingers, which were not knuckles but spiral seams, like clay thrown on a wheel and then assembled. It dragged its fingers along the tiles as it went, not idly but testing, feeling for flaws. Where it found a rough edge, it pinched the tile, and the edge smoothed beneath its touch.

They do not travel together, or not usually. I saw two once, standing on either side of a wide fissure. They did not look at each other. They faced the same direction, toward the horizon, and between them a tile passed slowly across the open crack. It did not move by hand. The first one set it down on the near edge, and the ground itself carried it across, as if the fissure were not a break but a tongue passing a morsel. The second one received it and pressed it down. No word. No gesture I could see. Just the low percussion through the floor, a single note like a mallet on wet clay.

Their speech, if it is speech, happens below the hearing. I feel it when I rest my palm flat on the tiles: a pattern of taps, far apart in time, like a code made of footsteps. Sometimes it is only the settling of the ground. Sometimes it is not. I have tapped the ground with my heel. Nothing answered, but the next day the tiles near my camp had been rearranged into a small spiral.

I used to think they were working alone, each one a solitary repairer. Now I think they are something else. They are the fingers of one body, the body being the plain. The makers rise out of the ground when the pressure beneath becomes too great. A fissure opens, and one of them pulls itself out, wet and ash-gray, unfolding like a fern made of mud. It stands in the rain until the water glazes its skin. Then it walks off, and the fissure closes behind it. That is the whole birth.

And when one is old or damaged, it does not fall. It kneels, and the ground takes it back. I found a kiln once, cold, with handprints on its inner walls. The handprints were not the work of a potter. They were the shapes left by someone who had lain down inside and slowly become the kiln. The chimney was too narrow for any fire to draw well. But it was not a kiln for fire. It was a resting place.

There are smaller ones, too. I saw one sitting in a shallow pool after a rain, no bigger than a child. It was not working. Its long hands floated on the water. Its chest gave a low, continuous sound, and the water around it began to grow its mineral skins faster, building terraces of white and rust-orange. It was tending the pool the way a gardener tends a bed. After an hour, it stood and walked into the steam, and the pool had become a neat, low mound of salt and sulfur and iron.

They do not eat. They do not sleep, not as I do. They rest by standing in one place until the rain softens their outlines and the steam erases their edges. They drink from the mineral pools. They bend and lift the water into the basin of their heads and let it run through them. The water comes out through the cracks in their sides, carrying a fine silt. That silt they collect and press into tiles. They are not separate from the landscape. They are the landscape’s way of touching itself.

I have tried to draw one. The drawing is wrong. All drawings of them are wrong, because they will not hold a single shape. Their height changes with the heat. Their arms lengthen in the rain. Their heads turn from chimney to shallow dish to a crown of three overlapping tiles. The more I look, the less certain I am of their edges. They are like the floor itself: always becoming.

They are not the only life here. The steam has forms. Sometimes a shape detaches from a column and drifts low across the floor, like a hand made of fog. It passes over a crack, and the crack seals behind it. I have seen these steam-shapes cluster around a standing maker, curling over its shoulders, into the basin of its face. They are not separate beings. They are the maker’s breath, given a temporary body by the rain.

What are they, finally? I do not know. I know only what they are not. They are not animals. They are not plants, though they grow from the ground. They are not spirits of the dead. They are not gods. They are the plain’s own raised flesh. An inhabitant comes from elsewhere; these do not. They are the floor standing up, the tile remembering its wetness, the kiln stepping out of its dome. They live here the way a wave lives in the sea—not as a thing in the place, but as the place’s movement, repeated and repeated, until the rain stops and the steam clears and there is nothing but a world being made.
