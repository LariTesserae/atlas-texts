The people are not the first thing you see. The plateau is too full of sound and heat and distance for that. What you see are the belfries, each one a dark hulk on the red plain, humming in its own haze. What you see are the white tongues of fog climbing the escarpment at dawn, the glitter of running water in the shallow channels, the black desert pavement between the plants. But the people are there, folded into the noise, and you learn to read their presence by the small signs: a ring of fitted stones too deliberate to be flood-sorted, a cairn of balanced rocks that has no natural reason to stand, a tripod of charred poles hung with carved bark tablets. The tablets are the first hint that the people are not just passing through. They are keeping records. They are listening.

They are lean, sun-darkened, and built like the land itself: no extra weight, no loose edges. Their skin is the color of old copper, their hair cut short or pulled back tightly, because anything loose becomes a nest for the living dust. They have high cheekbones and narrow noses, dark steady eyes set in faces weathered but not old. They stand with their weight slightly forward, toes spread on the hot stone, as if the ground is talking to the soles of their feet. They do not wear much—a skirt or loincloth of pounded bark fiber, a hooded cloak for the dust, and strings of copper beads around the neck and wrists. The beads are not decoration. Each one is hammered to a different pitch, so that when a person moves they carry a faint chime that harmonizes with the tick-singers. A person’s string of beads is their public name, a sequence of notes that can be rattled across a basin to say *I am here*.

Their ears are not like yours. The outer fold is thick and can be pinched shut by a small muscle, a trait shaped by the plateau’s unending sound. They close their ears during the evening overlap, when the day-singers and night-singers trade places and the sound becomes a physical wall. They close them during the worst midday simmer, when the noise rises to a pressure that can shake the teeth. But in the deaf wells—the still points high in the crown where the sound crosses itself and cancels—they leave their ears open, because there the silence is not a wound but a place to hear the body’s own pulse. They say the first people were born from a deaf well, where the world’s sound crossed itself and left a space for a heartbeat.

They speak with their hands as much as with their mouths. The plateau is too loud for speech over any distance, so they have a signed language, full of small precise movements, that can carry across a fog basin or from one root-pillar to another. Their spoken language is low and close, meant for whispers and for words that need to be felt in the chest. They have no single word for silence. They have a word for the silence before a predator moves: *the held breath of the rock*. And they have a word for the silence that comes when a belfry is sick: *the wound where sound has been cut out*.

A family group is rarely more than eight or ten people: two or three adults, a few children, an elder or two. They call themselves by the belfry they are currently following, so a person might be *of the Split Crown* or *of the Root-Stone*. They move between belfries in a slow rhythm, following the fog and the singer blooms. They are not nomads in the usual sense. They have no herds and plant nothing. The belfries are not crops; they are older than the people, and they need no gardener. But the people manage water and sound, which is not tending the tree but tending the conditions that let the tree’s own heart beat. They carry sections of woven fog-net, made from bark fiber, which they stretch between two poles at dawn. The fog condenses on the net, and the water runs down into clay-lined skins. They drink it warm, tasting of dust and resin. They build low windbreaks of woven bark on rock shelves, not as permanent homes but as shelters for a season. They dig clay-lined cisterns at the edge of fog basins, and they balance cairns of stones to mark seeps. They leave carved bark tablets on tripods as water records and territory marks and warnings: *we passed here after the second fog, the water is good, avoid the root-bridge beyond the eastern pan*.

Their tools are simple but not crude. They knap flint into blades and points, and they fire-harden wood for digging sticks and throwing sticks. They use bone needles to sew bark cloth and animal hide. They hammer native copper into beads and small tools, working it cold on stone anvils. They make bone flutes and copper rattles and drumming stones. With these they do not make music in the way you might think. They use sound to manage the belfries. When a belfry’s knell becomes irregular, when the water columns are on the edge of cavitation, a sound-shaper will climb onto the fused cables and press an ear to the warm bark. They may stay there for hours, listening to the tree’s own frequency. Then they begin to hum, a single low note that matches the tree, and the trunk steadies. They call this *re-sounding* the tree. It is not medicine; it is a kind of listening from the other direction.

They eat what the plateau gives. They gather the waxy nectaries from the base of the needle-leaves and boil them into a thick, sweet paste that lasts for months. They dig boom-grubs from the outer bark with fire-hardened sticks and roast them on hot stones. They collect the shelled animals from the root-basins after the fog-drip and boil them in their own shells. They hunt the kite-lizards with throwing sticks, but they take only the old males, because a breeding female is taboo. They do not hunt the larger things. They know their signs—a shed scale like a shield, a spine left in a dry channel, a shadow crossing a gap too fast to parse—and they move away from silence patches, because quiet often means a large predator is moving through. Of the larger things, they speak rarely. They have names for them—*ghost-that-crosses*, *stone-back*, *the one who drinks the wind*—but they do not call them animals. They are forces, like the dust devils, to be read and avoided.

The children are raised in the sound. They are born not with a cry but with a bell. The mother, in the last moments, is placed near a belfry trunk so that the first sound the child hears is the tree’s knell. The afterbirth is buried in a root-basin, and a copper bead is hammered for the child. Later, at a deaf well, the child is given their private name, which is never spoken outside that silence. Their public name is the string of beads around their neck, a sequence of notes that can be rattled in the air. The children learn to sleep through the roar, and to wake instantly when a single note is missing. They play games of listening: one child hides in a deaf well, another must find them by the rhythm of their breathing. They learn to walk barefoot on the hot stone, testing the temperature with each step. They learn the hand language before they learn to speak, because the sound is too loud for a child’s voice.

When a person dies, they are carried to a belfry’s root system and placed in a hollow chamber. The tree grows over them. Their copper beads are hung on the aerial roots, where they chime in the wind and add a new note to the wall of sound. The people say the dead do not leave; they become part of the sound. This is why they do not fear the noise. To them it is not noise. It is the sound of the dead, the living, and the standing ones all at once.

On a clear afternoon, you might see a group at the edge of a fog basin, just after dawn. An old woman is squatting on her heels, repairing the stone ring around a small pool of green-black water. Her hands move with the patience of water. Behind her, two children are playing a game of silence among the root-basins, stepping from stone to stone, trying not to make a sound, but the plateau makes noise around them—drip, tick, boom—so the game is not about true silence but about becoming invisible within the noise. A man is climbing the aerial roots with a fiber bag, collecting the waxy nectaries. A girl is sitting high in the crown, near a deaf well, with her eyes closed and her hands resting on the smooth spiraled wood. She is not sleeping. She is listening to her own blood, and to the far-off hum of the next belfry, three kilometers away. She is learning to be a sound-shaper. She is learning that the plateau is an instrument, and the people are its fingers.

At dusk, during the awful overlap, they stand together in a line, chest-deep in the wall of sound. They do not cover their ears. They let the pressure pass through them. And then, as the night-singers take over and the day-singers fall quiet, a new key emerges, lower and more resonant, like a bell that has been struck and is still ringing. The people breathe out. The fog begins to climb the escarpment again. The plateau is not silent. It never is. But the people know that as long as the sound continues, the water will move, the belfries will stand, and the living will keep their place among the instruments.
