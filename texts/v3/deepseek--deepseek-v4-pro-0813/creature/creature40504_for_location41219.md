There are no ghosts here. The city is too full for ghosts. Every doorway, room, and collapsed roof is occupied by something with gills, tentacles, or a shell. No one here breathes air. Air is a dry myth. The city’s new tenants are all shaped by water and by the old rooms.

The water itself is the first resident—not the water as a thing, but the life suspended in it. Copepods, larvae, drifting eggs, spores, the thin planktonic haze that makes the water taste of lime and plankton. You would notice them before any fish. They are the city’s new dust, its pollen, its weather. They rise and fall with the tide, and in the warmest month they become so thick the water turns faintly milky. Every surface becomes a nursery, and the city is not just inhabited but reproducing.

Then the walls. The walls are alive. Tubeworms, barnacles, sponges, and coral-like animals have laid their own skeletons over the stone. They have rebuilt the city’s edges. A windowsill is no longer a stone line; it is a reef of mouths. The old surfaces are layered, encrusted, near-touching. The walls do not simply hold the buildings up anymore; they feed. When the current moves, the filter-feeders open their feathery crowns, and the whole street becomes a breathing surface.

In nearly every doorway that still has a lintel, a moray eel is waiting. The morays are the city’s most patient tenants. They use the doorways not as doors but as frames for their own bodies. They hang with their heads just at the threshold, mouths opening and closing to breathe, teeth showing not as threat but as architecture. Some are green-brown, some grey, some nearly black. They know the exact width of their door. When a small fish passes, the eel does not chase; it lets the fish come close enough to be wrong. Then the doorway is briefly a mouth. In the tenement rows, every third doorway has one. They keep their distances, communicating with slow body-sway and chemical signals. Mostly they wait. Waiting is what the city taught them.

On the ground floors, the rooms are full of groupers. They are the weight of the city now. A grouper sits in what was once a fishmonger’s stall, its body too large for the space, its pelvic fins resting on the stone floor. Its gills open and close like slow bellows. The current moves around it. It does not move for hours. When it does move, it turns with the whole room. The walls have been worn smooth by its sides. In the customs house, the largest grouper in the district has taken the main hall. She is old; her lower lip is pale, her eyes are gold-rimmed. She has been there long enough that the doorframe has grown to fit her, or she has grown to fit the doorframe. She is not a ruler, but when she decides to cross the room, every small fish in the hall re-sorts itself around her, as if the architecture had shifted.

Above the market square, the jacks circle. They are not residents in the way a grouper is; they are a moving district. The school turns as one body, a single silver weather. It circles the old square even when the tide changes, as if the square’s shape has become a memory in their lateral lines. When a shadow passes—a long silver fish, maybe a barracuda, maybe a young shark—the school folds into a stairwell. The stairwell becomes a column of fish. After the shadow passes, they re-emerge into the square like a thought returning.

The urchins graze the walls in herds. They are the city’s engravers. Their teeth scrape the encrusting life, leaving pale trails on the coral and sponge. Over years, they have cut new lines into the stone, not sharp, but visible: spirals, arcs, the cursive of slow hunger. They do not know they are writing. The walls are covered with their records.

Near the old cistern, a patch of dark algae waves in the current like fine hair. Small shrimp move through it like combs, picking out whatever the algae has caught. Elsewhere, in the rubble, crustaceans clatter. Snapping shrimp fire their claws in the old alleys, and the sound is a sharp crack, like a stone splitting, but it is not a stone—it is a declaration. The shrimp are the city’s telegraph. Their snaps carry farther than any other sound. A grouper may feel the snap through its lateral line and know something has changed in the street. The city is mostly quiet, but sound travels strangely here. A grouper’s tail thuds against a wall. Barnacles click. The distant crumble of masonry slumping into an alley sounds like a soft exhalation. There is no surf, no wind, no engine. The loudest sounds are these small ones, carried through warm, silty water.

There is no fire, but there is light. At night, or what passes for night, the city lights itself. A comb jelly blooms pale blue in an alley. Tiny crustaceans rise from the silt like green sparks. A fish passes with a line of bioluminescence along its belly, and for a second the old market square becomes a map of itself: the arcades, the central hall, the boulevard opening like a lit vein. The light is not for seeing. It is a kind of speech. The residents flash and glow and signal, and the city, which has no lamps, is briefly legible.

At the level of the old windowsills, there is a membrane. The warmer, saltier water below lies beneath the upper water, and it shimmers when a fish crosses it, like a glass floor. This is the border between the upper city and the lower. Above it live the drifters: the larval clouds, the comb jellies, the small translucent fish. Below it live the crawlers and the waiters: the urchins, the crabs, the groupers, the worms. Some creatures cross through it slowly, as if stepping through a membrane. A jack may come down through it and hesitate, then return. A grouper may rise into it only to breathe, then sink back. There is a thin fish that lives exactly along the halocline, a needlefish or a pipefish, half in the upper water and half in the lower. It is never wholly in one city. It is the border’s only permanent resident.

Follow the old boulevard out from the market square, past the customs house, through the tenement rows, and the residents change. The buildings thin. The mud takes over. Here there are no doorways, only low hummocks and collapsed walls. The residents are buried: rays lie under the silt with only their spiracles and eyes above, breathing through holes like little geysers. A shark may rest on the bottom in what was once a road, its body half-covered in fine silt. The water is darker here, and the fish are larger. There are whole districts you cannot see, where the residents have no names and no use for the old city. They are the far neighborhoods, the suburbs of the drowned.

In the oldest district, the coral has grown so precisely into the shape of windows and doorways that it is hard to tell what was built and what was secreted. The walls have become a living tissue of polyps and shell. The city is not dead; it is becoming an animal. The true resident of the drowned city is not any single fish or eel or grouper. It is the composite body they make together: the polyps that lay skeleton over stone, the urchins that carve it, the fish that fill its streets, the spawn that renews it, the tide that turns it. The city is a life made of lives.

If you ask who lives here, ask the water, and it will answer with a cloud of larvae. Ask the wall, and it will answer with a thousand small mouths. Ask the customs house, and the old grouper will turn her gold-rimmed eye toward you, not because you matter, but because the current changed. She is the closest thing to a tenant with a name. She does not know the city was drowned. She only knows the warm dark, the taste of lime and plankton, the slow bellows of her own breath, and the fact that the doorway is hers.
