You do not see them at first. The town seems to have been abandoned to the steam and the pumps, and whatever population it once had has evaporated with the water. Then a shape detaches from a rail outside the pump house: a figure in black rubber, standing so still that the mineral haze has already crusted along the shoulders. She is not waiting for anyone. She is listening to the deep rotary thud through the soles of her boots. When she moves, she does so the way a sluice gate moves—easy, unhurried, as if some larger hand has applied pressure.

After that, the others appear. A boy in a canvas apron crosses a catwalk with a bucket of brass fittings. Two men in oilskins walk the weir path, stooping every few paces to scrape at a mineral ledge. An old woman sits in the market stall, her face lantern-lit, stitching a replacement gasket. You could count the visible people on one or two hands. The population of the place is small—thirty, maybe forty—and it has been small for a long time. Children are few but not rare. They are raised in the steam like pale ferns, taught to walk quietly on slatted floors and to recognize the sound of a dry pump before they can read.

The people are built for the wet. Their clothes are not uniforms, but they share a grammar: waxed canvas bibs, long rubber coats with hoods, waders that reach the thigh, copper-toed boots gone green at the seams, fingerless gloves stiff with mineral salts. Their hair is cropped or tied back, bleached by the water to the color of old rope. Their faces are smooth from constant steam, but their hands are cracked and pale as boiled tubers. Many have a faint rust-red staining around the wrists, the ankles, the collarbones, as if the iron-rich water has begun to map their bodies. Their eyes are pale and unblinking in the amber twilight, and they speak in low voices that sound as if they have been strained through a condenser.

Their homes are not separate from the machinery. They are bolted to the sides of the larger buildings or set on stilts above the hot pools. Inside, the floors are slatted so the warm air and the sound of water rise through. They sleep in hammocks slung over pipes, or on narrow cots with rubber mattresses. The walls are lined with shelves of bakelite switches, glass jars of mineral specimens, brass gauges, small tools. A household might have one or two electric lamps, a hot plate, a basin that fills from a tapped pipe. The walls sweat. The bedding is never dry, but they do not mind. They say dry bedding is for the uplands.

They bathe often, not for cleanliness but to keep the mineral from building on their own skin. The water is warm and slightly slippery with dissolved stone; it leaves a pale film on the tub, and on them. After a bath they scrape their heels with a pumice stone, and the scrapings hiss when they fall back into the water.

There are not many jobs here, but every person has a function, and every function is about keeping the process alive. The pumpmaster lives in the pump house, in a small chamber lined with felt, where the main bearing turns beneath a hinged floor. She does not sleep so much as lie still with one hand on the iron casing, and if the rhythm changes she is awake before anyone else. The condenser-tenders are younger, mostly, with greenish light on their faces from the long glass roof; they wipe the coils with soft cloths and listen for the hiss of a leak. The weir-keepers walk the channels with long iron keys, adjusting the sluice gates by quarter-turns, never by force. The sinter-mason scrapes the boardwalks and white terraces, chipping away the mineral crust where it threatens the doors and the tram line, and sometimes, when a deposit has taken an interesting shape, he leaves it and labels it with a small brass tag. The lamplighter tends the electric lamps, not because they are gas lamps that need lighting, but because the lamps here need cleaning almost daily; the mineral water and the algae and the steam form a film on the glass, and if she does not wipe them, the twilight thickens until even the locals cannot see the edge of the walkway. There is a salt-wife, who keeps the covered stall and sells provisions and tells the children which weeds are safe to touch, and there is a conductor for the tram when it runs, and there is a listener, a thin man with a brass stethoscope and a small mallet, who can tell by tapping a pipe whether it is about to choke with scale.

They do not shout. The noise of the place makes shouting useless. Instead, they tap the railings with their knuckles or their tools, in patterns that travel through the iron and into the feet of whoever needs to hear. A double tap means “come.” A slow triple tap means “water rising.” A sharp single tap means “hold,” and if you hear it, you hold, no matter what. Their own speech, when it comes, is economical: “South weir is slurping,” “Number three is running warm,” “The fine rain is back.” They listen more than they talk, and they look more than they listen. Their eyes are always moving over the water, the crust, the needle on a gauge, the small white exhalation of a pressure-release stack.

The absence of animals is not a mystery to them. It is a rule. They will not say exactly what happens if the rule is broken, but they will tell you not to bring a live animal inside the ring, and they will say it with the flat certainty of a law. They know what happened to the canaries that outsiders once brought—the cage still on the shelf, the bird dead by morning, no mark, no heat-stroke, only a stillness that looked like a station between two seconds. They do not keep cats or dogs. They do not keep birds. Some of them have never seen a live mouse. When the tram arrives from beyond the rim, the conductor checks the cargo for stowaways, not for pests but for animals, and if a pigeon lands on the loading spur, someone is sent to walk toward it slowly, flapping an oilskin, until it turns away. The children do not ask for pets. Instead, they keep pieces of sinter that have hardened into shapes—a toad, a tiny bird, a fish that never moved—and they set them on their windowsills and blow steam over them from their mouths to make them seem alive. The town’s bestiary is mineral. It never eats, never cries, never dies, except to dissolve slowly back into water.

The plants are different. They are allowed. They are necessary, perhaps. The people know which ferns curl with the pump stroke and which reeds hiss with the valves. They do not eat the glossy leaves, and they warn outsiders not to chew them. A few garden beds are kept for food, but they are not the lush giant foliage of the basin; they are small, pale vegetables in boxes of imported soil, grown under glass and lamplight, tasting faintly of minerals no matter how often the soil is changed. The people eat simply: bread brought by the tram, dried seaweed, root vegetables, salt from their own shop. They drink the condensed water, which is clean enough but always tastes of iron, and they add a little salt to it in the hot season. No one goes hungry, but no one is full either. They eat like people stoking a small fire, not like people feeding an animal appetite.

Their bodies have begun to belong to the place. It is not talked about openly, but you can see it if you stay long enough. The pumpmaster’s left arm, the one she rests on the bearing casing, has a thin white crust along the outer edge, smooth as the terraces. She scrapes it off with a copper scraper every few days, but it returns, a little thicker. The oldest weir-keeper has a patch of mineral scale behind his left ear, where the steam licks him as he bends to the sluice. The children have games in which they dip a finger into a hot pool—never for long, never too deep—and then let the mineral water bead and harden, making a tiny stone cap on the nail. It is not exactly frightening. It is more like the place is slowly signing its name on them.

They are not ill, mostly. Their lungs are wide and wet-sounding, but the heat and the constant steam keep them free of the dry coughs that plague the uplands. Their sleep is deep, except for those who sleep near the expansion joints and wake at every clank. They age slowly, or they say they do; the mineral water is supposed to be good for the joints, though it stiffens the skin. Accidents are rare and specific: a steam burn from a valve, a foot gone through a soft board, a man lost in the mist and found hours later sitting by the wrong pool, his boots already crusted white. The place does not kill often. It has no need. It is more patient than that.

What they love is hard to describe. They love the pumps, but not the way a person loves a horse. They love them the way a person loves a house, or a climate, or a recurring dream. They will say, “Number two is breathing well today,” with something like affection. They will pat a cold condenser pipe the way you might pat a wall. The listener speaks to the pipes while he taps them, not in words, but in small rhythmic taps of reply, like a man completing a refrain. The lamplighter has a favorite lamp, the one on the corner by the salt stall, because it burns the warmest orange in the steam. The children name the pumps: Mister Thud, Aunt Hiss, Little Bump. They are not lonely. The absence of animals has made room for a different kind of companionship, one made of iron, water, and attention.

There are no births here often, and when there are, the child is held up in the steam by the pumpmaster, who says, “You are water and you are iron. Keep moving.” The old die quietly, usually in their sleep, and they are laid in the terraces, where the mineral water slowly covers them with a pale, smooth stone. The family marks the spot with a small brass tag, and over time the shape of the body becomes one of the shapes in the sinter. The town does not say “buried” or “cremated.” It says “returned.” And when the steam curls from the mouth of a new sinter shape, the children say that the person is still breathing.

You could leave. The tram runs up to the rim, and beyond the rim there is sun, birds, insects, a world with normal rain. Some do leave. But most stay, and those who stay know why, though they cannot always say it. The water is warm. The work is endless and clear. The noise is so constant that it becomes a kind of quiet. The light is dim enough that the eyes can rest. The rules are few and firm. The machines need you. The plants move with you. And in the hot, green, half-lit bowl, there is no animal fear, no animal hunger, no animal death—only the slow, breathing negotiation between water and iron, and the people walking its channels, keeping the water moving, keeping the condensers cold, keeping the pumps from running dry.
