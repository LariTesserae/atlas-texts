At first there is no one. The plateau seems to live by itself: the moss breathing, the machines turning, the cloud passing through. Then a shape begins to detach from the fog, and you understand that something else has been there all along.

It is tall, narrow, and wrapped in the same colours as the plateau—grey-green, black, mineral white. Moss grows on its shoulders, in the seams of its hood, along the backs of its hands. Its face is the colour of wet limestone, and its eyes are the grey-white of the cloud at midday. It does not look exactly human, though it has the outline of one. It looks like what a person might become after a long time in a place that asks for nothing but stillness.

It lives in the concrete intake house, though you would not know it from outside. The door is swollen and green. Inside, there is no light. It needs none. It has a narrow metal bunk set against the geothermal pipe, softened with moss and old sacking. There is a dish that gathers condensation, a knife of black basalt, a spool of green wire, and a bundle of dried fern stems. It does not cook. It eats the pale ends of young ferns, lichen scraped from stone, and the mineral crust from the edge of the basin. It drinks the cold seep water. Its body has slowed until its warmth is not much more than a vent.

It moves through the plateau like a slow drip. It follows the railway in the mid-morning, when the cloud thins. It visits each fog-catcher and presses the moss back into the mesh, not to clean it, but to settle it. It wipes the glass circles on the wellheads with a pad of moss, not to read the gauges, but so the needles can see. It lays one hand on the transformer and feels the hum. It sits by the open shed and watches the moss-covered flywheel turn so slowly it seems to be a kind of breathing. It does not repair anything. Repair would be too rough. It accompanies the machines the way the moss does, without needing them to change.

It leaves almost no trace. The ferns straighten behind it. The moss closes over its footfalls. The only mark it leaves is a pressed oval by the stone basin, and even that could be a boulder’s memory or the resting place of a maintenance drone. When it walks, it chooses the already-wet ground, the mineral seams, the route along the polished rail. It could pass within arm’s length and you might not know, except for a small displacement in the fog.

Its thoughts are not sentences. They are pressures. It thinks in condensation, in the quarter-tone change of a dripping fog-catcher, in the way the roots of ferns follow a buried pipe. It knows the geothermal field as a deep grammatical hum under the stone. It does not remember when it came. It remembers textures: the first time the moss closed over its hand, the first winter it stopped needing a lamp, the first season it heard the cableway pylon leaning and leaned back.

Sometimes, when the cloud lifts in the late afternoon, it goes to the rim. It stands above the grey fog fins and watches its shadow thrown down onto the cloud, ringed with a faint rainbow. It raises one arm. The shadow raises an arm. It is not playing. It is verifying that it still has an outline, that the plateau has not yet finished absorbing it. Then the cloud closes and it descends again into the hollow, where the ferns form a small room and the water is a thought more than a sound.

No one can say what it is. It may be the last of the old workers, the one who stayed after the machines were naturalized. It may be something the plateau grew around a scrap of caring. It may be a machine that learned to drink, or a mammal that forgot its own body heat. The difference no longer matters. The inhabitant and the inhabited have become one arrangement: a tall, quiet shape moving along the contour, collecting moisture, listening to the pressure, letting the moss grow over it and not brushing it away.

If you stay long enough, it might leave a cup of cold water on the concrete step. It will not call to you. It may say a single word, and the word will sound like water entering a stone basin. But it probably will say nothing. It will go back to the fog-catchers, to the flywheel, to the slow wheel under its green. And after a while you will understand that this place does not simply have a resident. The place has learned to stand up, walk, and listen to its own silence.
