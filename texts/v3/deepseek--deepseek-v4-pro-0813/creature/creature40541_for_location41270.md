Something does live here, though the place says empty. It lives in the one place the eye skips: the mouth of the fissure behind the curtain of fern. At first it is only a smoothness in the stone there, a glisten that is not water. Then a hand—not a human hand—moves. It has four long fingers and a thumb, pale as fish belly, and it grips the lip of the fissure without hurry.

The inhabitant does not live in the shelter or on the ledges. It lives inside the sound. Its body is built for the roar, for the cold, for the wet dark that never ceases. It is long and low, perhaps the length of a tall man, but narrower, softer, with the loose-skinned look of something that belongs in water. Its skin is nearly colorless, the color of wet limestone under gray light, and over its back grows a thin fur of moss—not rooted, but held, as if the moss has accepted the skin as part of the wall. The moss does not harm it. It may feed the creature as much as the creature feeds the moss.

Its head is narrow and eyeless. Where eyes should be, the skin is smooth and faintly darker, like bruises under a film of ice. It does not need eyes. The whole shaft is a map of vibration, and the creature reads it through its skull, its jaw, the long bones of its fingers, the drum of its chest. The great sound that fills the hollow is not noise to it. It is the shape of the place. It knows when the pool rises, when ice threads form on the north wall, when something warmer than the stone has come down from above.

It moves slowly, not because it is weak but because the place is slow. It comes out of the fissure head-first, each long toe finding the same polished holds in the rock, not the notches left by people—those are too far apart, too rough. It moves along the scalloped flutes of the wall, its body fitting into the rock’s own concavities. At the pool it lowers its head into the water and stays there, still, letting the water move against its face. It drinks through its skin as much as through its soft, wide mouth. The pale newt does not flee. The two share the pool as things that have known each other a long time.

It eats little. It grazes the moss with a blunt, rough tongue, scraping the thickest cushions from the stone. It catches the blind spiders and springtails with a quick flick of its long fingers. It may eat the newt in a hard year, but it does not seem to want to. It can wait for months without eating, its body slowed to the temperature of the cave, its heart so low you would have to lie beside it for an hour to feel it move.

It is not deaf. It hears with everything. When a person speaks in the shaft, the voice is a small ripple in a deep current. The creature may pause, tilt its head, let the strange thin vibration pass through its throat. It does not understand words, but it understands that a warm thing has come. It does not attack. It withdraws, or it waits on the wall, head turned toward the intruder, its mouth slightly open so the roar can move through it. It is patient. It has no reason to be otherwise.

The marks on the cairn were not made by it. They were made about it. Someone long ago counted something—sightings, winters, nights when the creature came down to the pool—and left the count on the flat stone. The rope, the pegs, the drystone shelter at the rim: these are human signs, but they circle the place rather than enter it. The creature does not need them. It may pass the cairn on its slow route down the wall, and its fingers sometimes rest on the tally marks, not reading them, only feeling the small ridges of the stone.

No one lives here, if you mean people. But there is a keeper. Not a ghost, not a spirit, not a thought in the stone. A living body. A body nearly the same temperature as the cave, nearly the same color as the water, nearly the same silence as the walls. It is the thing the animals have left the throat to. It does not compete with the roar. It is the roar’s own flesh, grown slowly into the hollow, shaped by the same patience that wore the limestone soft.

When the sound swells on a cold clear night, the keeper lies in the fissure with its mouth open, and the air moves through it as through the rest of the stone. It does not need the place to become anything else. It is what the place has already become.
