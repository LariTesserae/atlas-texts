They are not many. If you stood on the high spine and counted the moving figures across the white terraces, you might see four, or six, or nine—never a crowd. They are pale as the stone, and they move like water that has learned to walk upright.

Their skin is the color of fresh travertine: not white, but a soft cream with undertones of rose and grey where the blood runs close. The fine lime dust settles on their eyebrows, their forearms, the faint hairs of their cheeks, so they look as if they have been rolled in flour or left out in the mineral fog. Their hair is pale too—flax, ash, the color of dried moss, sometimes nearly green where the water has stained it. They wear it long and tied back with cords of plant fiber, or cut in a bowl shape that reveals the long, slow line of the neck.

They are not tall, but they are long in the limb. Their hands are shaped by stone: knuckles large, fingers square at the tips, palms covered with a white, slick callus that has become almost stone itself. Their feet are bare, the soles broad and cracked, able to read the texture of a terrace the way you might read a map. They wear simple tunics and loose trousers woven from a fibrous plant that grows in the wet channels—undyed cloth in shades of cream, grey, and faint green. But the mineral water has stiffened the cloth over time, so their garments hang like soft paper and crackle quietly when they move.

They are the only animals in a country where animal life never took hold. They keep no dogs, no goats, no fowl. They eat only what the wet land offers: a bitter cress that grows in the mineral pools, a starchy root that must be soaked in running water for three days to be edible, and a certain moss that can be dried and pounded into a flour that tastes of earth and rain. They cook in stone pots set into the warm springs, or they let the water and the slow air ferment the moss in sealed clay jars. They eat little, and they eat slowly. Hunger is a low, familiar ache, like the ache of a wall waiting for the next film of stone.

Their work is the work of water: to clear, to direct, to wait. They carry tools of stone and wood—chisels of hard river rock, mallets of dense wood, clay buckets, cords of fiber. They tend the channels, scraping away the new sinter where it threatens to close a sluice, but never scraping too much. The stone is both their material and their adversary. They know how to set a wooden frame in the path of a seep and leave it for a decade, so that the water grows a wall around it. They know how to read a pool’s edge, how to tell by the color of the water whether the carbonate is high or low, how to taste a spring and know if it will build or only wet. They are not engineers. They are gardeners of stone.

Their speech is sparse, but not because they are mute. Their language is small and precise, made of short words that sound like the drip of water or the tap of stone on stone. A sentence might be one word; a long story might be told over an evening, a word at a time. They understand each other mostly through gesture, through the subtle signals of posture and breath, through the way a person tilts their head toward a sound. They can send messages over distance by striking the stone channels—three taps for *come*, five for *the wall has shifted*, a slow rolling rhythm for *a child is born*. The stone carries the sound for miles, and the people listen with their fingertips as much as their ears.

They are not solitary. They gather at the cisterns, at the water stairs, at the wide courts where the canals meet. They gather for work—clearing a blocked sluice, repairing a settling tank, raising a new wall. They work in silence, but it is not the silence of strangers. It is the silence of people who have known each other so long that words are unnecessary. A young person learns the work by watching an elder’s hands, by feeling the weight of a chisel, by tasting the water at different points along a channel. There is no school, only the slow transfer of attention.

Their children are few. A birth is a quiet event, not a celebration but a new sound in the world. The child is bathed in a shallow pool of clear water, then laid on a bed of moss and left to listen. They grow slowly, not in body but in presence. A child of three knows not to shout. A child of seven can sit for an hour, watching a drip form and fall, watching the mineral skin grow on a twig. They are not taught to be quiet; they learn it as they learn to breathe. When a child cries, it is a sound like a stone dropped into a still pool—the adults turn, not in alarm, but in gentle surprise, and the child is soon soothed.

Their dead do not leave. When a person dies, the body is carried to a private basin, a hollow in the travertine where a thin stream of mineral water has been directed over the years. The body is arranged with care: hands folded on the chest, face turned toward the sky. The water is allowed to flow over it, a film so thin it might be mistaken for light. For days the family watches, adjusting the flow, lifting away any fallen leaf. Then they go. Over months, the body is encrusted with new stone, its shape preserved as if caught in a slow white sleep. The oldest terraces are full of such figures—half-shaped bodies emerging from the walls, their faces blurred by layers of sinter, their hands reaching out as if to hold the water. The living do not pray to them; they simply remember. They will point to a lump in a wall and say, *My mother is learning to be a wall.* It is not said with sadness. It is the way of things.

They have no gods, but they have a deep, practical reverence for the water. They call it *the builder*, *the rememberer*, *the one who does not forget*. They know the water is not alive, but they treat it as if it were, because it gives them their walls, their tools, their food, their dead. They do not worship it; they serve it. They are, in their own words, *the ones who keep the water’s path clear*. They do not think of themselves as owners of the land. They are caretakers, or perhaps guests who have stayed so long they have been accepted into the stone.

A stranger might find them cold or numb. They are not. They are warm, like the springs, but their warmth is slow. They love slowly, with small gestures: a hand on a shoulder, a cup of clear water offered, a silence shared. They grieve slowly, over years, the way a pool loses its heat after the sun has gone. They anger rarely, and when they do, it is a sharp crack of words, like a stone splitting, and then it is gone, and the water flows on.

They are not happy, and they are not sad. They are what the land has made them: a people of stone and water, of thin green moss and long quiet. They are the Pale Steps made flesh, walking through a world where time has become so slow it feels stopped, and they have learned to live at that speed—not fighting it, not hurrying, but simply moving with the water, one step at a time, through a country that was built before it was ever lived in.
