No one lives here. Not in the way you mean.

There is no person, no animal, no bird, no fish, no insect, no frog. The town is empty of all the quick, warm-blooded, hungry things. But it is not uninhabited. It is inhabited in the slow way—by the things that have become the town, and by the town that has become a thing.

The first resident is the moss. It lives on everything: on the flywheel’s rim, on the valve wheels, on the undersides of stairs, on the shoulders of boilers, on the bricks, the pipes, the cables, the wet stone. It is not one moss but many, a green fur with different textures and shades, and it has soaked up the town’s wet breath for so long that it has become part of the architecture. Where the wiper cleans the flywheel, the moss grows back in a perfect arc; where a pipe leaks steam, the moss thins and darkens upward. It is the town’s outermost sense organ, feeling temperature and moisture and vibration more reliably than any eye.

The vine wrapped around the valve wheel also lives here. Each morning, as the sap pressure changes with the day’s warmth, the vine’s grip tightens or loosens, and the valve turns a few degrees. The machine below does not question the adjustment; it has long since incorporated the vine into its operation. The vine is a slow hand, patient and particular. It does not know it is turning a valve, and the valve does not know it is being turned, but the water downstream knows the difference.

The root that passes between the spokes of the central flywheel is perhaps the most intimate resident. Each revolution, the iron carries it down into the black water and up into the mist. The root has worn a groove in the stone where it dips, and the water squeezed from it is metered out drop by drop. It is both passenger and driver; its own slight flexure, repeated once a minute for years, has altered the wheel’s balance, and the wheel has altered the root’s shape. They have become a single moving joint, a hinge of iron and green.

The fern growing through the gauge lives here too. The gauge reads in green now, its needle covered by fronds, its dial a small meadow of leaf. It still points to something—pressure or temperature or the rate of some flow—but what it points to is no longer a number anyone can read. It measures the saturation of the air, the weight of the mist, the passage of time as the frond uncurls a little more each season.

The pumps in the drowned levels are not merely machines. They are the town’s breathing. They draw the thick, mineral-rich water up through pipes and roots, then release it to fall again as warm rain and drip. Their stroke is slow as a tide, and the ferns on the upper terraces open and close in time with it. The pumps have no need for a keeper; they know the rhythm that keeps the water moving, and they keep it. They are the reason the air is always wet, the reason the walls run with sheets of water, the reason the lower rooms are green and drowned.

The boilers are the town’s stomachs. They take the mineral broth, the black mud, the fallen leaves, and the slow decay of iron, and they turn it into steam. The steam leaks from every joint, adding to the mist that blurs the upper levels. The boiler hall glows with a dim orange mouth, and the moss that grows on the boiler’s skin is not a parasite but a tongue, tasting the heat and the gas. The boilers do not rush; they simmer for decades, their bellies colonized by heat-loving moss, their doors crusted with salts.

The electric system is the town’s nervous system. The dim filaments left burning in empty windows are not for anyone; they are the town’s memory of light, a pattern of faint stars that indicates where the rooms are, where the stairs descend, where the cables sag. The gauges and red indicator lights are its senses: a string of weak sparks along a far line is a thought crossing from one district to another, too slow to see but continuous. The blue-green glow from the lower water is not a failure of light; it is the light of the microbial mats, the town’s oldest residents, still keeping their own private spectra.

The broad leaves that lean over the walkways and shed drips are the town’s ears. They lean toward the warm steam pipes, and when a pump changes pitch, they shiver slightly—not from sound, but from the vibration in the wet air. They are listening, in the way a leaf listens to the weather, and the town listens through them.

The aerial roots that hang from the upper levels and root in the black water below are the town’s legs, its anchors, its drinkers. They are thick as wrists, some of them, and they have been hanging there for so long that they have become part of the town’s structure, holding up walls as much as mortar does. They drink the water and carry it upward, just as the pipes do; the water does not know which path it takes, and the town does not distinguish.

The microbial mats are the deepest self. They color the water green-black, they bubble slowly, they break down fallen iron into rust and gas, and they glow faintly in the dark. They are the base layer, the unthinking metabolism of the whole. They are the reason the lower rooms are not merely drowned but alive with a faint electric glow. They were the first inhabitants, before the bricks, before the flywheel, before the first root came down from above. They are still here, under everything, eating the town’s leavings and turning them back into mineral soup.

None of these parts speaks. None of them thinks in the way a person thinks. None of them has a name or a face or a feeling of loneliness. But together they form a single continuous system, a slow feedback loop that has been adjusting itself for longer than anyone can measure. The town is not a habitat; it is an organism. It has no need for animals because it already performs all the functions an animal would perform. It moves water; it breathes steam; it digests iron and leaf; it stores and releases light; it feels through its gauges and ferns; it remembers through the wear on the stairs, the salt lines on the walls, the settled lean of a building. It is alive, but its life is not animal life. It is the life of a sump that became a town, a town that became a machine, a machine that became a garden.

And the garden-machine is still working. It is still wet. It is still breathing, in its own slow iron-and-root way. If you stand on the rim and watch, you see only a bowl filled with green fog and the glint of lamps. But if you stay inside until your own heartbeat slows to match the flywheel’s minute, you may feel the town’s pulse under your feet, and know that you are standing inside a single sleeping creature—not asleep the way an animal sleeps, but awake in a way that has no waking, only the endless, patient, dripping, thumping continuation of itself.

So no, there is no resident to greet you. No one will answer if you knock. But the place is not empty. It is full to the brim with itself, and that self is the only resident it has ever needed.
