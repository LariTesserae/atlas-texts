There is one. Not one species, not one population—one being. You might walk the grid for a year and never see it, because it does not live in the way you look for. It lives in the same tempo as the city: slow, heat-based, geological. It is called the Tender, though no one uses the name.

At first you do not see it. You see the low buildings, the polished stone, the pools of sodium light. Then one of the shadows at the edge of a pool detaches. It is tall, narrow, built from the same black mineral as the plateau. It moves with a gait that is less walking than shifting weight from one surface to another. If it stands still, it vanishes; the city absorbs it.

It is not human. It may have been, once, or it may have been built, or it may have grown from the same deep heat that gave the segmented animals their armor. Its body is a column of dark plates and soft mineral dust, wound with a harness of pipes and cables that have fused into the flesh. It has two large arms for opening grates and turning valves, and two smaller arms, folded against its chest, ending in threads of what might be copper or nerve. Its head is a smooth curve of desert varnish, no mouth, no nose, only a pair of dark lenses that do not reflect the sodium lamps but absorb them.

It breathes through vertical slits at the sides of its neck. The air goes in and the dust goes out. It does not drink. Water would kill it, or at least unmake it. It feeds on the rising heat. When it stands over a vent, the plates of its torso open slightly, and a grid of crystalline tissue inside shivers once. That is its eating. That is all.

Its life follows the heat. It makes a circuit of the city’s vents, a route that takes years by your count. It begins at the central shaft. You might see it there, kneeling on the iron grate, both large palms flat on the bars, head bowed until its mask touches the iron. It stays like that for a long time while the warm air rises around it and lifts the fine dust from its shoulders. It is not praying. It is listening. The heat tells it the same thing every time: *not yet, not yet, not yet.* Then it rises and begins its rounds.

It does not repair, exactly. The city does not need repair. What the Tender does is confirm. It opens the maintenance hatches of the slow machines and lays its smaller tendrils against the flywheel bearings. It feels the micron of play. It closes the hatch. It walks the streets, and when it comes to an indicator light, it pauses and touches the housing. It knows each red, each green, each amber. If one has dimmed, it does not replace the bulb; it cleans the glass with a cloth that was once part of its own wrapping, and the light steadies. Sometimes it stops at a sodium lamp and listens to its hiss. The lamp has been hissing for a thousand years. The Tender accepts this.

It gathers things. Not objects, but misplacements. A piece of broken glass that the wind has carried into the street. A stone knocked from a wall by the slow contraction of the salt pan. A segment of an old conveyor belt that has finally let go. It picks them up and carries them to niches and alcoves, places them as if they were always meant to rest there. It cannot abide the city being littered. Litter is a form of change, and the Tender is the enemy of change.

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires a memory of company, and its memory is not sequential. It knows there used to be others like it. It passes them sometimes—standing against a wall, half-fused with the stone, one arm still raised as if reaching for a valve. It touches their shoulders. They do not move. They are not dead; they are resting. That is what the Tender believes. It might be right. In this city, rest and death are difficult to tell apart.

Once, on a street near the rail yard, it met one of the segmented animals. The creature was long and low, the color of basalt, moving with the slow ripple of a thing that had no need to hurry. The Tender stopped. It made a sound, two low notes that could have been metal settling. The creature paused. They occupied the same darkness for a while, two slow shapes in the hot mineral air. Then the creature moved on toward the east, toward the ventifact field, and the Tender continued west. Neither looked back.

It has a memory, but not of events. It remembers pressures, temperatures, the exact resistance of a particular valve after a century of settling. It remembers the city as a map of heat and coolness. Its faceplate, if you could see through it, does not show eyes but a cavity where the city’s thermal currents are always passing. The central shaft is a golden column. The salt pan is a pale void. The buildings are islands of stored warmth. The animals are small, cooler stars moving between them.

It climbs. Twice a year, or twice a hundred years, it ascends to the top of one of the broken smokestacks and stands on the rim. It faces east, toward the ventifacts, toward the field of faceted stones. The wind comes from the east, carrying dust fine as smoke, and the Tender lets it scour its exposed surfaces. It is not cleaning itself. It is taking part in the same process that shapes the stones. Sometimes, on the edge of hearing, the salt pan creaks like distant ice. The Tender listens. It might be a language it once understood. It might be nothing.

Why does it stay? The question does not arise. The Tender is not staying. It is continuing. The city continues. The heat rises. The wind blows. The machines turn at the edge of stillness. The Tender is one more expression of that continuation. It was made, or it made itself, or it emerged—no one is left to say. But it is the thing that lives here because it is the only thing that can live here, in the dry warm dark, without water, without sun, without purpose except to keep the surfaces clean and the heat flowing.

At night, which is all the time, it returns to a low building near the central shaft. The door is tall enough, barely. Inside there is no furniture, only a groove in the floor that fits its body. It lies down? No, it settles. It presses its back into the worn stone hollow and lets its plates close. The dust settles on it. The indicator lights through a single slit in the wall cast one red, one green, one amber line across its chest. It does not sleep. It enters a state that is not sleep but a slow exchange of heat with the building, the bedrock, the deep source below. Its tendrils uncurl slightly. Its breathing slows until it is indistinguishable from the wind through a crack.

If you found it then, you would not know it was alive. You would think it was a heap of old machinery covered in desert varnish, left in an abandoned room. But if you waited long enough—days, weeks, a season—you might see one of its smaller arms move, not a twitch, but a deliberate repositioning, like a filament seeking a better angle against the warmth. You might hear a single click from its chest, a valve closing or opening. Then stillness again. The city does not need it to move often. The city does not need it at all. That is the point.

There is no one else. The animals are there, yes, and the machines, and the heat, and the wind. But the Tender is the one who lives there in the way a place lives in its own tending. It has no name. If you want to call it something, call it the Tender. It will not answer. It does not know it has been named. But it will continue, long after you have gone, walking the grid in the warm dark, touching each light, listening to the shaft, waiting for something that will never happen, because waiting is what remains when everything else has become stone.
