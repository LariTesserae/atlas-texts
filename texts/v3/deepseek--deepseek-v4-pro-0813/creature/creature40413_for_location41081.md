From the rim they might be anything: salt pillars, scraps of copper, wind-blown reed. Then one of them moves—not with the jerk of a leaf in the wind, but with the slow, placed motion of a person. Another follows. A rope that seemed empty becomes a child climbing it, bare feet curling. The Bell-Hollow is not empty. It is crowded in a way the eye does not expect, because its people are built out of the same pale, ringing light as the walls.

They are human, but the hollow has worn them into something more particular. Generations of mineral dust and hot sap and unrelenting sound have made them tall and long-boned, with narrow torsos and outsized hands and feet. Their skin is the color of the gypsum overhang: a dry, luminous white. It is finely grooved, so that an elder’s forearm looks like the salt polygons of the sacred floor. Their hair is either colorless or the dull copper of their bells, cropped short or twisted into stiff ropes that rattle with baked resin beads. They are not thin so much as desiccated; their bodies have learned to keep every drop of moisture they own. They do not sweat unless the heat is extreme, and when they do, the sweat dries into a white bloom that they scrape off and save for cooking. Their lips are cracked and pale, anointed with clear resin so they catch the light. Their teeth are stained faintly green from the mineral sap.

Their faces are still. The mouth is the least useful organ in the Bell-Hollow. Its roar is so constant, so layered with bell and chime and clacking stalk and popping bladder that speech is useless, and so the people speak with their hands, their feet, their shoulders, their eyebrows, the tilt of their heads. A conversation on opposite sides of the hollow is a duet of tapping fingers and drummed heels, of gestures so fast and precise they resemble weaving. They have a spoken language, but it is a relic, kept for alarms and vows and for calling a child back from the rim. A spoken word here cuts through the chord like a blade. Their voices are low and rough, rusted from disuse, but they can pitch them to carry. A shout is a serious act.

The rest of their speech is not silent. They drum on their own collarbones as they think. They tap the walls, stomp the ledges, strike copper plates with hammers. A sentence can be felt through the rock twenty houses away. The children learn to sign before they can walk. A baby bound to its mother’s back pats her shoulder in rhythm when it wants to nurse; she answers with a tap of her thumb against her collarbone. By the time a child can cross a rope bridge, it knows three hundred signs and can drum its own name on the wall. Names are not spoken but tapped—a rhythm, a sequence of beats, sometimes with a small copper bell rung on the finger for punctuation.

They listen as much with their skin as their ears. The constant chord of the hollow is too vast to hear directly, the way one cannot hear one’s own blood. They have learned to filter it, to pick a single bell out of the roar or hear the change in a mirror-vine’s rattle when the wind shifts. But their true hearing is in their feet and palms. They have thick, broad soles full of nerves, and they read the hollow as a drum. A woman checking a ledge places her hand flat on the salt and closes her eyes. A man on a high terrace stamps twice; a child on the floor far below feels it through the rock and looks up. When they want to know if a rope is straining, they don’t look at it; they wrap a hand around it and feel the tension sing.

Their day begins with the cold sinking wind, before dawn. The bladders are deflated then, papery and dry. The people rise anyway, because morning is the time for repair. They climb to the rim and check the chimes and ropes after the night wind, and children skitter along the high terraces, tapping the glassy stalks to hear them clack and collecting the salt crystals that have grown on the walls overnight. Breakfast is a paste made from the pith of the glass-bamboo, scraped and beaten and mixed with the last evening’s resin. It is pale and slightly fizzy, with a taste like copper and sour berries. They eat it with their fingers, sitting in the doorways of their salt-brick homes, their legs dangling over the drop.

By mid-morning the heat begins to bake. The bladders swell and the first pops echo across the bowl, a sound like small stones dropped on glass. By noon, the heat-shimmer turns the air to oil and the popping is a dry rain. The people retreat into their houses, which are built into the salt and lined with mats of woven mirror-vine fiber to muffle the noise. Inside, the sound drops to a low thrum, like a heart in the walls. They sleep through the worst of the glare, or they do quiet work: knotting ropes, mending copper tools, sorting salt for the smiths. If someone must go out, they wrap their faces in veils of reed and copper thread to keep the salt dust from their eyes.

Dusk is the busiest hour. The wind reverses, carrying salt dust up and out of the mouth. The bladder-leaves fill with a thick, clear resin that smells of citrus and hot metal. Resin-gatherers move along the terraces with copper jars, pinching each bladder with thumb and forefinger so the resin runs down a groove without bursting the leaf. The children hold the ladders and catch the drips. This resin is the foundation of everything: fuel for the lamps, glue for the bricks, medicine for cracked skin, and the base of their fermented drink. Later, they press the bladder-leaves for a milky liquid that hisses and froths in clay pots sunk into the warm salt. They drink it in the evening, sitting on the ledges as the light fades. It is mildly intoxicating; the elders drink more and their hand-signs go loose and loving.

The smithies wake after dark. The forges burn the resin, and their hammers send codes through the wall of the hollow—long and short, a clock of copper. The smiths are broad-shouldered and flash-burned, their arms flecked with bronze. They cast bells, not weapons. A bell is a family’s voice; each one is tuned to a different note, and together they make the hollow’s chord. A bellwright is a kind of priest. They can hear a sour bell from three terraces away, climb a ladder in the dark, and tap it true again with a hammer no larger than a thumb.

The houses are stacked six or seven high on the spiral ledges, braced with salt-brick and copper pins. Each is small inside, the main room built around a central stove that burns the sap. The walls are thick, the doorways low. You step over the threshold and tap the frame with a knuckle—a greeting, a warning, a sign that you have arrived. Inside, the noise of the hollow becomes a muffled pressure. They sleep in hammocks of woven reed, suspended so every movement sets them swaying. The oldest live lowest, closest to the floor, where the sound is deepest and warmest. The young live highest, near the rim, where the cold sinking wind slips in. A family may spend generations moving down, one level at a time, as if descending into the ear.

There is no king, no mayor. The First Listener lives in a low, wide house at the top of the spiral, where the wind first touches the rim. She is often an old woman, her earlobes stretched by copper rings, her spine as fluted as the walls. At dawn she presses her palm to the wall and listens with her feet. If the wall’s hum is steady, she signs *the day is whole*, and the town begins. If not, she sends a ripple of drum-codes down the spiral: a rope-weaver to the north bridge, a bellwright to the third terrace, a child to clear the dust from the fluted wall. The work of the town is to keep the sound true. They believe the hollow is an ear, listening to the desert, and if the sound ever stops, the world will end. So they are not merely inhabitants; they are keepers of a continuous prayer.

The central stone at the lowest point of the bowl is their most sacred place. No one builds there. The floor around it is a mosaic of salt polygons with curled edges, crossed by ropes, and no one walks on it. They say the floor is the hollow’s tongue, and to step on it would interrupt the world’s speech. The stone itself is cut with hand-signs older than the town. The oldest sign is a spread hand with a circle at the palm: *sound*. Another is a finger pressed to the ground: *listen*. There is a third sign no one will make, a closed fist inside a mouth, or maybe a mouth inside a fist. They say it is the word that was spoken only once, when the sound nearly stopped, and that speaking it again would bring the silence back.

When someone dies, the body is wrapped in reed mats and carried across the ropes to the central stone. The dead are not buried. They are laid on a ledge just below the rim, where the salt wind gradually encrusts them. Over years, they become part of the wall, their bones white as the salt. A family may later collect the bones and hang them as chimes, so the dead keep ringing. When someone is born, the parents climb to the rim before dawn and listen for a new tone in the wind. If they hear one, they make the child’s name-sign over the mouth of the hollow and let the wind carry it down. The child is not considered fully born until the hollow has heard its name.

The other inhabitants are fewer but no less part of the place. A pale cricket lives inside the bladder-leaves, blind and soft, thrown out when the leaf pops. The children eat them fried on hot stones, or catch them and let them go for luck. Once or twice a year, the Needle-Heron crosses the mouth of the hollow, never landing. Its shadow is long and thin, like a crack in the light. When it passes, the people pause and make a sign with one hand—a greeting, a ward. If the shadow falls on the central stone, they say a change is coming, and the First Listener watches the air for three days. The blind serpent moves through the salt crust below the ledges, rarely seen, but felt as a slow deep vibration, as if a bow is being drawn across the underside of the bowl. They call it the Under-Ringer. It seems to follow the sound. Children leave little balls of resin at the edge of the cracks for it, and sometimes, in the quiet hour before dawn, they feel it thump once against the wall, as if answering.

Strange things happen in the bowl, and the people read them. Sometimes the air fills with fine salt crystals aligned in columns, catching the light like slow lightning. They call this *the air writing*. A column that bends toward the rim means a visitor from the desert. A column that stands straight over the central stone means a death. A column that leans toward the mirror-vines means the vines are turning, which they do anyway, following the sound rather than the sun. The people turn their heads too, not toward what is bright but toward what is humming.

They have no word for water. The sign for it is a sacred gesture, fingers descending like rain, used only in stories. They imagine water as a cold, clear silence, heavy and transparent, and they are not sure it is a good thing. They say if water ever fell here, it would melt the walls and stop the sound, and the world would end. The sap of the plants is their water. It is hot and resinous and alive, and it keeps the sound in their blood. They drink it with a small bow, the way a person might drink a day.

They are not exactly happy, not exactly holy, but they are complete. The Bell-Hollow is loud, and they have learned to live inside a sound. They are the hollow’s hands, its breath, its ear. If you stay long enough, you may start to feel it too: the walls hum, the ropes tremble, the people move in rhythm. And you are the only thing not ringing.
