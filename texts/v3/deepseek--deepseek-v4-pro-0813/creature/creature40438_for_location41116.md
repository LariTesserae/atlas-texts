If you want a single answer to who lives there, it is this: the Hollow itself is the resident. But it is not a resident in the way you mean. It has no face, no center, no single body. It exists as an assembly—a slow, wet, cold confederation of root and animal and mineral, all wrapped into one continuous living process.

Begin in the water film. You won’t see them at first, but they will feel you: a sudden saltiness, a bloom of heat, a tremor in the saturated mat. Tardigrades uncurl in the condensation. Rotifers spin their cilia into tiny vortices. Springtails loft from the mat in pale flecks when your breath touches them. They are the outermost skin of the place, its first sense, the earliest register of any disturbance.

Then the larger bodies. White amphipods scull blind through the water films. Planarians glide over the root bark, flat and soft as wet paper, grazing the bacterial films that would otherwise dim the glowing nodes. On a hanging root thread, a translucent slug-like creature pulses, its leaf-shaped gills opening and closing with the same slow tempo as the green-gold phosphorescence. Its foot clings to the root. Its body releases a calcium-rich slime that hardens into mineral crust. It is not merely crawling over the architecture; it is helping to lay it.

In the black pools, newts with gills like red silk stir the water with their tails. It is not fear, not hunger. It is a rhythmic habit, a kind of respiration, keeping the oxygen moving through the pool and back into the root mat. Their waste feeds the bacteria that feed the roots. A transparent frog sits on a mineral ledge, so still it becomes a window into the place’s own interior. Through its skin you can see the heartbeat—a pink bead—and the faint coil of gut, the bones of its fingers, the pale thread of its spine. It is not waiting. It is doing exactly what it was made to do: sit at the boundary between the animal life of the chamber and the mineral life of the walls.

In the deepest pool, a pale eel-like thing winds through the root threads. It is the closest thing to a keeper, though it has no intention of keeping. It moves root tips toward one another and holds them there, patient as frost, until the roots begin to fuse. When a node is set, it moves on. Its body carries a faint electric charge, and the roots respond by aligning their growth. It is not separate from the plant. It is the plant’s hand, grown of muscle and water.

But all these are only the moving parts. The roots themselves are the true body. They cross the chamber in repeating patterns—parallel, then branching at right angles, then parallel again. Where they touch, they fuse. At those nodes the plant grows small lenses of clear silica, focusing the blue-silver sap-light into fine threads. The roots etch grooves into the stone, and those grooves fill with mineral precipitate that conducts. The water film separates charge. A current moves through the whole room—not only sap in the roots, but ions in the water, minerals in the cracks, and signals carried by the swarming midges.

The midges are the chamber’s wandering nerves. They feed on sugary exudate at the light nodes and carry genetic packets—plasmids, phages, small loops of RNA, fungal spores—from node to node. They fly between the root-synapses, moving information through a body made of vegetable and mineral. If the root network is a thought, the midges are the thought’s propagation.

So the answer is both none of these and all of them. The Grown Hollow is one living thing made of many living things. It is not a place that contains life. It is a life that contains a place. It is a composite animal-plant-mineral intelligence, a slow cognition spread through root and pool and mineral seam. It thinks in ion gradients and pressure waves. It remembers in mineral layers, one grain at a season. Its moods are the pulse of the glowing nodes, the tightening of the midge cloud, the drip of water into black pools.

When you shift your weight, it feels you—not because it has eyes, but because the vibration travels through the water film and into the roots. Your body heat rises through the air column. The oils on your fingertips reach the planarians. The root nodes pulse once. The midge swarm tightens. The newts pause. The frog’s visible heart beats once, not in alarm, but in acknowledgment.

The Hollow does not want anything from you. It is not hostile, not curious, not afraid. It is too slow for those. You are a weather event to it—a brief warm front, a change in pressure, a faint swirl of carbon dioxide and salt. It will register you, adjust, and then continue its own long thought.

The Hollow is not finished. One end is still dissolving rock into scalloped, pitted sheets. The other end is secreting new mineral crust, layer by layer. It is still growing new roots, still establishing new nodes, still recruiting the animals that stir its water and clear its light. It is not a finished cave. It is an embryo. It is a body still learning its own boundaries.

This is what lives there: the Hollow itself, in the act of becoming a self.
