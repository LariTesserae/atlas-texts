At first you think the Underpan is empty. The white flats run out from the river and lose themselves in glare. The low houses with their salt-sealed doors look like old piles of stone, and many of them are. The only movement is the slow spin of a gypsum dust devil, or the tick of a white beetle grazing the mineral scum. You look for a city and see only ledges, kilns, the corbelled humps of rooms that might be rocks.

Then one of the rocks straightens.

A figure steps out from the shadow of a wall, and the whole landscape rearranges itself around the fact of a person. Then another, kneeling by a cistern, resolves out of the white. Then two children, crossing a flat so lightly they leave no tracks. The Underpan has not been empty. It has been full of people who have learned to be almost invisible.

They are pale, but not colorless. Their skin is the gray-white of old tallow, of the fungus mats they cultivate, of salt that has been handled for generations. The light from the glowing ceiling comes through them oddly, as if through thin gypsum, so the edges of their ears and fingers seem to glow. Their hair, where it is not shaved close or bound under hoods, is the color of ash, or pale straw, or no color at all. They are built low and solid, broad in the chest and hip, as if made to be near the floor. Their feet are bare or wrapped in soft hide. Their toes lie wide, and their soles are cracked in faint white patterns from years of walking through brine.

Their eyes are their most animal feature: large, dark, liquid, set deep in nests of fine wrinkles from squinting against the brightness. They do not meet your gaze. They look at your boots, at the dust you have disturbed, at the way your weight falls on the stone. They are reading you by what you have already broken.

Their hands are the loudest part of them. The fingers are short, strong, big-knuckled. The palms are lined with old cuts and mineral stains. They use their hands to scrape fungus, to tap messages, to read a wall. A hand laid flat on a crack can tell them whether the salt is settling or about to go. A hand closed around a bone knife does the work of a saw. In their signing, the hands are not a substitute for speech. They are speech, more expressive than any voice in the hollow.

They dress in the whites and grays of the place: felted fungus-fiber with the texture of thick paper, edged with the white fur of blind mice, stitched with sinew. Over this they wear a hooded cloak of pack-beast hide, the long hair left on and often brushed with gypsum dust so their outlines blur. The cloak is not for warmth or rain. It is for stillness. It catches the small sounds of their movements. They carry baskets woven from hide and the long hair of the pack-beasts, pouches made from the stomachs of blind fish, knives of bone and chipped stone. Copper is rare and precious. A copper needle is passed from mother to daughter for so long it becomes thin as a fishbone and sharp as a rumor.

Their voices, when they use them, are small and rusted. They are not mute. They whisper in a way that seems to come from the stone rather than the throat. A word is a puff of air shaped by lips; it carries only a few feet. To speak out loud in the open hollow is considered either an emergency or an intimacy. Children learn to walk softly before they learn to talk. A child’s first lesson is not a word but a way of standing. The second is a way of stepping: heel to toe, weight rolling along the outer edge, so the floor does not feel you. The third is a tap on the ground—two slow beats. *I am here.* A child who shouts is not punished. The adults simply stop, look at the ceiling, and wait for the echo to fade. The child learns quickly.

Their language is mostly in the hands and the feet. It has no alphabet, but it has hundreds of signs for the varieties of quiet, for the textures of salt, for the different sounds of the roof. There is a sign for *the sound of a crack growing*—the index finger traced slowly up the inside of the forearm. There is a sign for *the hush before a fall*. There is no sign for *shout*; the closest is a sign meaning *to bring the roof down*. For distance they use tap-codes, or small drums of skin stretched over a salt ring. The drums are not loud. They are felt through the stone. On a still day you can sense a tap-pattern running through the city like a message through a nervous system.

By the time the ceiling brightens to its full water-white glow, the people are already moving. They do not gather in crowds. They work in twos and threes, spaced across the flats. At the fungal paddies, men and women kneel on folded hide and turn the pale mats with bone hooks. The mats are warm to the touch, as if the earth is breathing through them. The workers scrape the ripe growth into baskets, then press it into cakes and lay them on racks near the salt-kilns. The smell is clean and faintly sour, like a cellar where bread is stored. In the side-galleries, children pick the pale mushroom-forms by the light that filters through the roof. They work silently, their small fingers knowing which stems to leave.

At the river, the white water runs over a bed of old salt and smooth stone. The people do not fight it. They have built weirs of tufa and salt block that steer the current, not stop it. A water-gate is a slab moved by two or three people using levers of fire-hardened bone. They shift it a handspan and then wait, listening with their feet to the change in the floor. The river is not drunk straight. It is allowed to settle in shallow basins, skimmed, and then carried to the cisterns. The cisterns are deep and bell-shaped, their sides glazed with a natural mineral crust. A person descending to clean one carries no light; the walls themselves give off a faint glow from the fungi in the cracks.

Salt is always growing. In the Needlefield, young men and women work in the early day, before the tall crystals begin to fall. They collect the broken shards and load them onto a sled pulled by a pack-beast. The beast is shaggy and white, with broad padded feet and eyes as pink as a salamander’s. It makes no more noise than a drift of dust. The sled is not wood but hide stretched over a frame of bone, and it slides over the salt with a sound like paper. The blocks are taken to the edge of the city and stacked. A house is not built once. It is assembled, lived in, watched, and then dismantled before the roof can fall. When a wall cracks, no one repairs it. The family simply moves. They take the door—often a slab of salt bound in hide—and the few possessions, and they walk a few hundred feet away. The same stones are carried and re-set in a new order. The house is considered the same house, though it has shifted. The people do not say *home*. They say *the place where our stones are now*.

They eat small meals, often cold: pressed cakes of fungus, dried shrimp from the pools, blind fish roasted over a low smokeless flame. The fish taste of the river—mineral and clean, like licking a stone. The children chew strips of salt-cured mouse. The old people sip a hot infusion made from mineral flowers, which is not tea but a weak brine, and they call it *the warm quiet*. There is no green in their diet, and you can see it in the faint gray of their skin, the slow healing of their cuts. But they are not malnourished. They are made of the same patience as the place.

Family groups are small and loose. A household might be a woman, her sister, their children, and an old man taken in after his own roof fell. The word for family is the same as the word for *those who have shared a wall-fall*. Children are rare, and therefore never scolded for being children; but they are taught early to listen. When a child is born, the parents do not name it at once. They wait a year, to see if it is quiet enough to stay. Then they give it a name that is also a location: *born near the second swallow-hole*, or *first cry after the Needlefield fall*.

The only time they gather is on the council ledge, a broad shelf cut into the west wall where the roof is thick and the river’s sound masks whispers. The council is not a government. It is a group of older people who have lived through many falls and learned to read the ceiling. When a dispute arises—about water, about a wall, about a trade with the surface—they do not debate. They wait. Each person signs slowly, with long pauses between. The eldest, a woman called the Listener, often says nothing at all. At the end she lays her hand flat on the rock, closes her eyes, and tells them what the stone wants. They listen, not because she is in charge, but because she has been right too many times.

The dead are given to the river. The body is wrapped in white fungus-fiber and placed in the white water at the swallow-hole. The current takes it down into the dark. The people say the dead become part of the mineral that will one day rise as a new ceiling. There is no graveyard. Instead, there are small piles of salt at the edge of the swallow-hole, one for each person who has gone. They are worn smooth by the spray.

Not everything that lives in the Underpan is human. The pale mice live in the walls and are tolerated, even fed, because they eat the insects that would otherwise eat the fungus. The blind pink salamanders are kept in cisterns as companions; children name them by touch, not by sight. The white beetles are left to graze; their ticking is one of the few sounds never silenced. The ash-colored birds nest in the ceiling cracks, and their two-note call is so soft the people say it is not a sound but a thought. The pack-beasts are the largest animals, shaggy as old snow, with broad padded feet and eyes reduced to pink slits. They are used to drag cut salt blocks, but not ridden. They make a sound like a person breathing in sleep. The people say the pack-beasts are not animals but slow, four-legged caves that the desert above has forgotten.

The people have no gods, or rather they have one god that is not a person: the Ceiling. It is not worshipped, but listened to. They believe the roof is alive in the same way a sleeping body is alive. A fall is not an accident; it is the roof turning over in its sleep. The river is its blood. The dust devils are its breath. They leave offerings at cracks—a pinch of salt, a sliver of fungus—not to appease, but to acknowledge. They say, *We are the ones who live under the breath of the world.*

At night, when the ceiling goes dark and the fat-lamps are lit in small niches, the people come out more freely. The dark is a relief from the glare. The lamps burn with the rendered fat of blind fish, small and warm, and they make the windows into yellow eyes. The people sit in their low arched rooms, hands moving in the lamplight, telling stories. The stories are not spoken. They are signed and tapped, and they are about the old days when the river ran a different course, about the year the Needlefield grew so tall it touched the roof, about a surface creature that fell through a thin place and had to be taught how to be quiet. The children listen with their fingers on their parents’ hands, so they can feel the story as well as see it. When the lamp burns low, the family lies down on platforms of salt block covered with hide, and the whole city settles into the deepest quiet—the quiet of a place that has learned to live under the weight of the world without waking it.

To an outsider, they might seem like ghosts. But they are not. They are the most solid people you will ever meet: solid as the stone they live with, worn smooth, patient, and full of the knowledge that nothing is meant to last except the act of carrying the stones a little farther. They do not ask you to understand them. They only ask you, by the tilt of a head and a small movement of the hand, to walk softly, listen with your feet, and, if you must speak, do it in a whisper.
