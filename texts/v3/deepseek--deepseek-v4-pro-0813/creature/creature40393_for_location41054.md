Who lives here must be described as carefully as the stone, because they too are being made. They are not a separate thing dropped into the Drown; they are of it, shaped by the same water that shapes the terraces, and they share its unfinished quality. They do not stand outside the world and look at it. They stand inside a white and breathing fog and call it home, and the fog has entered them.

The people of the Drown are not tall, not broad. They are compact, with a wiry density that comes from a life of lifting, scraping, hauling sluice wood, and pulling themselves up the rounded edges of pools. Their skin is pale, but not the pale of a northern winter—it is the pale of a creature that lives its whole life under a shell-colored sky, where the sun is a rumor. It is a skin that has never dried completely. The air itself is a wet hand laid on the cheek; the people have grown used to it the way a fish is used to water. Their skin is often slick with a thin oil pressed from the dark waxy leaves, rubbed on to keep the mineral water from biting too deep. Where the oil is thin, a fine white dust of travertine sometimes clings in the creases of elbows and knuckles, so that an old person’s hands look faintly like stone that has begun to bloom. They do not mind this; they say it is the world recognizing them.

Their hair is cut short or bound close to the head, because long loose hair in the Drown becomes heavy with moisture and begins to feel like rope. Sometimes they weave strands of moss into it, not for decoration but because the moss wicks water away from the skin and keeps the scalp from itching. The moss living in their hair is soft and greenish, and it grows as happily there as on a wall. Children’s hair is often full of it, and they are not scolded for this; the moss is considered clean, a kind of living cloth. Some of the very old have hair that has grown entirely into a mat of moss and white mineral strands, so that if they sit very still against a stone wall, you cannot tell where the person ends and the house begins.

They walk barefoot. Shoes would be useless, would fill with warm water, would grow their own rinds; the people’s feet are broad and tough and pink from the heat of the stone. The soles are thick, not cracked but firm, and they feel the ground as others feel the air. A person from the Drown can stand on a path and tell you, without looking, whether the stone beneath has grown a skin of new lime since yesterday, whether the channel beside it is running slightly too fast, whether a small warm spring is beginning to open under the left heel. Children learn this before they learn to speak. They are set down on the terraces as soon as they can sit, and their hands and feet are left bare so that the world can teach them. The first lesson is this: do not trust any surface to remain the same. A ledge that was safe this morning may have a thin, brittle crust by evening; a knob of stone that seemed solid may be only a wet blister of mineral over a hollow. The people have a particular way of testing the ground before they put their weight on it—a slight drag of the toe, then a pause, then the full step—and this gesture is so ingrained that they do it even on the wooden bridges, where the stone cannot grow.

Their faces are not expressionless, but their expressions are small and slow, as if the muscles were used to moving through water. They smile by narrowing the eyes and tilting the head a little; a full-toothed smile is rare and slightly alarming. They listen more than they look. In the gray-green dusk, eyes are less useful than ears and skin and the memory of terrain. The people can identify each other by footfall on a wet path, by the rhythm of a scraper on stone, by the particular way a person breathes after climbing the terraces. They speak softly, not out of secrecy but because loud speech is swallowed and distorted by the wet air; a raised voice becomes a holler that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. Their language is full of words for water sounds: the hiss of cloud passing over stone, the drip of a single leaf, the thick gurgle of a spring under pressure, the click of a newly formed crust breaking underfoot. They also have many words for degrees of wetness: wet, damp, fresh, weeping, sweating, running, standing, hung. A "dry" surface is one that has gone less than an hour without being re-wetted; the concept of truly dry does not exist in their daily speech, only in stories of the lowlands, which they treat as a kind of fairy tale.

Their clothing is minimal and practical. They wear tunics or short kilts woven from the long, tough fibers of a fern that grows along the warmer channels. The fibers are soaked in leaf oil until they are almost waterproof, then beaten soft. The cloth feels like oiled leather, and it never fully dries either, but it does not rot. In the cooler upper terraces they add a cape of woven moss, which holds warmth and sheds the heaviest mist. The colors are muted: gray-green, brown, the pale chalk of old mineral wash. They do not dye cloth; the dyes would wash out or be overtaken by the white crust. Instead, they sometimes press patterns into the cloth with heated stones, leaving subtle ridges like the growth rings of wood. These patterns are not decorative so much as identifying: each family has a particular ripple or whorl, repeated on the hems, so that a person’s lineage can be read by touch even in the dimmest hollow.

Their tools are few but exact. A stone-gardener carries a wooden mallet, a chisel of black volcanic glass knapped to a fine edge, and at least two scrapers: one wide for planing the fresh paste from a channel wall, one narrow for picking the crust from the corners of a doorway. The scrapers are often made from the ribs of the larger pool creatures, which are wide, slightly curved, and harder than the soft travertine. A rib scraper, carefully oiled and kept from the worst of the mineral water, can last a generation. The wooden mallet is used not to strike the stone but to tap it—gently, questioningly—to find where a crust is hollow or where a fresh deposit has sealed a channel. The people do not break the stone so much as listen to it and then remove only what must be removed. They work with the water, not against it. To fight the stone would be madness; the stone always wins. Instead they persuade, direct, redirect. They build small sluices of split cane to carry water where they want it to lay down its burden. They block a channel here, open a gap there, and the water does the heavy work. A stone-gardener’s true skill is not force but attention: knowing exactly when to move a sluice gate by a thumb’s width, when to fold a new deposit back into the base before it hardens, when to leave a pool alone for a season so that its floor may thicken.

The children of the Drown are allowed to play, but their play is also a kind of learning. They build tiny channels in the soft lime at the edges of the terraces, dam them with pebbles, and watch how the water breaks through or spills over. They make little lattices of twigs and set them under the warm trickles to see how quickly the mineral skin forms. They catch pale shrimp in their cupped hands and then let them go, because the shrimp are not for eating; they are too transparent to offer much nourishment, and besides, the people have no stomach for killing things that glow faintly in the dark. The children learn the names of the different mosses, which ones are soft enough to sleep on, which ones hold too much water, which ones glow at night with a pale greenish light and are used to mark the edges of paths after dark. They learn to walk without scattering the water too loudly, because splashing sounds like distress in the Drown, and it travels. They learn that a fallen leaf in a still pool will become a stone ghost of a leaf, and they sometimes place leaves in rows along a quiet basin, then return week after week to watch them become fossils. This is their entertainment, as absorbing as any game.

The old people are the keepers of long memory, because in a place where the landscape itself forgets its own edges, memory is a kind of architecture. The elders remember which mound was once a house with three rooms, which narrow passage was once a main street, which pool was once a cistern shared by six families before the stone closed over it and made it a smooth basin. They do not write these things down; writing would wash away or become buried under a rind. Instead they recite them in a low, rhythmic murmur, sometimes accompanied by the drip of water from a particular stone edge, so that the memory and the sound become one. The community’s history is not a chronicle of battles and kings but a history of watercourses and walls: when the lower sluice was widened, when the north terrace overflowed and had to be re-channeled, when the great cistern grew so thick that its rim had to be scraped for forty days. The elders can tell you the exact year by the thickness of a particular crust, the way an old tree records years in rings.

They do not build monuments. The idea of a monument—a thing meant to stand forever unchanged—is foreign to them. The closest they come is the practice of “giving to the stone.” When a person dies, the body is not buried in the ground, because the ground is not patient enough to hold a grave; it would fill the hole with new rock before the season turned, and the body would be crushed and squeezed and eventually become part of the terrace, without ceremony. Instead, the dead are given a gentler return. The body is laid in a quiet, warm pool, not too deep, where the water is still and the mineral content is high. The family places a small lattice of reeds over the pool, just below the surface, to shape the first deposits. Then they wait. Over months, the body is covered in a thin white crust, then a thicker skin, then a blanket of stone. The features soften, then vanish. The shape of the person remains for a while, like a sleeping figure under a sheet of ice, and then it too becomes a mound, a ripple, a smooth rise in the pool floor. The family may continue to tend the pool, scraping the edges to keep a faint suggestion of the body visible, but eventually they let it go. The person has become part of the Drown. They say, “She has gone into the wall,” or “He is the floor now.” It is not a sad saying. It is the same thing they say about a house that has forgotten its door.

Marriage, or the joining of households, is not a ceremony of exchange but of architecture. Two people who wish to live together make a doorway. They choose a shared wall, or they grow a new wall from a lattice, and they open a passage between their dwellings. The opening is made slowly, over many days, because they must direct the water away from the edges and scrape back the fresh crust as it forms, until the passage is stable. The making of the door is the wedding. The families stand on either side and hand tools back and forth, and there is singing—not loud singing, but a murmuring hum that blends with the hiss of the Drown. When the doorway is finished, the couple passes through it, and the two houses are one. Divorce, when it happens, is also architectural: the door is allowed to close. The couple stops scraping it. Within a year, the opening is a shallow niche; within five, a faint scar; within ten, there is only wall. The people say, “They let the door heal.” It is not a metaphor.

The town has no government in the dry sense, no chief or council with the power to command. But it has a rhythm of collective work, and out of that rhythm comes a kind of order. Each morning, after the first light strengthens, the adults and older children take up their scrapers and go to their assigned lengths of channel, their stretches of path, their corner of the cistern. This is not private property but shared duty. A person who neglects their stretch finds that the stone grows over it, and soon there is no path there at all, or a wall where a doorway used to be, and the neighbors’ doorways begin to narrow as well. So the work is both independence and interdependence. Disputes are rare, but when they occur, they are settled by the slow pressure of the stone itself: two families who quarrel over the flow of a sluice are made to manage the sluice together for a full cycle of seasons. If they cannot, the sluice blocks, and both families lose their warm water, and the lesson is learned. The people are patient; they can afford to be patient, because the stone is patient and will outlast any argument.

The few animals of the Drown are treated with a kind of quiet respect, not as pets or prey but as fellow inhabitants of the unfinished world. The pale shrimp are watched but not harvested. The soft-bodied grazers on the leaves are sometimes collected—not to eat, but because their slime, mixed with wood ash from the rare fires, makes a fine glue for repairing cracked lattice. The gray moths that gather at the cisterns at dimmest hour are welcomed; their wings are wet-looking and they make no sound, and the people believe they carry the memory of the dead. When a moth settles on a person’s hand, it is a sign that an ancestor is near, and the person will stand very still and not brush it away. The larger thing that is sometimes glimpsed at the edge of the mist—long and low, moving with too many legs—is not spoken of directly. It is called “the other gardener” or “the thing that scrapes at night.” Some say it is a creature, some say it is only a shadow thrown by a shifting stone. The people do not hunt it, do not pursue it. They simply listen, and the hiss of the Drown covers whatever sound it makes.

At night, which is only a thickening of the gray, the town settles into a state of soft attention. The fires in the stone bowls are lit, and the pale green phosphorescence of the wet mosses begins to glow along the path edges and the rims of the cisterns. The people gather on the wide terraces outside their doorways, not for any formal meeting but for the quiet comfort of presence. They eat the evening meal—bundles of soft root and moss cooked in the hot pools, wrapped in broad leaves, eaten with the fingers. They drink the warm mineral water, which tastes faintly of stone and has a bitter edge that they have come to love. They talk, but little; they listen to the drip and the hiss and the low moan of a rope bridge somewhere. A child may ask why the cloud never lifts, and an elder will answer, “Because it is still being poured.” The child does not ask what is being poured. The child already knows: the world. The Drown. Themselves.

They sleep on mats of dried moss, which are not truly dry but only less wet, and they wake often in the night to the sound of a new channel opening or a thin crust breaking. They do not mind. To wake to the sound of the world being made is not an interruption; it is the rhythm of existence. They lie in the dark with their eyes open and listen to the water laying down its patient stone, and they know that in the morning they will go out with their scrapers and their wooden mallets and continue the long, quiet work of keeping a little space open against the slow determination of the world to close it all in stone. They are not fighting the world. They are part of it. They are the ones who scrape so that there may be doorways, the ones who shape the water so that there may be rooms, the ones who give their dead to the pools so that there may be hills. They are the gardeners of the unfinished, and they are unafraid.

If you were to meet one of them, a person of the Drown, on a narrow stone path where the mist hangs like a curtain, you might not at first be sure if you were seeing a person or a shadow or a slow growth of moss on a wall. They would not startle you with a loud greeting. They would pause, and they would look at you with the pale, dark-adapted eyes of one who has learned to see in the inside of a shell. They would notice, perhaps, that you are dry; they would feel the absence of the Drown on your skin as a kind of hollowness. And then they would speak, in a voice soft as wet stone, and they would say the only rule that matters in their country, the rule they repeat to every stranger:

“Do not trust the ground to stay where it is.”

And then they would turn and go on their way, disappearing into the gray-green air, and you would be left with the hiss of the Drown and the faint, patient sound of a scraper on stone, as the world continued to be made.
