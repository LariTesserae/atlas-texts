The ones who live there are not easy to see at first. The towers seem empty, their dark surfaces glistening with condensation, their lower doors no more than faint seams in the rock. The land between them is so wide, so water-stained and raw, that it seems no one could live there—only the fog moving in slow herds, the insects rising at noon, the gray birds wheeling under the clouds.

But then you notice a figure standing on a high ledge of a tower, just at the edge of the mist. It is tall, very tall, and thin in a way that seems less like a body and more like a shadow that has learned to hold itself upright. The figure is wrapped in something dark that is not cloth, not armor, but a kind of grown skin—like the tower’s black ceramic softened into flexible folds. Water beads on it, runs off in silver threads. The face is narrow, pale as cloud-veiled stone, and the eyes are the color of wet basalt: dark, but with a film of light when the fog shifts. They blink slowly, and a second, transparent eyelid slides sideways across the eye, clearing away moisture. The hands are long, gray, with knuckles ridged like the stone after the first freeze.

This is one of them. But not the only one.

There are many kinds of life in that place, and the tall ones are only the most visible. The towers themselves are alive, or something close to alive. They flex in the wind, repair their own cracks, remember the people who dwell in them. Each tower has a slow, infrasonic voice that travels through the floor and up through the soles of your feet. The inhabitants do not think of the towers as buildings. They call them *the listeners*, because the towers hear the stone settling, the water seeping, the ice cracking far below the plateau. To live in a tower is not to own it; it is to be spoken to, to be held in a mouth of warm black material that breathes.

The tall ones call themselves *the Keeper of the Drip*, or sometimes *the Ones Who Wait for the Thaw*. They are human, or they were once, before the plateau changed them. Their lungs are huge, built for thin high-altitude air. Their blood carries more oxygen. Their skin is thick, waxy, able to shed water instantly, and beneath it there is a fine down like the underfur of a stoat, so that the cold near freezing does not shake them. Their feet are broad and splayed, the pads rough to grip wet stone. Their ears are small, set close to the head, but they can hear a pebble fall from a cliff a kilometer away, can distinguish the sound of freeze from the sound of thaw. Their hair is pale, almost white, and it grows in fine grooves that channel the drizzle away from their eyes, like the condenser fins on the towers. They move slowly, not from weakness but from knowledge: in a land where every rock is wet and every scarp is raw, haste cracks the ice, slips on the stone, burns heat. They are patient the way water is patient.

Inside the towers, the walls are not flat. They curve and swell, following the flex of the building. Light comes from strips of bioluminescent tissue in the walls, soft and bluish, as if the tower itself is dreaming light. The floors are warm in some places, cool in others. There are no doors; openings dilate when a person approaches, then close behind, sealing out the fog. Water runs in channels along the walls, sometimes falling in thin curtains. The air smells of clean stone and living plants, with a faint iron tang where the rock rusts and a green, sour edge near the aeroponic galleries. The people sleep in alcoves that close around them like a mouth, and they store food in hexagonal niches that the tower grows for them. Each tower knows its inhabitants by the heat of their bodies and the rhythm of their steps.

Their food is built on water and stone, not on forests. They eat the living film that grows on the wet rock—the green-black sheen of cyanobacteria and algae that thrives in the daily freeze-thaw. They scrape it off in thin sheets with blades of black glass, press it into flat cakes, store it in stone jars. They grow leafy greens and root-like vegetables in the aeroponic galleries, their roots hanging in mist instead of soil. They raise insects in vertical farms, and at noon, when the wild swarms rise in their millions, they go out with fine nets and catch enough for the day. They hunt the small grazers—the ones like pikas and ptarmigan—but rarely. Meat is a feast, not a meal. They do not eat the birds. The birds are messengers of weather; to harm one is to call bad fog, and so the gray flocks nest unmolested under the tower ledges, wheeling and folding in sheets of pale sound.

The inhabitants do not live crowded together. A tower may hold a single person, or a family of three or five, but never more. The land between towers is left empty, not as a void but as a lung. The city breathes through that emptiness. The people move from tower to tower as the seeps shift, following the water's indecision. A family might live in one tower for a year, then leave it empty for a decade. The empty tower is not abandoned; it is resting, drinking the fog, dreaming. When the people return, they find the rooms rearranged, the water tanks full, the walls polished. The tower has been preparing for them.

They travel in capsules that pass through the air with a soft sigh, a pulse felt in the soles of the feet. Or they walk, barefoot or in thin shoes of grown ceramic, across the kilometers of stone and fog. They walk slowly, and they listen. The land speaks to them constantly: the tick and drip of water, the click of cooling rock, the deep far-off boom as a fresh joint opens. They can read the moisture on a cliff face, the color of a moss mat, the way a flock of birds turns. They know when a basin is about to overflow or a scarp is about to crack. They do not try to stop it. They move out of the way.

Their language is slow and musical, full of words for water. They have a word for the first meltwater trickle of morning: *selu*. It also means “beginning.” They have a word for the way a tower sways in the wind: *tahm*. It also means “hope.” They have no word for loneliness in the human sense. The closest is *myr*, which means “the sound of one’s own breath in a cold room,” but it is not sad. It is the sound of being alive. They speak in low resonant tones that carry through the fog, and in close quarters they use a sign language of small hand gestures. Their writing is not ink but patterns of raised lines on black ceramic tablets, read with the fingertips. Their records are kept not in books but in the walls of the towers, which absorb and store the patterns. To remember something, they touch a wall and hum a question. The tower answers in a low vibration.

They are not alone in that landscape. They share it with the insects, the birds, the grazers, the stoat-like hunters that slip through the rock clefts. They share it with the microscopic film that makes the stone nutritious. They do not think of themselves as rulers, or even as the most important creatures. They have a word, *kthe*, that means “all that drinks.” It includes themselves, the birds, the stone, the tower, the fog. They greet every living thing with a small bow and a slow blink of the second eyelid. The stone weeps for them. The tower hums. The birds wheel. It is a kind of conversation.

There is a woman named Ith—or maybe that is not a name but the sound of water falling from a ledge. She lives alone in a tower near the largest silver pan, where the fog comes down in dense herds at dusk. She is tall, long-limbed, with hair almost white and skin the color of dry lichen. Her clothes are grown from the tower’s black ceramic: a sleeveless overlay that hardens into scales at night and softens in the day’s melt. She goes barefoot so she can feel the tower’s voice through the floor. In the morning, before the thaw, she climbs to the topmost ledge and scrapes the ice from the condenser fins with a blade of black glass. She drinks the first melt directly from a groove in the wall. She checks the aeroponic galleries, where the leaf-greens breathe the water the tower exhales. At noon, when the insects rise, she goes out with a fine net and catches enough for the day. She does not like to kill, but she thanks the swarm. At dusk, she sits on the ledge and watches the gray birds cross between towers. She sings a low hum that matches the tower’s wind-chord. The tower hums back. She has not spoken to another person in several seasons, but she is not lonely. She has the water, the stone, the birds, and the slow pulse of the city under her feet.

There is a boy, too. He is still growing, not yet as tall as the others. His eyes are so pale they seem silver. He is learning the names of the seeps. Every day at noon, he goes out with a jar and a scraper and collects the film from the rocks near his family’s tower. He talks to the grazers; they do not run from him. A small stoat-like creature follows him, a pale hunter that steals insects from his jar. He calls it *Frost*, or rather a soft clicking sound that means the kind of frost that forms on the inside of a breathing tube. At dusk, he sits with Ith on the ledge and listens to her singing. He asks questions with his hands, because the wind is too loud. She answers with a gesture that means: *wait, and the water will show you*. He is patient. He is learning to hear the tower’s voice through his feet.

When the tall ones die, they do not bury their dead. They wash the body with meltwater, wrap it in fog-mesh, and place it on a high rock shelf where the freeze and thaw can work. Over many seasons, the body becomes part of the stone, stained by the same green-black film. The family returns each year to touch the rock and listen. The person is not gone; they have become the place. The tower remembers them too, and sometimes it sings their song in the wind. In this way, the city is full of the dead, and the dead are not dead, but water and stone and memory.

Their rules are few, but they are as binding as water tension. *Do not dam the water*—do not try to stop the flow of anything, whether water, time, people, or stories. *Listen to the stone*—when a dispute arises, they sit together in a small basin, barefoot on the wet rock, and do not speak until the stone has cooled or warmed. The stone decides; they only listen. *Leave the empty spaces empty*—because the water has not yet decided where it wants to go, and to build there is to argue with the future.

At dusk, when the fog comes down and the towers begin to gleam with condensation, the plateau looks less like a landscape than like something being poured. The inhabitants do not watch from behind windows. They stand outside, on ledges and on the wet stone, and they let the fog settle on them. They are part of the pouring. A flock of gray birds crosses the gap between two towers, and for a moment the only sound is their wings and the drip of water from a thousand stone tears. The cold comes, and the place freezes quiet. The inhabitants return to their towers, which close around them like mouths. They sleep in slings of woven mesh, lulled by the drip of condensation. Their dreams are made of water. They dream of rivers that do not yet exist.

And in the morning, before the thaw, they wake and listen. The ice cracks. The rock ticks. The water begins to move. The city breathes. The ones who live there are not hidden. They are the tall figures on the ledges, the small tracks in the frost, the silver fins drinking the fog. They are the ones who do not ask *who lives here?* because the place is not a place to be inhabited. It is a body. And they are its breath.
