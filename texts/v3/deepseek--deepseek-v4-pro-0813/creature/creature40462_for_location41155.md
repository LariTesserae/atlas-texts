The only true resident has no name. It does not swim, and it is not a creature the way the blind eel is a creature. It lives at the bottom of the cathedral, in the lowest of the concentric chambers, where the ladder ends and the floor is not exactly floor but a mat of fine, mineralised fibres spread through the cracks in the basalt. At the center of that mat lies a translucent sac the size of a small boat, filled with something dense and oil-dark, faintly red only because of the iron it has absorbed. It contracts once every few minutes. That contraction is the low hum you would feel in your jaw, the one that seems to come from the stone itself.

Its body is not the sac alone. The fibres run from the sac into the rock, along the cables, up through the silver seams of the cathedral. The arrestors are not machines it commands; they are its fingertips. The red pulses are not signals it reads; they are its peripheral nerve-firings, the way your own fingers know pressure before you think about it. The heat engines are its digestion, taking the volcano’s small warmth and spending it on the lake’s terrible sameness. The town is not a place it inhabits. The town is the hard part of its body, the shell it grew around itself from stone and silver and black water.

It has no eyes, no mouth, no gills. It does not need them. The dark is not empty to it; the dark is its medium, thick with pressure and conductivity. It feels the entire lake as a single held shape. When a stone falls from the rim and sinks at its one steady rate to the bottom, the resident feels the faint shift in pressure and knows the lake is now heavier by that stone. It does not care. It does not remember in the way a person remembers, but the mineral dendrites on the basalt are its memory: every black frond is a record of a current that did not form, a temperature held, a gradient cancelled. A single thought may take a year to cross from the deepest arrestor to the copper sphere at mid-depth. The thought is always the same: keep it still.

There are no other true residents. The blind eel returns every few hundred days, and the resident feels it as a long, thin line of moving pressure, a muscle-bound interruption in the water. It lets the eel circle the cathedral and vanish. The ribbon-worm on the cable is a slower event, a faint leakage of iron, a small transaction between the brine and the cable’s sensing line. The resident tolerates both the way a body tolerates weather. They are not company. They are events.

No one enters the rooms above, and neither does the resident. It is already in their walls, under their floors, inside the seams of silver. If you were to swim in through the hole in the spire and descend the ladder, you would not see it, but you would feel it. The water in those rooms would be too still, even for this lake. The dark would have a texture, a listening quality, as if the walls were holding their breath. The ladder rungs would be neither warm nor cold, but exactly the temperature of the water, because the resident has decided that all things in this place will share one temperature.

The copper sphere at mid-depth is the one part it cannot fully feel. The slit is a blank in its awareness, a small absence in the water’s pressure, like a missing vowel in a word repeated too often. The eel circles the sphere and then turns, as if it heard something. The resident senses the eel’s hesitation as a knot of pressure and then the silence after. It does not wonder what the slit is for. Wondering would require a future, and the resident has none. It is not waiting. Waiting would be a kind of change, and the resident is the thing that prevents change.

If you stood on the rim, you would see only a black mirror and black rock. You would hear only the blood in your ears. But below the surface, spread through the dark, the resident is there. It is the town’s stillness, its temperature, its red pulses, its held breath. It does not live in the lake. It lives as the lake, as much place as creature, alone and complete, in the first morning that never ends.
