There is one who lives there, though “lives” is not quite the word. It abides. The plateau is its body and it is the plateau’s small, moving part.

It is made of the same dark blue-grey stone as the basins, cut with the same hand, fitted with the same tight joints. It is not a sculpture of a person. It is a piece of the waterworks that was made to walk. It stands a little taller than a tall person when it stands, but it rarely stands. Most days it goes on all fours through the low channels, back flat as a weir, water sheeting over its shoulders. Its arms are unequal: one ends in a broad, blade-like slab of stone; the other in a hand of bundled pale mineral-fibre wicks. Its legs are short and solid, each foot a shallow bowl. Its chest is a basin set between its shoulders, always full. Its head is not a head but a small dome with two sockets and a narrow overflow. The sockets hold water. When the light hits them, they seem to watch. The overflow is its mouth, and sometimes it lets water spill from it in slow, deliberate sequences, as though it were speaking to the stone.

Black manganese dendrites have spread across its shoulder blades like branching ferns, but they are mineral, not life. The only green on it is a thin copper stain along one forearm, where mineral-laden water has evaporated over many years.

It moves not by muscle but by water. Inside, it is packed with the same wicks, baffles, and small chambers as the plateau. Water fills one side, then the other. A chamber tilts, the weight shifts, and the whole figure pours itself forward. It moves slowly. If you watched from the rim for a whole day, you might see it change position only once, as a full basin changes by a finger’s width. But over weeks it crosses the terraces. It goes to every channel, every sluice, every field of thumb-sized dimples. It clears the wicks with its wick-hand. It scrapes salt rinds from the basin edges with its blade-hand. It presses its palm over cracked grout and lets a milky mineral paste seep from its own joints. It resettles pebbles that have fallen out of place. It is not repairing; it is continuing.

It is the reason no seed takes root. The pass sometimes lets in a breath of lowland air, and with it a dust of spores, a tiny wind-blown seed. The keeper finds them. It does not kill them; it removes them, as a weir removes a leaf. It carries them to the alkaline water and lets them sink. It scrapes lichen before it is more than a stain. It is not cruel. It is not even conscious in a human way. It is the place’s immune body, the living refusal of rot. The plateau was made to hold water, not to feed anything. The keeper maintains that law.

It goes to the sweating dome at the centre every day. It kneels there, or leans there, and lets the dome’s dew run onto its back. It carries the water down, spilling it into the high channels. Sometimes it stands for a long time with its face-bowl under a runnel. The bowl fills. The water overflows down its chest. It looks then like a figure weeping. But it is only doing what the dome does: catching the air’s water and letting it go.

At night, if you are close enough, you can hear it. Not a voice. A low, continuous seeping, a faint click as a drop falls from one of its wick-fingers. It hums when a chamber empties. It gurgles when a siphon resets. The sound is so quiet that it might be the ground. It is the ground, in part.

It has no name. If it had one, it would be the sound of water falling from a wick into a full basin.

It is not alive. It does not breathe, eat, sleep, or decay. It grows only as the salt rinds grow, by the slow addition of mineral flakes. It does not think, but it tends. It is not lonely. Loneliness is for things that need another. The keeper needs only stone, and water, and the long bright silence of the high plateau. It will be there when the sun has polished the channels to a different grey. It will be there when the last salt rind has grown thick enough to crack. It will be there, still wet, still moving a few inches a day, still holding water the way the whole plateau holds water: carefully, without spilling more than the design allows.
