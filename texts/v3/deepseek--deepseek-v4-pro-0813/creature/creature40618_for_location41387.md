At first they do not look separate from the place. They move along the terraces with the same unhurried economy as the vines. A shadow detaches from a doorway, pauses at a ledge, and becomes a person. They are small—not childlike, but compressed, as if the crater has shaped them the way it shapes the stone. Their shoulders are narrow, their hips narrow, their feet broad and long-toed, with toes that grip the worn ladder-rungs. Their skin is the color of old mud plaster, darkened by smoke and dust and oil, and it is painted in fine clay lines that change with the season and the work: spirals for the cistern-keepers, long strokes for the rope-wrights, a dotted branch for those who tend the fog-trees. In the dim green light they look like pieces of the wall that have learned to move.

They do not strut. Every motion is placed, because space is short and the drop below is long. They pass each other on ledges by turning sideways in a practiced, intimate way, one hand touching the other’s shoulder, not for balance but for the body’s memory of where the other was. They speak close to the ear, in a language of low clicks, hums, and dry fricatives, under the dense noise of wild things. Their voices are not separate from the crater’s sound; they fit into its gaps like stones into a wall.

Their clothing is made from the same materials as the crater. They wear skirts of knotted bark-fiber, capes of grazer-wool felted with their own hair, belts of plaited insect silk. The edges of their capes are fringed to hold dew; at dawn the fringes are heavy, and they suck them as they work. Their skin is coated with a thin paste of clay and oil, which keeps the dry air from stealing too much moisture from the flesh. The paste is reapplied each morning with water that has already been used to rinse leaves. It is not waste; it is a second skin.

Their hands are the most distinctive part. The fingers are long, the first and second longer than the rest, the knuckles enlarged from gripping wood and stone. The pads are thick and dry. They can pick up a single bead of water from a leaf without breaking it. A child practices this before learning to speak, sitting on a terrace with a waxy leaf and a steady hand.

The day begins before the fog. A woman on an upper terrace opens a shutter and stands with her face turned toward the rim. Her hair is short and oiled into a dark cap. She breathes slowly through her nose, tasting the air. Then she taps a hollow stick twice against the stone. The sound travels down the wall. Shutters open, ropes uncoil, clay pots are unstoppered. The crater begins to wake.

The woman is a fog-waker. She has done this for so many seasons that the skin of her palms is as hard as the stone. She climbs to the rim with a long wooden horn under one arm. At the rim she sits in the mineral dust and waits. The cold air of the high plain slides over the edge. She holds the horn into it. The horn’s wide mouth gathers the moisture, and a thin trickle runs down its groove into a cup no bigger than a thumb. When the cup is full, she drinks it and turns to face the settlement. She opens both arms, then closes them slowly, like a mouth. The signal means: the fog is coming.

Down in the terraces, children trot along the ledges with covered pots on their backs. They are quiet except for the slap of their bare feet. They stop at every stone gutter, every waxy leaf, every hollow in the rock. They coax the dew with small curved sticks into their pots. Older children climb into the fog-trees and shake the leaves gently over stretched cloths. The water that falls is brownish, full of pollen and dust, but they pour it into covered jars without spilling a drop. An old man works a rope that lowers a basket from an upper ledge. He hums a low note, and the basket comes back up full of wet moss. He squeezes the moss into a clay basin, then lays the moss in a wooden frame to dry. His hands move with the certainty of a prayer.

In the evening the whole crater receives the fog together. The people come out onto the ledges with open hands. They do not sing, or if they sing, it is low and wordless. The fog rolls down the terraces, and for a while every person is beaded, every animal’s back is slick, every leaf is shining. Then they begin again: the cloths, the pots, the cupped hands, the slow work of carrying the sky’s water down into the dark.

They call the crater *the throat*—not a throat that speaks, but a throat that drinks. The rim is the lip, the terraces are the palate, the deep floor is the belly, and the warm air rising is the breath. They are the hands that carry the water from the lip to the belly. That is the whole of their law.

They have no true word for rain. If a trader describes it, they listen with the same polite blankness with which they might hear of a river that flows upward. They call rain *the drunk sky* or *water that falls without being asked*. They have, instead, dozens of names for fog: the white net, the slow wave, the breathing stone, the visitor who leaves by noon, the wet sleeve, the cold hand on the neck. They greet the fog as it rolls over the rim: *You have come. Sit. There is not much, but there is enough.*

Their water is not stored in one place. It is scattered through the crater in buried cisterns, in the swollen trunks of the fog-trees, in the flesh of the animals, in the clay of their own bodies. To be a person is to be a kind of cistern. They do not forget this.

They have no king and no army. The closest thing to a ruler is the keeper of the Ledger, an old person of any sex who sits in a deep room near the warmest vent and keeps a set of knotted cords. Each knot is a task done or a share of water owed. The Ledger-Keeper does not give orders. They only remember. If two people dispute a share, they sit in the Ledger-Room and tell the history of every drop, and the Keeper runs the cords through their fingers until the right memory surfaces.

Names are not fixed. A child is called Small-Noise or Dust-Foot or One-Who-Falls-But-Does-Not-Spill. When they take on a work, they earn a work-name: Fog-Waker, Leaf-Turner, Cistern-Ear, Rope-Hand, Bird-Caller, Lamp-Tender, Stone-Breath-Listener. Some keep these names until death. Others are renamed many times. The oldest names repeat across generations, so that there has always been a Two-Hands-in-Leaves and a Hears-the-Stone, and the crater sometimes seems to be worked by one continuous person.

The people are not the only ones who live here. The woolly grazers are more like dust-colored mounds than animals until they lift their heads. Their fleece is not soft but wiry, and every strand ends in a tiny hook that catches moisture from the air. When they move through the lower vegetation, their flanks come away wet. The people do not shear them for cloth; they comb the loose wool with bone combs and press the water from the combed clumps into jars. A grazer is a walking cistern.

The flightless birds are fat and bald-necked, with skin that flushes pink when they are excited. They are kept on the dark, warm lower ledges, where their feathers grow long and oily. They lay eggs with shells the color of old teeth. The people eat the eggs but never throw away the shells; the shells are ground to powder and mixed with plaster to seal cistern walls. The birds are stupid and loud, but they can smell moisture more sharply than any person. In the dry season, the people follow them down into the deepest cracks, where the stone’s breath condenses in hidden seams.

The insects are the most numerous, and the people know it. They keep them in woven cages hung from the beams. The insects spin cocoons of pale silk, which the people unwind into thread. At night, when the wild animals take over the sound, the domestic insects tick in their cages like tiny hearts. The people say that if the insects ever stopped ticking, the crater would stop too.

The wild animals are neighbors, not background. The broad-backed browsers tear at the succulent leaves with flat grinding mouths, and the people know individual animals by the pattern of missing leaf-tips. The pale-eyed predator is seen only as a change in the leaves. It is long and low, and it hunts the browsers. The people do not kill it, because when it makes a kill, the water in the prey is returned to the crater through blood and breath. They say it is only the fog, taking back what the fog gave.

They are not gentle. They are economical. There is no room for waste, and there is no patience for the clumsy. A person who leaves a water jar uncovered is made to sit with an empty cup on the sunniest ledge for a day. But the others bring the offender a wet cloth to suck on at dusk, because punishment that wastes water is also waste. A person who kills a fog-tree without planting another is exiled to the plateau above for three days. Most survive, but they return with cracked lips and a new understanding of the word *thirst*, and they are welcomed back with a cup of water and a long silence.

They do not fight with metal or with fists. They fight with words, in a formal way, before the Ledger-Keeper and three witnesses. Each person is allowed one cup of water to hold while they speak. If they raise their voice, the water shakes; if it spills, the argument is lost. This makes them speak very carefully.

They eat the eggs of the flightless birds, the flesh of the grazers when they die, the small sweet tubers that grow in the litter, and the insects, which they roast over warm stones. They drink the milk of the grazers only at certain times, because the milk is mostly water and must be accounted for. They chew the waxy leaves of the fog-trees for the moisture and the salt. Their food is not abundant, but it is enough.

They do not measure time in hours. They measure it in fogs and dry spells, in the interval between a leaf’s first furling and its water-drop, in the life of an insect cage. The past is not a line; it is a set of terraces going down into the dark.

At night, the crater does not sleep. The people retreat into their low rooms, but the walls remain alive with wings and chittering. The lamps are lit—small dish lamps burning seed oil, with wicks of twisted moss. The light does not reach far. It catches the underside of a jaw, the curve of a water pot, the gleam of an obsidian blade being resharpened. A mother sits in a doorway with her child, pointing up at the rim, where the last light has turned the dust haze the color of old bronze. She tells the child not a story but a list: *The fog came late. The cistern is full. The tree by the fifth ledge is rooting new leaves. Tomorrow we will check the gutters.* The child repeats it back. This is how history is kept.

Then they sleep, but not all at once. A woman walks the terraces with a small lamp, touching the stone gutters, clearing dust from the leaf-lined channels, listening. She is the Night-Ear. She hears the water moving through the rock, the slow drip of condensation, the ticking of insects. She is still walking when the first bird calls, and the cycle begins again.

The people are not merely residents. They are the latest expression of the crater’s long patience. The crater has shaped them as surely as it has shaped the stone. Their foot is broad and silent; their ear is tuned to the sound of a drop; their mind runs in cycles of fog and dry heat. They do not imagine themselves as separate from the crater. When they say *we*, they mean the walls, the leaves, the animals, the fog, the hidden warmth. The crater is not a place where they live. It is what they are.
