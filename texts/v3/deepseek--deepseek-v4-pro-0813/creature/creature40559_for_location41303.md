The ones who live in the Works are not the first thing you notice. The first thing is the dust, then the rust-mats, then the salt-oxen folded into the distance like boulders that have forgotten to move. But then you see a figure on a graded lot, standing so still that the fine salt powder has begun to settle on its shoulders and the brim of its hood. It might be a pylon, or a spoil heap, or a wind-carved stone. Then it moves—one arm, very slowly, to adjust a valve on a standpipe—and you understand that it is a person.

They are not human, though they are people. They call themselves the Slow, or the Keepers, or sometimes simply the ones who wait. They have been on the Salt Bench so long that their bodies have taken on the logic of the place. They contain no water. If you were to cut one—and it would take a great deal of effort, for their skins are hard as packed halite—no clear liquid would run. A slow amber oil would well up instead, smelling faintly of hot metal and sulfur. Their tissues are fibrous and mineralized, their blood a translucent oil that does not evaporate. They do not pant or sweat. Their breath is a dry exhalation, warm and faintly sharp, like the air near a refinery. Their eyes, when they turn them toward you, are lenses of salt crystal or silica, with a faint iris of copper oxide. They do not blink often. Their mouths are wide and flat, with grinding plates instead of teeth, suited to the rust-mats and the grit-mites they eat.

Their bodies are built for stillness. They are tall, slightly stooped, with joints that look like knobs of fused silica. Their skin is fissured into polygons, a mirror of the salt pan underfoot, and dust gathers in the cracks. They have no hair; instead, a crest of fine mineral fibers runs along the skull and down the back, and this crest stirs when the wind shifts, so that they know the weather without looking up. They radiate heat not through sweat but through thin vanes under the arms and along the ribs, which open and close with a faint clicking sound, like the cooling fins of an electrical substation. When they stand still, they can stand for hours without fatigue, and often do. The wind feathers the edges of their robes, but they do not sway.

They dress in long coats of woven mineral fiber, the color of the dust, the color of the ground, the color of everything. The cloth is not spun from plants, for there are no plants. It is made from the fibrous flesh of salt-oxen, or from extruded silica threads, or from the fine mycelium of the rust-mats themselves, pressed and cured. Their hoods have narrow slits fitted with lenses of mica, to keep the dust from their eyes without narrowing their vision. Their shoes are wide and flat, or they go barefoot, their soles thick and cracked like old gypsum. They carry tools: long-handled scrapers for harvesting the rust-mats, probes for testing the leachates, jars of fused silica for carrying the hot mineral fluids they drink. Some carry hammers with heads of sintered iron, and these they use sparingly, as if each blow were a word in a language that should not be wasted.

Their food is the food of the Bench. They eat flat cakes of pressed rust-mats, seasoned with iron salts and the pale gold crust of the slow-fires. They eat stews of grit-mites, which are so abundant that a single sweep of a fine net can gather thousands. They eat the flesh of salt-oxen, but only when an animal has died of age or accident; they do not kill quickly, for quickness is not in their nature. Their drink is the dense anhydrous leachate that rises from deep beds, hot and sluggish, darkening the salt to brown and green. They tap it from standpipes and sip it from cups of fused salt, and it tastes of iron and sulfur and the deep heat of the world. They do not drink water. They know water as a mineral, a rare crystal locked in gypsum and clay, and they treat it with the respect one might give a dangerous curiosity. In their oldest stories, water is a substance that falls from the sky, an impossible event that would soften the world and wash the dust away. They call it the wet death, or the soft fall, and they do not wish for it.

Their work is the Work. The Works is a city in the way a railway is a city—linear, sparse, industrial—and the people are its keepers. They run the refineries that crush and leach and electrolyze the salts, extracting metals and ceramics. They tend the wind turbines that turn lazily on the rim, and the geothermal plants that tap the hot mineral fluids. They walk the rail lines and listen to the creak of the rails. They clear the dust from the switches and the signal lights. They watch the dials and the gauges, and they adjust the valves with a patience that seems to have no bottom. The machines are enclosed in sound-baffled sheds; their noise is a low thrum, and the people move through the refineries in that thrum the way fish move through water. They do not rush. A shift may last a full day-night cycle, and a repair may take a week, because the thing that is broken has been broken before and will be broken again, and the way to fix it is to fix it slowly, so that it stays fixed for a generation.

They live in housing blocks with small sealed courtyards, the lower floors half-buried in windblown dust. The walls are of sintered salt-brick and corrugated steel, colored by the local minerals: pale tan from halite, red from iron oxides, green from copper stains. Nothing is painted. Inside, the courtyards are the only green the people know, though it is not the green of leaves. It is the maroon and black and pale gold of the rust-mats, grown in long trays and low mounds, tended with the same patience as everything else. A courtyard may have a slow-fire at its center, a mound thousands of years old, its surface convoluted like an old brain, and the family will eat their meals around it, and the children will be taught to touch it only with the backs of their hands, gently, as if touching the face of a grandfather. There may be a small fountain of leachate in the corner, warm and sluggish, where grit-mites gather in their thousands, and the sound of their movement is like fine sand sliding. The rooms are lit by salt-crystal lamps that give a pale, steady light, and the air is kept free of dust by heavy cloth filters and airlocks. It is not uncomfortable. It is a kind of quiet that has its own texture, like the inside of a shell.

Their speech is not like ours. The air is heavy, and sound does not travel far or fast; a hammer blow a kilometer away might arrive as a soft click. So the people speak in a language of low tones and clicks that travel through the ground more than the air. They have sensory pits on their soles and palms, and they can hear a message tapped on a rail line from a great distance. Their conversations are long and full of silences. A greeting might be a single click, answered after a long pause. A question might be followed by a silence of several minutes, and the answer, when it comes, is considered more carefully for the wait. They do not interrupt. Interruption is a kind of violence. They have names, but the names are not fixed; they are titles of work and place. A person might be called Rail-walker, or Leachate-tapper, or Dust-reader, or Slow-fire Tender, or the one who listens to the pipes. When a child is born, it is given a temporary name—Small-voice, or New-step—and only later, when it has taken on a role, does it earn a true name. Some die with their temporary names still on them, and this is not a tragedy. It is only a way of saying they were still becoming.

Children are rare. The people reproduce slowly, as everything does here. A child is small, with softer plates than an adult, and for the first years it is kept in the sealed courtyards, where the dust cannot bury it and the wind cannot carry it away. It learns to walk on the packed salt floor, and then on the open pan, and then on the edge of a slow-fire without disturbing the crust. It plays with grit-mites, building little corrals of pebbles and watching the mites pour over them like a slow tide. It is taught the names of the slow-fires, the sounds of the wind, the taste of the leachate, and the way to read the shadows of the wind-fish. It is taught to be still. Stillness is the first lesson, and the second, and the last. A child who cannot stand still for an hour by the age of five is a worry. A child who cannot stand still for a day by the age of ten is a sorrow. But most learn, because the world teaches them. The world is very patient.

Their society is not governed by a single authority. Each cluster of the Works has its own council of elders, those who have seen many dust storms and have not been blown away. Decisions are made slowly, by consensus, over years. A proposal to build a new refinery wing may be discussed for a decade before the first stone is cut. A dispute over a rail line may be settled by waiting: the one who can wait longest is considered to have the stronger claim, because patience is the only real power on the Bench. There is little crime. Haste itself is a kind of crime, and the environment punishes it. A man who moves too fast will break a tool, or fall into a leachate seep, or disturb a lure-talker. The people say that the Bench does not punish haste; it simply does not accommodate it. There are no jails. A wrongdoer is assigned to the slowest work: counting grit-mites, watching a rust-mat grow, listening to the wind for a change that does not come. After a year of such work, they are either broken or made wise, and either way they return to the Works changed.

They live among the animals as among cousins. The salt-oxen they follow but do not herd. The herds move a few meters an hour, and the people know their paths, and they build their rail lines around them, and they harvest the old and the dead. The wind-fish they read as signs of weather; their shadows over the salt are like the shadows of leaves that do not exist, and the people say that when the wind-fish turn their edges to the sun, the wind will shift. The lure-talkers they respect and avoid. A lure-talker stands motionless over a rust-mat, its head a cluster of glass rods, clicking to imitate the sound of grit-mites, and when a curious grazer comes close, it strikes—slowly, but with a suddenness that seems impossible from such a patient animal. The people say that the lure-talker is the only creature on the Bench that understands impatience, and that it is the most dangerous thing in the world. The copper-gnats at dusk are a kind of evening bell. When the gnats rise from the spoil heaps, their mica wings catching the low light like sparks, the people pause and listen to their faint chime. It is the closest thing to music that the Bench makes on its own.

But the people make music too. Their instruments are of mica and salt and iron, and they produce low tones and chimes that sound like the hum of power wires and the creak of rails. They do not have songs in the way we might understand; they have long recitations that take days to perform, and during them the listeners sit in the dust and do not speak. Their art is made of dust. They sweep patterns into the salt pan—great spirals and branching lines, visible from the tops of the refineries—and the wind erases them, and they sweep them again. This is not futile. It is a way of saying that the wind is the only river, and that to make a mark is to feed the river, and that the river must be fed. They have no written language. Their records are kept in the arrangement of mineral grains in sealed jars, or in the growth rings of slow-fires, or in the memories of the elders, which are long. A history may take a year to tell, and it is told only once, and then it is gone, except for what the listeners carry.

They measure time not in hours or days but in the advance of the rust-mats. A good year is one in which the mats grow a millimeter. A generation is a slow-fire adding a new fold to its brain. A lifetime is the time it takes for a yardang to lose the sharp edge of its nose. They do not celebrate birthdays. They celebrate the rising of the leachates at dawn, and the first wind after a dust storm, and the day a new wind-fish is seen, and the day a child stands still for an hour, and the day a slow-fire blooms—though blooms is not the right word, for it is a slow unfurling of crusts, a change in color from black to maroon to pale gold that takes a season. They gather then, and they touch the mound with the backs of their hands, and they recite the names of the ones who tended it before, and they do not rush.

The people of the Works are not separate from the place. They are a part of the ecology, the slowest part, the part that builds and tends and waits. The city is an extension of their bodies—industrial, quiet, mineral, patient. The rail lines are their veins; the power wires are their nerves; the geothermal plants are their hearts. They do not conquer the Bench. They live on it the way a rust-mat lives on a rock, by oxidizing what is already there, by making a home in the dust, by growing at the pace of the slow-fires. They know that they will not finish the Works. The Works is not a thing to be finished. It is a thing to be kept, like the rust-mats, like the salt-oxen, like the wind. And so they keep it, and they are kept by it, and the wind blows, and the dust settles, and the slow-fires grow a millimeter in a good year, and the people stand in the sun and watch the shadows of the wind-fish move over the salt like the shadows of leaves that do not exist.
