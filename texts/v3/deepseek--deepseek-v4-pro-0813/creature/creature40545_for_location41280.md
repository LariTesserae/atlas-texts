They are not seen at first. The hollow looks like an abandoned garden of dry growth and black stone, too still to be inhabited. Then, if you watch until the light shifts, you notice that some of the walls are breathing.

The inhabitants are the colour of the walls because they are built from the walls. Their bodies are not made of flesh so much as laid down in layers: mineral and resin, shield over shield, the same amber chemistry as the vegetation. An adult might be as long as your forearm, but low, many-legged, its back a raft of overlapping plates. Between the plates, dry fibres catch the light and make a soft halo of dust. The head is small, carried close to the ground, and the two antennae are long and fine, moving with the slowness of a plant's tendril. If there are eyes, they are only dark dimples; the creature reads the world through vibration and the taste of the air.

A step takes minutes. A limb lifts, pauses, extends, and then the body pours its weight over it. It is not clumsy. It is deliberate, like a piece of the landscape that has decided to go elsewhere. The claws at the end of each leg open and close once before settling. Even the breath is slow: a row of slits along the flanks opens every few minutes, releasing a faint warm smell of hot iron and cloves. Their blood is not blood. It is the clear, oily sap of the vegetation, and if a shield is cracked, the wound beads a resin that hardens to amber. The injured one may pat the amber into the crack with a claw and go on.

They are everywhere once you learn to see them. Some are as small as seeds, clinging to the walls, combing the felt with their mouthparts. Others are larger, carrying flakes of dried growth to the storehouses. In the nurseries, the young lie in cells woven of white fibre, pale and soft, pulsing once or twice an hour. The pulse is the only clock the city keeps. Attendants stand over them, antennae resting on their skins, exchanging chemical sentences that tell the young what to become.

In the workshops, the workers chew the flakes into a paste. Their mouths move in a slow spiral, grinding mineral and resin together. Half the paste they swallow; the other half they press into the seams of the city. A wall is not built but accumulated, one smear at a time. The newest layer is still sticky and smells of clove oil. After a season it hardens to amber. After a generation it becomes indistinguishable from the stone. They do not use tools. Their tools are their bodies, their mouths, their resin.

They communicate by leaving trails of resin that harden into script. A conversation between two workers might begin with a touch of antennae and then a long stillness. An hour later, one lays a thread of amber across the path. The other follows it, then adds a thread of its own. By the end of a day, the conversation has become part of the floor. There is also a sound-language. In the galleries, the hollow stems of the vegetation stand like organ pipes, and a worker will sometimes tap a code with its claw. The taps are too faint for human ears, but they travel through the pipe and into the stone. Another worker, two chambers away, taps back. To the city, it is a constant low murmur, a slow conversation between the body and its parts.

At the centre, the spire rises from the mineral leaves. Inside, the air is warmer, and the walls glow with a cold light that comes in pulses, once or twice an hour. The glow is not made by lamps. It lives in the skin of the one who waits below. In a bell-shaped chamber at the base of the spire rests the founder-mother. She is pale and enormous, a soft sac of oil and eggs, too large to move. Her abdomen shows the slow tide of light through her skin, and the pulse of it is the pulse of the city. The workers pass in single file, so slowly that the line seems like a vein of pale stone. They touch her flanks as they pass, leaving behind a fine dust. The wind never stirs it. She takes the dust into her body and, in time, gives it back as new shells, new workers, new walls.

Nothing here is wasted. The old do not die so much as settle. When a worker's body slows beyond use, it folds its legs and lowers itself into a gap in the floor. Others walk over it for years. Dust fills the spaces between its plates. The living wall sends a thin root of mineral growth over it. In time, it is the floor. The city grows by this accumulation. A new chamber is added once a generation, not because they plan it, but because the dead and the born are the same substance.

The whole hollow is one animal, laid out in architecture. The creatures are its hands, the vegetation its blood, the spire its heart. They are not separate from what they build. The city is not a place they inhabit; it is the shape they take, at a speed of one breath per hour.
