They are not the first thing you notice. In Low Meridian, the eye is taken by the light, the plant, the strange architecture of grown and bolted things. The people move inside that world the way animals move in a forest: at the edge of awareness, half-seen, deliberate. They are the color of the root-mat in shadow—pale grey-green, the color of the underside of a leaf. They wear clothes cut from the same felted stems, dyed with mineral oxides: copper-rust, iron-black, the blue of rare earths. Unless they move, they vanish into the walls.

They are small, wiry, long-boned. Their skin is waxy and cool to the touch, and it does not sweat. There is no moisture in the air to cool them, so their bodies have learned not to need it. Their blood runs clear and thick, more oil than water, and you can see it moving under the skin of their wrists if you look closely—a faint, steady pulse like sap in a root. Their faces are narrow, with high cheekbones and small noses that can close against the chemical air. Their eyes are large, round, and dark, with a third lid that slides sideways to protect against the dry wind and the occasional acid spray. They have no hair on their heads. Instead, a fine downy fuzz grows along their skulls and down the backs of their necks, sensitive to changes in air pressure and to the low frequencies that travel through the plant. Their ears are small and set far back, shaped to catch those same frequencies. When they speak, the sound is quiet and papery, as if they are unwilling to disturb the enormous hush of the cavern.

They dress in layers: an inner garment of soft, absorbent plant-felt; an outer layer stiffer and oiled against the dry air. Their boots have soles of compressed root-hide, which grip the springy floor without tearing it. Around their waists they wear belts of braided copper wire, from which hang their tools: pruning knives with curved blades, acid-ampoules stoppered with wax, signal-tappers of polished brass, and small glass vials of sap-solvent. Some wear masks fitted with filters of charcoal and dried moss when working near the cutting faces or the smelting vats. The masks are not for poison; the air is clean, if strange. They are for the fine, sharp dust that rises when the plant is cut, and for the fumes of the distillation coils.

They move with a slow, rolling gait, distributing their weight carefully on the living ground. Rushing is considered rude and also dangerous. The plant can feel haste, they say, and it grows thorns where it is startled. Their speech is supplemented by a system of taps and strokes made with the knuckles or with a small brass rod against the root-mat. The signal carries through the fibers for hundreds of meters, a kind of telegraph through the earth. Long-distance messages are sent this way, from district to district, in a rhythm like rain on a drumhead, though there is no rain here. For visual signals across the open root-plains, they use small mirrors of polished metal to flash the ceiling-light—a code of bright and dark that crosses the distance faster than a person could walk. The whole city is alive with these quiet communications, most of them too subtle for an outsider to notice.

Their homes are not separate from the city. They are hollowed into the living walls of the buildings: chambers cut into the thick stems and lined with a soft membrane that the plant secretes in response to their presence. The doorways are round or oval, framed with blackened steel, and often covered by a curtain of woven root-fiber. Inside, the walls are warm from the slow metabolism of the plant, and the air smells of dry spice and hot oil. They sleep in hammocks of woven fiber suspended from the ceiling, or on low platforms grown from the floor. Light comes from small cultured globes, like the great ceiling lamps but smaller, which they feed with mineral salts and which glow a soft amber. There is no true darkness in Low Meridian, only dimness. They prefer to sleep in dimness, because the plant dreams in the dark, and sometimes its dreams are restless.

They eat little, because their bodies are efficient and the plant is generous. Their staple is a dense, chewy paste made from the inner pith of young stems, pounded and fermented in stone jars until it becomes sour and slightly intoxicating. They also eat the flesh of the six-legged grazers, cut into strips and dried in the warm air of the factory vents. It has a taste like smoked copper and salt. From the sap of the plant they press a thin, clear oil that they drink warm. It is not water; it is more like a solvent, but it carries minerals and sugars in a form their bodies can use. Water is so rare in this place that it is kept in sealed glass and used only for ceremonies, for medicine, or for the birth of a child. They do not miss it. They have never known a world where water falls from the sky.

The adults work in shifts, though the light never changes. Time is kept by the slow pulse of the plant, by the cycling of the engines, and by the grow-quakes that ripple through the ground at irregular intervals. Some tend the roadways, pruning back the new growth with steam-cutters and acid-sprays, keeping the streets open and the walls smooth. Others work in the factories, tending the muscle-ovens and the distillation coils, extracting copper, iron, sulfur, and rare earths from the sap. Still others are root-tappers, who walk the deep fissures and listen to the rock, marking where the plant’s roots have found new ore. They are not miners in the old sense. They do not dig. They guide the plant, and the plant does the digging, and then they harvest what the sap brings up. It is a slow, biological mining, and the people are its shepherds.

At the center of the city, in the tall stem-tower with the spiral staircase, lives the engine that is also an organ. The people call it the Heart, or sometimes the Winder. It pulses and glows faintly through the translucent walls, and it winds the plant’s growth, or is wound by it. A small guild of Heart-keepers tends it. They do not command it; they listen to it, and they adjust the flow of sap and the tension of the living cables. They are revered, but not above others. In Low Meridian, there is no king, no council of elders. Decisions are made by a kind of slow consensus, carried through the root-mat in tapping signals, each person adding a phrase or a pause until the matter settles. It can take days to decide something simple, but the plant is patient, and so are they.

Children are born small and pale, already with the third eyelid and the fine down on their skulls. They learn to tap before they learn to speak, pressing their palms to the floor to feel the messages of the city. They play in the root-plains, chasing the slow grazers and catching needle-moths, but they are taught early not to run too fast or shout too loud. The plant feels everything, they are told, and the plant remembers. The plant is not a god to them, not exactly. It is more like an ancestor, a partner, a vast and quiet mind that has not yet decided whether to notice them. They speak of it as “the Undergrove” or simply “the Root.” They do not think of themselves as its masters. They are its hands, its pruners, its harvesters. They believe that the plant is not one organism but many joined so tightly that the distinction no longer matters, and that they, too, are part of that joining. When a person dies, their body is laid on the root-mat in a quiet place, and the plant grows over it, breaking it down into oil and mineral. There is no burial, only reabsorption. They say the dead become part of the road, part of the wall, part of the next generation’s food. This is not sad to them; it is a kind of continuity.

When the grow-quake comes—a long, slow shrug of the ground, a heave that makes the buildings lean a fraction and the animals pause—no one panics. The work crews stop their pruning. The trams halt on their rails. The children look up from their games. The people of Low Meridian stand still and listen, feeling the vibration through their feet. Some place a hand on the nearest wall and close their eyes. They are not afraid. They are reading the quake, reading which root has found what, which new chamber is being torn open in the fresh rock beyond the lamp-light. After it passes, a signal-tapper sends a message through the mat: a new seam of ore, a new district beginning. Somewhere in the far dimness, a crew is already gathering their steam-cutters and acid-sprays. The city is growing, and they will help it.

Consider one of them, a woman named Silt. She is a root-tapper. She carries a staff of cured stem-wood, tipped with a copper point that she presses to the floor to listen. She walks the edge of the settled area, where the lamps end and the raw plant thickens into a tangled wilderness. Her job is to find the places where the plant’s roots have hit a rich vein of ore, and to mark them for the harvesters. She does not hurry. The plant does not hurry. She feels the ground through the soles of her boots, through the staff, through the fine down on the back of her neck. When she finds a promising seam, she kneels and places her palm flat on the mat, and she waits until the plant pulses back to her in a slow, answering wave. Then she taps a brief message into the root: *here, and here, and here*. Behind her, a crew begins to move, carrying steam-cutters and coils of copper wire.

Silt does not think of herself as a person separate from the place. She is part of the Undergrove, as much as the road, the wall, the animal, the ore. When she dies, the plant will take her back, and the city will continue. In the meantime, she walks the edge of the light, listening to the deep earth, and she is content.

This is what lives there. Not just people, but a way of being alive: slow, dry, chemical, quiet, and utterly at home. They are the Meridians. They are the hands of the Root. And they have been there long enough that the difference between them and the plant is only a matter of attention.
