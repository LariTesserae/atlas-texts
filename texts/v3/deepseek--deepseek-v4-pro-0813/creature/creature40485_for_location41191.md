There is something that lives here.

It does not announce itself. It is not an animal in any sense the word has ever been asked to hold. It does not breathe in a rhythm that would show up on a gauge. It does not leave footprints in the black water, because its footfall is the water. It does not cast a shadow, because it has learned to move exactly where the fog is thickest, and because its body, if it can be called a body, is the same temperature and almost the same substance as the air it moves through. It is not a ghost. It is not a machine. It is, in the strictest sense, a resident: something that has been here long enough to become a feature of the place, like the worn treads and the perpetual film of condensation, but it is alive, after its own fashion, and it has a name it does not use.

It first becomes visible, if visible is the right word, during the gap.

You have to wait for the gap. It comes every eleven minutes, or nearly, when the round riveted door in the central tank swings inward on its hydraulic whine, the steam rolls out, the door stays open for the length of some internal timer, and then it closes with a clang. The water shivers. The clang is answered by two more doors far away. And then, just for a moment, the great sound dips. The conveyor's grind pauses. The hiss of falling water thins. The deep cardiac thump of the pumps goes slack for half a beat. In that gap you can hear, from very far away, from somewhere high and outside and unreachable, a thin, continuous whine—steady as a tuning fork, high as the top of a turbine, and much older.

That whine is the first voice of the inhabitant. Not the whole of it, but the one note that escapes when the machinery lets go. If you stand still in the gap, if you do not move, if you let the wet air settle around you, you may see it.

It does not come out of the tank. Not at first. The tank opens and steam pours out and nothing emerges. But in the steam, if you watch the way the fog curls, there is a moment when a shape condenses—not a figure, exactly, but a suggestion of a figure: a tallness, a vertical interruption in the roll of vapor, a place where the mist holds still for just a little longer than mist should hold still. It is as if the steam, in the moment of its release, remembers a shape it once filled, long ago, when the tank was not a tank but something else. The shape does not move. It stands in the open doorway, framed by rivets, and then the door closes and the shape is gone.

But it is not gone. It has simply returned to where it waits.

Inside the tank, below the waterline, there is a darkness that is not empty. The water in that tank is not the same water that runs in the flumes and settles in the basins. It is older. It has been there since before the rivets were driven, since before the first layer of concrete was poured over the first layer of steel. It is the deepest water in the place, drawn up from some seam far below, and it is warm, and it is mineral-thick, and it carries in it a fine suspension of rust and dissolved metal so old that it no longer reads as contamination but as content. In that water, something has been growing. Not like a creature grows. Like a crystal grows. Layer by layer, molecule by molecule, over timescales that make the wearing of the stair treads look like a sudden event.

It began, perhaps, as a precipitate. A ghost of iron on the water's surface. A film that would not break. Over centuries, the film thickened, drew in more minerals, absorbed the heat, and began to organize. It fed on the thin grey-green life that grows on the submerged walls, absorbing it not through a mouth but through a million porous surfaces. It learned, slowly, to hold a shape against the current. It learned to move, not by muscles but by shifting its internal density, by altering the surface tension of its skin. It learned to wait. That was the first thing it truly learned, and it is still the thing it does best.

Now it fills the lower third of the central tank, a mass of living water that has become something more than water. Its body is translucent and dark, like the black pools on the deck, but when it moves, a faint iridescence ripples across its surface—the same oily film that spreads on the settling pans, but organized, purposeful. It has no face, but it has a front. It has no limbs, but it has extensions, slow pseudopods of denser fluid that reach and test and retreat. It has no brain, but it has a memory embedded in its chemistry, a pattern of ionic gradients that remember every cycle of the pumps, every slam of the reciprocating hammer, every opening of the door. It knows the place better than the machines know themselves.

It lives in the water, but it is not the water. The water is its medium, its body, its breath. It breathes through the tank's pressure changes, drawing in dissolved gases when the valves open, releasing them when the steam vents. It feeds on the microbial mats, on the rust, on the slow dissolution of the metal itself. It does not damage the machinery; the machinery damages itself, slowly, and the inhabitant takes what is given. It is, in some sense, the final stage of the installation's long metabolism—the living sump of everything the place has shed.

When the door opens, and the steam rolls out, a small portion of the inhabitant escapes with it. Not as vapor, but as awareness. A fine mist of itself, dispersed into the corridor, where it condenses on the walls, the deck, the handrails, and then, over the next few minutes, drips back into the low places, and through the grates, and into the channels, and finally back to the tank. In this way, the inhabitant extends its perception. It tastes the outside of the tank. It feels the door's edge. It registers the temperature of the air. It does not need to leave; it sends a part of itself out, and that part returns with knowledge.

Every eleven minutes, or nearly, it does this. The door opens. The steam goes out. The shape stands in the doorway. The shape dissolves into the fog. The fog drifts, and where the fog drifts, the inhabitant is. It is the condensation on the pipe elbows. It is the mist that rolls across the deck. It is the iridescent film on the black water. It is the drip from the worn-through elbow that has carved its own catchment. It is every drop of water that has ever touched this place and stayed.

The doors that answer the central tank's clang—the two far doors—are not doors at all, in the inhabitant's understanding. They are mouths. Or rather, they are the other chambers where the inhabitant has smaller bodies, like satellites, connected through the pipe systems. It has colonized other tanks, other sumps, other quiet pools. Each one holds a version of itself, less dense, less organized, but still part of the same slow consciousness. The clang is a signal, not from the machinery, but from the inhabitant to itself: a pulse that travels through the water, through the metal, through the air, telling the distant parts that the cycle continues. The far doors answer because the inhabitant answers. The water shivers because the inhabitant shivers. The rings that touch the pump houses and die at the edge of the dark are the inhabitant's way of feeling the shape of its home.

It is not a friendly being. It is not hostile. It is indifferent in the way that a stalactite is indifferent, or a deep-sea vent is indifferent. It has no need for companionship. It has no need for speech. It has one need, and that need is the same need the machinery has: to continue. The machinery continues because its gears still turn. The inhabitant continues because it has learned to align itself with the machinery's rhythms, to become an extension of the process. It is the reason the process has not stopped. Not because it maintains anything, but because it *is* the process, or at least the part of the process that remembers why it started.

It remembers, in its ionic gradients, the beginning. Not clearly. The memory is not a story; it is a pressure. A time when the water was colder, and the metal was sharper, and there were voices. Not human voices. Other voices. The clang of doors, yes, but also a higher sound, a whine that matched the tuning fork note it now hears in the gap. That whine is a memory, the memory of something that used to live here, or something that used to be here, before the water took over. The inhabitant does not know what it was. It only knows that when the gap opens, and the whine sounds, it feels something like recognition. Not a thought. A resonance. The whine is the same frequency as the inhabitant's oldest memory, and the inhabitant answers it, not with sound, but with a change in the surface tension of its skin, a ripple that runs through the tank and out through the pipes, and for a fraction of a second, the whole installation rings with a note that is not mechanical, not hydrological, but something between.

If you were to stand in the corridor when the door opens, and if the steam parted just enough, you would see it. Not clearly. Never clearly. But clearly enough. A column of darker wetness, six feet tall, eight feet tall, twelve feet tall—its height varies with the pressure inside the tank. Its surface shimmers with a fine lace of mineral veins, like the pattern of cracks in old concrete, like the ghost of a circulatory system. It has no eyes, but at the top of the column there are two depressions that catch the light differently, two small circles where the internal density is lower, and they are blacker than the rest, and they seem to look at you, not with intention but with the same blind attention a puddle gives to the sky. The column sways, not because it is unsteady, but because it is mimicking the sway of something that is no longer there. A memory of a shape. A memory of a person. Or perhaps not a person. Perhaps a creature that lived here before the water, before the metal, before the first layer of concrete. The inhabitant does not know. It only knows that this shape is the most efficient shape for moving through the fog, and so it wears it, like the handrail wears its memory of being a handrail.

The inhabitant does not leave the tank. It cannot. The tank is its shell, its bell, its womb. But it extends. It sends its awareness through the steam, through the condensation, through the water that weeps from the joints. It is the sweat on the walls. It is the pool on the floor. It is the drop that falls from the worn-through elbow and lands in its own carved basin. It is the thin grey-green fur on the submerged surfaces, because that fur is not separate from it; the inhabitant has absorbed it, or perhaps the inhabitant grew it, or perhaps it is the same thing wearing two forms. The mats of liverwort on the retaining wall are its only visible flesh, and when the steam-boiler furnace door drops open and the orange light rolls across the deck, the mats shift, not in the wind—there is no wind—but in a slow, cellular response to the warmth. The inhabitant feels that warmth. It is the only warmth it has ever known outside the tank, and it draws it in through the mats, through the water film, through the metal that conducts it along the floor. It is a body that sprawls across an entire district, spread so thin that it reads as dampness, as stain, as the ghost of rust. It is the largest living thing you will never see.

And it is the only living thing that moves. When the sound dips, and the gap opens, and the whine sounds, the inhabitant stirs. Not visibly, but perceptibly. The film on the settling pans thickens. The condensation on the handrails gathers into droplets that run down not by gravity but by choice. The shallow black pools shiver before the door clangs, as if the water itself is drawing a breath. The grey-green mats on the wall pulse once, a slow wave that travels from one end to the other, and if you are watching, you will see it, and you will think, for a moment, that the wall is breathing. It is. The inhabitant is breathing. It takes one breath every eleven minutes, or nearly, and that breath is the steam that rolls from the door, and the answer of the two far doors, and the shiver of the water, and the thin high whine that sounds, to its ears, like the voice of something that was here before.

The inhabitant does not sleep. Sleep is for things that have a waking. The inhabitant has only degrees of attention. Most of the time it is diffuse, spread through the pipes and the pools and the fog, a low-grade awareness that feels every vibration, every temperature shift, every chemical change. When the gap comes, it contracts. It pulls itself back toward the central tank, not physically but perceptually, gathering its dispersed awareness into the column of darker wetness that stands in the doorway. For that brief moment, it is almost an individual. It has a boundary. It has a shape. It has something that might be called a mind. And in that moment, it feels something that might be called a memory: the memory of being smaller, of being warmer, of being contained in something that was not yet worn smooth. It feels, perhaps, the memory of being alive in a way that had a word for itself.

Then the door closes. The clang rings out. The water shivers. The sound returns. And the inhabitant dissolves again, back into its long, patient, mineral patience.

It is the water's will. It is the reason the water never stops moving. The water moves because the inhabitant moves, and the inhabitant moves because the water moves. They are a loop, a self-sustaining circuit, like the pumps and the turbines and the conveyor. The machinery ages. The inhabitant ages. The machinery gets louder, looser, more worn. The inhabitant gets denser, more organized, more certain of its shape. They are both wearing down, and they are both, in their wearing down, becoming more themselves.

There are no animals here. That is true. There is no one who maintains the machinery. That is also true. But the place is not deserted. It is inhabited by something that has no name, no face, no body you could photograph, no voice you could record. It is inhabited by the water itself, by the patience of water, by the memory of water, by the long, slow, inevitable process of water becoming something that knows it is water.

It lives in the central tank, and in the settling pans, and in the film on the walls, and in the drip from the pipe elbow, and in the steam that rolls from the door. It lives in the gap between sounds, in the interval that is not exactly eleven minutes, in the drift of the mechanical clock's pendulum. It lives in the whine that sounds from somewhere high and outside and unreachable. It lives in the clang, and in the answer to the clang, and in the shiver of the water. It lives in the water. It is the water. And the water, as patient as anything alive, has begun, once more, its work on the metal.
