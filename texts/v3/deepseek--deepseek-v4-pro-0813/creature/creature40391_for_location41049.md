The plateau is not empty, though it often looks so. Its inhabitants are not separate from it; they are expressions of the same process, the same slow manufacture of fog and time. First among them is the ground itself.

**The Mat-Body**

The ground is one continuous living thing, a skin of peat, leaf, mycelium, and vascular cable several metres thick, stretched over the finished stone. It is not a population but a single body, and it is always working. It ferments its own decay. It taps the electrical potential between the wet sky and the mineral floor. It condenses the cold mist into sugars through chemistries that do not quite belong to biology. The engines—the hollow reed-chimneys, the chapel-sized pods, the leaf-blade turbines—are its organs, grown rather than built. The whole plain is its body, and its thoughts move through the mycelium as slow chemical pulses, too slow to notice a footstep but fast enough to remember a season. It dreams in domes that push up overnight and burst in the cold morning. It wakes in the rerouting of water channels and the opening of stomatal apertures. It does not live on the plateau; it is the plateau’s living will.

**The Glass-Shears**

Between the engines, the glass-shears move. They are long and low, six-legged, their bodies like smoked glass over a green furnace. You can see their fermenting stomachs churning the cropped mat, see the pale electric organ along the spine pulsing with stored sky. They have no eyes; a row of heat-pits and electroreceptors runs along their snouts, so they follow the steam columns and the blue crackle of the turbines. They graze in loose herds of a dozen or so, cropping the photosynthetic skin with jaws of serrated silica. The sound is exactly like many pairs of scissors.

Calves are born as translucent sacs laid in waterlogged depressions; they root into the mat for a month before they pull themselves out, already walking. The herds are not gentle, but they are necessary. Their cropping keeps the mat’s upper skin from sealing shut, lets gas escape, lets the engines breathe. They carry static in their bodies until they can no longer graze, then they seek out the black mirror stone and press their foreheads to it. There is a blue arc, a smell of ozone, and the stone is polished a little deeper.

The glass-shears have learned to walk around the negative-space bowls. A herd will part around such a depression without ever looking down, leaving a gap in their line like a missing tooth. If one of them does enter, it may come out smaller, or older, or not at all. The others accept the corrected one, but they leave a space where it walks, and they do not graze on its shadow.

**The Arc-Flies**

Above the grazers, in the electric fields around the leaf-blade turbines, the arc-flies turn. They are the size of thumbnails, with wings of mica film and bodies like coiled copper wire. They are born from eggs laid in charged droplets on the vents; their larvae burrow into the mat and feed on the voltage between fog and mineral floor. When a storm-state builds, they pupate and emerge all at once, so many that the air hums like a load-bearing cable.

They are not individuals; the swarm is the animal. It flows between the engines, attracted to the static discharge, and individual bodies flare blue and vanish, becoming a small new circuit. The rest do not mourn; they adjust. Their sound is a high, crisp crackle, like glass breaking in reverse. The swarm passes over the glass-shears and the glass-shears do not flinch. The arc-flies are the sparks made flesh, the plateau’s nervous system taking to the air.

**The Reed-Throats**

Inside the hollow reed-chimneys live the bellowers. A reed-throat is a long, pale, segmented animal that clings to the inner wall with rings of root-like claws. It has no eyes and no face, only a bell-shaped chamber of cartilage and muscle at its lower end. It feeds by filtering the warm vapor that rises through the reed, extracting sugars and microbial life; what it excretes thickens the reed’s lignified wall, so it is both tenant and builder.

Its call is a low infrasonic boom that it makes by forcing air through its bell. Because the air is always at the exact point of condensation, the sound wave condenses a cloud of steam before it reaches you. You see the exhalation first, white and round, and then the sound arrives—a small looping of cause and effect that the place does not bother to correct. The reed-throats are solitary; each reed hosts one, or sometimes a mated pair that never meet but call back and forth through the mist. They are said to be the oldest animals on the plateau, but they have no way to tell you, and you have no way to ask.

**The Corrected**

Then there are the corrected, the ones who entered the negative-space bowls and came back wrong—or right, depending on how you look at time. They are not a species. A corrected glass-shear may be the size of a dog but with the worn joints of a hundred years; it grazes a few seconds out of step with the herd, and the others walk around it. A corrected arc-fly can be as large as a fist, flying slowly with wings that shed old weather; when it passes, you smell a day that has not happened yet.

The corrected do not seem to suffer; they are simply out of phase. They linger near the bowls, as if listening for the moment they left. Sometimes a corrected glass-shear stands at the edge of a bowl for hours, its reflection not showing in the black water. Then it turns and walks away smaller than before. The other animals have learned to avoid the corrected, not out of fear but because their presence makes the present feel optional.

**The Listeners**

Finally, there are the Listeners. They are the only ones who might be called people, though they would not call themselves that. They are tall and thin, with skin like damp birch bark and legs that end in spreading root-like toes. They wear nothing, but their bodies are covered in a fine living layer of moss and lichen that changes color with the chemical signals of the mat, so they are always in conversation with the ground. They have a horizontal mouth and two large, dark eye-spots that reflect a sky from another age.

They do not build. They sleep standing up, their toes rooting slightly into the peat, and in the morning they pull themselves free with a sound like a boot lifting from mud. They follow the glass-shears at a distance, not herding but learning. They enter the negative-space bowls deliberately, and when they emerge they are older or younger or simply more informed. They sing rarely, and when they do, the reed-throats pause to listen. Their song is not language; it is the sound of the plateau thinking about itself.

The Listeners are few, and they do not gather often. When two of them meet, they stand apart and let the moss on their bodies exchange color in long, slow waves. They do not use names, because naming is a kind of building, and they build nothing. They have no artifacts, no walls, no roads. Their only tool is attention. They are the plateau’s senses, its occasional self-awareness, the temporary inconsistency that the process has not yet corrected.

---

These are the inhabitants. They do not possess the plateau; they are possessed by it. The plateau has no name in any living language because the Listeners do not speak it, the mat does not need it, and the stone beneath has forgotten it. To live there is to be a temporary consistency in a process that has been running forever—and may, at any moment, be corrected.
