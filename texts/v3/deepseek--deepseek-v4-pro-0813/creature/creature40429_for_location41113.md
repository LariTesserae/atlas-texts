The first one you see is not a person. You think it is a piece of the wall, a slumped column of wet stone, maybe a collapsed lintel crusted with salt. Then the stone stands. It has been resting with its back to the causeway, letting the rain crust over it, letting the mineral scale grow across its shell like a fresh coat of paint, and now it turns. The body is broad and low, like a cart, like a boulder, like a shrine that has learned to walk. Its shell is not worn armor but living sediment, striped with the same tidelines as the walls. Water runs from it in all directions. On its back there are pools trapped in pockets of the shell, and in those pools tiny pale swimmers circle. The creature does not notice them, or if it does, it does not care; they are part of its body, grazers on its mineral skin.

It has legs—six, maybe eight—jointed columns of black alloy and pale chitin. Each foot is a fan of fingers that spreads on the wet stone, tasting the vibrations. Its head is low and retractable, a cluster of eyestalks and breathing fronds and a soft, ceaselessly working mouth. When it sighs, steam comes out of gill slits, and the air around it fills with a faint mineral breath, as if the city itself had exhaled. It is not alone. There are more of them, all along the causeway, but you didn't see them because they were not moving. They were being the wall.

Now one of them is working. It moves with the slow gait of something that has never been in a hurry. Its weight makes the stone creak underfoot. One of its arms trails behind it, and from a gland near the wrist a pale slurry oozes. It seals the cracks it passes. The slurry hardens quickly in the wet air, first to a soft paste, then to the same laminated stone as the causeway. The creature is not repairing the city; it is laying down the next layer of itself. Where it walks, the city is remade.

Another one surfaces in the canal. This one is sleeker, with a long keel-shaped shell and limbs like oars. It rises half out of the water and uses two small hands to adjust a sluice that is not a gate but a muscle of living shell and metal. The water changes direction. A geyser on the far side of the plaza stops, and a dozen tiny wading animals scatter. The keeled one blinks its eyestalks and sinks back under, leaving only a trail of bubbles and a new pattern in the mineral film.

They do not seem to have separate houses. The city is the house. When they rest, they settle into the architecture, becoming part of a wall, a buttress, a stairway. Their bodies are made to be confused with stone. The oldest of them are so encrusted and so fused with the city that they no longer move at all, except for a single arm or a pair of eyestalks that tend a plaza, a sluice, a geyser vent. They are not dead; they are the city's memory. The layers of their shells are records—every ring of sediment is a year of floods and droughts, a season of geysers, a new canal dug. They remember by being built.

The young ones are small and quick, by comparison. They have less shell and more bare chitin, and they race along the wet causeways on too many legs. They learn by touch, following an adult and laying their own thin trails of slurry, which harden into miniature walls and are immediately washed away or absorbed. They are not scolded. The city has no need for perfect children. The imperfect walls become niches for animals, which is also a kind of building.

Their food is the city's food. They eat mineral film, the same pale paste they use to build, but they ferment it first in a crop or a gland, turning stone into living tissue. They also eat the small shell-backed scrapers, crushing them with slow, patient mouthparts. And they eat the synthesized food that wells up in public troughs, a warm, sour jelly that the city itself seems to produce. They feed the grazers, and the grazers feed them, and the city feeds them all.

They communicate not by sound but by vibration. The air is too loud for voices, so they speak by drumming on the stone with their knuckles, or by tapping the water with their oar-limbs. The messages pass along the causeways and through the canals and up the walls. You can feel it, if you stand still: a constant, coded thrumming, like a second heartbeat under the noise. Sometimes they touch each other, pressing their eyestalks or their hand-fans together, and the conversation becomes more intimate, chemical, slow. They have no words for "dry" or "green." They have many words for different kinds of wetness: the wetness of rain, the wetness of condensation, the wetness of the sea, the wetness of the body's own breath.

They are not humans. They are not animals, exactly. They are the city's keepers, or its organs, or its children. They call themselves something that might be translated as the Unfinished, or the Deposition, or the Ones Who Walk the Layer. They do not think of the city as a place to live. They think of it as a body they share. When a tower folds and becomes wet sand, they do not run; they move toward it, because that is where they are needed. They form a ring and begin to exude slurry, and the new wall rises under their hands.

They have no art, or their art is the city itself. They have no music, or their music is the drumming that never stops. They have no separate life from the wet, mineral, noisy world. They are saturated by it, like the animals, like the stone. And if you watch them long enough, you begin to wonder if they are the inhabitants at all, or if the city is the inhabitant, and they are just the way it moves.

Eventually one of them notices you. It is not alarming. It turns its eyestalks in your direction, and a few of the hand-fans lift from the stone. It drums a short sequence. The vibration reaches you through the ground, through the water in your boots, through the bones of your jaw. You understand it is not asking who you are. It is asking whether the new crack in the wall is yours, or whether you would like to help fill it. Because in a place with no outside, every stranger is either a new material or a new hand. And if you stay long enough, you will become one of those things too.
