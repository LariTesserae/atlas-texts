The people who live in the Ledges—the Cradle-born, the Unfinished Range’s stubborn tenants—do not call themselves by any name that has stuck. Lowland traders call them Ledge-folk or Fog-crews. The people themselves say simply “we here,” or “the crews,” as if existence is a shift that must be worked.

They are built like the place: low-slung, broad-shouldered, deliberate, with edges that sometimes do not quite hold. Their skin is pale from the perpetual gray-green twilight, but it is not the pallor of caves; it is the mineral gray of seep-water and the green of moss-light filtered through fog. Many have a faint metallic sheen on their forearms and cheeks from years of handling ore and breathing the distilleries’ steam. Their hair is coarse, cropped short or braided with copper wire to keep it from snagging on cables. Their hands are thick-fingered, callused into rivet-like ridges, scarred from hot pipes and sharp schist. They move with a particular rhythm: a tap of the boot heel or a small hammer before each step on unfamiliar stone, listening for the sound of a surface that has not yet decided to be solid.

Their eyes are the most telling feature. Wide in the dimness, but with a second, deeper focus—a double vision that watches both the present street and the ghost of the street that might replace it. Children born on the high ledges sometimes have one eye that sees a half-second ahead or behind; midwives bind a copper coin over that eye for the first year until the vision settles. Those who spend too long near the unfinished cliffs begin to blur at the edges: a hand momentarily transparent, a shadow that lags half a step, a voice that arrives a breath before the lips move. It is not a sickness. It is exposure. The cure is work—heavy, repetitive, loud work that hammers a person back into the real. Foundry labor is prescribed for the blur-touched, and many of the oldest anchor crews are made of people whose edges have been worn back into sharpness by steam and iron and the constant re-pouring of concrete.

Clothing is practical, layered, almost always damp. Waxed canvas over wool, leather aprons, high boots soled with ridged rubber cut from cinder-crow gutta, gloves with the fingers removed for fine work but reinforced palms for climbing cables. Almost everyone wears a scarf or a mask over the nose and mouth, not only against the smoke—though the smoke is thick—but against the ghost-midges and the faint ozone taste of half-formed air. On their belts or around their necks they carry anchors: a rivet, a gear tooth, a loop of copper wire, a smooth stone from a particularly stable ledge. Children are given an anchor at birth, usually a nail pulled from a beam that has been in place for at least seven pourings.

The Ledges are organized not by families but by crews and districts. A child is apprenticed young, and the trade becomes the surname. The main crews are:

**Pourers**—foundry workers who pour and re-pour foundations, bridge abutments, stair risers. They are the most solid people in the city, coated in concrete dust that forms a second skin. They sing in low, rhythmic chants matched to the stomp of the stamping press, and their footfalls are so heavy the stone seems to remember them.

**Tallymen and Talliwomen**—the counters and record-keepers. They walk the stairways and bridges, counting steps, marking the number on chalkboards at each landing. Stair Nine is their obsession; its step-count changes from day to day, sometimes from hour to hour. They maintain long paper rolls of counts, arguing over whether the extra steps are real or a trick of the fog. They carry abacuses and stubby pencils, and their eyes are red from constant squinting.

**Brush-gangs**—teams with saws and machetes who cut back the vegetation that would swallow ledges, stairways, windows. They work in full leather, hooded, because the moss can grow over a boot in a single shift. They burn the cuttings in portable braziers, and the smoke smells of burnt tea and lightning.

**Pipe-walkers and steamwrights**—the maintainers of the steam pipes, gauges, vents. They know every hiss, clank, and drip; they can tell from a change in pitch whether a flange is loosening or a section of pipe is about to become “unfixed.” They carry wrenches strapped to their backs, and their hands are permanently scalded in branching patterns like tree roots.

**Fog-readers**—the nearest thing to priests. They stand on the highest platforms with brass instruments—whistles, glass prisms, tuning forks—and interpret the movement of the fog, the shape of the clouds, the groans of the wind-eaters. They issue warnings: *The third switchback west of the Tin Quarter will be unmade by evening; reroute the cable tram.* Their predictions are often right, but not always; when wrong, they blame the unfinished wind.

**Crow-keepers**—tenders of the cinder crows, not as pets but as necessary, half-wild neighbors. They scatter mineral grit on the roosts, collect eggs from chimneys, and train certain crows to fly between districts with small copper tubes containing messages. The crows are clever and spiteful; they steal anchors, drop stones on workers, and sometimes speak words they have overheard. Yet the crow-keepers love them fiercely, sharing their food and mending broken wings.

**Sketch-chasers**—the most unfortunate and the most valued. These are volunteers or exiles who map the unfinished streets, the alleys that appear and vanish, the ledges that are only outlines in the mist. They carry charcoal and slate, drawing what they see and pinning the maps to public boards. Sketch-chasers are always slightly blurred, and they often climb stairs that end in midair. Some never return; others return with maps of places that cannot exist.

There are many other trades: moth-tenders who harvest the silk of veil moths; lichen-dyers who make pigments from rust-orange moss; urn-keepers who maintain the public cisterns and bellows-toads; quarrymen on the far rim who set the charges that boom three times; and the children, who run errands, gather cliff-urchin plates, and learn to count the steps.

Their food is strange to outsiders. The staple is **pipe-fungus**, a dense, pale mushroom that grows only on the warm steam pipes; it tastes of mineral and smoke and is eaten boiled or fried in crow-egg batter. They also eat lichen cakes, pressed from the bright growths on the rock, which crumble into a salty powder. Cliff-urchins are herded on the steepest slopes, their stone-colored plates used for roofing and their flesh smoked into tough jerky. Bellows-toads are kept in cisterns for their eggs, a delicacy, though the toads themselves are never killed—their mournful notes are considered good luck. They drink seep-water, filtered through charcoal and copper, with a faint electric tang. Mead is brewed from heather and lichen in steam-heated vats; it is said to make the drinker temporarily able to see the world as it might be.

Homes are carved into cliffs or built on ledges, with thick walls of stone and brick. The windows are small, glazed with mica or oiled paper, and often sealed against the damp. Inside, the floors are stone or wood worn smooth; heat comes from steam radiators that tick and hiss. Furniture is solid, bolted to the wall in case of a slip. Each home has a **threshold line**—a raised ridge of stone or metal across the doorway, meant to keep the unfinished out. They step over it carefully, never on it. Above the door hangs a bundle: a sprig of fern, a crow feather, a copper nail, a twist of wire. It is not a decoration but an anchor.

The people are not without art. Their music is the rhythm of the machines, but they also play instruments: steam-whistles tuned to a scale, drums made from oil drums, long horns that echo the wind-eaters’ groans. Their songs are call-and-response, repetitive, meant to be sung while working; the words often count steps or list the parts of a machine. They carve figures from cliff-urchin plates and stone, but the figures are always half-finished—one side smooth, the other rough—because to finish a carving is to lie about the world.

They form partnerships by **exchanging anchors**: each gives the other a rivet from their own belt, and the two rivets are joined by a blacksmith into a single loop. Divorce is rare and is done by cutting the loop and casting the halves into a deep chasm. Children are named after weather, machines, or plants: Throttle, Drip, Clinker, Soot, Fern, Mistral, Bellows, Static, Latch, Cinder, Piston, Tallow. Surnames come from their district or trade: Mothkeep, Pipeweld, Staircounter, Tinward, Fogbrace, Quarryboom.

Death is handled with pragmatism. When someone dies, their anchor is removed and hung on the district’s anchor-tree—a dead ironwood trunk covered with hundreds of rivets, gears, nails—while the body is carried to a crack in the rock and covered with quicklime and moss. The name is carved into a beam in the district hall, and the crows are given a portion of the funeral feast so they will not steal the name. If someone has blurred too far before death, the community holds a **fixing**: they trace the person’s outline on a stable wall with chalk and charcoal, then hammer nails along the lines, so the image is held. The body is buried as usual, but the outline remains, and passersby touch it and say the name.

The greatest fear is not death but **unraveling**—losing one’s edges until one becomes a Sketched. The Sketched are the not-quite-people who drift through the unfinished streets, made of fog and charcoal and the memory of a life. Some are former inhabitants who dropped their anchors or walked too far into a blurred district. Others are possibilities, the people who might have lived here if the mountains had finished differently. They are seen as figures in the mist, repeating a gesture—a hand raised to knock, a step taken over a threshold, a mouth forming a word. They have no voices, or their voices are the sound of wind through a loose cable. They are not hostile, but they are hungry for definition. If you speak to one, you give it a little of your edge; if you give it your name, it may take your place. The fog-readers say that the Sketched are the mountain’s way of asking what should exist, and the city is the answer, but the answer is incomplete.

The nonhuman residents are just as much a part of the city. The cinder crows are not mere birds; they have their own politics, their own territories, their own grudges. A crow will remember a face that threw a stone for ten years and will drop cinders on that person’s hat. They mimic not just speech but the exact clang of a dropped wrench, the hiss of a valve, the count of a tallyman. Some crow colonies have learned to operate simple latches to steal food from storehouses. The people say that if you listen to the crows at dusk, you can hear them discussing the next day’s work.

The wind-eaters are not pets but neighbors. They drift above the smog in slow circles, and when one descends to eye level, people stop and speak to it in low voices, offering a pinch of lichen or a drop of seep-water. The wind-eater cannot speak, but its tendrils brush the offerer’s hand, and the fog-readers claim this is a form of acknowledgment.

And there are the machines. The old machines are treated like elders. The great piston pump in the Lower Works, which beats once every few seconds, has a name—Old Thump—and the pipe-walkers oil its fittings and speak to it before each shift. The stamping press is called the Door-Shutter, and the cranes are given names like Slow Ansel and Half-Turn. No one knows if the machines are alive, but everyone knows that the city’s solidity depends on their continued rhythm, so they are cared for with the same attention as a sick relative.

A typical day begins not with a bell but with the first piston-beat of the great pump, a thud felt through the stone like a heartbeat. People rise, touch their anchors, step over their thresholds, and go to their shifts. In the foundry district, the pourers light the furnaces; the heat pushes back the fog, and for a moment the edges of the buildings are sharp. On the high bridges, the pipe-walkers clip their harnesses and walk the trestles, tapping every joint. The tallymen begin their counts, chalk dust on their fingers. The crow-keepers call the crows down from the chimneys with a whistle that sounds like escaping steam. The children scatter to collect lichen and cliff-urchin plates, staying away from the unfinished ledges unless accompanied by a sketch-chaser.

At midday, the fog lifts slightly, and the wind-eaters drift above the smog, their groans echoing down. People pause to listen; the fog-readers take notes. In the afternoon, the giant stamping press falls with a sound like a door shutting in bedrock, and everyone, even the crows, is silent for three seconds. Then the noise returns.

Evening is not marked by darkness, because the dimness never deepens much; instead, the arc lamps brighten, and the sodium lamps glow like moons in the mist. People gather in the public galleries, drinking seep-water and lichen mead, playing cards made from pressed moth wings, or listening to the tallymen read the day’s counts like a litany: *Stair One, forty-four. Stair Two, forty-four. Stair Three, forty-five…* The children fall asleep to the rhythm, and the adults continue their quiet talk, never raising their voices above the ambient drone, because the city is loud enough to carry all their words.

Consider one of them, to see the whole:

Marna Pipeweld is neither old nor young—age is hard to track when the world itself has not decided how long a year is. She is a pipe-walker, broad-shouldered, with a face like a weathered gear. Her left eye is clouded from an arc-flash; her right eye is sharp and yellow. She wears a waxed canvas coat over three wool shirts, and on her belt is a loop of copper that was her mother’s anchor and her father’s before that. Every morning she climbs the trestle over the East Chasm, tapping each joint with a small hammer. She listens for the sound of a pipe that has become less solid overnight. When she finds one, she does not panic; she marks it with chalk, wraps a wire around it, and speaks its name aloud—*You are the North Vent, third section from the east tower*—as if reminding the metal of itself. By evening, the pipe is solid again. Marna is not magic; she is, like all her people, a living argument for existence.

These are the ones who live in the Ledges: not a nation, not a tribe, but a maintenance crew for a world that will not finish itself. They are damp, scarred, stubborn, and loud. They are the answer the mountain keeps asking for.
