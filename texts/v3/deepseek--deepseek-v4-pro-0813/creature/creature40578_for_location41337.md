At first you think the place is empty of anything larger than a moth. The noise is so constant that it erases individual voices, and the warm wet air makes everything look like it is breathing. But then, when you stay still, the lives emerge.

The first ones you see are the masons. They are not ants, though ants live here too. They are smaller than your smallest fingernail, soft-bodied, the color of old candle wax, with no eyes left to speak of—only a faint dark dot where each eye might have been. They move in lines, but not the frantic lines of ants. Their movement is slower, more deliberate, each one carrying something: a bead of red clay, a splinter of stem, a fragment of leaf, a pale egg, a dead sister. They touch one another as they pass, not with antennae only but with their whole bodies, stopping a moment, exchanging food, scent, information, then moving on.

If you follow a line downward, it leads to the wet places where the walls sweat and the red clay is soft. There the masons gather the clay, rolling it into beads with their forelegs and their mouths, coating it with a shining fluid from their abdomens. In their mouths the clay becomes something else: not mud but mortar, smooth and dark, barely wet, hardening slowly like resin. They carry the bead up the wall, through root tunnels and over old egg cases, and fit it into place wherever the wall has cracked or sagged. They do not lay bricks; they melt the bead into the existing surface, pushing and shaping it with their heads, then go back down for another. They repeat this for their entire lives.

Some of them have larger heads and pinching jaws. These are the guards. They stand at the edges of the trails, antennae lifted, and if you touch one it will bite with surprising strength for such a soft body. The workers ignore the guards. The guards ignore the workers. But if you watch long enough, you see that when a worker returns with a bead of clay, a guard will sometimes touch her head and take the bead, carry it a few steps farther, and pass it to another worker. The line is not a single column; it is a chain of small transfers, a relay of clay.

They are not the only builders. Higher up, where the canopy opens in a few tea-colored rays, there are wasps with long thin waists, black and bronze, that build open paper combs under the branches. There are potter wasps that make round mud pots the size of fists, each one a single nursery for a single larva. There are silk tents woven by caterpillars, whole galleries of gray silk that sag with rainwater and then dry into stiff paper. And everywhere there are roots: roots that push through the walls, split them, then heal them; roots that follow the mason trails; roots that grow through the bodies of dead builders and feed on them.

But the masons are the oldest, or so it seems. Their tunnels run deeper than any other builder's, down through the accumulated strata where the walls turn black and mineral-stained. Down there, in the lower galleries, the air is close and nearly unbreathable, thick with carbon dioxide and the smell of wet clay. The masons keep gardens there—not green gardens, but fungal gardens, pale and blind as they are, tended on beds of chewed wood and guano. The workers feed the fungus; the fungus feeds the workers; the queen, if she can be called that, is a long pale body in a chamber the size of a human fist, attended by dozens of workers who groom her and carry away her eggs. She is not a ruler. She is an ovary, a slow engine of replacement.

Above the masons, on the same walls, the frogs sit. They are small, most no bigger than a thumbnail, with smooth skin and huge dark eyes. They do not hop much; they press themselves into the moist hollows, in the cup of a bromeliad or the crotch of a root, and they call. The sound is not a chorus; it is a city, a continuous metallic ringing that rises and falls, shifts key, doubles and trebles. Each frog is a pinpoint of sound, but together they are a body of sound, a second atmosphere. They lay their eggs in the pools of water held in leaves and hollows. The tadpoles are tiny black commas that feed on organic debris. The frogs do not need to leave the walls; the walls are their pond, their forest, their sky.

The birds come for the frogs and the masons. They are small and gray-green, with long toes and short stiff tails, and they hang upside down from the walls like leaves, waiting. One of them, a male with a patch of rust-colored feathers on his throat, has staked out a section of wall near a mason trail. He watches the line of pale workers, then darts forward, plucks one, and returns to his perch to swallow it. He does not eat the clay bead; he drops it, and it falls to the trail below, where a worker picks it up and continues on. The bird's nest is a soft cup of moss and spider silk glued to the wall, with two eggs the color of river foam. When the chicks hatch, they are fed on a paste of masons and small frogs.

In the upper lattice, troops of small primates move through the branches, shaking down water and leaves. They are dark, almost black, with long tails and pale faces, and they move not in a line but in a loose scatter, calling to one another with soft clicking sounds that are almost lost in the frog noise. They drink from bromeliad cups, eat fruit and insects, and sometimes take a frog or a bird's egg. They use the built terraces as highways, following the same routes for generations, wearing shallow depressions in the moss. They are not the architects, but they are part of the architecture: their fur carries seeds, their dung fertilizes the walls, their bodies host mites that feed the birds.

All the while, the plants are not passive. The tree ferns and small palms are rooted in the walls, their trunks thick with moss and their skirts of dead fronds adding to the structure. Their roots pry open the mason galleries, then are swallowed by new wall. A strangler fig wraps a pillar of old nests, its roots becoming a ladder for the primates and a gutter for the rain. Epiphytes catch the mist; their leaves hold water; the water holds frogs. The plants are not just the furniture of the place. They are its skeleton and its skin, stretching and growing and dying into the architecture.

In the darker, wetter chambers, where the ponds have formed in collapsed galleries, there are creatures that never leave the water. They are pale, without pigment, with gills like red feathers on the sides of their heads. They lie in the black water and breathe the slow oxygen that seeps through. When a frog tadpole or an insect larva drifts down, they rise and swallow it, then settle back. They are not fish; they are something older, with tiny feet and a slow, rippling tail. Their eggs are attached to the roots that grow down through the water.

The bats come and go from the lower galleries, pouring out at dusk in a cloud of leather and piping. They roost in the deepest chambers, hanging from the roots that pierce the ceiling. The floor below them is deep in guano, and the guano is not waste but wealth: it feeds the fungal gardens, the roots, the masons, the flies that swarm in thick mats. The bats bring back food from outside the valley—moths, beetles, even small birds—and their bodies return nutrients to the built ground. They are the sky's contribution to the structure.

And there are others, less visible: the beetles that bore through the softer walls, leaving tunnels that the masons then seal; the moths the size of hands that flop through the dim air, their wings like wet bark; the lizards that flatten themselves against the warm mud, waiting for a fly; the snake like a dark rope that pours over a root and disappears into a round hole, perhaps to eat a nestling, perhaps to be eaten by a larger snake. None of them are guests. Each one is a cell in the body of the place.

The most startling thing is not any one creature but the way they are woven together. The masons do not know the frogs; the frogs do not know the birds; the birds do not know the bats. But their lives are braided so tightly that you cannot pull one thread without the whole thing shifting. The masons build the walls; the walls hold the water; the water holds the frogs; the frogs feed the birds; the birds drop guano; the guano feeds the masons. The place is not a habitat. It is a body, and its inhabitants are its organs, its blood, its breath.

If you sit still for an hour, in the warm wet dark, with the sound pressing on your ears, you may begin to feel that the distinction between living and non-living is not as clear as you thought. The mud walls are made of clay and saliva. The roots are alive but feel like stone. The water moves and carves but has no will. The frogs are alive but become sound. The masons are alive but become architecture. The whole valley is a single organism, built of billions of small selves, each doing what it must, in the dark, in the noise, in the warm wet air.
