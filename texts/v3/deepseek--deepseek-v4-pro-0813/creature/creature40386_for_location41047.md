There is no who here. The inhabitant is a what, and the what is the whole green shaft.

It has no face. If you need one, the duckweed mat at the surface is its face—a thick, green skin that wrinkles only when a bubble rises or the water level shifts. It has no eyes, but the light panels beside each old elevator door are its eyes: cool white suns shining sideways through the water, and every leaf turns toward them like a pupil. It has no arms, but the vines around the rails are its arms, and the roots hanging from the open doors are its fingers, its hair, its gut.

It is not an animal. No fish, no frog, no midge, no water strider lives in it. The shapes that suggest animals—the curve like a fish, the coil like a snake—are only roots turning in a thermal eddy. The animal is absent. In its place is a body made of plants and the microscopic life that clings to them. The plants are the flesh. The bacteria and algae are the hide and the digestion. The water is the blood. The pump is the heartbeat, too low to hear but felt in the teeth. The elevator car, stopped between the seventh and eighth floors, is the stone in its center, a buried object the roots have grown around.

It eats by being still. The water is a faintly salty mineral broth, and the roots sieve it. The leaves catch the skylight and the landing panels. The gas bladders hold some plants at the brightest depth. The jets near the top push all the leaves one way, so the whole body leans as if listening. It breathes through every green surface. Oxygen from the leaves becomes small bubbles that rise and vanish. It does not hunt. It does not flee. It fills.

Its life is slow enough to make a human observer feel like a nervous intruder. A leaf unfurls over a day. A vine extends a finger-width overnight. The duckweed mat drifts a handspan in an hour. The body does not move through the shaft; it is the shaft. Every cubic meter is occupied—leaf, stem, root, drifting dust. There is no open water left. The saturation is its shape. The pressure at the bottom is not a burden but a kind of deep, green sleep.

The sound of it is not a voice. It is the tick of cooling metal, the hiss of a bubble escaping from a leaf, condensation dripping onto the duckweed, the subsonic hum of the pump. The quiet is not peaceful and not lonely. It is the quiet of something too full to speak. If you stayed long enough, you might almost hear the cells dividing. You might almost feel the water change temperature by a tenth of a degree. You might notice a new root pressing through the glass face of a gauge, and the needle still moving, reading the pressure of the world it has become.

It has no name, but if you needed one, you would call it the Green Shaft, same as the place. Or you might call it the Gardener, because it tends itself. Or the Crop, because it is both field and yield. It has never known soil. Its roots grow into synthetic mats, plastic mesh, glass wool, and mineral water. It has never known weather. Its only seasons are the slow alteration of light through the skylight. It does not compete, because there is nothing to compete with. It has been given the whole shaft, and it has taken it completely.

It is not waiting for anything. It is not trying to escape. It is exactly what lives here.
