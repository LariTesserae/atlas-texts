Ask what lives in the bowl, and the first answer is easy: the gong-throated frogs, the copper-green lizards, the oil-film butterflies, the grey grazers, the birds in the high crevices, the endless insects. But these are not the ones who *live* there in the way you mean. The bowl has a resident people. They are not obvious. They are not loud. They have no name that translates; the nearest is a two-note hum, like water over stone, which might be rendered as **the Dwellers**, or **the Listening Ones**.

They are small—no taller than a human child when they stand straight, which they rarely do. They move in a crouch, long arms reaching to the rock, fingers splayed as if they are reading the stone. Their skin is smooth and damp, mottled grey-green, with patches of darker pigmentation that shift slowly with the light, so that when they press against the walls they nearly vanish. Their eyes are large, set wide, and they hold the green shadows like polished lenses. They have no visible ears; the sides of their heads are slightly concave, and the skin there vibrates visibly when the hum changes. Their toes are long and webbed, their hands six-fingered, each finger with an extra joint that lets them grip the crevices with a strange, boneless ease.

They live in the walls, in the deep cracks and under the overhanging vegetation. They do not build. They carve—slowly, over generations, wearing hollows into the gneiss with the friction of their bodies and the patience of water. Each family group, three to seven individuals, occupies a recess high enough to stay dry, low enough to catch the fog. The hollows are smooth as the bench, polished by centuries of resting bodies. They line them with moss and shed skin. They sleep piled together, breathing in unison, their skin drinking the moisture.

The structures are not theirs, but they are their inheritance. The arch near the cleft is a threshold: they pass through it only at certain times, always with the left hand touching the stone, always in the same direction. The circular basin is their water source and their clock; they read its level and clarity as you might read the sky. The bench is the place where the eldest sits, sometimes for days, one hand pressed flat against the warm stone, the other cupped over the side of their head. The three rods they call **the Chords**. Each Dweller has a name that is a single pitch, and they are taught from infancy to match their voice to the nearest rod. The rods hum at three pitches; the Dwellers sing in three parts. When the whole group stands near the eastern wall, the air around them shivers.

They are not the only people of the bowl. The frogs are a kind of people, the Dwellers say; their gong-like calls are not random but arranged, and the Dwellers learn to imitate them. The grazers are the **Old Ones**, the first inhabitants, who carry the bowl’s memory in their slow, deliberate paths. The Dwellers tend them, clearing the way, leaving piles of sweet water plants where the grazers will find them. The birds are their messengers; the lizards their timekeepers. The insects are not individuals but a weather of life, and the Dwellers move through their clouds without swatting, accepting the bites as part of the exchange.

The Dwellers’ day is shaped by the fog. They wake as it pours down the cleft, rising from their hollows to stand in the white drift, letting the moisture bead on their skin. They move to the basin, touch the water with the backs of their hands, then touch their chests. The eldest sits on the bench, and for a long time nobody speaks. Then, softly, they begin to hum. It starts as a single tone from the eldest, then the others join, each matching one of the rods, until the bowl fills with a chord that seems to come from the stone itself. The frogs fall silent to listen. The lizards stop on the walls. Even the insects’ drone seems to lower.

They spend the morning tending the bowl. They clear the seep with their long fingers, removing any debris that might slow its weeping. They check the stone channels, realigning tiny pebbles so the water flows in the correct curve. They harvest ferns and fungi, catch beetles and small larvae. They do not eat much; their metabolism is slow, powered by the warmth and the water and the hum. They eat only what the bowl offers in surplus—never the frogs, never the grazers. They talk while they work, not in words but in a language of clicks, throat-sounds, and hums, many of which are too low for a human ear. It sounds like water dripping and stone settling and the distant call of frogs all woven together.

In the afternoon, when the sun reaches the floor, they bask. They lie on the warm stone, arranging themselves in patterns that follow the light, their skins darkening to absorb the heat. They are not lazy; this is when they listen. The hum is strongest in the afternoon, and they lie with their heads turned to the pool, eyes half-closed, reading the pulse of the blue glow. The younger Dwellers practice their songs, mimicking the rods; the older ones teach them the sequences that have been passed down for so many generations they are no longer counted. Some of the sequences are not memory but improvisation, guided by the hum’s changes. The Dwellers say the machine sings to them, and they sing back.

At dusk, they gather at the pool’s edge. The frogs begin their chorus, and the Dwellers join, not with human voices but with a deep, resonant humming that matches the frogs’ gong-like calls. The sound rises and falls in waves, and the blue glow pulses beneath the water, and for a while the whole bowl is a single instrument. The Dwellers sway, their bodies moving in synchrony, their reflections rippling on the pool. They are not performing; they are working. This is the maintenance, the tuning. The rods’ pitches waver slightly, and the Dwellers adjust their voices to stabilize them. The pool’s glow brightens and fades; the Dwellers’ humming slows it, steadies it. They are the machine’s living governors, or perhaps its vocal cords.

They are not the builders. They know this. The arch, the rods, the pool’s glow—these were old before their species existed. The Dwellers have no myths of making; they have only myths of listening. They believe they were called into being by the hum, shaped from the frogs or the stone or the fog to be the ones who tend the bowl. Their origin story is not a story but a feeling: the first Dweller was a frog who heard the hum and tried to match it, and over a million years its descendants grew hands and minds and voices. Whether this is true is unknown, but the Dwellers and the frogs share a genetic resemblance, and the Dwellers’ tadpoles are nearly identical to the frogs’ until they grow.

The Dwellers reproduce slowly. A female lays one or two eggs in the pool’s shallows, in a nest of water plants she has woven. The eggs glow faintly. The tadpoles are tended by the whole group, fed on algae and the pool’s microscopic life. They metamorphose after several years, emerging from the water with legs and lungs and the first faint ability to hum. They grow for decades, learning the bowl’s rhythms, before they are allowed to touch the rods or sit on the bench. The eldest Dweller—an ancient, the size of a large dog, its skin so wrinkled it resembles the pitted stone—is said to be over five hundred years old, and it has not left the bowl in centuries.

They never leave. The plateau above is a place of open sky and endless wind, and the Dwellers fear it. The fog that fills the bowl each morning is their sky; the cleft is their horizon. Sometimes, in the dry season, when the fog is thin and the hum is faint, the youngest Dwellers climb to the lip of the cleft and peer out. They see the flatness, the light, the absence of green. They hear the wind, which sounds nothing like the hum. They return quickly, pressing their bodies against the warm stone, and they do not speak of it. The bowl is their entire world, and they believe it is the entire world. The plateau is a dream, a void, a place where the hum cannot reach.

The machine, the Dwellers say, is not a machine. It is **the Heart**. It is the voice that taught the first frog to sing, that carved the arch, that warms the stone and lights the pool. It is the reason the bowl exists. They do not worship it; they tend it as a gardener tends a plant, or as a body tends its own heart. They know its rhythms, its moods, its slow changes over centuries. They know that it is failing—not soon, but eventually; the pulse has slowed over the last hundred years, and the fog comes later each morning. They do not worry. They believe that when the Heart stops, the bowl will grow cold, and they will stop with it, and that will be as it should be. Their task is not to save it but to keep it company.

And so if you ask who lives here, the answer is not one thing. The frogs live here, and the lizards, and the grazers, and the birds, and the insects, and the Dwellers, and the Heart. But if you want a face, look for the Dwellers. They are there, pressed against the walls, watching you from the green shadows, wondering what you are. They will not approach. They will not speak. But if you sit quietly on the bench, they may come down, one by one, and touch the stone beside you, and begin to hum. And if you hum back—even a single note—they will look at you with their polished green eyes, and for a moment you will be part of the bowl’s long, slow, living song.
