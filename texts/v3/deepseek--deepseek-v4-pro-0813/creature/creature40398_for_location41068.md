There is a tenant. Not a ghost; a tenant. It is the thing that makes the room feel occupied even when the only movement is dripping water. You will not see it at first, because it has learned to be part of the wall. But wait.

First there is a new sound. The room already has a drone and a hiss and the rattle of loose rivets. But if you listen, there is a lower note, a kind of soft percussion: a weight being placed on the open grating, spacing each step so carefully that it sounds like a slow heartbeat. It is not the building settling. It is something walking.

Then a shape forms in the steam. Not a man. It only looks like a man if your eye is tired and wants to make a man out of a shadow. It stands near the central column, where the pressure gauges are, and it wraps one long arm around the column as though it is listening to the pipes. It is tall, but it does not stand tall; it folds into the space between the pipe runs and the wall, so that it becomes part of the tangle.

It wears the room. Its back and shoulders are covered in moss, thick as a fist, the same moss that grows on the north wall. Ferns uncurl from its elbows and from the knobs of its spine. A small bromeliad sits in the hollow of its collarbone, holding a spoonful of water. Vines with gluey pads loop around its upper arm and follow the line of its bicep like a living sleeve. It has no clothing; its skin is the color of the copper pipes, a green-brown verdigris on the knees, the elbows, the knuckles. It shines faintly, as if it has been polished by the same condensation.

Its face is long and narrow. The eyes are round and lidless, the colour of old pressure glass, and they do not blink. The mouth is a small lipless slit, open just enough to breathe the steam, and its breath is a thin green fog. It has no nose, or only a ridge where a nose might be, and its ears are holes plugged with fine moss. It does not need to hear with them; it hears with its feet, through the grating, with the whole room as a drum.

Its arms are too long, and they end in hands that are not quite hands: six fingers, each tipped with a soft pad like a gecko's, the skin between them webbed. It can hold a hot pipe without burning, not because it is immune, but because its palms have grown a callus as thick as the insulation on the main condenser. It can turn a small valve with one finger. It can read the temperature of the green pipe by laying the back of its hand a hair's breadth above the metal. It never touches the green pipe. It knows the yellow valve is seized, and it never forces it; instead it licks the valve stem with a long, moss-green tongue, once, twice, and leaves it.

It moves with the patience of rust. Each step is deliberate. It walks the outer ledge, close to the wall, where the grating is strongest. Its feet are broad, with long toes that curl around the diamond plate. It knows the galvanized plates at the south end have thinned; it will not step there. If it drops something—a dead frond, a snail shell, a shed piece of its own skin—it does not watch it fall. It drops into the shaft and is gone.

It tends the room. Not as a caretaker with a list of tasks, but as a body tends its own skin. It plucks dead fronds from the pipe joints and lays them in the drain channels. It rights a frog that has fallen on its back and sets it back into its bromeliad. It drinks from the bromeliad at its collarbone, then from a cup on the wall, tipping it with one long finger, never taking more than half. It eats the blind fish-things from the warm runoff, one by one, without hurrying. It eats the moss, pulling a pinch from its own shoulder and chewing slowly. It is both the garden and the gardener.

The other life in the room knows it. The frogs climb onto its feet and sit in the webs of its hands. The geckos run along its arms as if they were hot pipes. The white spiders build their wet webs between its fingers. It does not disturb them. When it sleeps, curled in the mouth of the old condenser, between the soft green insulation and the warm metal, the frogs call at the same pitch as the turbine, and the tenant's breathing is the slow expansion of the joints. The room and the tenant breathe together.

It has no name. If it ever had one, it has been absorbed the way a dropped tool is absorbed by the sump—returned, years later, as a mineral-encrusted lump no one can identify. It is not a person, though it may have been one once. It is not an animal, though it breathes and eats and sleeps. It is not a plant, though it is green and still. It is the thing that happens when a person stays too long in a place that has stopped distinguishing between organism and apparatus. It is the room's keeper, or its child, or its scar.

It did not arrive. It grew, the way moss grows on a rivet, the way a vine follows a torsion cable. There may have been a man once, a last operator, or a hermit, or someone who came to shut the plant down and found the steam too warm. But the room does not remember, and neither does the tenant. It has no language for before. It only knows the pulse of the expansion joints, the eleven-hour valve, the nine minutes of fog. It has been here as long as the moss, and the moss does not ask.

It does not speak, but sometimes it hums. The hum is not a song; it is the exact frequency of the turbine, so low and steady that after a moment you cannot tell if the sound is coming from the machine or from the hollow of the tenant's chest. When it hums, the frogs stop. The whole room becomes one note. And for that moment, the tenant is not a thing that lives in the room. It is the room, with the room living in it.
