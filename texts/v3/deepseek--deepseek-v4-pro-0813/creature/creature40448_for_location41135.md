The first resident is the wind. It is the oldest and the only one that never has to hide. It lives in the long corridors, along the combed ridges, through the arches and swallow-holes, and it turns the stone into one continuous note. Every other inhabitant has adapted to that note, not by leaving it but by fitting into its gaps.

Then there are the ones who live in the air. The white-rumped swifts are the most numerous birds on the plateau. Their nests are in the pockets of the undercut walls, in shaded grottoes where the wind cannot tear at their eggs. The swifts are black-brown with a band of white above the tail; in the high light they are not birds at all but slivers of the shaded stone come loose. They drink from the black basins by flying low and scooping the water. They gather aerial insects—small flies, spiderlings—lifted off the moss by the updrafts. They do not land except at the nest; their legs are small, their wings long. They live most of their lives at speed, entering the west wind like needles into cloth. The corridors are their streets. In the evening, when the light goes high and grey, they pour down into the slots and vanish, and the stone seems to absorb them.

At the plateau’s western rim, where the ground breaks toward the icefields, the choughs live. They are black, with red legs and long curved red bills. They are playful in a way that the wind makes possible. They spend hours above the rim, wings tilted, riding the pressure wave without a single stroke. Then one will fold its wings and drop, rolling or slipping sideways, before spreading its wings again just above the stone. Their calls are sharp and ringing, “kee-aw,” and they carry over the chord like small bright scratches. They eat snails and insects in the moss, and sometimes they follow the eagles to a kill. They nest in the deep crevices, on ledges too narrow for any mammal. The choughs are the only inhabitants that seem to enjoy the wind as a thing in itself, rather than simply endure it.

The eagles are the fewest. There are perhaps two pairs, dark, enormous, hanging over the western icefields. They do not hunt the open plateau often; the pickings are too thin and the ground too exposed. They take marmots when they can, and pikas when they are careless. They are more a condition of the sky than a presence, small black crosses in the blue, a reminder that the food web here is long and thin and every animal’s watchfulness has a reason.

On and under the stone, the mammals are small and old-fashioned. The pika is the most common. It is a round, nearly tailless animal, the colour of pale weathered limestone, with small round ears and a rabbit’s face. It lives in the cracks between the old rounded blocks, in the low talus and in the solution pockets at the base of the walls. It never crosses the wide pavement if it can help it. Its territory is a small circuit of exits, sunning stones, and grass patches. It gathers the fog-grass and saxifrage, spreads it on the warm slabs to cure, and stores it in hidden heaps under the stones. Those haypiles are the only storehouses on the plateau. The pikas are diurnal and nocturnal by turns, because the pale night is not fully dark and the cold never buries them. Their voice is a single high “eenh” that cuts through the wind like a needle. If you stand still in a corridor, the pikas will call from all sides, and you will understand that the stone is not as empty as it looks. Their call is pitched not to sound like the wind but to be separate from it.

The marmot is the heavy one. It is the colour of old paper and shadow, with a dark cap on its head. It lives in the deeper fissures, where enough windblown dust has gathered to form a kind of soil. It sits upright at the mouth of its den, forepaws folded on its chest, looking out over the stone. Its whistle is high and urgent, a single descending note, and when it sounds, the pikas freeze and the choughs lift. The marmots here are not fat; they are dense, muscular, long-furred. They move with a cumbersome care between the damp moss patches, never far from their bolt-holes. They cannot hide in the open, so they have become watchfulness itself. They are the eyes of the plateau, the ones who notice the eagle before it is more than a speck.

The rock hyrax is harder to see. It is not the large African hyrax; it is a mouse-sized, pale-grey animal that lives only in the dry overhangs on south-facing walls. It is a relict, an old lineage that has shrunk and paled until it fits the high limestone. It keeps a cache of plant material under a slab, arranged like a small fan. The cache is ferried from the fog-grass patches, one stem at a time, and left to dry in the shadow. It is not a nest, not a food store in the pika’s sense; it is a little arrangement, tended with the same patience as the stone. The hyrax moves in short, pausing runs, and freezes against the rock when the swifts’ shadow passes.

The water is the other center. In the black basins, the frogs live. They are small, thumbnail-sized, black or very dark brown, and they sit half-submerged with only their eyes and a strip of back above the surface. They are the plateau’s only engineers. In the spring-like pockets, a frog will dig a shallow channel from its own cistern to a lower basin, using its hind feet, sometimes over several nights. The channel is no wider than a finger and winds along the stone, and the frog maintains it, clearing away silt and the pale algae. The lower basin then fills, and the frog uses it for tadpoles. It is a small behaviour, invisible unless you look, but it is deliberate and repeated. It is the closest thing to agriculture or irrigation the plateau has. The frogs do not call in a chorus; their voice is a low wooden tap, easily swallowed by the wind. But in the quieter hollows, if you lie down beside a basin, you may hear it: a sound like a pebble knocked against wet stone.

In the same pools live the fairy shrimp. They are almost transparent, rowing upside down through the cold water with a fine, feathery motion. They graze on the thin algae and on the dust that settles on the surface. They have no hurry. Their pools rarely dry fully, so they have become long-lived, and their eggs are less a necessity than a memory. The caddisflies are the other water-dwellers. Their cases are made of tiny limestone grains, and they crawl in the runnels and channels, their cases exactly the colour of the stone. They are so small that the water seems to have a slight movement, a fine grain, as if the plateau had begun to walk.

The spiders live in the dry overhangs, in the still air just behind the stone’s surface. They are pale, almost colourless, with long legs. They spin small webs across the mouths of rock pockets, and the webs catch the insects that rise from the damp moss. The wind destroys the webs by afternoon, and the spiders rebuild in the slack hours of the night. They are the only creatures that make a new structure every day, and even they make it as lightly as breath. Their silk is visible only in the low light, when the sun slants across the corridor.

The highland jackdaws are the most complicated of the birds. They live in the slots and large pockets of the deep corridors, in family groups of three to ten. They are black with a grey hood and a pale eye. They are not numerous, but they are social, and their calls are the strongest voices on the plateau. They use pebble hammers to break snail shells. A jackdaw will select a flat stone, often a hard ledge at the base of a wall, and carry snails or pebbles to it. The anvil stones are not made; they are found, but they are kept. The centers are polished white from years of use, and around them lie small crushed spirals of shell, like a midden. The jackdaws pass these anvil sites down, it seems, because the same stones are used season after season, and the birds return to them even after long absences. They use their hammers precisely: they pick up a small pebble in the bill, strike the snail’s shell, then eat the soft body. Sometimes they drop the snail from a height, but the pebble is more certain. This is not tool use as a human would recognize it, but it is real. The jackdaws are the only animals on the plateau that use one stone to act on another, and they have been doing it long enough for the stone to remember.

The blackbird of the plateau is rarer. It is the colour of the black patina on the limestone, with a dark bill and a dark eye. It lives in the shaded slots, where the dampness lasts and the snails are easiest to find. It uses the same anvils as the jackdaws, waiting until they have left before picking over the broken shells. Its voice is low, a single note repeated twice and then a pause, as though it has learned the rhythm of the wind and then silenced it. In the late light, it drops a snail onto a polished stone, and the little crack of the shell is the only separate sound you will hear all day.

And then there is the other order of inhabitant, the one that is not a species but a pattern. The swifts, choughs, pikas, marmots, hyraxes, frogs, shrimp, caddisflies, spiders, jackdaws, and the single blackbird are not merely sharing a place. They are part of a standing wave. The wind is constant, and so the swifts’ flight paths are constant. The moisture is rhythmic, and so the frogs’ channels are rhythmic. The stone is durable, and so the jackdaws’ anvil stones endure. None of them builds a city. None of them leaves a mark that will outlast the season, except the polished white centers of the anvils. But they are all inhabitants in the same way the fluting, the basins, and the columns are inhabitants: not arrivals, not colonists, but parts of a place that has already become itself.

The plateau does not have a human story. It has a bird story, a pika story, a frog story, and those stories are told in the same tense as the wind. If you listen long enough, the chord stops being noise and becomes the sound of many lives fitting themselves into a single, unprogressive world.
