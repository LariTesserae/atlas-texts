At first, the chamber seems uninhabited. The light is too even, the air too hot, the mineral edges too fresh. You could stand for ten minutes and see nothing but steam and glowing stone. But if you wait until the pool domes and spills, you may notice a small interruption in the water.

The oldest resident is not the animal. It is the grey film on the wall near the vent. It is a living mat, a biofilm of archaea and bacteria, grey as wet ash, with a faint bloom like graphite. It does not photosynthesize. It has no leaves, no roots, no mouth. It feeds on sulfur and iron carried in the steam. It is older than plants, older than the idea of a leaf. Its body is a thin sheet of cells, each one a sack of hot chemistry. It grows inward from the wall, not fast, but constantly, following the condensation line. Where the steam touches it, it thickens. It may take a year to spread a hand’s width. Its edge is not a clean line; it is a frayed shoreline of living and dead cells, new cells pressing outward, old cells hardening into mineral. If you touched it, it would feel like warm wet velvet, but you would not want to touch it long. It is not dangerous, but it is delicate and older than your kind. Its life is a slow breath: it takes in hydrogen sulfide and iron, and it excretes a faint acid that etches the rock. That etching may be why the sinter grows a little differently near its edge, a little more crystalline, a little more fragile.

The amphipod is the only animal, and it is easy to miss. It is colourless, not white but transparent, a grain of rice with a curved spine and a head too small to read. It has no shadow, because the light comes from all walls. It is blind, or nearly so. It does not need eyes; the light is constant and directionless, and in such light eyes would tell it nothing about depth or distance. Instead its two antennules trail forward and down, tasting the water for temperature and sulfur. Its body is segmented like a tiny crystal, each segment a chamber of clear muscle and gut. You can see its heart, a faint pulse near the back of its head. You can see the grey thread of its gut, which after a good visit to the wall runs dark with the film it has scraped away.

It lives in the pool, but not in the whole pool. The pool is too hot at its throat and too mineral-charged. The amphipod lives in a narrow band near the surface, where the water has cooled just enough and where oxygen gathers as the steam condenses. It does not swim aimlessly. It waits. Every seven or eight minutes, the pool surges. The hot water rises from below, lifts the surface into a low dome, and spills over the sinter rim. The amphipod rides that surge. It does not fight it. The surge carries it toward the rim, where the water is shallow, cooler, and touched by the air. There it turns on its side and scrapes the waterline with its mouthparts, taking a few cells of the grey film. Then the surge recedes, and it sinks back with the water, down to the deeper layer, where it is safer from the air and the mineral crust. It does this again and again, not because it thinks, but because its body is tuned to the pool’s pulse. It is the pulse made small.

If you watch for an hour, you may see it rise six or eight times, always in the same region, always with the surge. It is a rare animal. There may be two or three in the whole pool. They do not school or call. They are solitary, but not lonely. Their whole population is held in a single pool, and their only food is the grey film, which grows slowly. They are not preyed upon. Nothing here wants them. The only danger is the heat, which would cook them if they strayed too deep, and the mineral, which would coat them if they stayed still too long.

Once, if you were patient for days, you might see a moult. The amphipod would split its old cuticle along the back and pull itself free, leaving a perfect transparent husk in the water. The husk would drift for a few minutes, then begin to gather a fine white fur of silica. Within an hour it would sink, a ghost of itself becoming part of the floor. The new body would be even paler, almost invisible, and for a few hours it would hide near the coolest part of the pool while its new cuticle hardened. If it is a female, she would carry eggs in a brood pouch under her body, tiny spheres visible through the pouch, and in time they would hatch as miniature amphipods, already knowing the surge. The young do not learn by watching. They learn by being born into the pulse.

The mineral light on the walls is not alive, but it is easy to mistake for a living thing. It glows brighter after each flood, as if breathing. It is a natural optical cavity, a photonic crystal of silica and water. It turns the deep heat of the rock into a blue-white light. That light is the amphipod’s sky. It is also the temperature regulator that keeps the condensation steady. The film and the amphipod do not see the light as you do, but they are shaped by it: the film grows where the water condenses, and the amphipod rises where the water is cool enough, so the light sets the boundaries of their shared room.

So the answer to “who lives here” is not a single creature. It is a small knot of beings. The oldest is the grey mat, older than plants, eating the earth’s breath. The only animal is the transparent amphipod, riding the surge, cropping the mat. And between them, the non-living light pulses, making the room bright and breath-like. It is not a rich ecosystem. It is a narrow, extreme, patient one. But it is inhabited. The emptiness is not absence; it is the space these lives require. In that hot white hollow under the ground, that is enough.
