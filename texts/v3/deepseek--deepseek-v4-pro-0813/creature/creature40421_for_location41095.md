They are not the first thing you notice. The first thing you notice is the stillness, and then the fact that part of the stillness has a face.

The people of the Sort, the Upcast, the Ledger—call them what you will—are built like the city: slender, layered, deliberate, and slightly damp. They are pale in the way of things that have lived for many cycles under frosted lamps, their skin the color of unrusted aluminum or wet concrete, though some have a faint greenish bloom on their forearms from tending cable-moss. Their hair is kept short or shaved or oiled flat, because long hair collects the condensation that beads on every surface. They wear layers of waxed cloth and soft rubber, garments that do not snag on rivets or pipes and do not make noise when they move. Their shoes have soles of perforated gum or felted moss-fiber, so they walk in a hush that is less silence than a sound pressed into the deck.

They move slowly, not out of weakness but out of an old agreement with the stairs. They pause at thresholds and touch the painted handprints. They do not trust a straight corridor longer than two hundred meters; after that, they walk with one hand trailing the wall, fingers reading the seams. Their faces are smooth and still, but their eyes are never still: large, dark, slow-blinking, always checking the doubled shadows for the one that lags. Their breath is shallow and patient, used to thin air pulled through long ventilation shafts.

If you were to speak to one—if you could get close enough, because voices do not carry here—you would hear a voice that is more like the drip of water than a bell. They speak in short phrases, half of it with their hands: a flat palm for stop, two fingers for a stable door, a tap on the rail for *I am here*. Their names are not always names. A person may be called the woman who oils the north stair, the boy who counts the moths on the fourth gantry, the keeper of the seventh sump. They carry small tags stamped with numbers and trade-marks, but these are for the ledgers, not for introductions.

They may once have been human, or they may be what humans become when the only ground is made.

The place has tiers, and the people have settled into them like water finding its level. Near the upper lamp fields live the ones the locals call the Upcast, or the Lamp-Haunters. They are the palest, thin as the high railings, with eyes adjusted to the huge even light. They maintain the lamps and the moths, for the moths are not pests but part of the light’s living maintenance; a lamp-haunter can tell by the flicker of wings whether a panel will fail before it dims. They wear long gloves and carry hooked poles with small cups to gather drinking water from the overhead pipes. Their rooms are high narrow roosts beside the warm panels, and they sleep with their faces turned to the light, dreaming in the buzzing spectrum of the lamps.

Below them, in the dense middle stacks, are the Ledger-Folk, the cable-clerks, the map-keepers. They live in rooms the size of cargo containers, each with a small bright window and a rack for documents. They are the ones who argue about the straight corridors. They draw maps with dotted lines for *unstable*, and red handprints for *proved*. They know the city is not exactly Euclidean, but they have learned to file that knowledge somewhere between architecture and weather. They wear spectacles with tiny metal shutters, because the light is too even and the eyes need edges. Their fingers are stained with oxide ink and oil. They are not cowards, but they are cautious; they will not walk a new route without first comparing three maps and leaving a wire shrine at the corner.

Around the workshops and patient conveyor belts are the Sort-Folk, the ones who keep the slow industry moving. They are named for the sorting floors where parts come down the belts and must be separated by touch, by weight, by warmth. They know the machines not as gods but as roommates: which flywheel runs warm, which piston exhales a little more steam in the long dimming. They wear heavy aprons of waxed canvas and thick gloves with the fingers cut away. Their hands are dark with graphite and oil, and their arms are crosshatched with old burns from warm metal. They sleep in hammocks slung near the belts so they can feel the rhythm change even while resting. They are more solitary than the other dwellers, but not unfriendly; they simply prefer the company of machines that do not require small talk.

Deeper still, around the wet seams and sump-catchers, are the Sump-Folk, the gutter-wardens, the ones who live with the sound of water. They are the most at home in the place, or the least separate from it. Their clothes are always damp, patched with rubber and fermented moss. They wear high boots of softened hide made from the eel-things, and they move with the slow patience of a drip sliding down a riser. They tend the drip-ferns and the pale fungi, harvest the eel-like creatures that never surface, and keep the channels clear. They know the plateau’s tilt before the map-makers do, because the water changes its route across their floors. They adjust their door thresholds with small wedges of cast resin, and they retrain the runoff into new gutters with lines of soft paste. They are not poor, though they live low; they have water, and water is the only weather.

There is also a wandering guild, the Path-Markers, or the Handprint-Painters. They belong to no tier and go everywhere. They carry pouches of pigment made from rust, copper, moss pulp, and ground enamel from the beetles. They repaint the handprints, build the little wire shrines, and test the stable paths. They are the ones who know that a staircase descending half a kilometer can arrive at the same landing, but only on certain days, or only if you do not count your steps. They are not priests exactly, but they are treated with the nervous courtesy you give to someone who knows which doors are lying. Their hands are stained in five colors. Some of them are said to have one eye that sees the world as a map and one eye that sees it as a room.

There are others, too, more specialized: the Machine-Listeners who press stethoscopes to warm pipes and listen for the city’s breath; the Stair-Tenders who feel the five-minute pistons in their bones; the Sump-Weighers who measure the water’s slow pulse; the Bird-Counts who keep track of the sparrow-things that roost in the cable runs and never sing. None of them are exactly separate from the rest. In the evenings—such as they are, when the light dims by a fraction and the mist releases on schedule—they gather at the wet markets on the wider decks, trading moss-cakes, eel meat, lamp-parts, maps, and small jars of luminous fungus. They speak in low voices, and sometimes they only nod. The silence is not unfriendly; it is the same silence that keeps the machines from waking too loudly.

Their food is as quiet as they are. They eat cakes made from cable-moss and ground beetle-shell, pale and crumbly, tasting of minerals and rain. They eat the eel-things from the sumps, boiled in condensation water until the flesh falls into soft white flakes. They grow shelf fungi on the walls of the dampest rooms and scrape it into broth. They drink the water that drips from the pipes, and they do not waste it. A spilled cup is not a disaster, but it is a small insult to the gutters. Children are taught early to catch the drip with their hands and return it to the nearest channel.

They do not have a religion so much as a posture. They believe the city is an earlier version of itself, still building, still repairing, still arguing with its own geometry. They say the machines are not dead, and the people are not entirely separate from them. They call the spatial anomalies *the city dreaming*. When a corridor repeats, or a door opens onto a street that should be elsewhere, they say the city is remembering something that did not happen yet. They leave offerings at the wire shrines: a clean bolt, a drop of oil, a folded paper map with one route crossed out. They touch the painted handprints for luck, but it is not luck; it is a way of reminding the city that someone has passed this way and would like to pass again.

They do not fear the weirdness. They do not even call it weirdness. It is ambient, like the mist. A shadow that lags is just a shadow with a different schedule. A reflection that shows the lanterns burning in a different order is a reflection with an opinion. The birds above the fourth gantry are sometimes twice as many as before, but the Bird-Counts mark it in their ledgers as *increase, provisional*. They have learned not to look too closely at certain things, but they have also learned exactly how closely to look at others: the warmth of a flywheel, the film of water on a threshold, the number of steps between two handprints.

The children are the strangest, because they have never known anything else. They play on the gantries and in the sump-channels, quiet games of hiding and returning. They catch wall-lice and race them on the clean metal, leaving little trails of polished deck. They make toys from bolts and wire, small figures with joints that move if you breathe on them. They are told not to chase the eel-things too far into the deep gutters, not to trust a corridor that goes straight too long, not to speak too loudly because the city is listening. They obey, mostly, but they also test: they drop coins in certain rooms to see how long they take to land, and they dare each other to stand where the shadow lags. They are not afraid. They are curious in the way of creatures who have grown up inside a riddle and find it comfortable.

The animals live among them but are not pets. The pale moths sleep on their sleeves sometimes. The sparrow-things open their beaks in what might be song, and the people tilt their heads as if they can almost hear it. The wall-lice are tolerated, even encouraged, because they eat the condensation film and leave the metal bright. The eel-things are food but also company; the sump-wardens say you can tell the health of the city by the way they turn in the water. The beetle-things with enamel bodies are collected by children for their bright shells, and their empty cases are used as beads, buttons, or tokens. There is no separation between built and living; everything is made of parts, and everything is alive in its own way.

If you watch them long enough, you notice they are not so much inhabitants as features of the place—like the drip-fern, the cable-moss, the warm flywheels. They have been pressed into the city’s volume and kept there by rivets, just like the buildings. But they are not miserable. They have the quiet satisfaction of a system that is running as it should. They have water, light, warmth, work, and a kind of intimacy with the impossible. They do not ask for nature. They have never seen it. They assume the world is all made, all decking and gantries and lamp-panels, and that *ground* is just a story for children, a flat thing that used to support the first builders.

At the long dimming, when the lights sink to their lowest and the mist lies thick on every rail, they walk the stable paths with small lamps and painted hands. They do not sing, or if they sing, it is a sound too low for the air to carry, felt in the soles of the feet, passing from deck to deck like a pulse. They touch the handprints as they go. They leave the wire shrines a little oil, a little water, a clean bolt. They listen to the city breathing—the slow pistons, the water in the gutters, the sub-audible hum of the lamp fields—and they know, without having to say it, that they are exactly where they are supposed to be.
