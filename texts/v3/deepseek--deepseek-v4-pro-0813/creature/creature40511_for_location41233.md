At first the Boiler Gardens seems occupied only by water and machinery. But that is an error of speed. The true resident is not moving on your clock.

If you drift down through the golden weed and wait, you will start to notice maintenance. A lamp that was half-crusted on one visit shows a clean brass rim. A slipped belt on the wooden cog has been walked back into place. A hand-width of rust has been scraped from the waterwheel’s lower spoke, and the scrapings are piled in a neat half-circle in the silt. Between the slate blocks there are tracks, not exactly footprints: broad, long-fingered impressions, as if someone had pressed a soft webbed hand into the sediment, then rested.

The resident of the Boiler Gardens is not a person. It is a Tender. It may have been a person once, or it may have been a newt that found the warm water and stayed. Time and brine have worn away the distinction. It is about as tall as a small adult when it briefly stands, but it prefers to move on all fours, body held close to the street so the waterwheel’s rhythm comes up through its sternum. Its skin is pale, translucent in places, and furred with a short living nap—green across its shoulders, rust-red along its belly and thighs, a clean watermark at the level where the fresh water meets the salt. Its hands are four-fingered and webbed, with soft, unscuffed palms. Its head is broad, its mouth a wide slow curve. Where eyes would be, there are two dark slits sealed by a film. It sees mostly through its skin and through the fine fern-like gills that hang behind its jaw. The gills open and close with the water pressure, and they are never still.

It lives in the Pump Hall, in a nest beside the battery jars. The nest is not a bed; it is an arrangement of old tiles, a few valve shells, kelp blades dragged in from the roofs, and one half-melted brass gear that it keeps for no clear purpose. It sleeps curled with its tail over its mouth. A faint blue-green light sometimes moves across its back while it sleeps, borrowed from the bacterial films on the walls.

Its life is governed by the waterwheel. When the wheel’s slow note changes—when the tide outside presses harder through the collapsed tunnel—the Tender wakes and begins its rounds. It visits the battery jars first, pressing its face close to each amber cylinder, not looking but tasting the electric field in the water. It cleans the copper plates with a slow thumb, removing the mineral bloom. Then it moves through the slot streets, house by house, along a path it has worn into the sediment. It tends each machine not as a repairer but as a groomer. It nudges the leather belts back onto their wooden cogs. It clears algae from the flap valves. It runs a soft palm over the waterwheel’s spokes to find the one that has begun to loosen. It does all this with a slowness that would look like hesitation to a human. But its speed is the town’s speed. If it moved faster, it would break what it keeps.

The Tender feeds on the wall-films. It has a broad, rasping tongue that it draws over the bacterial mats, leaving a clean strip of dark stone behind. It grazes in rotation, never stripping one wall bare. Sometimes it drifts upward into a cloud of copepods near a lamp and flares its gills, catching them in the feathery branches. It also swallows the soft carbonates it scrapes from the battery plates, perhaps for the salt. The blind eel that appears once in a long while is not prey. When they meet, the two of them hang for a time in the shadow of the waterwheel, and then the eel leaves. The Tender does not follow.

It drinks from the boundary. At the place where the fresh upper water and the salt lower water meet, the Tender hangs horizontally for long periods, half its body in each layer. That is the only time its eye-slits open a little, showing a milky inward light. The boundary seems to pass through it, and the different colors of algae on its back and belly are not so much a disguise as an honest map of where it belongs. It is not a creature of fresh water or salt. It is a creature of the meeting.

The orange lamps are the Tender’s particular charge. It does not light them; it only keeps them from going out. It visits each lamp that still burns and licks the glass clean, then rests its forehead against the warm brass until the vibration of the current becomes steady again. It may not love the lamps, but it treats them the way a person treats a stove in winter. Its body is too cold to warm them, so it offers patience instead.

The doors of the Thirty Houses are open because the Tender has kept them open. It does not close doors; it does not understand doors as barriers. To it they are gateways for the slow current, and it moves through them as easily as water. It has lived in all of the houses at one time or another. Some contain only machinery; some contain a single lamp; some are empty except for a bed frame that has become a garden. The Tender treats them all as rooms of the same house.

If you were to visit, it would know you before you saw it. The water changes around you, and its skin would feel the difference. It would pause, gills flared, one hand resting on the nearest pipe. Then, if you stayed very still and let the sediment settle, it would lower its head and go on. It would not fear you. It has outlasted fear. It might even pass so close that you feel the water move: a soft, warm tug, like a door opening in a house that has forgotten it was ever above the air.
