The inhabitants of the Plateau of the Held Breath are not a fauna. They are not even a population. They are a single held breath that has learned to wear different bodies, the way a mountain wears different stones.

At first you say no one lives there. Then you say everything lives there. Then, after a long time, you stop saying “lives” and begin to understand that the plateau itself is the creature, and all the separate shapes are its slow organs.

---

**The Floor-People**

They are the first to resolve out of the mineral confusion. Flat, shield-shaped, fitted edge to edge until the ground seems paved with a single animal hide studded with stone. Each one is the size of a hand or a foot. Their dorsal plates are not shell, exactly; they are skin that has become geology. Between them run the seams: pale flesh, a faint pulse, sometimes an eye slit showing yellow or pale blue.

They do not blink, because blinking would be an interruption. They have lived so long without interruption that an interruption would not register as an event. If you step on them, the pressure travels sideways through a thousand linked bodies, not as pain but as information. To them, your footstep is a letter delivered to the whole plain. The plain does not answer quickly.

They feed on what rises from below, on the mineral exudates of the underlayer, on the pale syrups that never freeze. They do not multiply. When one of them finally subsides, its neighbors close over it like a slow sigh, and another is already there in its place—identical, seamless, as if the floor of the world had simply healed.

Some of them grow the grey-green plants out of their backs, roots threaded into the living seams. The plants do not sway. There is no wind, and there is nothing to sway toward. They drink the animals’ thin exudates and turn them into a dry green patience.

---

**The Soft Underlayer**

Beneath the fitted floor, in the narrow dark between mineral roots and old bedrock, live the pale ones. They are soft, eyeless, limbless or many-limbed in ways that seem to change when you try to count. They are the plateau’s deep digestion and its memory.

They move only when a pavement-animal dies. Then they rise to the seam and take the body apart into a fine white dust that later becomes the next animal’s shell. They are not scavengers. They are the underworld’s way of remembering its own skin. They never see the sky, but they know it as a change in the temperature of the ceiling. Their dreams are chemical. Their thinking is not sequence but recurrence.

When the ground ticks—that faint sound like cooling stone, like a glass jar settling—it is the soft ones, far down, tapping the undersides of the pavement. They are not calling to each other. They are counting. They have been counting for so long that the numbers have become mineral.

---

**The Hanging Dust**

The air is full of tiny flying things that do not fly. They are small as dust, with wings like flakes of mica. They hang motionless in such numbers that the light itself seems granular, thickened, as if the sky had been bruised.

They are not insects. They are the plateau’s exhalation, crystallized in midair. Their wings are not for movement; they are for hearing. Each wing is a thin crystal that vibrates when the ground ticks. Through them, the whole plateau listens to itself.

They do not hunt, do not mate, do not die in the usual way. If one is disturbed—by your breath, by your moving hand—it parts without sound and closes back into exactly the shape it held before. They feed on the difference between sun and shade, on the faint far-infrared glow of the living tile below. They are the air’s equivalent of the floor-people: a suspended pavement of the sky.

---

**The Standing Herd**

The tall ones are what remained when the mountains grew tired of being mountains. Six-legged, long-necked, narrow-shouldered, they stand at regular intervals, each exactly a body-length from the next, all facing the same direction. Their hides are weathered pale on one side, darker on the other. Salt has climbed their legs like pale stockings.

They do not move. Their eyes are open and faintly glazed, not with blindness but with a kind of long dream. They are not grazing. There is nothing to graze. They stand in the mineral breath of the pavement-animals and need nothing else. Their bodies run on non-aqueous solvents—a thin syrup of perchlorate and glycerol—so they cannot freeze or evaporate. Their breath may take a season. Their hearts may take a year.

The young beneath them are not young. They are the same animal at a different point in time, folded back on itself. When a new one eventually appears, it does not arrive by birth. It thickens out of the air where it has always been waiting, already standing, already facing the same direction. They are the plateau’s memory of verticality, of a time when the land still had ambition.

They breathe rarely. When one does, it is not air. It is a thin, pale vapor that rises and feeds the hanging mica-dust. The floor-people near its feet open their eyes. Then the breath is gone, and the plateau settles again.

---

**The Seated Ones**

The human-shaped inhabitants are in the huts, though you may not see them at first. Each hut is round, low, stone-built, with a doorway facing east. Inside, sometimes, a figure sits with knees drawn up, skin dried to the texture of old wood. The air has preserved them so well that they look less like corpses than like people who have simply finished.

They wear necklaces of animal eye-plates. Those eyes are still open. Each one holds a different memory: one saw the first hut built, one saw the last river sink, one saw the moon when it still rose and set rather than simply hung there. The seated ones are not dead, or not only dead. They are the plateau’s way of remembering that it once had builders.

Their tools are laid out beside them: flint scrapers, awls of bone, braided sinew, a broken clay bowl. In one hut, a single copper pin, green with age. The tools are not idle. They are being sharpened by the slow motion of the air. A seated woman in the third hut has one hand raised to thread a bone needle. The needle has been in the air for so long that the eye has grown a thin mineral skin. The sinew is not stone. It is still sinew. She is not waiting. She is between two movements, and the interval has lasted a thousand years.

They do not speak. If you sit with them long enough, you may begin to think they are the only ones on the plateau who ever chose to be there. They did not adapt. They refused. And refusal, here, became another kind of life.

---

**The Single Occupant**

After enough time, the distinction between these forms grows thin. The pavement-animals are the herd-beasts laid flat. The herd-beasts are the pavement-animals stretched upward. The hanging dust is the same shape shattered into a haze. The soft ones are the same shape turned inside out. The seated ones are the same shape remembering itself.

The plateau is not an ecosystem. It is a single organism at the end of its existence, moving so slowly that its life looks like geology. Its skin is the fitted floor. Its gut is the underlayer. Its breath is the suspended dust. Its bones are the standing herd. Its memory is the seated figures. Its hair is the grey-green plants.

It does not hunt. It does not migrate. It does not grow beyond its assigned place. Energy enters as light, leaves as faint far-infrared glow, and the material loop is closed. Reproduction is replacement, not increase. When one animal finally fades, another is already in its place, identical, and the world does not notice the difference.

If you stay too long, the plateau will begin to think you are one of its missing organs. You will see your own footprints in the dust ahead of you, though you have not yet walked there. You will catch your own face in a seam between two floor-plates. The seated ones will not look at you, but you will feel their attention like a weight on your chest.

Then you will understand that you are not an observer here. You are a new part, not yet assembled. The plateau holds its breath, and if you do not leave, it will hold you in it, too.
