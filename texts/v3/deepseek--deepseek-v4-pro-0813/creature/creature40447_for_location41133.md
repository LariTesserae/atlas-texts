The animals are the first to come into focus. They are not background; they are the true population. The six-legged grazers move over the mineral mats in slow, rust-colored herds, their backs warm and glossy. The featherless birds with their long bony snouts roost on every cornice and cable, singing in metallic whistles that match the pitch of the pipes. The eel-things in the canals coil and uncoil, their flat bodies turning the dark water into something like breathing. The air is full of small flying forms that never seem to land. Every surface, if you look long enough, is layered with presence.

But after a while you notice that some of the shapes moving along the terraces are not six-legged, not winged, not eel-slick. They walk upright, slowly, and they carry tools that catch the grey-gold light. These are the people. They do not have a name for themselves that translates easily. If you ask them, they might say “the ones who scrape,” or “the ones who keep the sluices clear,” or simply “we.” They are not many compared with the grazers, but they are enough. They are built like the city: low, dense, patient.

They have wide chests for the thin air, and broad feet with long toes that grip wet stone. Their skin is the color of wet concrete or old copper, often stained orange from the mineral film and green from the railings. Their hair is sparse; many shave it away so the grey-orange film cannot take hold. They move slowly not because they are weak but because haste is dangerous on slick ledges. Their eyes are dark and set deep, with a second lid that comes down in the steam. They wear waxed canvas and eel-hide, and they smell of oil, rain, and sour fermentation.

They live in the residential stacks, which are not separate from the animal city. Each balcony is a garden of cages, drying racks, and mat-trays. The cages hold small song-beasts, not quite pets, not quite wild, that the people use as air-pressure alarms and companions. The racks hold strips of mineral mat, curing in the wet heat until they are leathery and dark. The trays hold the grey-orange film, scraped from the walls and seeded with new spores. A child may sit on the balcony for hours, turning the trays so the film grows evenly. This is not a chore; it is how the family eats.

The mat is the staple. It is eaten fresh, dried, fermented, or pressed into cakes. It tastes of copper, salt, and something like mushrooms, but not exactly. It is boiled into porridge, or fried on hot stones, or left to sour in jars until it becomes a paste that makes the mouth tingle. The people also cull the oldest and slowest grazers, whose flesh is dark and dense and carries the mineral tang of the mats. The eels in the canals are caught with hooks and long lines; their fat is rendered into lamp oil, their skin into shoes and aprons. The eggs of the featherless birds are gathered from the bridges, but never all of them. To take every egg from a nest is considered an illness of the mind.

The city runs on maintenance, and maintenance is a kind of music. Each day, the people follow the schedules posted in the stairwells. The mat-tenders peel the film from the walls, leaving enough for the grazers. The canal-wardens wade into the warm water, their legs wrapped in eel-hide, and clear the sluices. The boiler-tenders feed the engines with dried mat-cakes and listen to the flames. The rail-walkers check the viaducts for rust and nests. The flock-callers whistle to the birds to keep them from the turbines, learning the songs of every engine and every flock. No one is idle. Even the children carry buckets of scraped film to the communal fermenters.

There is no king or council. Decisions are made in the stairwells, by whoever is awake and knows the problem. The respected ones are those who can read the water’s color, who know the sound of a failing pipe before it bursts, who can taste the mineral film and say whether it is ripe. They are called by their skill: Mat-Tender, Canal-Warden, Stack-Keeper, Boiler-Reader. They do not build monuments. Their monuments are the repaired walls, the cleared channels, the living animals.

They do not have gods in the way of other places. They have the Warmth Below and the Wet Above. The Warmth Below is the volcanic heat that makes the city breathable. The Wet Above is the rain that never really stops. These are not persons; they are forces, like gravity. The people give thanks by keeping the channels clear and the mats healthy. They say the city is not a machine they run but a body they live inside. They are not its masters; they are its gut, its skin, its lungs. The animals are its blood. The water is its breath.

A child born in the stacks is not taught to fear the drop. It is taught to respect the wet. Its first shoes are cut from eel-hide. Its first words are the names of the animals: grazer, whistler, eel, clinger. It learns to walk on sloped stone before it learns to run. It learns to whistle before it learns to sing. It learns that the city is loud, but the loudness is a kind of lullaby.

They speak in low, slow voices, not because of secrecy but because the city is full of high sounds and they would be lost. Their language has many words for wet: the wet of steam, the wet of rain, the wet of a sweating pipe, the wet of a grazer’s back. It has no word for dry, not in the way other places do. Dry is only a temporary condition, a pause in the wet. They have no word for wilderness. The city is the world.

When a person dies, the family lays the body on a roof with a view of the terraces, or places it in a canal for the eels. The body is not buried; there is no soil. Within days, the mineral film colonizes the skin. The grazers come, then the birds, then the smaller scavengers. The person becomes part of the mat, part of the animals, part of the city. This is not sad. It is the same slow exchange that has always happened. The dead are still here; they are in the steam, in the rust, in the whistle of the birds.

In the evening, when the fog turns the grey-gold light into something almost solid, the people sit on the terraces. They eat mat-cakes and pickled grazer. They drink a hot, sour infusion out of metal cups. The animals settle around them. A flock of featherless birds might roost on the railing, their bony snouts tucked under their wings. The six-legged grazers move slowly over the steps, grazing the film that has grown during the day. The people do not shoo them. They are all residents. The industrial chorale rises and falls. The people add their own low notes, a wordless hum that matches the pitch of the pipes. It is not a song of joy or sorrow. It is a song of being there.

They are not the heroes of this place. They are not the villains. They are simply the ones who scrape, who clear, who feed, who are fed. They are the city’s own breath made flesh. They live there. They have always lived there. They will live there until the water wears the rock to nothing.
