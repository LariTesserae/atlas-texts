You don’t see them at first because they are built into the texture of the city. The wall of sound is so dense that the eye learns to skip over small things. But then one of the small things moves.

A patch of bark on a lower terrace detaches itself. It is about the size of a cat, though its arms are long enough to wrap around a trunk. It straightens. It was not hiding; it was resting inside a shallow hollow, and its skin has taken on the exact color and grain of the bark around it. The resemblance does not break when it moves so much as change: now it looks less like a piece of the city than like a piece of the city that has begun to walk.

Its body is low and soft, built for slow climbing and wet air. The legs are short and strong, the arms long and thin. The hands have three long fingers and an opposing thumb, each tipped with a round, slightly flattened pad. The feet are similar, with four toes that curl around bark. The skin is gray-green, but over it lies a mottled bloom of rust, black, and pale lichen. It glistens with the same moisture that coats the leaves. The chest and belly are paler, almost milky, and they pulse with a slow amphibian breath.

The head is the most arresting part. It is wide and slightly flattened, not with a face so much as a cluster of sense organs. The eyes are large, amber, placed high on the skull, with pupils that contract to horizontal bars. There are no outer ears. Instead, two round tympanums—thin as soap film—sit behind the jaw, and they turn slightly with each new sound. The mouth is broad and soft, and beneath it the throat hangs in a loose sac. When the creature breathes, the sac fills and falls. When it is still, the sac trembles faintly with the city's deep noise, as if the creature is not hearing the sound but feeling it through its skin.

It does not walk so much as pour itself from branch to trunk to terrace. It moves slowly, deliberately, pausing often to place a palm flat against the bark. It is listening with its fingers. After a moment it may open its mouth and make a sound—a low, woody note, or a short sequence of three tones, or something that sounds like water dripping into water. The sound is not loud. But it is directed. The note goes toward a particular wall or doorway, and the wall responds. A crack widens into an arch. A floor thickens. A gutter shifts by a few inches to catch a new trickle of water. The creature steps through the arch and is gone.

Later, you see more of them. They are not many. The city is mostly empty, and they are one of the reasons: they do not crowd. They live in the dwelling-hollows of the lower terraces, one or two or three to a room, sleeping in the smooth, worn shapes that fit their bodies exactly. The hollows are not made; they are grown, and the residents keep them open by using them. Some of the rooms are small and round, like the inside of a throat. Others are long galleries where many of them rest in rows, their throat-sacs pulsing together, their eyes closed sideways under milky membranes.

They carry nothing. They have no tools, no clothes, no containers. Their hands are for gripping and listening, not for making. When they need to eat, they lick sap from a wound in the bark, or eat fallen fruit, or catch insects by waiting beside an ant road and letting the ants climb onto their fingers, then raising the fingers to the mouth. They drink from bromeliad cups and from the long gutters. When a cistern leaf grows too full, one of them will sing a low note at its stem until the leaf tilts and lets the water spill.

They do not build because the city builds. They do not plant because the city is already planted. What they do is tend the sound. For most of the day they move through the lattice, each one stopping here and there to press a hand to a wall and sing a small adjustment. It is not work in any human sense. It looks more like breathing. A wall has begun to close; one of them hums and the wall relaxes. A bridge of vine has grown too thin; another one calls a two-note phrase, and a new strand thickens along the old route. They are not repairing the city from outside; they are part of its healing, like cells moving through a body.

Their language is not made of words alone. It is made of pitch, place, and the city's answer. A phrase means one thing when sung into a dry gutter and another when sung into a cistern. To them, the city is not a place containing them; it is the other half of every sentence. A doorway is a word that is held open by a sound. A stairway is a long sentence repeated until it hardens. They have no names in the way of separate labels. A resident is known by the note it first learned and the room where it sleeps, and to call that one, you sing the room's shape and the note into the nearest wall. The vibration travels through the root-sponge and comes out as the same sound, slightly softened, in the place where that other resident rests.

They share the city with everything else, and they do not pretend to rule it. Lemur-like creatures ride on their shoulders, grooming salt from their necks. Geckos sit on their knuckles. Frogs breed in the same water cups. Predators take the old and the slow, and the residents do not interfere. They seem to understand that being eaten is also part of the city's metabolism. When one of them dies, the body is left in a root basin or a hollow, and the ants, fungi, and wet air dissolve it into the plant. The city grows a small shelf or a new dimple where the body lay. In this way, the residents continue as architecture.

The young begin in water. Once a year, after the longest fog, the residents gather near the great cisterns and sing together. Their voices make a chord that is not beautiful in any sweet way; it is low and rough and pressure-changing. The cisterns shiver. The plant grows small cup-shaped leaves along the margins, and the females lay eggs into them. The eggs are clear, the embryos visible as dark commas. They hatch as gilled, translucent tadpole-like creatures with fine whiskers around the mouth. They graze on water mites and frog-spawn. Over a season, they grow legs, lose their gills, and climb out onto the lip of the leaf, where an adult waits, humming a simple two-tone interval. The young one, still soft and not yet the color of the city, tries to copy it. The leaf bends a little under the sound, making a ramp from the cup to the trunk. That is the first lesson: the city listens to you, and you to it.

If you watch long enough, you stop thinking of them as animals living in a city. They are more like the city’s own small mobile voices, its fingers, its ears. The largest resident of the bowl is the city itself. These creatures are not separate from it any more than a tongue is separate from the mouth. They are the part of the city that can walk from one wing of itself to another, listen to its own pulse, and sing a doorway into being. When they look at you, the expression is not savagery or curiosity in a human way. It is the city looking out from behind a pair of amber eyes, wondering, briefly, whether you are another sound, another animal, or a new part of its body beginning to arrive.
