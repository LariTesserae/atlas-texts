**What Lives Here**

The first inhabitant is the plant itself. It is not a background; it is the landlord, the weather, the ground, the walls. It breathes in the long, slow way that deep stone does, and the animals and the near-people live inside that breath, in the hollows and galleries, in the warm ducts and the cold shafts, their own small pulses laid over the enormous one.

The one who returns to the dome above the warm duct is a shepherd. If you see her at all, you see her pale as the inner pith of the plant, rising out of the floor where a section of braided stem unrolls like a lip. She is taller than a human, but she does not stand fully upright; the galleries are not made for standing. Her arms are long and rope-tendoned, and her knuckles brush the root-fibers when she walks. Each hand has six fingers: four long ones, two shorter thumbs set at opposite angles. That is the first thing you notice, after the paleness. That, and the way her eyes are set a little too far to the sides of her face, as if she is already watching the passage behind you before she has looked at you.

The shepherds are near-human but not human. The wanderers call them Hollowkin, or Six-fingers, or simply the Shepherds. They call themselves something else, a rhythm tapped on the collarbone or drummed on a hollow stem: *the ones who follow the sweetness.* It is a good name. They do not speak very much with their mouths. The plant is full of sound already, and spoken words disappear into it. Instead they tap and knock, using the hollow stems as a kind of telegraph. A message can travel for miles along the resonance-ribs: three taps, pause, two taps, a long fading tap. *Herd is moving. Loper at the third gallery. Fog coming down from the north ridge.*

The sweetness they follow belongs to the sap-aphids. The aphids are not quite insects and not quite worms. Each is the size of a child’s fist, a translucent sac filled with stolen plant sap, and when they feed, their abdomens glow faintly with the plant’s own blue-green light. They move in slow herds over the vascular cords, their many hair-thin legs rippling like water. Their mouthparts make that low wet crackle you hear in the walls. They are blind and nearly deaf. The shepherds guide them by sound: a low tapping on a gallery wall tells the herd to slow, a high double-tap tells them to shift to a new cord. It looks like herding, but it is more like negotiation. The plant tolerates the aphids because the shepherds keep them from stripping one cord too far. In return, the aphids give honeydew, which the shepherds collect in little cups made from seed husks. The honeydew ferments in the warm breath of the plant and becomes a drink that keeps a shepherd awake for days of long herding.

The shepherd in the dome carries a copper knife. It is old, but its edge is still sharp. She uses it to cut only dead or dying fronds, and only when she must. She also carries a staff of hollow stem, taller than she is, which she strikes against the floor to read the space ahead. The echo tells her where the gallery opens into a cavern, where the floor is thin, where a shaft drops away. She does not trust her eyes. No one who lives here trusts their eyes.

The blind pale loper that follows her herd is a good reason for that. A loper is about the size of a wolf, but it is hairless and smooth as a water-worn root. It runs on four long legs, and its face is a blank wedge of bone with no eyes at all. It listens with its whole body. Along its jaw and spine are hollow ridges that pick up vibrations from the plant-body. It can hear a sap-aphid’s tiny footfall from a hundred yards away, and it can hear a shepherd’s heartbeat from farther still. It will follow a herd for weeks, patient, silent, waiting for one aphid to fall behind or for a shepherd to make a mistake. The shepherd knows this loper. She knows the small depression of air that comes into a tunnel before it does, the way the wire-flies go silent in a long ragged line. Sometimes she strikes her staff against a wall and sends a false herd-sound off to the west. The loper follows it, usually. When it doesn’t, she has the knife.

Above the plant canopy, above even the closed lid of cloud, the bladder-hawks ride. They are not birds, exactly. A bladder-hawk is mostly a sac of warm air with wings. Its body is a translucent bladder, veined and taut, and its wings are membranes stretched over four long ribs. They rise on the thermals the plant itself makes, and from the ground they look like pale scraps of torn sky. When one dives, it folds its wings and falls, and the sound is exactly like cloth tearing. The shepherds listen for that sound. One hawk can flush a whole gallery of hidden life, and a sudden tearing above you means somewhere close by something small is about to be taken.

The wire-flies are the smallest things that matter. They are not flies. They are spirals of dark wire, each with two wings that beat too fast to see, and they ride the updrafts in thin glittering columns. They are almost silent until they strike a hollow stem, and then they ring it like a bell. A swarm passing through a chamber sounds like a net of silver bells thrown into the air. The shepherds read the ringing as weather: a high tight chime means cold air is coming off the high peaks; a low scattered chime means fog is on its way. In their mating swarms, the wire-flies will fly into any warm opening—a nostril, a mouth, the hollow of an ear—and die there, still faintly ringing. The shepherds cover their faces with waxed fronds when the swarms are thick.

In the deepest hollows, there are things that are not quite animal and not quite plant. Some fruits hang from the ceiling and make a sound like the cry of a lost aphid, or a shepherd child, or a small animal in pain. The sound is too perfect. A real cry has a wet, irregular edge, a breath behind it. The fruit’s cry is round and clean. When a predator comes close, the fruit seals shut, and the predator is gone. The shepherds teach their young to listen for the shape of the echo. *The perfect sound is a mouth,* they tap on the wall. *Never follow a sound that has no breath behind it.*

The humans who come from the low country are called wanderers. They are not like the shepherds. They talk too much with their mouths. They build fires too high and too hot, and they walk without sounding the floor. They come up in the brief season when the cloud deck thins, trading salt and metal for honeydew and guidance through the hollows. Most of them wear wax earplugs and still cannot sleep. The shepherd in the dome tolerates them because they bring copper, and copper holds an edge against the plant’s tough rind. She watches them from the braided doorway and does not invite them in unless they are quiet. Few are quiet. Some learn. A very few, it is said, stay so long that the plant’s pulse replaces their own, and they become neither wanderer nor shepherd but a third thing: pale and patient, living in the deep galleries, speaking by tapping on their own ribs.

The dome is not a home. It is a waystation. The shepherd stays there only for the season when the vascular cord below this lobe runs rich. When the herd moves on, she will scatter the ashes from the black-stone fire pit, roll the mat of shed fronds, and leave the bone hooks hanging by the door. The signal tower on the ridge will keep moaning in two tones after she is gone, telling any other shepherd who comes close that the herd was here, that the third gallery is clear, that a loper has been seen near the western shaft. The moan is not language; it is a landmark made of sound.

If you stay until she returns, you might learn her name. But names are not spoken here. They are tapped on the sternum or drummed on a hollow stem. Her name is a rhythm: three taps, a pause, two taps, a long fading tap. It might translate as *one who walks inside the sound*, or *the listener who is not alone*, or simply *she who returns to the dome above the warm duct*. But she would not call herself that. She would tap it once on your sternum, gently, and then she would ask you, with her six-fingered hands, what you are called in the place where the ground is not a living body.
