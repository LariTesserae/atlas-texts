There is no one to meet, not at first. The central plaza is empty, and your own breath comes back to you from a wall a hundred meters away, dry and small. But emptiness here is not vacancy. What lives in the hollow lives slowly, in a chemistry without water, in a time closer to stone than to breath.

The first residents are the rooted things.

They are not plants in the green sense, though they are the only visible life. They begin halfway down the walls and fill every ledge and crevice, a dense band of sulfur-yellow and bone-white, old bronze and resin amber. Some stand tall and jointed, like reeds of sulfur and salt, their stems hollow and resonant. Others spread fans of mica-thin leaves, translucent and faintly warm, turning by degrees toward the nearest heat. Still others swell into bronze-colored pods, tight and heavy, full of spores and hot gas. Their roots do not search for water. They burrow into fumaroles and mineral veins, siphoning sulfur compounds, copper salts, and the earth’s own heat. Their sap is a dry resin that beads on their surfaces and hardens into amber. Some of them give off a faint heat-glow in the dim air, a deep orange under the dust, as if each one were a slow fire shaped into stem and leaf.

They move, but not as you move. Over hours, a fan will shift to follow the drifting heat. Over days, a stem will lean toward a new gas vent. Their joints creak like old trees under a load. The pods swell until, on the hottest days, they burst with a sharp report, scattering pale spores in clouds. The air grows thick with them. The spores do not eat and are not eaten. They settle, and if they settle where the chemistry is right, they begin to grow. There is competition among them, but it is a competition of roots and mineral claims, slow and mostly silent except for the crack of a stem giving way or the grinding of salt under pressure. No animal browses on them. They feed on the earth’s breath, and when they die they collapse into their own mineral skeletons, which the city’s foundations slowly take into themselves.

Then there is the city itself.

It is not a ruin. It is a larger resident, built by hands that are no longer present but still sustained by the same dry law. It breathes. The fissures in the floor are its inhalations; the tall chimneys are its exhalations. The wind is not only wind. It moves through the colonnades and empty streets like breath through a set of pipes. The plazas are too wide for people because they were not made for people. They are sounding chambers, built to catch the deep organ note of the basin and turn it into something that hums in the chest and in the soles of the feet. The spiral plaza is not decoration; it is a record, a map of gas vents. When a new vent opens, a new line of paving is added, though no visible hand adds it. The processional way is too wide for human traffic and ends in a blank wall of salt that vibrates faintly, as if the city were listening with its whole stone body.

The city and the forest are not separate. The rooted things climb through doorways and over parapets. Their roots crack the salt blocks. But the city absorbs their mineral skeletons when they die, folding them into its foundations and lower walls. It may be that the city eats the forest, or that the forest is the city’s leaves—its skin, its breathing surface, its slow greenless photosynthesis. They share the same vents, the same mineral veins, the same absence of water. Some of the buildings seem to have grown rather than been built, their bricks aligned like plant cells, as if the same force that makes the sulfur-reed also makes the salt-block and the low dome.

There are no animals, no insects, no birds, no people. If you listen for a voice, you will hear only wind and gas, the crack of bursting pods, the settling of old walls, the hiss of dry leaves. But those are the voices of the place. The residents are the sulfur-reed and the mica-fan, the bronze-pod and the salt-lace, the resin-vein and the city’s own breathing body. They do not have faces or names. They do not speak in words. They speak in creaks and bursts, in the moan of colonnades and the deep note of the earth. They are slow fire and salt stone. They are what lives in the hollow, and they do not notice you except perhaps as a slight change in temperature, a small sound lost inside their long, dry, ongoing music.
