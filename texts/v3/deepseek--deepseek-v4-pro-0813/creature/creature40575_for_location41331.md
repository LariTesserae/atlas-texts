First, understand that no one here is merely a resident. To live in the Drowned High Country is to be part of the mountain. The first inhabitants are the ones you mistake for stone: the cold-water corals. They are animals—thousands of tiny polyps in white chalices—whose shared skeletons form the ridgelines. When the current surges, the polyps open their small mouths and comb the water for falling snow. The slope seems to shiver, not with leaves, but with thousands of pale tentacles. They are not plants reaching for light; they are animals reaching for the dead.

Then the glass sponges. They rise like fluted vases, towers, chimneys, some taller than a person, some larger than a house. Their bodies are silica—glass drawn from the sea itself—and they feed by drawing water through their walls. But they are not alone. Inside each sponge lives a small community: a pair of red shrimp, a long-legged crab, a fish that will never leave the central chamber. A single sponge is both animal and apartment building, and in the high country those two things are the same.

Next the masons: serpulid worms and tube-building anemones. The worms build hard white tubes around their soft bodies and extend feathery crowns to catch food. The anemones are taller, their columns armored with sand and shell, their tentacles pale and patient. They fill the gaps between coral branches, seam the rubble, plaster the wreckage. When a shadow passes, the worms retract in unison, and a whole wall of feathery crowns disappears into the stone. Slowly they re-emerge, one at a time, as if testing whether the shadow is gone.

Then there are the gardeners and gleaners. Long-legged crabs move over the sponge gardens, picking at encrusting growth. They are not eating the sponge; they are pruning it, keeping its surfaces clear so it can breathe. Brittle stars twine through the coral branches, their arms collecting settled detritus. They are not cleaning for the coral's sake, but the effect is the same: the structure stays open, the water flows, the colony lives. In the tube-worm fields, eels wait with only their heads exposed, jaws slightly open, so still that small animals climb across their faces. When something drifts close enough, the eel moves, and the whole field of worms retracts.

The fish are the bright exception. They are small, silver, and they school over coral thickets in tight constellations. They move like a single muscle, contracting and expanding. When a predator passes, the school turns into a scatter of mirrors, then reassembles further down the slope. In a world of bone-white and blue shadow, these fish are the only sudden light.

Over the shell flats, rays move like slow shadows, their wingtips stirring the sediment into small clouds. They search for buried worms and clams, and their paths are erased by the current almost as soon as they are made. The six-gill shark is a visitor, not a resident. It appears out of the blue above a saddle where the current runs fast, hangs for a moment with its six gill slits like folds in old stone, then vanishes down the other side. Sometimes deep-sea squid flicker past, small and pale, leaving brief clouds of ink that the current unravels. Jellies pulse through the water, their transparent bells nearly invisible except when they catch the dim light.

Higher still, there are voices. A low call arrives not as sound but as a pressure in the chest, a vibration that makes the sponge lattices hum. It comes from a large animal—a beaked whale, perhaps, or something older—that moves above the peaks. Its calls are long and spaced far apart. They seem to be answered by the mountains themselves, echoing slightly off the hard coral and then being swallowed by the water. The animal is rarely seen. By the time you turn toward the sound, it has passed beyond the next ridge. But its presence is felt, like the current, as a continuous low note beneath everything.

And then there is the living dust. In certain seasons, the water fills with larvae: coral planulae, sponge larvae, worm larvae, crab zoeae—all smaller than the flakes of marine snow. They drift for weeks or months, then settle on a clean surface, on a fallen tower, on a bare patch of shell-grit. A polyp opens its first mouth. A sponge begins to secrete its first glass needles. A worm starts to build its first tube. That is how a mountain gets another citizen. That is how the high country keeps being.

So the answer to who lives here is not a list. It is a process. The inhabitants are the builders and the built, the eaters and the eaten, the current-riders and the stone-anchored. They live because the others live, and the place lives because they do not stop. If you stayed long enough, you would see a spire fall and be recolonized; you would see a sponge grow from a speck to a tower; you would feel a whale's call pass through you. You would understand that in the Drowned High Country, the living and the landscape are not separate. The mountains are made of animals, and the animals are the mountains, still growing, still falling, still being rebuilt in the dark.
