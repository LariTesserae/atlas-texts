The lower pump room is not uninhabited. No one breathing lives here; the last pocket of air left long ago. What remains has gills, or patience, or iron in place of lungs.

The first resident is the pump. It has been here since the water rose, and it has never stopped. Its motor is still warm enough to hold a small climate around the switchgear. Its flywheel turns with ancient, deliberate patience, as if it no longer remembers what it pumps but knows it must continue. Every six minutes the intake valve clicks, the water surges, and the pump strokes twice. That is the room’s breath. The amber lamp on the control panel is its one open eye. The pressure gauges are its memories: some busy, some full of water, some dead but still pointing at a number no one will read again.

The closest thing to a face belongs to the wolf eel. It lives behind the bank of switchgear, where the cables still give off a faint heat. It has fitted itself into a rusted cable port so completely that the port has grown into the shape of its head. It is grey-brown, blunt, thick as a man’s arm at the shoulder, patched with the same white calcareous crust that covers the motor. Its jaws move constantly, not always from hunger, but as if it is tasting the water for changes—a rise in copper, a change in the motor’s pitch, the first click of the intake valve.

It is old. One eye is clouded, but the other never blinks. It has learned the room’s weather exactly. When the valve clicks, it backs deeper into the switchgear, letting the surge comb over it. When the first thud comes, it relaxes. When the second thud comes, it is already watching the intake for whatever the storm has brought: a crab, a stunned herring, a torn piece of fish from the world above. It does not need to leave its den. The water brings the room to it.

It was not born here. It came in through the discharge line when it was thin as a bootlace, following the taste of oil and crushed mussel. That was years ago. Now it is too thick to leave through the pipe it entered, but it does not want to. The sea outside is wide and uncertain. The room is small, warm, and reliable. It knows the lobsters’ ledges, the anemones’ seasons, the exact distance from the cable port to the pressure gauge where the octopus lives.

The octopus is the second mind of the room, smaller and more speculative. It lives inside the copper case of a pressure gauge, curled in the space behind the dial. It is the color of verdigris and old rubber, faintly freckled where the lamp touches it. Its arms feel out between the glass and the rim, delicate, muscular, tasting the water. It has learned the gauge’s moods. When the discharge line charges, the Bourdon tube flexes; when the pressure drops after the second thud, the gap in the case shifts by a fraction, and the octopus slips out to hunt. It moves along the wall, over the red mass of wrenches, past the fur of hydroids, and picks off shrimp too slow to scatter. When the lamp flickers, it turns white and folds itself back into the gauge, pulling its arms after it like a secret being put away.

Beneath them, the room is full of tenants. The lobsters in the concrete sump have made a society under the grating, their antennae waving from the gaps like slow weeds. The school of herring or silversides turns in the open water as a single animal, flaring and tightening when the pump changes its rhythm. The anemones are thousands, gray-pink and patient, and when the intake opens they lean into the current and open their mouths as if the sea were feeding them. The whelks and sea stars and flatworms keep to the lower surfaces. The hydroids and bryozoa grow over every thread and flange, a fine fur that turns the machinery into landscape.

The room itself is the true occupant. It is a machine worn down until it almost seems grown, and the sea has settled into it the way water settles into a lung. The walls are iron, but they act like stone. The lamp is the sun. The pump’s cycle is the tide. The intake surge is the storm. Nothing here breaks the ordinary rules. The water is cold. The iron is old. The animals are ordinary. The lamp burns amber. And every six minutes, the room takes one deep, loud breath.

If you need a single inhabitant, it is the wolf eel behind the switchgear, jaws moving, waiting for the next thud. But it is only the part of the room that looks back.
