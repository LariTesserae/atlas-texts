At first, the Weeping Vault seems to be inhabited only by water and stone. The city is too complete, too old, too evenly lit. Then you see a hand withdraw from a channel, or a pale shape shift on a high ledge, and you understand that some of the stone is breathing.

The people are not easily seen. They are pale, the color of mushroom gills or the underside of a blind newt. Their skin is damp and cool, with a fine dusting of white mineral in the creases of their knuckles and the folds of their necks. They are short and narrow-boned, built for tight galleries and low doorways. Their arms are long in proportion to their bodies, their hands wide and clever, the fingertips flattened from generations of pressing against wet stone. Their feet are bare and splayed, the soles tough as waterlogged leather. They grip the worn steps with a slow, sure weight.

Their hair is colorless or nearly so, fine grey-white strands that they cut rarely, with flakes of flint or blades of ancient bone. Many braid it back with strings of calcite beads gathered from the gutters. Some older men and women let it hang loose, so that in the blue lamplight they look like figures seen through deep water, their hair moving with the same deliberate drift as the newts.

Their faces are narrow, cheekbones high, lips thin. Their eyes are their most startling feature: large and dark, the pupils so wide they leave only a thin ring of grey iris, like a moon in eclipse. They see well in the dim mineral light, but they do not rely on sight. They hear with their entire bodies. The sound of falling water is not background noise; it is a second blood. They can tell from two galleries away if a single basin has shifted its pitch, if a clay jar has developed a hairline crack, if the hiss of the wall-sheet has grown too loud.

Because of this, they move as if movement is a kind of speech. Each step is placed to avoid interrupting the water’s meter. When they walk, their bare feet make a soft, wet sound, not a slap or shuffle, and they time their footfalls between the drips. In the narrow lanes, two passersby turn sideways and smile without showing teeth, and their shoulders brush with a noise like water over stone.

They do not speak as we do. Their language is a sung rhythm fitted into the gaps between the water’s notes. A sentence is not a string of words but a pattern of pitch and pause. If a phrase falls on a drip, it is repeated; if it clashes, it is reshaped. The water is not a metaphor for their conversation; it is the metronome and the editor. They also have a language of taps, knuckles on stone, for things that must be said quickly or privately, though there is little privacy. The taps are always tuned to the dominant notes of the nearest channel.

They have no names in the surface sense. A child is given a sequence of sounds that matches a point in the city’s music—perhaps the high ping of the copper basin near the north gallery, followed by the double plunk of three clay jars, and then the low gurgle of the drain under the eastern stair. This sequence is their name, and it is not repeated while they live. When they die, the name returns to the water, and eventually another child hears it and is given it again, though by then it has changed so subtly that only the oldest listeners can tell.

They number three hundred. This is not approximate; it is exact, and it has been exact for as long as anyone can remember. When a person dies, a pregnancy follows soon after, often before the body has been laid in its stone basin. The people do not speak of this as cause and effect. They simply know that the city requires three hundred voices, and it gets them. There is a census kept not in writing but in a row of small clay beads on a wire of ancient copper. Each bead is a person. When one is taken off, another is strung. The beads are so old they have been worn smooth, their colors faded to the same blue-grey as the walls.

They live in a web of responsibilities. Each person tends a length of channel, a cluster of basins, a corner of the mineral garden, a lamp, a drain, or a wall. The work is repetitive and holy. They scrape the calcite crust from places where it would clog the flow, but only just enough. They patch the clay linings with wet clay and ash. They tune the baffles that shape the fall of each drop. They fill the blue-flamed lamps with mineral oil and trim the wicks. They mend the old ropes and the cages of bone and hide that hang from the balconies, though no one remembers what the cages once held. They do not build new channels or carve new rooms. The city is finished. To add a new thing would be to introduce a note that had no place, and that would be a kind of death.

The children learn by touch. A baby is held against the wall, its small hand placed on the vibrating stone, and the adults around it sing until the baby’s fingers curl into the rhythm. Before a child can walk, it can tap the first bars of the water’s song. By the time it is old enough to tend a channel, it knows the note of every basin in its gallery. Education is not about learning facts; it is about learning to hear what the city is already saying.

There are no families as we know them. A child belongs to the gallery where it was born and to the stretch of water that runs through it. It is raised by its parents, yes, but also by the keepers of the nearest cistern, the menders of the adjacent wall, and the old woman who tunes the high copper basins. The child calls all of them “the ones who hold the edge of the note,” which is the closest thing to a word for kin. Love here is not a private fire but a long, shared listening.

There are attachments, of course. Two people may become “a chord,” which means their footfalls and breath and speaking voices have learned to interlock so perfectly that they can walk through the tightest lane side by side without breaking the water’s rhythm. Such pairs do not marry. They begin to sleep in the same carven niche and tend the same channel, and the city accepts their sound as one sound. When one of them dies, the other sometimes loses the ability to walk without knocking against the walls. They are given a smaller, quieter duty, perhaps polishing a single basin for a year, until the water has taught them to walk alone again.

They are not always gentle. They can be sharp, stubborn, full of hidden pride. There are long quarrels that take place entirely in the rhythmic tapping of a wall, so subtle that only the participants know they are fighting. There are people who hoard small prettinesses: a perfect gypsum flower, a black pebble from the deepest cistern, a shed newt skin. There are those who fall in love with the sound of their own voice and try to sing against the water, and they are corrected, not cruelly, but by being made to sit in the loudest place in the city, where the water’s voice is so strong that no human voice can lie.

They live long, by our measure, though they do not count years. They measure age by the thickness of the calcite on their favorite step, or by the number of times they have scraped a particular channel. The oldest among them are so still that they seem to have taken root. They do not grow moss, but they grow a kind of mineral sheen on their skin, especially on the backs of their hands and the tops of their feet. The very old are said to be “turning into wall,” and it is meant as a compliment.

When a person dies, the body is taken to a low, wet room off the main chamber, a stone garden where the water drips slowly and continuously. The body is placed in a shallow basin shaped by the first builders. Over years, the mineral-rich water covers it with a thin layer of calcite, gradually thickening until the body becomes a pale shape, a suggestion of a person, part of the stone. The people visit these basins. They do not mourn in our way. They sit and listen to the water falling on the stone that used to be a person, and they say that the dead have joined the seep. The walls of the city are very old, and the people know that they are, in part, made of their ancestors.

The blind newts are the only truly wild things. They are small, no longer than a finger, with translucent bodies and feathery gills like red threads. They live in the deepest, coldest cistern, where the water is so still it seems not to move. They are said to be the water’s dreams. When one appears, swimming up from the dark, the people stop. They do not capture it or touch it. They sing a note they did not know they knew, and the note is repeated by the walls, and for a moment the whole city is one voice. Then the newt drifts back down, and the sound returns to its usual layered rhythm. No one has heard a new note in living memory, but the people are not sure whether the newt causes the new note or the new note causes the newt.

The mineral gardens are not alive, but the people treat them as if they are. Gypsum flowers curl from the walls in pale, brittle petals. Aragonite needles cluster like frozen rain. Calcite crusts spread in the shape of ferns and feathers. The gardeners tend these growths, scraping away the excess, redirecting the water, preserving the shapes that have been there for centuries. They do not see this as separate from tending the channels. It is all one task: keeping the city exactly itself.

There is no day or night. The lamps burn at a constant height. The people sleep in shifts, not because they need to but because it is safer to have some always listening. A sleeper lies on a bed of packed clay and old hide, in a niche carved into the wall, with a thin curtain of mineral beads to break the sound into a softer rain. They dream, but their dreams are made of sound and stone, not of light or open air. They dream of the water climbing the north wall. They dream of the Weeping Mirror, which they visit only once in a lifetime.

The Weeping Mirror is the north wall, where the water does not fall but climbs, drawn upward by the stone’s fine pores, hanging in a permanent silver fur of droplets that never fall. The people go there alone, after long preparation. They stand before it and let the droplets catch the blue lamplight. They do not look for their reflection. They look for the city as it was when it was first built and as it will be when it is last seen, and they find that those two cities are the same. Some come away weeping; some come away silent. Both are considered correct.

If you were to sit on the narrow ledge long enough, you would begin to feel that the people are not separate from the place. They are the place, breathing. They are the water’s way of listening to itself. They are the stone’s way of moving. They are the city’s way of staying the same. And they would not call this a burden. They would tap twice on the wet stone and lay a palm against the wall, and the vibration would travel up through their bones, and they would smile—not with their mouths, which are still, but with their whole bodies, which are tuned.
