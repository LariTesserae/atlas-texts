They do not announce themselves. That is the first thing to know. The place is so full of its own noise—frogs, insects, falling water, the low hum of the towers—that you could walk for days and think you were the only one breathing at human height. But you would be wrong.

They live in the spaces between the sound. Not in silence—there is no silence here—but in the places where the sound has become so old it has settled into the ground. They are tall, though not thin; their bodies have the solid, water-worn look of the basalt columns, as if they were poured rather than born. Their skin is a deep, wet brown-black, and in the creases of their elbows, behind their knees, and along the ridge of their shoulders grows a fine green moss that gleams after rain. Their hair, if it is hair, is a mass of dark filaments that clumps and drips slowly for hours after the sky clears. Their faces are broad, with high cheekbones and wide-set eyes that reflect the gray-white noon light without narrowing. They blink rarely. When they stand still, water beads on their shoulders and runs down their arms in small rivulets, and the moss drinks it.

They are not born the way you might expect. The young ones appear in the steam glades, where the mineral terraces step down one into the next. A patch of pale tufa will begin to gather more than the usual orange and white bacteria; it will thicken, and over many seasons a shape emerges—a small figure, pale as tufa, already sitting or crouching as if it has been there for a hundred years. The adults come to the glades not to give birth, but to notice when a new one has accumulated enough to stand. They do not raise the young. The young are already old enough to walk, and they follow the paths by themselves, learning the extra steps the way a child learns a language. By the time their skin darkens to basalt, they are full-grown, though no one could say how long that takes. The terraces keep their own time.

They are not the makers of the towers, or if they are, the making is so far back that it has become a body-memory, not a story. They live among the scattered shelters, but not permanently. Each shelter holds one, or sometimes two, for a phase of the towers’ long pulse. They keep the shelters empty enough that the odd features remain: the wall that reflects the forest upside down is used as a mirror for checking the moss on one’s back; the too-narrow doorway is used as a test for whether one has eaten too many tree-fruits; the corner that tastes of cold metal is avoided during the hours when the frogs fall silent. They do not build new shelters. They do not repair the old ones. They simply use them, and their use is enough.

They walk the paths, but not quickly. Each step is deliberate, the foot placed so that water wells up around the toes and then sinks back. They do not avoid puddles. They are the ones who taught the paths to be longer than they should be, or perhaps they are the ones who learned the extra distance. When they walk, they sometimes pause and press a palm flat against a warm stone, and the warmth passes into them and leaves them a little warmer. It is not a gesture of worship; it is the way another person might steady themselves against a wall. If you follow them, you will notice that the path takes longer for you too, but not unpleasantly. The extra steps do not feel like motion; they feel like being in the same place more thoroughly.

They eat little. They drink the tea-dark water from stone basins, and they eat the soft white pith of tree ferns, the orange mineral crust from the steam terraces, and the small blue-black fruits that fall during the noon rain. They do not cook; they prefer things as they are. Their mouths are lined with a thick, heat-tolerant tissue; they can sip water from hot springs that would scald other animals. They eat slowly, and they share by leaving food in the shelters for the next person, not by handing it over. A bowl of fruit on a bench, a piece of mineral crust left on a windowsill—these are their letters.

They rarely meet. When two of them meet on a path, they do not speak at first. They stand side by side, facing the same direction, and let the sound of the frogs fill the space. Then one may make a low humming sound, like the towers, and the other answers with a click from the back of the throat. They may exchange something—a piece of pale mineral, a bundle of moss, a small object that is not carved but grown. Then they pass. There is no ceremony; it is the way water passes around a stone.

They are the ones who hear the second pulse. The towers release their mist rings at dawn for anyone to see, but the longer pulse—the one that takes decades—is felt only by the people who live there. They feel it as a change in the warmth of the path-stones, or as a shift in the taste of the cold-metal corner, or as a restlessness in the moss on their own shoulders. When the long pulse turns, they move to a different part of the plateau. They do not decide this; their bodies decide, the way a plant turns toward a gap in the canopy. They call this *the turning of the warm ground*, though they do not say it aloud often.

They are not a happy people, or a sad people. They are a patient people. Their emotions are slow and deep, like the water that pauses at a stone before sliding over it. They do not hurry. They do not fear the drop at the rim, though they sometimes stand at the edge and watch the clouds below, their faces wet with cloud drip. They do not build new shelters. They do not repair the old ones. They simply use them, and their use is enough.

The animals do not flee from them. The pale frogs sit on their knees when they rest by the hot pools. The water-running lizards use their shadows as bridges. The backwards-flying beetle lands on their shoulders and cleans the moss with its legs. The birds with white shoulders follow them in noisy troops, because the people disturb the understory just enough to flush out insects. The people do not hunt. They have no need to. They are not at the top of a food chain; they are another strand in the web, one that hums and waits and drinks hot water.

At night, if you are near a shelter, you may see one of them sitting in the doorway, not waiting. The fireflies rise around them in dense spirals. The tower glow is faint on the horizon, like the moon seen through water. They hum very softly, a note that matches the tower’s frequency. Their body is still, but the moss on their back grows a little in the dark. They are not guarding the place. They are part of the place, and the place is part of them.

They are called, in the old local word, *the Unattended*. Not because no one attends to them, but because they do not attend to themselves. They have no names, or rather they have names that are not spoken—a particular warmth of the path, a certain taste of the cold-metal corner, a pattern of moss on a shoulder. They know one another by these things. If you ask one of them who lives here, they will look at you with their wide, dark eyes and say nothing. Then they will turn and walk along the path, taking the extra steps, and you will understand that the answer is not a word but a way of walking.
