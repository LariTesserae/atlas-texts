At first, the plain seems uninhabited. No tracks, no burrows, no birds, no insects starting up out of the sparse green. The silence feels like an absence with weight. But the emptiness is a trick of scale. The things that live here are so small, so joined to the mineral world, that you have to stop walking and look at your feet.

The first inhabitant is a color.

In the shallow water at the edge of a terrace, a dark green skin lies over the mud. Up close it is not a stain but a fabric: cyanobacteria, filament by filament, woven into a mat. The surface is faintly slick, though it may look dry. If you kneel and watch, you might see tiny oxygen bubbles caught in it, the whole mat breathing in slow motion. It is not one creature. It is a billion, or a trillion, arranged into something with a common texture and a common need: to stay wet enough, salty enough, lit enough, without being killed by the light.

Around the green mats are other neighborhoods. Rust-red patches spread where iron and sulfur meet. These are sulfur bacteria, stacked in layers so fine they seem like brushstrokes, each layer running a different chemistry. In the saltiest pools, the water itself turns pink, then deep red. Halophilic archaea, little more than membranes around a kind of patience, pour out pigments to shield themselves from the sun. They do not move. The water moves, and they move with it, blooming when the salt rises, resting when rain dilutes the brine. Under the dry crust, they survive in tiny fluid pockets between crystals, waiting.

Then there are the plants. Not many. A clump of saltgrass here, a sprawl of samphire there, separated by so much distance that each one looks like a rumor. They grow where a freshwater seep dilutes the salt enough for roots to endure. Their leaves are small, fleshy, gray-green, built to lose as little water as possible and to store what they cannot refuse. Some of them sweat salt. Crystals form along their stems, and in the late afternoon they glitter as if decorated. They have no flowers worth the name, or flowers so reduced they would not satisfy a bee if a bee ever came. Nothing comes. They are wind-pollinated or they spread by shedding seeds into the wetness. Still, they are the only upright residents, the only ones with roots and leaves, and they hold their small territories against the mineral tide.

But the true residents are the mats. They are the oldest living thing here, and in a way they are the builders. They trap sediment with their filaments. They secrete a glue that binds new crystals together. When a flood brings fine clay, they catch it. When the water dries, they crack and curl, but the next moisture revives them. They grow over their own dead layers, and those dead layers become stone. In the terrace pools you can see the beginning of this: a soft dark cushion at the edge of the water, and beneath it, a hard white rim. The cushion is alive. The rim was alive once. The land is being made by them as much as by evaporation. The salt crust and the biofilm are not enemies; they are partners in the same slow construction.

There is no animal life anywhere, and that absence shapes everything. The mats are not grazed. The plants are not browsed. Nothing burrows through the crust or digs for water. The only disruption is weather: rain, wind, the cracking of salt, the sudden collapse of a hollow crust. That is why the microbial world is so evenly spread, so undisturbed. It grows in sheets because nothing has ever taught it to grow otherwise. It has no thorns, no toxins, no defenses against being eaten, because eating has not arrived. It is old in its chemistry but innocent in its ecology.

If you listen, you will not hear them. The only sounds are still the crystalline ticks and the low hiss of wind over the flats. But if you watch a pool from morning to night, you can see the life respond. As the sun climbs, the mats on the shallow edges wrinkle slightly, producing more of their protective pigments. As the water warms, gas bubbles rise. By afternoon, in the hardest light, the green mats look almost black, shutting down into a kind of suspended animation. When evening comes and the temperature drops, they ease back into full metabolism. Dew forms on the salt, and the mats swell with the new moisture. At night, under the stars, they are not sleeping; they are working in the dark, using the water and the stored chemistry of the day.

The place has no animals, but it is not without community. In a single square meter of wet crust there are more living cells than there are visible animals in a grassland. They trade molecules. The cyanobacteria make oxygen; the sulfur bacteria use it; the archaea make pigments; the algae store oils. They live in layers, each tuned to a different intensity of light, a different salinity, a different depth in the sediment. It is a city without movement, a market without voices.

If you ask who lives here, the answer is: the green film under the water, the pink bloom in the brine, the gray-green clump of saltgrass bending in the wind, the fine dark lines that trace the edges of last season’s pool. They are all still being made. They are all part of the same process. The plain is not a stage for life; it is life and mineral growing together, one layer at a time.

And if you wait long enough—longer than a day, longer than a year—you may begin to feel that the land itself is alive in the same slow way the mats are alive. It breathes water upward, exhales crystal, and sleeps under the stars. There is no builder, no animal, no tool. But there are residents. They are small, patient, and they are the first thing you learn to see.
