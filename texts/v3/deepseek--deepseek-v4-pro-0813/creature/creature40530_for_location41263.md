At first she is only a shape among the shadows, a patch of slightly smoother darkness against the braided bark. She has been still for so long that a pale gecko has crossed her ankle without alarm, and a drop of condensation has gathered in the hollow of her collarbone. Her breathing is slow, matching the give and settle of the limb beneath her; she and the weave are taking the same long breaths.

When she moves, it is not with a start but with an unfolding. Her fingers uncurl from a strand one by one, each joint creaking like a green stem. She shifts her weight onto the ball of her foot, and the moss beneath her compresses without sound. The light—green-amber and heavy with pollen—falls on her skin, which has the texture of old bark. Along her back and shoulders grows a fine, velvety moss, the same species that blurs the edges of the world. Her eyes are large and dark, with a faint green sheen when the light strikes them; they are not eyes that see color so much as depth and tension, the way the wood holds or the pattern turns. Her hands are long, four-fingered, with extra knuckles, and her toes are the same, suited to gripping the roundness of the world.

She is a Tender. Her name is not a sound made in the throat but a sequence of taps—*tik-tik-tah*—like water on a leaf. The others use it when they are far apart, sending it through the strands.

Today she is working a section of the outer reach, where the weave grows thin and the heat from below rises in slow, wet breaths. She carries a pouch at her hip, woven from inner bark, and inside it a blade of flint, a needle of bone, and a coil of cord. She has been following a young liana that has begun to twist out of pattern, curling against the grain of the net. She stops, crouches, and takes the fiber cord from her pouch. Her fingers move with the patience of seasons; she bends the liana back, not forcing it, but giving it a suggestion. She ties the cord in a loose figure-eight around it and a guide strand, then plucks the knot to test its tension. The vine will grow along this path now, or it will not; she has done her part.

A beetle with a polished, seed-like carapace crawls over the back of her hand. She lets it pass. Her attention is on the wood beneath her, the faint vibrations traveling through the fibers. She closes her eyes, and the world becomes a map of tensions: a herd of grazers moving slowly three layers down, their weight sending soft pulses; a glider launching from a high strand, the release of tension sharp and quick; a blind cricket ticking in a damp recess. She reads it all without thought, as a human reads the weather from the pressure of the air.

Then she stops. It is not a sound that stops her, but the absence of sound. The ticking of the crickets fades, not because they have gone quiet, but because the wood itself is not there to carry them. Ahead, the limb widens, and there is a place where the pattern stops. A true hole. She sees it as a flatness, a gap that is not a gap. Its edges are too clean, as if the world has been cut with something sharper than flint. The air around it is still; the water drops that roll along the bark hesitate and curve sideways, refusing to enter. She has seen them shift—a gap that was there at dawn may be gone by dusk, or reappear a hundred arms to the left—and she does not try to understand; understanding is not the way. She makes the tying-off gesture, her fingers moving in a slow, deliberate knot—not to seal the hole, but to acknowledge it, to keep it from unraveling further. Then she steps to the far side of the hollow and does not look back.

Far away, a tapping reaches her. She pauses, her fingers resting on the wood, and feels the message: *tik-tik-tah*, then two slow, three quick. It is her sister, three strands to the north, saying that the north span has softened and must be re-tied before the next rain. She taps back an acknowledgment: *tah*, then a single knock. The sound travels through the living fibers, a conversation in the bones of the world. Her sister is the only other Tender she has seen in the last cycle of seasons; they meet at the crossings, share fruit and cord, and then go back to their separate reaches. But the signs of others are everywhere—a fresh trim, a new knot, a seed pressed into a favorable gap.

She alters her route, stepping from one woven limb to another. The gaps between strands are enormous, but she moves without hesitation, her feet knowing the give of each branch. Below, the warm mist rises, carrying the smell of wet bark and something floral. A cloud drifts past, white against the blue-gray haze, raining into a lower chamber. She pauses to drink from a leaf-cup where water has pooled, the liquid warm and tasting faintly of pollen. She plucks a pale fruit from an orchid, splits it with a thumbnail, and eats the soft pulp, spitting the seeds into a crevice where they will find purchase. She pushes a windblown seed into a gap in the moss, pressing it gently into the fibrous soil. Nothing is wasted. The fallen leaves, the bones of small animals, the dead stems—all become the dark, fibrous compost that feeds the next growth, and she facilitates this as naturally as she breathes.

The light never gets bright here; it is always a perpetual late afternoon under heavy clouds. But by the time she reaches the north span, the haze has shifted to a deeper amber. She finds the softened place, a section of the braided floor that has begun to fray, the strands dark and pithy with rot. She kneels, takes her bone needle, and begins to loosen the dead fibers, pulling them away one by one. She works for a long time, her movements unhurried, until the wound is clean. Then she cuts fresh cord, soaks it in the warm water of a nearby pool, and begins to re-weave the gap. The living strands accept the cord; within a season, they will grow around it, and the repair will become part of the world.

When she is finished, she sits back on her heels and feels the net settle. The vibration of her work has traveled out, and the wood has answered with a soft, almost inaudible sigh. She taps a message to her sister: *done*. The answer comes back quickly, a satisfied pattern like two raindrops close together.

At night, though the light only dims by a degree, she returns to her nest. It is a hollow in the crook of a great branch, lined with dried ferns and the shed skins of geckos. She curls into it, her long fingers wrapped around her flint blade, and listens to the world settle. The water drips. The wood creaks. The true hole remains, somewhere out there, a silence in the net. She does not dream, or if she does, the dreams are of threads pulling taut and knots holding. She breathes with the weave, and the world breathes with her. In the morning, she will rise, check the new shoots, and continue. There is always a thread that needs guiding, a hole that needs tending. The pattern is ancient and incomplete, but it holds. And as long as it holds, so does she.
