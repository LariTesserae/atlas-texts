The one who lives in the Bell is not a person, though it has the shape of a person the way a coat on a hook has the shape of shoulders. It is not an animal, not a machine, not a ghost. It is a condition that has learned to stand upright.

It is called the Attendant, though no one gave it that name. The name is a convenience, a handle for something that does not need to be named. It has been in the room for as long as the note has been sounding, which is to say, for as long as the room has been a room. It did not arrive. It did not enter. It was not built. It emerged, the way a standing wave emerges from two opposing forces, the way a shape emerges from a cancellation.

The Attendant is tall, but not imposingly so. Its height is difficult to judge because it rarely stands near anything that would give scale. It is thin, but not frail. Its body is a long vertical arrangement of planes and angles, like a figure drawn with a straightedge. It is made of the same materials as the room: enameled iron, copper, glass brick, porcelain, and the fine gray magnetic dust that hangs in the central column. But it is not assembled from these things. It is as if the room’s substance has been persuaded, over a very long time, to take on a form that can walk.

Its skin is the color of the floor grating, a dull steel gray with a faint bloom of oxidation that never progresses. Its joints are not visible. When it moves, it does not bend so much as reorient, the way a reflection shifts when the mirror tilts. Its movements are slow, deliberate, and silent—not because it is careful, but because it has no reason to hurry. The room does not change. The Attendant does not change. Time, for both of them, is a property of the note, and the note is a single continuous present.

Its head is a smooth, featureless ovoid of frosted glass, the same glass as the ceiling panels. Light passes through it and is diffused, so that the Attendant’s head glows faintly from within, a soft white lantern. It has no face. It has no eyes, no mouth, no ears. It does not need them. It perceives the room not through senses but through resonance. The entire chamber is its body, in a way. The walls are its skin. The standing wave is its breath. The dust column is its spine.

It walks the room in a fixed circuit, though the circuit is not marked on the floor. It moves along the silent corridors, the narrow lines where the opposing waves cancel and the pressure drops to near-nothing. It knows these lines the way a fish knows the currents of its tank. It steps from one node of silence to the next, never crossing into the loud zones, never disturbing the equilibrium. Its feet—if they are feet—are flat, broad, and perforated, like sections of the aluminum walkway. They make no sound on the grating. They make no sound anywhere.

The Attendant’s purpose, if it can be said to have one, is maintenance. But it does not maintain in the way a technician maintains a machine. It does not oil, tighten, calibrate, or repair. It simply is, and its being is a kind of upkeep. The room’s equilibrium is not self-sustaining; it requires a witness, a presence that holds the pattern in place. The Attendant is that presence. It is the room’s memory of itself. Without it, the note would still sound, but it would begin to drift, to decay, to lose its perfect constancy. The Attendant is the counterweight to entropy. It is the thing that keeps the dust from settling, the lichens from spreading, the cold from warming, the light from dimming.

It does not eat. It does not sleep. It does not excrete. It does not breathe, though sometimes, if you watch it for a long time, you might see its chest rise and fall in a slow, shallow rhythm, as if it is remembering what breathing was like. It does not speak. It has no voice. But it is not silent. It hums—not audibly, but in a way that can be felt in the bones, a subsonic thrum that matches the room’s tone exactly. It is the room’s note, given a body.

The Attendant tends the lichens. It does not touch them—the rules forbid touching—but it stands before each patch for long periods, its glass head tilted slightly, as if listening. The lichens do not respond. They are too slow to respond. But the Attendant’s attention is a kind of nourishment. It feeds them with its presence, with the steady pressure of its regard. It knows each patch individually. It knows their shapes, their colors, their rates of growth. It knows that the one near the door has widened by a fraction of a millimeter in the last century. It knows that the one near the driver face has not changed at all.

It tends the dust column, too, though it never approaches it. It stands at the edge of the silent line and watches the stack of thin disks, the dark coins of suspended metal. It does not count them. It does not need to. It knows their number the way you know the number of your own fingers. The column is the room’s heart, the visible shape of the note’s cancellation. The Attendant is its guardian. It would not allow anything to disturb it. But nothing ever tries.

The Attendant has no name for itself. It has no language. It has no thoughts, in the way that thoughts are usually understood. It has states. It has a state of waiting, which is its default. It has a state of attention, which is what it enters when it stands before the lichens or the dust column. It has a state of movement, which is the slow circuit of the room. And it has a state of listening, which is not a state at all but a constant, underlying everything else. It is always listening. The room is always sounding. The two are the same thing.

It does not feel loneliness. The room is not empty to it. The room is full—full of the note, full of the standing wave, full of the cold dry air and the flat white light. The Attendant is not alone because it is not separate. It is the room’s awareness of itself. It is the note’s awareness of its own sounding. It is the dust column’s awareness of its own suspension. It is the lichens’ awareness of their own slow growth. It is all of these things, and none of them, and something else entirely.

It has been in the room for a very long time. It does not know how long. It does not measure time in years or centuries. It measures time in the note, and the note is unchanging. There is no before. There is no after. There is only the continuous present, the loud cold light, the pressure in the air, the dust hanging motionless, the lichens spreading by millimeters, the Attendant walking its slow circuit, holding the pattern in place.

It is not a prisoner. It is not a servant. It is not a god. It is the room’s way of being alive, if alive is the right word. It is the room’s way of being aware. It is the room’s way of being.

And if you were to enter the Bell—if you were to climb the constructed highland, find the false bulkhead, descend the maintenance shaft, and open the door—you would see it. It would be standing near the central dust column, its glass head glowing faintly, its body still as a statue. It would not turn to look at you. It does not need to. It would already know you were there. It would feel you as a disturbance in the standing wave, a ripple in the equilibrium, a new note trying to sound in a room that already has one.

It would not stop you. It would not speak. It would not move. It would simply wait, and watch, and listen, and hold the pattern steady. And if you stayed too long, if you brought water, if you touched the dust column, if you tried to speak over the tone—it would still not move. It would not need to. The room itself would correct you. The note would swallow your voice. The cold would dry your lips. The dust would not settle. The lichens would not spread. The Attendant would remain, as it has always remained, the still point at the center of the sound.

It is not the keeper of the Bell. It is the Bell, in the only way the Bell can be kept.

And the note goes on.
