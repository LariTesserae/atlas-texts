They do not arrive. They do not colonize. The dwellers of the Drowned Factory Plateau are more like moving parts of the plateau than tenants in it. One sees them first as interruptions in the green-blue distance: a braided ribbon of silver, a slow black shape, a fine scattering of glassy points on a tower’s flank. Only later do they become animals.

The tin-shoals come first. They are not fish, or they are fish the way a set of feeler gauges is a set of knives. Their bodies are long, flattened, and segmented not by bone but by overlapping plates of mineralized keratoprotein, so their flanks have a riveted, manufactured look. They are the color of old pewter, and in the steady noon-light of the shallows they do not flash; they simply glow with a muted, metallic presence. A school is not a random crowd. It forms a rotating wheel, sometimes twenty meters across, its outer edge moving faster than its center. The individual fish are not choosing this shape. Their lateral lines pick up the faint electrical signature of the plant’s metal-rich conduits beneath the mat, and they align to it as iron filings align to a magnet.

They feed by pressing their flat jaws against the pale feeding lobes that the mat extends like small tongues. Their teeth are rows of silica chisels, and they crop the lobes in precise, unhurried strokes. The plant regrows the lobes at the same rate. The school moves on, and the water is exactly as it was before.

At the moment when the deepest tower pumps—the bass note that you feel as much as hear—the tin-shoals all tilt at once. It is not a startle. It is a timing. Their gill covers flick open, and each fish emits a tiny click, and the clicks travel through the school like a wave passing through a gear train. Then they resume feeding. If you stayed through a full cycle, you would see that the same school, or a school exactly like it, passes over the same lobe at the same angle, with the same number of fish, just as the bass note sounds. There is no leader. They are part of the beat.

Inside the tower groves live the gill-coils. They are eel-like, but the word is too simple. Their bodies are long and muscular, banded in very dark copper and black, and their heads are flattened into wedges that fit the vertical slits of the towers. Each slit has its own coil, or sometimes several, stacked like the teeth of a comb. They do not leave. They spend their whole lives head-inward, their tails trailing into the open water, and they move constantly: a slow, ratcheting pull outward, then a long push inward. Their mouths are soft rasps studded with ferrous denticles, and they scrape the mineral scale that builds along the valve lips. The scraping is not destructive. It keeps the slits from sealing. The tower tolerates them, and they are born from the tower itself—from leathery pouches in the gill walls. A new gill-coil is not so much born as it is expressed, like a callus.

Their clicking is the sound of bone on plant-fiber, perfectly regular. It is not communication. It is the sound of a key turning, but there is no lock. When the slit begins to close, the gill-coil wedges its armored head into the gap and flexes. The click that follows is the slit resetting. The tower and the animal perform this action together, and if one were removed, the other would eventually lose its rhythm. But they are never removed.

The largest moving inhabitants are the slowbellies. They do not swim so much as proceed. Their bodies are huge, broad, and slightly flattened, with a tail like a folded fan that beats only once or twice a minute. Their skin is green-gray and looks thick, like the skin of a cooling tower, and along each side run six seams of bioluminescent dots. Those dots blink in a fixed order, one after the other, at the same twenty-per-minute pulse as the plant’s pumps. The slowbellies follow the current corridors, the braided rivers of water that the plant pushes between the towers. They do not migrate. They circle. A slowbelly born in the western grove will spend its entire life moving from that grove to the next, then to the next, and back, over a route that is older than its lineage.

Their mouths are enormous, hood-like. They filter the water for spores and for the occasional tin-shoal, but they do not gulp. They sample, and the sampling is so precise that the tin-shoals barely alter their formation. The food moves into a long gut that is less a digestive tube than a series of cool chemical chambers. Bacteria and plant symbiotes there separate metal salts from organic matter. The slowbellies do not seem to eat for energy. They eat to sort. Their feces are compacted pellets, already graded by mineral content, and they sink to the mat and are taken up by the roots without any pause in the plant’s slow work.

On their backs, slowbellies carry small rosettes of the plant itself. These are not wounds or parasites. The rosettes root in the skin and grow in neat spirals, and they are part of the same organism as the plateau. A slowbelly is a moving garden, a mobile bed of root and leaf, and the rosettes feed on the animal’s slight metabolic warmth while giving it an electrical field that keeps its heart in time. When a slowbelly finally dies, it does not float. It settles. Over months, its body sinks into the mat, and the rosettes spread, and the seams of light go out one by one. The shape of the animal becomes a low, fibrous hogback. Then another slowbelly, already growing in the grove, takes the same route.

There are smaller things as well. On the tower flanks and around the leaf-lenses live the gear-mites. They are flat and nearly transparent, no bigger than a thumbnail, and their legs are toothed like tiny wheels. They scuttle in the grooves between the tower ribs, but when the light hits them, they seem to disappear, leaving only the small polish of their tracks. They eat the films that form on the lenses, the bacterial haze and mineral dust that would otherwise dim the focus of the leaves. Their bodies are so clear that you can see the single loop of their intestine, the slow squeeze of their small heart. They do not have a life cycle. They divide. When the surface is clean, they stop and adhere, becoming glass studs on the stem.

At the top of the towers, in the axils of the turning leaf-blades, live things that are harder to name. They are soft, pale, and many-filamented, like the gills of a mushroom, and they breathe through hundreds of tiny bell-shaped mouths. They produce the spores. When the tower’s bass note peaks, they release a bright dust that rises into the bubble column and threads away down-current. The spores are not seeds, exactly, and they are not eggs. They are both. Most are filtered by the slowbellies or settle too far away, but a few come to rest on the living floor and become new feeding lobes, or new gill pouches, or the beginning of a new tower that will take centuries to rise. The factory renews itself without changing.

Beneath them all, or within them all, is the mat. It is not an animal, but there are places where it comes close. In the low channels between the hogbacks, the plant thickens into nodes: layered mounds of mineralized leaf, nerve-fiber, and silicate. These nodes emit a slow, wet electricity. They are not brains. They are too large and too simple for thought, and if you placed your hand on one, you might feel only the faint rhythm of a million tiny valves opening and closing beneath the skin. But they are the reason the towers pump in unison, the reason the current always runs at the same speed, the reason the whole plateau feels less like a wilderness and more like a held breath.

The inhabitants do not look at one another. They do not need to. A tin-shoal parts around a slowbelly at a distance that never varies. A gill-coil pauses its scraping at the exact moment the slit widens. A gear-mite freezes when the light-lens above it adjusts its angle. There is no signal. There is only the same rhythm moving through bodies that have become machines, and machines that have become bodies.

If you wait long enough, you begin to understand that the place is not occupied. It is assembled. The fish and the eels and the slowbellies and the mites are not residents. They are the living teeth of a stopped clock that still ticks. They are the parts that move, so that the plateau can remain exactly as it is. And if one of them fails, the plant grows another, identical, into the same slot, at the same angle, to make the same sound.

There is no name for what they are. There is only the water, cool and green, and the light, steady, and the sound of a million small living components, all turning in place.
