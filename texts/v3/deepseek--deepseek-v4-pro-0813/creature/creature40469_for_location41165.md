The first thing you mistake for weather is alive. The golden-green glow that seems to come from nowhere is, in fact, the breathing of countless tiny bodies suspended in the liquid-air—a plankton of luminous motes, each no larger than a grain of dust, each pulsing with a faint cold light. They are the oldest inhabitants, the ones who made the world visible. They drift in slow spirals, following the same currents that shape the valleys, and their collective light is the day of this place. They are not plants, not animals, but a third thing: living light, consuming the dissolved minerals in the water-air and exhaling photons. They are the foundation, and everything else either eats them, filters them, or carries them within its own flesh as a kind of internal sun.

Above them, or among them, move the creatures you first took for jellyfish. They are called—if they are called anything—the veil-drifters. They are larger than you think, some with bells the size of your torso, their bodies so transparent that only the faint outline of their internal organs is visible, like glass organs floating in a glass body. They move by slow contractions, pushing the thick medium through their bells, trailing long tendrils that are both mouth and hand. Each tendril is covered in fine cilia that comb the water-air for the luminous motes, and in the darkness of the deeper valleys, the drifters glow with the accumulated light of their meals, becoming moving constellations. They are solitary, but not lonely; when two meet, they do not fight or flee. They simply drift together for a while, their tendrils touching, exchanging a few luminous motes, and then separate, as if they have shared a thought. They reproduce by splitting, or by releasing tiny, almost invisible buds that float away to grow in the thick medium. They have no brains, but they have a kind of distributed awareness—each part of the body sensing pressure and light, so that the whole creature is a slow, pulsing idea. They are the most numerous of the large animals, though "numerous" means you might see one every few hours. Their rarity is not a sign of scarcity but of spacing; each veil-drifter needs a vast volume of liquid-air to filter enough food, and so they spread themselves across the plateau like calm, wandering stars.

Closer to the ground, where the medium is thicker and the light dimmer, live the bark-slugs and the moss-mimics. These are small, flattened creatures, no longer than your hand, their bodies covered in soft, photosynthetic skin that grows patches of moss and algae. They graze on the spongy bark of the trees and on the dense mats of detritus that cover the soil. They move so slowly that, from a distance, they appear to be just another lump of moss or a growth on a trunk. Only if you watch for a long time—minutes, sometimes hours—do you see the slow ripple of muscle beneath their skin, the slight shift of their body as they slide from one patch of food to another. They have no eyes, but their skin is sensitive to the faint light, and they respond to the shadows of passing drifters by freezing, becoming indistinguishable from the moss around them. They communicate with each other by releasing thin trails of scent into the liquid-air, chemical messages that linger for days, telling others where the best bark is, or where a tree has fallen and the detritus is rich. They are the grazers of this world, the slow herds, though they never gather in large numbers. Their slowness is their defense; in a world where everything moves slowly, to be fast would be to stand out, and to stand out is to be eaten.

There are predators, too, but they are even rarer. You might see one if you are patient: a long, ribbon-like creature, half-transparent, with a row of tiny lights along its length. It moves through the liquid-air with a sinuous, eel-like motion, its body undulating in the thick medium. It hunts the bark-slugs and the moss-mimics, not by chasing them, but by drifting over them and then dropping a net of sticky tendrils that trap them in a slow embrace. It is not fast; it is simply patient, waiting for the grazers to move beneath it. It might take an hour to engulf a single slug, and when it is done, it drifts away, its body glowing with the stolen light of its meal. This creature has no name, or if it has one, the Weathered have not shared it.

But the Weathered are the ones you will remember, if you remember anything from this place. They are not the only intelligent life—there are others, more diffuse, more ancient—but they are the ones who have bodies, who walk the plateau, who tend the memory-pools. They are humanoid, but only in the way that a standing stone is humanoid if you half-close your eyes. They are tall and thin, their limbs long and their hands and feet webbed. Their skin is smooth, like polished soapstone or tanned hide, but it is not opaque; in the dim light, you can see the faint glow of their internal organs, the slow pulse of a heart that beats once every few minutes. Their faces are a suggestion: two dark hollows for eyes, a slit for a nose, a mouth that rarely opens because they do not need to eat in the way we eat. They absorb nutrients through their skin, from the liquid-air itself, and they drink only from the memory-pools. They have no hair, and their heads are smooth, sometimes covered with a thin moss that grows over their scalps like a living cowl. They do not age, or they age so slowly that a century is to them what a season is to us. Their bodies are worn smooth by time, like the stones scattered across the plateau, and in some cases, moss and lichen have grown over their limbs so thoroughly that they look like part of the forest floor. It is said—though who would say it?—that the Weathered are the descendants of the civilization that built the standing stones and the walls, the ones who broke reality and then stayed to live in the breakage. They do not remember this. If you ask them, they will not answer with words; they will simply look at you with their dark hollows, and you will feel the weight of their forgetting.

What they do remember is carried in their bodies, not in their minds. Their bones are shaped by the old gravity, their skin remembers the old sun, their lungs remember the old air. They are living fossils of a world that no longer exists, but they are not relics; they are alive, and they are the caretakers of this broken place. They tend the memory-pools. Not as priests, not as keepers, but as gardeners. They plant the spiral-growing plants around the pools, coaxing the stems into geometries that should not exist, and they know that these spirals are a kind of writing. They do not read them—they feel them, the same way you feel a familiar word in your mouth. The spirals are records of what the pools have shown them, and the pools show them everything: the shapes of the old world, the echoes of the reality that broke. When a Weathered bends over the pool and drinks, they are not drinking water; they are drinking memory. It is their only sustenance, and it is also their only history. The water-air itself remembers, and the Weathered are the ones who remember by drinking.

They communicate with each other not with sound, but with pressure and light. Their skin is covered in faint bioluminescent patterns, slow ripples of color that travel across their bodies like weather. A conversation between two Weathered might take hours, even days, the patterns shifting and responding to each other in a language of pure nuance. They also touch, and when they touch, their skins merge for a moment, and they share everything—not just thoughts, but memories, sensations, the taste of the pool, the weight of the liquid-air. This touching is not intimate in the way we understand intimacy; it is simply the only way to be sure that the other is real. Their bodies are so porous, so worn, that the boundary between self and other is easily crossed, and they have learned to cross it without losing themselves.

They are solitary, each one claiming a territory of hundreds of square miles, but they meet at the memory-pools at certain times, when the currents shift and the light changes. These gatherings are slow, silent, and luminous. The Weathered stand around the pool, their bodies glowing in patterns that interweave, and for a while, they are not individuals but a single, slow dialogue of light. They share what they have seen, what they have drunk, what they have remembered. Then, one by one, they drift apart, returning to their own valleys, their own stones, their own moss. They build nothing, and they leave no marks. Their only architecture is the spiral of plants around the pools, and even that is grown, not built. They are, in the truest sense, part of the landscape.

And then there are the Remembered—the ones who have gone too far. Some Weathered, after living so long that their bodies have become more mineral than flesh, walk into the deepest part of the valley and do not come out. They wade into the pool, and the water-air closes over them, and they dissolve—not into death, but into memory. They become part of the medium, part of the water-air itself. You can see them sometimes, if you stand at the edge of the pool and wait: a faint outline of a face, a hand, a whole body, shimmering in the dim light. They are the shapes that the water-air holds, the echoes of the old world and the old people. They are not ghosts; they are not alive, but they are not gone. They are the memory of the place, given form. And the Weathered come to the pools to drink them, to take them in, to carry them inside their own bodies. In this way, the Remembered become part of the living again, not as individuals, but as a kind of shared tissue of memory.

This is the life of the plateau. It is slow, and it is quiet, and it is almost invisible. If you were to walk through it too quickly, you would see nothing but plants and the occasional drifting light. But if you stop, if you stand still for a day, a week, a month, you would begin to see the movement: the slow rise of a bark-slug, the pulse of a veil-drifter, the distant glow of a Weathered standing among the trees. And if you stood for a year, you might see them move—a step, a gesture, a touch. And if you stood for a century, you would see them gather at the pool, drink, and dissolve. But you do not have a century. So you must take them on faith, these slow lives, these worn-smooth beings. You must believe that they are there, even when they look like stones, even when they look like the light.
