First there is no one. The hollow holds its mist like a thought it hasn’t finished. Then a doorway becomes a doorway again: a hide curtain shoves aside, a small figure stoops under a lintel that has thickened since yesterday, and a hand with a stone adze chips away the new white growth. That is the first fact of the people here. They are not the hollow’s masters. They are its counterweight, the ones who unmake just enough to keep a gap open for their own lives.

They are few—forty, fifty, perhaps a hundred in a season when the springs run high and a trader stays. They are small-boned and narrow in the shoulders, built for squeezing through lanes no wider than a man’s ribs. Their skin is pale in the way the stone is pale: cloud-gray, chalk-dusted, the color of water over limestone. A fine white powder lives in the creases of their knuckles and the rims of their ears. They do not scrub it away. A woman blows on her hands and the dust rises, then she returns to scraping a sill. They smell of wet hide, cold stone, and the faint mineral sweetness of the walls; after a day of work they are powdered like millers. Their hair is cut short or braided tight against the skull and wrapped in hide. Loose hair left wet becomes brittle and white, like threads of stone. Their eyes are the color of rain in a stone bowl. They move with a close, careful economy. A wide gesture could catch a wall. A full stride could crack a new rim. Their bodies have learned the hollow’s narrowness the way water learns a channel.

They dress in hide and leather cured in the lower pools. Tunics are sewn with sinew and bone needles. Some wear skirts of woven reed or cloaks of black moss felted until dense. Feet are bare or wrapped in hide strips. Bare soles grow thick and porous, almost pumice, and grip the wet stone. In cold weather they wear hide boots with moss-stuffed soles, but most prefer to feel the stone under them. A child’s foot, if it rests too long in a mineral trickle, will begin to grow a white shell. Parents scrape their children’s feet the way they scrape doorways.

The work begins before full light, because the water has been working all night. A rim may have cracked under new weight. A channel may have narrowed by a finger. A door may have sealed shut. Someone walks the lanes with a wooden mallet and taps the walls, listening for the hollow sound that means growth and the dull sound that means weakness. Others clear sluices, lift leather dams, carry baskets of broken crust to the terrace edges. The work has no end, but they do not speak of it as drudgery. They speak of it as breathing. You do not finish breathing. You keep the gap open.

Every person has a task by age and gift. Children scrape the stepping stones and learn which crust will bear their weight. Young men and women tend the stone-farm pools, setting basket forms into fast water so the water will weave them into jars and tiles. Older women keep the spring mouths clean with bone picks, clearing the grit that would seal a spring shut. Old men and widows watch the sluices and settle questions of water. The most respected are the rim-wrights: the ones who can tap a pool edge and know by the sound whether it will hold another season or fail before night. There is always at least one, a quiet person who keeps one ear turned to the ground.

Water is the only wealth. Every spring mouth has an owner. The right to a spring passes from mother to daughter, because, they say, water and women remember the same way. The upper springs are the cleanest and the coldest, and the family that holds them speaks first in any argument. The lower pools are poor water but good for retting reeds and curing hides. Between them, the hollow is a web of gates and leather dams and little stone weirs. A sluice is opened by the width of a thumb, for the length of a song, for the amount of water that fills a certain cup. These measures become law. Disputes are not shouted. They are brought to a small room near the center, where an old woman keeps a bundle of notched bones, each notch a right. She reads them with her fingers. If the matter cannot be settled, the whole hollow meets on the widest terrace and talks in low voices while the water drips behind them. They have no writing beyond the notches. What matters is kept in the hands and in the stone.

They live packed, like fingers in a fist. A house is not a private place. Walls are shared, floors are ceilings, and every person knows the footfall of the family above. They sleep in niches scraped into the stone, lined with hides and old reed, small as coffins and warm with body heat. They sit on low stools of stone or stiffened hide. They cook over small, guarded fires of black moss and dried reed. Wood is too rare to burn. The smoke is thin and bitter, vented through cracks in the upper walls. Food is spare: a green-black paste of boiled moss, pounded reed hearts, a few eggs in season, and the pale fish only on days of festival or necessity. Sometimes a trader comes up from the foggy lower ground with flint and black grain, and leaves with whiteware and hides. But the hollow does not depend on such visits. Mostly it eats what the water permits.

There is a child, Rill, nine years old, who scrapes the lane between her family’s room and the drinking pool. Her scraper is a sliver of dark flint set in a bone handle. She has already learned the first rule: do not leave anything in the water you do not want kept. She is learning the second: do not trust a new rim with your whole weight. Once she let her heel rest in a trickle until a white film formed. Her mother scraped it off with a hard edge, and now Rill walks quickly on the stepping stones, never resting. She is learning to tell new stone from old by color: new crust is whiter, bluer, and takes a nail mark. Old stone is grayer and holds. She has also learned the third rule: do not walk a path too rarely, because the water will close it.

Above the deepest pool lives old Mara the Fish-Keeper. She is not the fish’s owner, she says; she is their neighbor. They are pale as peeled willow, and they rise to her shadow. She feeds them scraps of moss paste and speaks to them in a low, damp hum. They are not for eating except in the hardest month. They keep the pool sweet and give her company. She has lived alone so long that the fish have names, though she will not tell them to anyone. At evening she opens a small sluice and lets new water whisper into the pool, and the fish turn in the current. Children sit on the bridge above her house, watching their own reflections hang upside-down beside the fish.

Callan the Rim-Wright is perhaps twenty-five, with a face too young for the white in his hair. He walks the terraces every morning before the light is full. He carries a little mallet of fire-hardened wood and a piece of bone. He taps each rim three times, then presses his ear to the stone. He can hear a crack before it opens, a dull note under the bright ring. When he finds a bad rim, he does not break it. He eases the water away, lets the stone dry, and braces it with a willow bundle. Once a rim failed under him and he fell into the pool below. The water kept his left arm stiff for a week with a crust of white. He scraped it off patiently and went back to work. He says the stone is only doing what the water tells it.

At the upper springs lives old Anra, the Warden of the Mouths. She is old but not bent. Her face is lined like a dry lakebed. She owns the cleanest water and gives it out by the width of a bone wedge. If a family has a new child, she opens the spring a fraction wider for a season. If a family wastes water, she closes it. She does not punish often, because to close a sluice here is to close a throat. But she can. She keeps the spring mouths clean with a bone pick, and she talks to the water as if to a busy girl: *Not so fast, not now, there is room below.* People bring her problems. She listens without speaking, then turns to adjust a sluice. That is her answer.

They do not pray. They maintain. If you ask a hollow child why she scrapes a doorway, she will say, *The water keeps the house; I keep the gap.* That is the whole theology. The water is not a god but a maker, a pourer, a builder. The stone is not dead but slow. People are the third thing: the unmakers, the ones who draw the line between enough and too much. They have a story that the hollow was once only a crack in the hill, and the water began to speak, and every word became a layer of stone. The people came later, not to stop the speaking but to make room inside it. The hollow is still being spoken. The walls are a sentence that has not ended.

The dead are not buried. That would be to offer them to the water, and the water would keep them too well. Instead they are laid in niches high on the back wall, where the mist is thinner, wrapped in hide, and a thin slab is cut across the opening. Over years the mineral breath seals the slab white. Once a year the names are re-carved, because the water would otherwise smooth them away. The names are simple: Rill, Crust, Moss, Weep, Stone-Eater, Fish-Child. They do not put dates. Dates are for finished places.

They are a quiet people because the hollow demands quiet, but they are not sad. They laugh in low, short bursts, like water knocking a stone loose. They sing one note, mostly, and the gray bird copies it. They live in the narrow space between the water’s making and their own unmaking, and they know that space is temporary. The hollow is not a ruin, and they are not a remnant. They are the other hand of a process, the chisel held to the white stone of the world. If you stay long enough, you will see them not as inhabitants but as a kind of breath the hollow takes: small, necessary, and always in danger of being sealed over by the next slow word.
