At first there is no resident. There is only a path—not a planned path, but a route of least resistance that has been walked so many times the mud is a little firmer, the salt crust broken into a pattern. The path runs from the pump house to the rail spur, then loops to the warm outflow pools and back. It avoids the softest ground. It pauses at each valve. It is not made by any machine.

Follow the path long enough and you notice it is kept. The algae is scraped from the rungs of the steel stair. The gauge faces are wiped clean. The salt is chipped off the valve wheels in small white crescents. Somebody or something touches the place with intention, but never enough to disturb it. The birds remain packed on the pipes. The crabs do not retreat farther than their own shadows.

In the orange mist, the resident is not a body at first. It is a change in the density of the fog, a tallness where there should be no tallness. Then the tallness moves. It moves with a slow, deliberate gait, each step tested before weight is given. It wears what the place would wear if the place had a body: layers of dark oilcloth gone whitish at the seams, rubber waders crusted to the knee, a hood of old canvas that has taken on the color of the mud. On its back is a load—not a pack but a frame of bent pipe and wire, hung with tools, coils of rope, mesh bags, a dented lantern. The load makes its silhouette irregular, like a walking section of the infrastructure.

Its hands are bare. The fingers are long, pale, wrinkled from permanent wetness, the knuckles crusted with salt. It uses them carefully on hot pipes, knowing exactly how long it can hold before the heat turns from warning to wound. Its face, when it turns, is not a face you can read. The eyes are pale, almost colorless in the sodium light, half-closed as if the world is too loud to look at directly. The mouth is covered by a cloth that was once dark and is now mineral gray. It does not speak, but it makes a small sound when it works—a tuneless, low humming that matches the diesel's double-beat, as if it must add its own frequency to keep the place in balance.

Call it the Tender. It is not a name the place gave; it is a word for what the Tender does. It tends the Low Works. It clears the intake screens of matted algae. It scrapes the mineral crust from the valve wheels. It oils the flywheel bearings. It checks the sagging cables with a hand, feeling for heat where heat should not be. It does not fix the machines as a mechanic would; it keeps them from becoming ruins. It keeps the warm water flowing, the steam pipes hissing, the lamps burning. The machines do not need much. They need regularity. The Tender is regular.

It lives in the pump house, in a narrow loft above the flywheel, where the heat collects and the air is dry enough to sleep in. The bed is a rectangle of old conveyor belt laid over boards. There are no blankets, only a pile of cloth that has been repaired so many times it has become a single stiff object. On the wall are rows of tools, each one oiled and hung by its own wire. There is a shelf of jars: salt, mineral samples, dried fish, crab shells, small pale snail shells. There is no chair. When the Tender eats, it sits on an upturned bucket or on the metal steps with its feet in the mud.

It eats what the basin provides. In the warm outflow pools, it cups fish with its hands; it knows to wait until they gather thick enough that they do not startle. It eats them raw or half-cooked on a hot pipe. It eats the stringy algae when it is young, before it becomes too salty. It eats crabs, cracking them with a small hammer and leaving the shells for the gulls. It drinks from a seep, not the warm water; it has learned which seeps are fresh enough. It does not grow thin, but it is not fat. It is dense, like wet clay.

Its days are not days. The light does not change enough to tell. It wakes when the engine's beat changes, and it sleeps when the valves are quiet. It has no clock but the pumps. It knows the sound of every valve: which one is about to stick, which one is leaking more than it should, which one is ringing because a bird has built a nest against it. It checks them by touch and ear, not by looking at gauges. The gauges lie; the pipes do not.

Birds do not avoid it. They have learned that the Tender does not lash out. When it scrapes algae from a pipe, the small fish follow the scrapings downstream. Crabs scatter from its feet but close behind. The gulls watch it with one eye, not because it is dangerous, but because it is a source of disturbed insects and open mud. It is not the owner of this place; it is more like a stone that moves.

Sometimes, in the warm dark, it stands still for hours with one hand on the diesel engine's housing, not fixing anything, just feeling the vibration. It might be listening, or it might be thinking, or it might be doing something that is neither. The engine, for its part, continues. It does not care. That is the closest thing to companionship the Low Works offers: a machine that does not need silence.

There is a story that the Tender was once a worker here, when the Low Works was being built. It is not a true story, or it is only true in the way that all long residents are built by the place they remain in. It has forgotten its name. It does not need one. The pumps do not need it to be human; they need it to be regular. The crabs do not need it to remember; they need it to step around the burrows. The midges do not need it to speak; they need it to stand still long enough for them to gather on its shoulders and drink the moisture from its cloth.

It is not lonely. The place is too full for loneliness. The noise is not an absence; it is a crowd. The Tender moves through it the way a fish moves through water, not cutting across it but moving inside it, part of its pressure.

The Low Works is a place of accumulation. It accumulates water, salt, heat, noise, life. The Tender is another accumulation: of mud on its boots, crust on its skin, oil in its cloth, and years in its bones. It is not at peace. It is saturated, like everything else. It does not live in the Low Works so much as it is lived by it, carried along in the same slow press of water and mineral and engine-sound.

If you ask who lives here, the answer is not a name. It is a process. The Tender is not the only thing that lives in the Low Works—there are millions of others, in the water, in the mud, in the air. But it is the one that has learned to touch the valves, to sleep in the heat, to hum in the frequency of the diesel. It is the one that will still be there when the next salt crust forms, when the next tufa lip grows, when the next pump sinks a half meter into its own pool.

It is not waiting. It is tending. And the Low Works, in its own way, tends it back.
