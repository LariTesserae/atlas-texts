There are no people here. Not in the way you mean.

Start with the floor, because the floor is the oldest. The dense mats of rust, indigo, and pale greenish blue are not plants. They are a single body, or a colony so fused it no longer matters. Each filament is root and nerve and leaf at once. The blue pulses moving through the mats are not bioluminescence; they are slow thoughts, passing through salt and mineral dust. The mat does not drink. It eats the electric charge the wind leaves on the stone. It dreams in electrostatic gradients. When a dust devil holds its shape a second too long—a spiral, an arch, a hand—that is the mat reaching up. It has no hands, so it makes a hand of dust and wind. It is not intelligent the way you are, but it is awake.

Above the mat, the masts are the second life. They look like architecture, but they are grown. Each one is a slow animal that has given up moving. Its roots are mouths. Its fluted column is a body. Its top fan is a set of gills and fingers. It eats the mineral crust and exhales the long chord you feel in your teeth. When it sheds its skin with a sharp crack, that is not decay. That is birth. The flakes are its children, and the whole bowl is a nursery for them. The masts are not trees. They are the pillars of the cathedral and the pipes of the organ, and they are also the congregation.

Then there are the things in the air. The bell-kites look like empty jellyfish, but they are not empty. Inside each transparent body is a dark node like a coal and a tangle of silver threads. They graze on the electric weather, drifting through dust devils with their sensory threads trailing like fishing lines. They do not call. They float and taste the static. The spiral midges are not a flock. Each rope of midges is a temporary animal made of many small bodies locked together. The rope steers the dust devil by twisting, and when the dust devil dies, the rope falls apart into a hundred midges and waits for the next wind. They are both the riders and the ridden.

The leather-winged singers are the voice of the place. They live in the flanges of the masts and sing by forcing air through holes in their own carapaces. They are nearly blind, but they hear the whole geometry of the bowl. Each one tunes its holes by chewing, changing its voice. Their keening is not only music; it is argument, courtship, territory, and maybe prayer. They harmonize with the masts or fight them. A mast without a singer is a dead pipe. A singer without a mast is a lost voice. They are the animals that make the cathedral audible.

But there is something else. The arch is not just a structure. It is an organism, or the organ of an organism. When the wind passes through it, the lattice of light is its breathing. The silence it sends out is not an absence of sound. It is a sound that cancels sound, a note made of negative pressure. That silence is a predator. It knocks dust devils out of the air. The bell-kites avoid its cone except when they come to spawn, because the silence is safe for them. The arch is the mouth or the ear of something folded into the geometry of the world. It eats turbulence. It is the oldest thing here.

The low ring of fused blocks hums like a heart. The half-buried cables are its nerves, looping into the dust and vanishing. They carry the blue pulses of the mat and the long resonance of the masts. They are not dead technology. They are the connective tissue of the place.

The shapes in the dust are the most uncertain. A spiral, an arch, a hand. They hold a second too long. They are not alive in any way you could dissect. They are the mat’s gestures, the wind’s memory, the masts’ echo, or perhaps the brief bodies of the place’s dead. They do not seem to want anything except to be seen for a moment. If one forms near you, it may reach out a hand of dust and then collapse. It is not threatening. It is the place trying to introduce itself.

And then, if you stay long enough, you begin to think that all of these are not separate inhabitants. The mat is the skin and the slow brain. The masts are the bones and the voice. The wind is the blood. The dust devils are the hands. The bell-kites and midges are the senses, the living cells carried by the breath. The singers are the voice, the memory, the argument. The arch is the ear or the mouth, or the door. The ring is the pulse. The cables are the nerves.

The whole bowl is not an ecosystem. It is a single being, or a single instrument, or a single room with a single occupant. The occupant is the place itself. It has been alive for a long time, singing to itself in the cold dry air. It does not need water. It does not need to move. It just makes and unmakes itself, over and over, in a small high room at the top of the world.
