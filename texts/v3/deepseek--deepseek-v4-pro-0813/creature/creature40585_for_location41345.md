The inhabitants are not animals. They are made of the city itself—ceramic, glass, gel, woven carbon, and the slow memory of the water.

The first things you would notice are the **maintainers**. They look like brittle stars, though no brittle star ever moved with such abrupt, mechanical purpose. A typical maintainer is the size of a dinner plate, with five or seven arms radiating from a central boss of pale ceramic, glazed in places to the color of old ivory. The arms are not jointed like a crab’s; they are hollow tubes of woven carbon filled with lithium-rich water, and along each arm runs a series of tiny bladders that swell and clamp in sequence, producing the quick jerking motion. When they are still, they flatten against the stone and vanish into the architecture, their pale surfaces taking on the blue-gray of the city walls.

They have no eyes. Around the rim of the central disc is a ring of small crystal lenses, but these do not focus images. They are the polished ends of thin glass fibers that run through the whole body, gathering light, telling the maintainer where the bright siphons are, where the shadowed alleys turn, where the upper mirror-slit widens at noon. They feel the water with every part of themselves. A fringe of gel threads hangs from the underside of the disc, tasting lithium, silica, oxygen, and the faint pressure-signatures of distant valves. They hear through the stone. Every tap of their own ceramic limbs, every tick of cooling basalt, every change in the tide arrives as a vibration in their shells.

They rarely swim. They grip and release. A maintainer will press several arms into a crevice, build pressure in its bladders, and then let go all at once, flicking itself across a street gap. They aim not by sight but by the lingering structure of the water. Because the colloid holds motion, they can sense the ghost of a current another maintainer left an hour before. They use these memory-threads as paths. In the widest part of the central shaft, you can sometimes see them crossing the bright open water one after another along an invisible spiral, each one riding the remembered wake of the one before, the line gradually decaying until a new maintainer re-cuts it.

Their language is percussion and exhalation. They tap sequences against ceramic pipes, against doorframes, against the ribs of the buildings. A valve-keeper will tap two quick beats, pause, tap a third, and the vibration runs through the mineral walls to a lamplighter three levels up. They also leave messages in the colloid itself—squeezing out small puffs of silica-rich water that unfold into faintly glowing ribbons. These traces persist for minutes, sometimes hours. A busy intersection can become a tangle of luminous sentences, each one a note about pressure differentials, a worn cable-basket, a request for help at the south ebb-jet. The old fired ceramic signs on the walls are no longer part of this conversation. No one now living uses that script. The maintainers polish the signs and fill their cracks, but they do not read them. They are older than the oldest maintainer’s pattern-memory, remnants of some earlier intelligence that either left or never became animal.

There are many castes, each fitted to a task.

The **valve-keepers** are the smallest, no wider than a hand. Their arms are so thin they can enter the narrowest slot in the flush-gates. They cluster near the four great apertures, waiting for the tide to shift. You can read the state of the tide by how many of them are moving. At slack water they stream out across the town, darting jauntily between levels. At full flood they are wedged into crevices, arms braced, feeling the water roar past. They adjust the gates’ ceramic seals, tap their percussive notes, and retreat before the pressure becomes too great.

The **masons** are older, blinder, more patient. They are the lobster-sized machines that crawl over the walls and never stop. Their bodies are pale ceramic plates, and from the underside extends a soft, many-lipped mouth. It licks the stone, tasting the mineral balance, and then extrudes a fine bead of calcium-silica mortar. Over days the bead becomes a rib. Over weeks a floor. Where a wall has grown too thick, a mason will pause and secrete a different acid, dissolving the old material back into a slurry that runs down to the mineral mill. They are the city’s builders and demolishers, and they appear to have no other desire. The buildings grow, thicken, crack, dissolve, and re-grow beneath their slow, constant attention.

The **gardeners** move among the vegetation. They have small curved blades at the ends of two specialized arms. They scrape the dark red and black photosynthetic film from the upper east wall, leaving neat grid-patterns behind, then carry the sticky mass to the mineral mill. They prune the pale gutweed near the round windows, not to eat but to keep it from blocking the light. They move with a slower rhythm than the valve-keepers. Sometimes they remain motionless for hours with their arms spread over a mat of film, as if listening to it grow.

The **breath-tenders** are the largest of the maintainers, nearly two metres across. They live at the bottom of the central shaft, around the large flexible membrane. They do not climb. They press themselves against the membrane and feel its tension with hundreds of tiny sensors. When the warm water begins to gather beneath it, they scatter, and a few seconds later the membrane exhales, sending a house-sized plume upward with a low organ-note. Then the breath-tenders return, checking the edges for tears, patting the membrane with their ceramic fingertips, listening for any irregularity in the next slow inhalation.

There are also **lamplighters** that climb to the upper mirror-slit to polish the light siphons. They are flattened and dark, so they do not bleach in the direct sun. At noon they can be seen around the lip of stone, moving in quick circles, clearing the glass fiber bundles of the scant mineral dust that settles there. Below them, the pool of brightness moves across the walls as they work.

No single maintainer is intelligent in the way a person might be. But together, and with the water, the city is awake. The central membrane, the flush-gates, the light siphons, the fusion tapes, and the maintainers all pass signals back and forth in a slow loop. The water’s memory is the medium. A pulse of warm water rises, a valve opens, a current reverses, a door swings, a maintainer waits on the other side. The city remembers its own turbulence and uses it as a kind of short-term mind. That is why the streets are arranged as they are, why the doors open after the current has reversed, why the whole town is a clock powered by its own turbulence. The maintainers are like moving thoughts, but the mind is the entire shaft.

New maintainers are not born. They grow from seed-tiles in the mineral mill at the bottom. When an old maintainer slows, or its ceramic shell cracks beyond repair, it descends to the mill and lies down in a shallow pool of warm slurry. Other maintainers remove its limbs and place them on the seed-bed, where they dissolve into a milky solution. Within weeks, small star-shaped nodes appear on the walls of the pool. They are covered with a soft organic gel that hardens slowly, and one day they flex, then crawl out to begin their work. No animal parent, no animal child. The city replaces its own cells.

The plants are the only carbon-based life that can tolerate this water. They do not breathe oxygen as surface plants do; they use manganese catalysts, and their colors are off—purple, rust-black, very pale green. The mats of red and black film on the upper east wall are not passive stains. They spread by a slow, almost amoeboid motion, thickest where the daylight pools at noon. When the light siphons tilt to follow the sun, the mats respond, shifting their growth toward the new bright spot. At night they contract into darker, denser patches. The translucent streamers near the mineral mill hang in vertical lines, each strung with tiny gas bladders that make it rise and fall. When the warm plume passes, they unfurl like flags. The pale gutweed in the windows is ghostly, nearly transparent, not the green of surface seaweed. The maintainers tend all of it. It may be that the plants are not food, not shelter, but a kind of chemical memory—taking the lithium, the silica, and the faint warmth and binding them into tissue, storing the city’s excess energy in a form that can be dissolved and re-used when the mill needs raw material.

And all around this activity, the water remains empty of animals. No fish will come to investigate your hand. No crab will skitter from the white crust. No worm will burrow in the sediment, because there is almost no sediment. The lithium-rich water is a barrier to nervous systems. A human could swim through the bright shaft and see every wall alive with ceramic motion, and yet not a single animal shadow. The only respiration is the membrane’s. The only heartbeat is the tide.

So the place is not empty. It is full of a different kind of life—one made of mineral, glass, and water-memory, a city that grows and repairs and knows itself only as pressure and light. The inhabitants are not individuals but a community of ceramic cells in a vast mineral body, hidden beneath the sea’s gray-green skin, still sharp-edged, still loud, still making itself.
