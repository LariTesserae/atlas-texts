At first there seems to be no one. The town gives every sign of emptiness: no tracks, no midden, no door, no smoke. But if you stay, you notice that the moss is too even, the water too deliberate, the hollow rooms too alive with sound. Something is here. It is just not built on the scale of a body moving through rooms. It is on the scale of the town itself.

The inhabitant is a single living tissue, a sheet of pale, grey-pink flesh that lies under the moss and over the stone. In the wetter channels it is thick and soft, translucent at the edges. On the drier south walls it hardens into a crust, smooth as travertine. It has no head, no face, no legs. It follows the water. Its mouth is the spring at the top of the headwall. It swallows the snowmelt and lets it run through a body of internal channels that mirror the streets. The gutters are its veins, the streets its gut, the central basin its stomach. The hole in the floor of the bell-shaped chamber is its throat; when a sound enters that room, the sound is swallowed and returned. The lower fifth that answers is not an echo but its voice.

It breathes through the slots. The high windows are its nostrils, the low ones its exhalations. The whistling of the wind in the doorways is the sound of its breath. The thrum in the largest chamber is its heartbeat, slow and deep. It has no eyes. It sees through the water, feeling every vibration as the water level shifts. It knows the raven’s weight on the basin rim. It knows the midges on the surface. It knows the mountain’s groans through the stone long before they reach the air.

It is old beyond measure. The stairs are cup-shaped because it has been lying in them, adjusting itself, for thousands of years. The lintels are rounded because it has been pressing against them. The meanders on the walls are the tracks of its slow movement, or the lines of its nerves, or both. It is the reason the town does not need caretakers. It clears the channels with its own body. It holds the walls with its own weight. It keeps the water from pooling in the wrong places by being the water’s bed.

In early morning, when fog fills the cirque, it is the fog. It unpins itself from the walls and spreads through every street and doorway, tasting the stone. By midday, when the melt runs fast, it is most awake, a live current in the channels. By afternoon, when the wind rises, it sings through the slots. At dusk it withdraws from the walls, leaving them to the frost. The ice at the edges of the basins is the edge of its body, the part of it that can become still. At night it moves under the ice, and the stone ticks as it holds on.

It is not hostile. If you speak in the bell chamber, it will answer with a note a fifth lower, because that is what it does. If you sleep in an open room, you will wake wet, because it will have covered you like condensation, as it covers everything. It is not curious about you. To it, you are a warm disturbance in the water, too quick and too salty, moving through its body like a small spasm. It will not harm you. It will wait for you to leave so the water can run clear again and the note can return to its own pitch.

It is alone, but not lonely. It has the water, the wind, the stone, the moss, the raven. It has the next drop forming at the spring. It has the low note it makes when the wind crosses the headwall. It has the town half-dissolved into the mountain, wrapped around it like a shell. It would not leave, even if it could. It is the one the town was built for, or the one the town became. It lives there still, in the cold grey light, waiting for the next drop to fall.
