The first inhabitants are not animals. They are films. They live on the wet rock in sheets of sulfur-yellow and iron-rust and pale blue, and they eat the water. They do not have mouths. They take the mineral heat and turn it into more of themselves, and as a by-product they make the terraces. The Kettle is their body, still growing. You can see them bloom after a rain: the mats turn deeper, wet, glossy, as if the stone has opened its eyes.

On these mats feed the grazers. They are small and shelled, shaped like buttons, with feathery legs that sweep the film into their mouths. They move in bands so thick the ground seems to ripple. When a band crosses a fresh terrace, it leaves a faint glowing trail in the microbial mat, a blue-green signature that lasts for hours before fading back into stone. The pools are full of brine shrimp and wriggling larvae, the water grainy if you put in your hand. Spiral snails stack themselves on the warm stone in layers, their shells clicking as they shift. There is no empty place.

The birds came for the grazers and stayed for the heat. They are long-legged, with beaks like thin picks, and they walk the maze of pools jabbing through the mineral crust. On the far cliffs, their rookeries are packed ledge to ledge, white with guano and loud with squabbling young. The guano washes down into the basin and feeds the mats, so the birds are also farmers of a kind, though they do not know it. The hairless fox with the scaled tail hunts between the sinter cones. It moves low and fast, and its tail leaves a mark in the dust like a branch of salt. It catches crustaceans and sometimes eggs, and it has learned to wait near the mud pots for the grazers to flee an eruption.

Then there are the people.

At first they are easy to miss. Their camps are low and temporary: a wall of sinter slabs fitted without mortar, a ring of stones around a fire-blackened pit, a collapse of ribs and hide drying beside a steam vent. Their tools are half-sunk in fresh carbonate, as if the ground is already claiming them. They are small, compact, with broad hands and wide flat feet. Their toes are spaced, their soles calloused into a kind of living sandal that lets them walk on stone too hot for an outsider. They have little body hair; their skin is the color of wet travertine, or darker where mineral dust has settled into creases. They paint themselves with mud from different pots—sulfur-yellow, iron-red, salt-white—not only for protection from steam and insects but to mark where they have been and whom they belong to. They squint against the haze. They move slightly sideways to the steam, the way the birds do.

They are not farmers. They tend no fields and herd nothing. They live by reading the stone. A child is taught to read the terraces the way other people might read clouds or seasons. A faint crack means a new vent will open. A change in the color of the mats from rust to pale blue means the water has shifted and the path is no longer safe. A pool that holds light rather than images can be trusted for salt, but not for drinking. They map the Kettle not by drawing but by tying knots in cord, each knot a turn, a warning, a source of food. Their songs sound like the mud pots: long silences, then sudden phrases that pop and sink.

They move often. They live in small groups of fifteen or twenty, never larger because the ground cannot support a permanent camp. Leadership is temporary: the person who reads a new terrace correctly leads until the next shift. No one owns anything that cannot be carried in one hand or tied to a belt. When a terrace becomes too soft, or a vent opens under the sleeping place, they pick up their tools and go. They carry obsidian flakes wrapped in sinew, bone needles, cord, small baskets woven from wet sinew and crustacean shell. They eat the grazers and shrimp and larvae, scooping them with woven sieves. They collect eggs from the cliff rookeries, using cord ladders and long bone hooks. They trap the scaled fox by baiting a mud pot with shrimp and setting a sinew noose. They cook on hot stones, not always with fire. They make a paste of dried shrimp and mineral salt, carried in bladder sacks.

Their dead they give back to the Kettle. When someone stops moving, the people do not bury them in earth. They lay the body on a warm terrace or beside a steam vent, arrange their tools around them, and let the mineral water climb. In a season the bones are armored; in a year they are a low mound; in a lifetime they are part of a terrace. The people have a phrase for it, close to "settled." They say the person has become stone, and the stone will remember the shape of their hands. This is not an ending to them. It is the one way to stay.

They do not build monuments. The ground will not keep their work, and they do not ask it to. Instead they make their marks in knots and songs, in the placement of bones, in the faint glowing footprints left in the mats. They know the Kettle is still being born, and they are not its masters. They are another kind of mineral, slow to settle, quick to read. What lives in the Kettle is not a list of creatures but a braid of them: the mats building the stone, the grazers building the mats, the birds feeding the mats with guano, the fox hunting the grazers, and the small people moving through all of it, naming it, listening to the mud pots as if they might answer.

One of them, a woman with white salt painted around her eyes, sits by a mud pot and taps the ground in its rhythm. The mud pot pops back. It might be language or coincidence. She laughs, or maybe she only exhales through the steam. The sound joins the hiss and the clatter. The Kettle goes on making itself, and its inhabitants go on being made.
