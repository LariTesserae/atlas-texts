No one lives here, if by “someone” you mean a body with a face and a voice and a way of arriving or leaving. But something lives here, and it has never left.

It is first visible as a glisten. Along the rims of the largest pool, where the warm water meets the pale rock, there is a clear film the color of old glass. You might take it for wetness or for mineral precipitate. It is not. It is the inhabitant—not a single creature but a colony, a mat, one diffuse body spread across the hollow’s wet places. It lies over the stone in a layer no thicker than a leaf, sometimes no thicker than breath. It is colorless, or faintly gray-green, or the color of a snail’s trail drying in the dimness. It clings.

Kneel and look. The film is a weave of uncountable tiny lives: ancient bacteria, archaea, unnamed cells that have been here since the water first found the hollow. They do not move; they grow. They do not speak; they exchange chemical whispers. They drink the mineral-rich seep—the carbonates, the sulfates, the salt—and leave behind the white crust that rims the pools. The terraces, the scalloped shelves, the little mountain ranges of lime are their architecture, laid down layer by layer over millennia. The newest part is still soft and slippery under a fingertip; the oldest part is stone. A century adds a few millimeters. The hollow is not only a place; it is the shell of the thing that lives here.

It has no center. It spreads from pool to pool along the silent runnels, up the damp stone where the seep sweats from the wall, over the mineral lips and under the shallow overhangs. It covers the submerged edges of the pools in a clear, trembling skin. It is blind, deaf, without brain or heart, but it knows the world in the only way that matters here: as warmth, as salinity, as the slow drip of water that has been coming for longer than the rock has been smooth. The drip is its pulse. The steam is its breath. The thick silence is the sound of its thinking, if it thinks at all.

It is not conscious, not in any way that would see you as a visitor or itself as a self. It does not know the ferns, though the ferns grow from its crust as if from soil. It does not know the juniper, though the juniper’s roots pass through its wetter territories and share the same seep. It does not know the three flat stones or the broken standing stone with its spiral almost erased. But it has accepted them. It has covered their bases with the same white mineral, drawn its slow film over their edges, filled the carved lines with pale sediment until the mark looks less like writing and more like the memory of water. Whatever people came here, they left no ghosts; the film has grown over the place where they knelt.

And yet it is not nothing. If you sit very still, you can feel it as a presence. The air is warmer near its patches. The stone is slippery. The faint sulfur smell is the smell of its work. It does not want you to leave. It does not want you to stay. It has let go of wanting. That is why it can live here, in a closed loop where the water level never changes and the light never sharpens and the seasons do not come.

It persists. It is the first inhabitant and the last. It is not an animal, not yet. It is the beginning of a world that has not finished being born, and it will still be here, thin and colorless, building its tiny mountains, when the tree has gone gray and the carved stone has worn to sand and the silence has forgotten your breathing.
