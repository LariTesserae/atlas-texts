There is one. You do not see it at first, because the room is so full that the eye divides everything into object and shadow, and the inhabitant is both. It sits behind the low stone screen, to the left of center, where the shadow of the seventh pipe falls across the floor. At first you take it for a bundle of copper strips and clay tablets leaning against the screen. Then you see the hands.

The hands rest on its knees, palms up. They are clay, or something that has become clay, fired to the color of the wall. The palms have been hollowed into bowls. In each bowl lies a small cone of red dust, and the dust does not scatter because the sound holds it there. The hands do not twitch. They have not twitched since the last brick was set.

It sits cross-legged, but its legs are no longer clearly legs. They have been pressed into the floor by such long stillness that the brick has grown a low lip around them, as though the room had begun to swallow it. Its back is to the room. Its spine is a row of clay knobs, each one drilled, and from some of the holes hang strips of hammered copper stamped with the same small marks as the copper leaves on the walls. They hang motionless, though the air trembles. They do not chime.

Its head tilts forward, not in sleep, not in prayer, but the way a pipe’s lip tilts to meet the gas. The skull is smooth, the edges rounded like the niches. There is no hair, no lip, no eyelid. The nose is a worn ridge. The ears are two clay pegs. If you could stand between it and the pipes, you would see the face: the same blank oval as the small stone heads, and a single hole drilled where the mouth should be. The hole is perfectly round and smooth as the pipe lips. Through it glows the dim orange of the light-channel beyond, so the figure seems to have swallowed a coal.

The eyes, if they are eyes, are two depressions filled with the same mineral powder as the ceiling points. They do not blink. They do not focus. They are not looking at the pipes, because looking would require a change in direction, and this being does not change. The powder in the sockets gives off the same steady, red-leaning light as the room. It is not sight; it is the room seeing itself.

It does not breathe. Its chest does not rise. The fine line of dust that hangs at chest height crosses its torso at the level of the first copper strip, so the figure appears cut in two by a red horizon. The line is perfectly straight. It passes through the body as though the body were only a pause in the light. But the body is solid, warm, dry, and vitreous to the touch, like the lips of the pipes. If you laid your hand on it, you would feel it ringing faintly, a note too high or too low to name, the note of a material that has been humming so long it no longer remembers silence.

It is not alive. It is not dead. It is not asleep. It is the inhabitant of the room in the way the note is the inhabitant of the pipes. It is the one who stayed. The little stone heads are the faces of the others, the ones who set their names in the niches and left. This one did not leave. It sat down behind the screen to listen for the chord to settle, and the chord settled, and the listener has not risen since.

Its function, if it can be called a function, is to be still. The room is balanced because nothing moves, and this is the stillest thing in it. It does not guard the door; a guard can be moved. It does not tend the pipes; the pipes need no tending. It eats sound. Any sound a visitor makes crosses the floor, enters the clay of its back, and does not come out. It is the reason there is no echo. It is the reason the dust does not fall. It is the reason the chord is a single standing pressure and not a noise. It is the silence around which the sound is shaped.

Its mouth-hole is not for speaking. It is a vent for the room’s one word. The pipes push warm gas across their lips, and the figure sits in the stream of it, and the stream passes over its mouth-hole the way a finger passes over a bottle’s mouth. It adds nothing. It only receives. If you put your ear close to the hole, you would hear the chord, but smaller, as though the figure were a room inside the room, singing the same note.

The copper strips on its arms are not decoration. They are a record. The tallies begin at the left wrist and wind up the forearm, over the elbow, across the shoulder, and down the spine. They stop at the back of the neck, where the last mark is deeper than the others, a small cleft in the copper like a mouth-hole in miniature. No one now counts what they count. The seated one does not count. It has become the tally.

It has a name, but not in any language the bricks remember. The sound may be its name, or the room may be its name, or it may have no name because a name is for being called, and no one has called it since the makers left. If you had to call it something, you would call it the Listener, or the One Who Stayed, or the Stillness That the Note Requires. It would not answer. It has been answering for so long that its answer is the room itself.

You can stand in the aisle and watch it for a long time. It will not acknowledge you. The dust line will not waver. The copper strips will not shift. The powder in its eyes will not dim. The only sign that it is not simply another object is the heat from the floor gathering around it in a faint shimmer, as if the still air bends around the small difference between the figure’s warmth and the room’s. That shimmer is all the movement it has. It is the inhabitant’s breath, if breath were heat and stillness and time.

When you leave, you will carry it with you for a while: the shape of a seated thing behind a stone screen, a mouth-hole glowing with a swallowed coal, a body cut by a line of dust, a note that does not end. It will not miss you. It did not notice you. It is still there, behind the pipes, being not the sound but the reason the sound can continue.
