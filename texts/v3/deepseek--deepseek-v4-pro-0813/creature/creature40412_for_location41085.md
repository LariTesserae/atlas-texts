The room has a tenant, though you won’t see it until you’ve stopped looking for a face.

First you notice the small lives. The glass midges rise in bright columns, their wings catching light like sparks off wet stone. The skaters write glowing loops across the basins. The translucent frogs line every pool’s edge and sing in bell-tones until the whole high chamber seems to be one clear, ringing instrument. The stone-colored lizards are almost invisible until they breathe. And the ribbon-predator drops from the dark, takes a frog, and pours itself back into the crevice like a muscle of the mountain. These are the room’s skin and breath, but they are not the resident.

The resident is harder to separate from the stone. Its signs are all around you before it appears. The drag-marks healing over in the floor are not accidental; they are the trail of its tail. The polished groove at the top of the half-staircase is where its head rests. The empty circular sockets along the low wall are empty only because its hands are elsewhere. Some part of the humming you assumed was the monoliths is actually its breathing.

At rest, it looks like a heap of wet black glass and tarnished silver moss beside the largest pool. Then it unfolds.

It is long, low, and made of the same raw stone as the room, but slightly warmer, slightly more alive. Its back is ridged like a row of unfinished spires. Its underside is paler and softer, leaving a faint heat-print in the new stone where it lies. It has no separate head, not really. Its front end is a rounded prow of dark mineral, pitted with breathing pores that emit the finest steam. Its limbs are temporary arrangements: six short walking legs when it crosses the floor, two long fine arms when it works at the sockets, none at all when it pours itself into a crack and disappears. Beneath its skin, lights move along seams—amber, blue-white, a slow deep red—exactly matching the mineral veins in the walls. Its voice comes from a slit in its throat: a note lower than the monoliths, a sound you would feel in your sternum before you heard it.

It is not the builder of the mountain. The mountain is building itself. But the creature is a tender, a corrector, a kind of walking regulator for a landscape not yet sure of its own shape. It crosses the soft stone with exaggerated care, lifting each foot high, because a careless print becomes a fossil here. It presses down places where the stone rises too fast. It lies across the black box until the amber pulse slows into rhythm with its own. It inserts its long fingers into the circular sockets and turns them, not like keys but like a bow drawn over a string; the monoliths brighten or dim, the fog thickens or pulls back, and the seepage responds. It kneels at the basins and works the copper and salt rings into the low wall, thickening it as if adding mortar to a wound. It visits the new spire near the back wall and touches its glossy tip with a pale, root-like organ, tasting the speed of the growth.

The animals treat it as weather. The glass midges roost on its back until it seems to be furred in living silver. The frogs climb over its shoulders and sing; their bell-tones pass through its body and come out changed, lower and farther-traveling. The skaters circle the places where its limbs enter the water, writing loops around it like punctuation. The lizards ignore it until it moves, then scatter. The ribbon-predator pauses in its drop when the creature is near. For a moment they exchange no sound, only a shared temperature, and then the predator takes its frog and is gone.

It feeds by lying in the warm mineral water and letting the dissolved stone enter through its pores. It drinks the water that runs uphill. It sheds a thin skin of stone each cycle, and that shed skin becomes part of the floor, another layer in the mountain’s slow, deliberate body. It has no eyes, but it sees by heat and pressure and the echo of the frog-song. It has no name that a mouth could say. The monoliths hold its name as a frequency at the edge of hearing.

When the fog rolls over the lip, it stands at the threshold and lets the mineral mist condense on its back. When the monoliths hum, it answers one note lower. When the blue-white seams dim, it folds itself into the half-staircase, fits its head into the polished groove, and sleeps. While it sleeps, the stone grows over its legs. When it wakes, it flexes, cracks the new rock, and rises out of the floor with a sound like a joint of the mountain grinding.

The pale shapes in the cloud sea below are its kin—too large now for this high room, or not yet ready to enter it. They call up in slow pressures that make the stone ring, and it answers from the lip. Its answer is the low grinding sound the rock makes when it shifts, as if the whole chamber is speaking.

If you stayed still long enough, it would notice you. Not as a shape, but as a new heat and a new wet rhythm in the room. It might come close and hold one warm, slightly porous hand against your sternum, reading your pulse the way it reads the black box. Then it would withdraw and go back to its work, leaving your heartbeat as a faint ridge in the soft floor. By the next cycle, the stone would have healed over it.

It is not the mountain’s master. It is the mountain’s housekeeper, its midwife, its walking immune system. It has been here since before the room was a room. What lives there is not a person, not an animal, but an unfinished part of the mountain that learned to move—and stayed to keep the growth from going wrong.
