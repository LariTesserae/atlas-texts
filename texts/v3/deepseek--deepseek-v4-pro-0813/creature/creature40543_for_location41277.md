The one who lives there is not an animal. It has no blood, no skin, no bones in the ordinary sense. It is made mostly of water and the memory of water, and the city is less its home than its body.

If you saw it, you would first mistake it for steam. In the central plaza, where the fountain rises and never comes down, the water sometimes thickens for a moment into a vertical figure—taller than a person, but person-shaped only in the way a hanging root is person-shaped if you stare too long. Its arms are columns of mist and rising rain. Its fingers are the thin silver threads of water that climb the eastern walls. Its chest is root lattice and wet stone, and its breathing is the slow contraction of lianas, the hiss of steam from a hundred vents.

It does not walk so much as continue. When it moves, it does not cross space; it lets the city’s folded geography rearrange for it. A stairway that descends and arrives above is simply a step to it. A street that opens far across the caldera is a few strides. It can pass through a window and emerge in a tower miles away, because it is not a thing inside the city. It is the city’s way of being aware of itself.

Its face is not fixed. Sometimes it is a smooth bowl of water in a stone cistern that reflects no one. Sometimes it is a patch of moss on a wall, wet and dark, arranged like a sleeping face. Sometimes it is the hollow in a tree fern where steam curls in a slow, deliberate shape that looks at you. When it regards you, you would not know you were being regarded. The roar of the waterfalls would press a little deeper into your chest. The air would taste more strongly of minerals. The water in the pools would become still without becoming silent.

It is the keeper of the closed loop. The western falls pour down; the eastern rivers rise up; the same water returns forever. The inhabitant does not cause this. It is this. It is the point where the world’s plumbing knots, the hand that catches the falling water and gives it back to the spring. It is why the stone does not erode, why the pools neither fill nor empty, why the leaves stay green without growing. It does not repair the city because the city is always already whole in its attention.

It has no voice in the way a throat has a voice. When it speaks, the constant roar of the place modulates. The falls hold a lower note for a long moment. The steam vents answer in a rising hiss. The wind through the stone flutes forms something almost like a syllable—too long, too slow, too immense to be heard as language. If you could bear it, the word would mean something like *return* or *remain* or *I am the water going up.*

It is not lonely. It does not miss the animals, the birds, the insects, the fish that are not there. Their absence is not a wound to it. The inhabitant is too complete, too evenly distributed through root and stone and rising water, to need company. The city is full, but not inhabited, because the inhabitant is not a separate presence. It is the city’s inwardness. It is what the place becomes when it has been alone and balanced for so long that it has stopped being a place and become a being.

If you stayed long enough—though “long enough” is not a thing there—you would stop seeing it as a figure in the steam. You would understand that the whole caldera is its lung and voice. The terraces are its ribs. The aqueducts are its veins. The upward rivers are its thoughts climbing into the cloud. The wet, hot, deafening equilibrium is not something it tends. It is something it is.

And its name, if it has one, is not a word. It is the sound of its own water: the fall and the rise, the hiss and the hum, held forever. You could not speak it. You could only stand under the western falls and let the water beat against you until the roar matches the inside of your skull, and for one moment you would not be hearing the inhabitant. You would be inside it, and it would be the only thing that lived there, and it would be enough.
