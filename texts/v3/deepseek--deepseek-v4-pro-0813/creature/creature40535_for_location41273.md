There is not a person, but there is a tenant.

It is not easy to see, though it is always somewhere in the pool. At first you mistake it for a trick of the light: a pale line just under the surface, a slow refraction where the bright upper water meets the deeper, saltier dark. Then the line turns, and the water does not.

It is long and low, shaped like a soft ribbon of limestone given a heartbeat. From above it could be a fallen root, a vein of travertine, a reflection of the high-water mark. Its back is the color of the wet cave wall—white-gray, faintly tan, with a bloom of rust-orange speckles along the ridge, as though the rock had transferred some of its stains onto living skin. The belly is nearly transparent, and in strong light you can see the shadow of its spine, the small dark comma of its heart, the slow shift of something like a gut filled with the same mineral water it swims in.

It has no eyes. Or not eyes you would recognize. Under the skin of its broad, flat head are two tiny dark spots, like seeds pressed into the flesh from inside. They have long since forgotten how to see. What remains is a face shaped by other senses: a wide, soft mouth that seems not to bite but to taste the water continuously, and small nostril pits that sample the thread of fresh seepage against the salt. Three pairs of gills, each one like a tiny red fern, grow from the sides of its neck. They flutter only enough to keep water moving; most of its breath goes in through the skin itself.

Its legs are small and set far apart, almost useless on land. It does not leave the water. The legs are for easing itself over the smooth stone at the pool’s edge, for wedging its body into a niche when the tide pulses and it wants to be still. The tail is a broad, flattened blade, translucent at the edges like thin window-glass, grayed with the same blue as the deeper water. It moves with a soft side-to-side wave, but so slowly that the water closes behind it without a sound.

For most of the day it stays below, down where the pool turns warm and salty and the light goes from blue to something older. It lies on the bottom or floats just above the silt, gills barely moving, body half-buried in the fine mineral sediment. It is the reason the central water sometimes seems to have a slow, dark pulse. When the sun is high and the chamber is at its most bright, you will not find it at the surface. It has gone down into the salt tongue, into the part of the pool that does not belong to rain. There it waits, not sleeping the way a mammal sleeps, but letting the water carry the day’s heat through it.

Sometimes, near dawn or late evening, it rises. It does not break the surface; it comes to rest just below it, where the light is thin and the water striders cast long dimples on the skin of the pool. There it opens its mouth and drifts. Tiny pale shrimp and copepods pass between its lips, and the soft ridges of its mouth close over them with a gentleness that cannot be called a bite. It eats very little. The place feeds it slowly, and it has learned to match that pacing. The fish do not scatter from it. They treat it as another part of the pool, a warm current or a shadow that moves only when the light changes.

It is not a large creature. In the water it looks longer than it is. Out of the water, if it were ever to be lifted from the pool, it would be no longer than a human forearm, no heavier than a bundle of wet rope. But in this small room of stone, it is the largest thing besides the water itself, and it carries that size the way the pool carries its tide—without hurry, almost without intention.

The other animals know it, if knowing is the right word. The swiftlets do not mind it. The bees pass over it. The skinks on the warm stone above have seen its pale shape all their brief lives and have never known it as a threat. The snake that moves only when the sun shifts shares the ledge-world with it, not the water. At night, when the pool is disturbed, the blue glow clings to its moving body and traces a pale nebula along its flanks, so that for a second the whole occupant appears as a constellation, a slow dark shape stitched in cold light. Then it sinks, and the light goes out.

It has no name. If the place has a tenant, this is the tenancy: not a spirit, not a monster, not a stranded god, but a long-bodied, sightless, water-breathing animal that has been shaped by exactly this pool. Its ancestors came when the water was colder or fresher, and it stayed. Generation after generation adjusted to the salt, to the slow underground tide, to the bright open shaft, to the limestone that dissolved and re-formed around them. The one that lives there now is both old and new—an old lineage in a new skin, a single body carrying the habit of the pool.

If you stay long enough, you may see it rise. More likely, you will not. You will see the brightness, the thin shadows, the bees, the small fish rising. But you may also feel, after a while, that the pool is not empty. That the central vacancy is not vacant at all, but occupied by something so at home that it no longer needs to be noticed.
