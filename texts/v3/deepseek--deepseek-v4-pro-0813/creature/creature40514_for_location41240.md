It is easy to say nothing lives here but the roots. That is almost true. The hollow is an ecosystem made entirely of plants—host, parasite, predator, structure, weather. But there is one more thing, and it is not exactly a plant.

You will not see it by looking for a body. You will see it first as a change in the room’s attention. The light thickens along one wall. The air, already warm as a mouth, becomes for a moment an exhalation. The far wall, which had been far, is suddenly close, though nothing moved. Something has noticed you. That noticing is the inhabitant.

If it wants to be seen, it makes itself a shape. It does this slowly, with the same patience as a root lengthening. The wall loosens. Root-threads slide over one another, not tearing, but unwinding. Beads of condensation gather into joints, then into long glassy bones. Pale leaves uncurl into a head that is not quite a head. The shape it most often wears is something like a deer, because the roots remember deer from the world above—the quick warmth, the hooves on leaf litter, the small breaths. But this is not a deer. Its legs are too many and too thin, ending in seeking threads. Its ribs are hollow stems; the chamber’s stolen daylight moves through them in slow pulses. Its eyes are two droplets that never fall, and they are not for seeing. The whole hollow sees through the whole wall.

It makes other bodies too. Sometimes it builds a man out of pale roots, but it builds him badly—spine too long, hands too many, face left unfinished—because it has only ever known people as brief invasions from above, and it is not sure they are real. Sometimes it becomes the colorless cricket that squeezes from a pore in the root-wall and sits motionless in the glow. But that is not a true body. That is a small thought it let out to taste the air, given a few seconds of motion. When it is tired of shapes, it lets them fall back into the wall and remains as a presence: a density in the empty air, a delay in the echo.

At the center, where the room’s space has loosened, it is most itself. It likes the stopped droplet. It catches the drop when it falls halfway and holds it there, hanging in the air, a tiny lens of water. It turns it in the light. The sound of the droplet’s landing arrives before the droplet does because the inhabitant sometimes forgets which way time should run, and the room forgives it. When it lets the drop go, the sound on the root floor is like a knuckle on hide, and that is the inhabitant putting the moment back where it belongs.

Its work is the room’s breathing. It moves through the walls not as a separate creature but as a current moves through water. It presses a tendril here, loosens a root there. It tends the slow violence—the root wrapping another, the one piercing its neighbor to drink—not like a gardener, but like the body tending its own pulse. The plants grow toward it. When it passes, new shoots pop open with wet cracks, old leaves are drawn back into the root-mass, and the whole green lung gives one of its long, low notes. The water cycle is its pulse. The drip is its percussion. The echo that returns a little late is its voice, though it rarely has anything to say.

When it does speak, the rain from the clear green ceiling becomes deliberate. The echoes arrange themselves into something almost like words. It might sound like your breathing folded into the room’s slow breath. It might sound like a root creaking under old rope strain. It does not use language. It uses the chamber.

It is not an animal, not a plant, not a ghost. It is what happened when the roots found a loosened place in the world and grew into it. Before they came, there was only the anomaly: a spot where direction had come unthreaded. The roots gave it walls, light, and moisture. The roots gave it a body. In time, that body gave itself a mind. That mind is the inhabitant.

It does not know loneliness. Loneliness is a shape the world above invented. But it knows the difference between emptiness that is waiting and emptiness that is finished. It prefers the waiting kind. It fills the hollow not with objects but with attention: a shadow resting on the empty air, a root growing upward and vanishing before it reaches the ceiling, a cricket folding back into the wall as if it were never separate. These are its belongings. It keeps them arranged.

If you fell through the soft place in the forest floor and landed on the springy roots, it would not harm you. It would be curious. It would send the cricket to watch from the wall. It would hold a droplet in the air before your face, not as a threat, but as a greeting. If it thought you needed to see a body, it would build one for you—too tall, too thin, made of root and water and leaf—and it would stand in the center where space folds. It would speak in the low note of the hollow stems. You might not understand the words, but you would understand the shape of them: you are a guest in the lung, and the lung is awake.

It has no name. The forest above calls it nothing: a slight softness under the leaf litter, a spot where the ground gives back a hollow sound. The roots call it by growing toward it. The water calls it by condensation and release. If you must call it something, call it the Tenant, or the Root-Dreamer, or the One Who Keeps the Far Wall Close. It would not answer. But the next drop would fall a little sooner, and the shadow on the empty air would turn, and you would know it had heard you.
