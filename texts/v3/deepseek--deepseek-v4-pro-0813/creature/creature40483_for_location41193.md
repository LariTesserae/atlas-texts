No person lives here. No hands built anything. But the place is not uninhabited. It is crowded, layered, overfull with lives packed into every fissure, drifting through every column of water, pressed against the black stone until the stone itself seems only a foundation.

At first the saddle looks bare. Then it does not.

The first lives are the smallest and the oldest here. They are the bacterial mats—orange, rust, white, violet-black—spread over the basalt in fine loops and blistered crusts. They follow the weak electrical potentials in the rock, reading the chemical seam where seep-water rises from below. They eat methane, hydrogen, sulfide—things the mountain gives out like breath. They do not need the sunlight that reaches down here, though the light comes anyway, blue-shifted and bright enough to cast shadows. The bacteria are the original weavers, and they are everywhere: on the stone, inside the mussels, inside the tubeworms, turning the water’s poison into food.

There are no plants. Nothing green. That absence is strange, but here the base of the world is not leaf or root. It is microbial and mineral.

Above the bacterial mats, the mussels are the foundation. Black shells layered over black shells, wedged into every crack, forming drifts that cover the rock. They are not planted; they are packed. Each mussel holds bacteria in its gills, feeding them with seep-water and siphoning the byproducts. They open and close with a hundred small clicks, a wet, continuous sound under everything. Their beds are dense beyond comfort, but comfort is not the point. The point is holding on.

From the mussel beds rise clumps of tubeworms. Their white tubes stand like organ pipes, and their red plumes are out in the water, feathery and bright. They retract when a shadow passes, snapping back into their tubes. They have no eyes, but they feel the change in light and pressure. They are animals that have given up mouths and guts, relying entirely on the bacteria in their bodies. They are rooted in place, but they are not plants. They are more like living chimneys, drawing the seep-water up through themselves.

The moving ones live among them. Crabs with pale, hairy arms move over the mussels, prying at shells with slow, patient strength. They are not gentle, but they are not cruel; they are simply hungry. Small armored snails graze the bacterial mats, their radulae rasping over the stone, leaving faint clean trails. Amphipods thicken the water like smoke; they drift in clouds and scatter when fish pass. There are so many that the water seems to have a grain, a living particulate haze.

Fish are everywhere. Silver schools move in sheets and braids, parting around the carbonate chimneys with a low whump. They are not permanent residents in the way the mussels are, but they are always here, flowing through the stone architecture. Skates lie half-buried at the saddle’s edges, their bodies flattened into the shape of the bare floor. They wait, almost invisible, their eyes looking up through the blue.

A wolf eel watches from a crevice. Its head is blunt and heavy, its mouth slightly open, its expression one of deep, unfathomable patience. It is not a wolf and not exactly an eel; it is a fish that has learned to live in a crack and wait. It does not chase. It takes what comes close.

A small octopus flows across a basalt slab, turning from black to white to black. It changes not only color but texture, becoming the rough stone, then the smooth water, then an animal again. It is the one who seems to look. It pauses at the edge of a brine pool, where the surface is a mirror and the black fangs reappear upside-down. It does not enter. It knows the water there is too heavy, too salty, too still. It turns away and continues across the rock, its arms feeling the way.

In the dimmer overhangs, glass sponges stand like living lattices. They are animals made of silica threads, filtering the slow cold water for whatever is small enough to nourish them. They are not plants either, though they look like translucent sculptures. They are quiet, slow, and ancient.

At the brine pools’ margins, where the water shimmers like oil and the salt begins to sharpen, a few specialized worms live. They are thin and red, segmented, and they live exactly where the salt begins. They are the only visible life at the edge of the mirror. They do not stray into the brine, and they do not leave its margin. They are creatures of the boundary.

The sound is loud and continuous. Snapping shrimp crackle like a field of static, so many that the noise becomes a bed under everything. The carbonate chimneys thrum as gas moves through their flutings. Some huff with a regular, piston-like beat. The mussel beds click as crabs work them. Fish schools turn with a low whump. Far away, whale song passes through the water and the rock, too loud to ignore, as though the mountain itself were singing. The whole saddle roars.

But for all the noise, the place changes slowly. The chimneys grow a few centimeters in a decade. The mussel beds thicken over generations. The gas paths shift only across decades and centuries. The rock is young, but on a geologic clock; the sea has not had time to round its edges. The whole place feels like a small, loud, crowded, mineral engine running on cold chemistry, under normal light, with no built thing in it—only stone, water, bacteria, and too many animals.

If you ask who lives there, the answer is not one creature. It is all of them at once: the bacteria that eat the mountain’s breath, the mussels that farm them, the tubeworms that feed the bacteria, the crabs that pry, the snails that graze, the amphipods that thicken the water, the fish that part around the chimneys, the skates that lie half-buried, the wolf eel that watches, the octopus that pauses and looks, the glass sponges that filter, and the red worms at the salt edge. They live there together, pressed into every crack and over every surface, in a place that looks like a factory but is not built, only grown.
