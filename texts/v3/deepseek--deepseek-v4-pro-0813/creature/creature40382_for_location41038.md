No one lives here as a single creature. The Cold Anvil is a place where the question “who lives here?” already breaks down, because the ground itself is partly alive and the animals are partly stone. If you walk into it, the first thing you understand is that you are not looking at a landscape that contains life. You are looking at life that has become landscape.

The oldest residents are not animals. They are films and mats and stains that feed on raw earth. They color the wet stone with rust-red, sulfur-yellow, black, and a blue-green that is not the green of leaves but the green of copper left too long in brine. These iron-eating, sulfur-breathing microbes are the base of everything. They grow thickest where the stone is richest in metal and where the weak electric currents of the ground move through the wet film like slow nerves. They are not plants, though they sometimes spread like moss. They are more like a mineral appetite, a hunger for the edges between rock and water, and they are the floor upon which all the rest of the basin is built.

Out of that floor rise the salt fleas. They are so small that individually they are almost nothing, but together they are a weather system. They jump in continuous waves, and their landing sounds like rain on tin, so constant that after a while you stop hearing it as sound and start hearing it as the texture of the place. They scrape the microbial mats with mouthparts too fine to see, leaving polished trails behind them. When a methane pocket ignites with a low whump, the shock throws them into the air in pale clouds, and for a moment the sky is full of fleas, and the newts rise to meet them.

The beetle pavement is the next thing you notice, though you may mistake it for ground. The beetles are the size of fists, each armored in black magnetite, packed so tightly that they overlap like the scales of some enormous, half-buried reptile. They do not scatter when you step among them. They shift only enough to admit your foot, then close again behind it, clacking faintly. They follow the magnetic lines in the stone, grazing the microbial film that grows thickest there, their shells aligning like compass needles. When one moves, a ripple passes through a thousand others, and the ground changes shape. It looks mechanical, but it is only old—so old that life has become a kind of geology.

On their backs, the beetles carry sinter-crabs. These are barnacle-like things, their shells fused out of the same minerals that precipitate from the water, porous and glassy. They anchor themselves to the beetle shells and hold out feathery legs to catch whatever the beetles stir loose. And on the sinter-crabs grow bacterial mats, so that each beetle is not one animal but a traveling country: magnetite shell, anchored crustacean, living film, and the slow electric hunger of the earth all moving together.

In the wet channels and pools, the sump-newts swarm. They are gray, soft-bodied, with gills like red-gray feathers that open and close in the cold brine. There are hundreds in every deeper channel, hunting anything small enough to swallow. They move in sudden darts, then hang still, their gills fanning the saturated water. They carry leech-things on their flanks and tails—pale, ribbon-like creatures that are not exactly parasites, or not only parasites. The leech-things carry smaller things in turn, and those carry still smaller things. The water is so thick with layered life that it pours slowly, not because it is viscous but because it is crowded. You cannot tell where one animal ends and the next begins.

Inside the mineral pipes that run through the crust—pipes worn by water over ages, not built by any hand—live the pipe-worms. They are the loudest inhabitants of the Anvil, though you rarely see them. Their bodies are pale, segmented, translucent, and their guts glow faintly with the metals they have swallowed. Their radula scrapes the metal-laden stone with a rasp that throws off small blue sparks, and the hammering of their bodies against the tube walls is the deep, metallic pulse you feel through your feet. Over ten thousand years, a pipe-worm does not simply live in a tube. It lengthens the tube, laying down mineral rings as it bores, so that the pipe is both home and by-product. The whole plain breathes through these living ducts, and the wind and gas move through them with a mechanical rhythm, as if the land had lungs made of worm-tunnels and stone.

Above all this, the fog-flies turn the air into a second fog. Their wings are films of translucent chitin so thin they are barely there, but in their millions they dim the already dim light. They crackle with static when they swarm, and at night the swarm sometimes glows faintly, as if the weak current of the day is leaking back out through their wings. They drink condensation from the air and feed on the dissolved gases in the mist. Anything that holds still for an hour will be covered by them: a second skin of fog-flies, and then the smaller creatures that feed on their bodies, and then the rust that feeds on those. Stillness is a form of invitation in the Cold Anvil.

But the strangest inhabitants are the ones you cannot separate from the stone. When an animal of the Anvil stops moving for too long, the mineral crust begins to grow over it. Legs fuse to the ground. Shells become part of the stone matrix. The animal becomes what appears to be rock—a knob, a boulder, a ridge of mineral—and it sits there for years, or centuries, or longer. Many of them are not dead. They are only paused, their metabolism slowed to an almost geological pace. Half of what appears to be ground in the basin is animals in torpor, indistinguishable from mineral until they exhale, or blink, or shift after a hundred years. You may see a crack in a stone widen into an eye, wet and black, then close. You may feel a pulse in a boulder that has no right to have one. The reverse also happens: some of the mineral growths twitch. Some of the rocks have pulses. The boundary between living and nonliving has worn thin, like the boundary between now and then.

If the Cold Anvil has one true resident, it is the place itself. The water splitting into oxygen and hydrogen at certain seams, the weak electric current moving through the wet ground, the gas pockets heaving and collapsing beneath the crust, the hollow pipes thudding and hooting—these are not just conditions. They are a metabolism. The plain rings like a boiler. It ticks as it cools. It rehearses sounds before it commits to them. It may not have a mind in any familiar sense, but it has a pattern, a set of responses, a way of continuing. The water runs uphill in places, not because the slope is wrong but because the slope changes its mind. A stream will pause, gather itself, and then flow against the pull of the earth as if the earth has forgotten which way is down.

The rules for living there, if you can call them rules, are these: Do not trust the direction of water. Follow the magnetic lines if you must travel, but know that they sometimes loop. Do not stand still for too long, because the crust will begin to see you as part of the land, and the fog-flies will settle on you, and the beetles will walk over you, and the mineral film will start to grow across your boots. Do not assume that a rock is a rock, or that an animal is an animal. If you hear a sound repeat three times, it is not an echo; the place is practicing. If the ground clangs beneath you, do not answer it.

Nothing here was built. No hand shaped the channels or set the pipes. The engines of the Cold Anvil are the work of water, metal, gas, and time. What lives there is not a list of species but a single crowded body made of beetles, rust, salt fleas, sump-newts, pipe-worms, fog-flies, sinter-crabs, leech-things, bacteria, stone, water, electricity, and the slow, exhaling dream of things that paused too long and became the ground itself. If you stay long enough, you will become part of that body too—first as a perch, then as a mineral outline, then as a place where something else lives.
