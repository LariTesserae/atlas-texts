The habitant of the Low Catchment is not a person who lives there; she is a person the place is still making.

She first appears as a smear of motion in a crevice between two lean-to walls, the kind of gap that would not register as a street to anyone from above, a place narrower than a workbench. Her body moves sideways through it, then drops into a wider channel of air where a rope bridge sags under its own damp weight. She does not step; she flows, hip-first, knees turned out, one hand always touching the nearest wall. Her fingers read the brick the way a tongue reads teeth. Her skin is the color of nearly-set mortar, gray-pale with a warmth underneath, and its surface is not smooth but faintly pocked, like unfired clay pressed by a thumb. Where old injuries have healed there are not scars but seams of something harder, a mineral sheen that catches the tallow light. Along her forearms, where she has spent years scraping bloom from the walls, the growth has taken root in her skin. It forms a lacy crust, green-white and delicate, around the wrists and between the knuckles. When she is startled, the crust blushes softly, outlining the bones of her hands in the dark. The bloom is in her—she chews it, tastes it in her saliva, passes it off in her sweat—and it has made her not fully human, or human in the process of becoming something the catchment requires.

Her eyes are not large, as one might expect from a life in darkness; they are small and set deep, and they flick across surfaces rather than into space. She sees by the absence of shadow, by the faint halo of phosphorescence the bloom gives off, by the orange beads of the tallow lamps distance has shrunk to warning stars. But her real seeing is her hands and her hearing. Her ears are broad, slightly flattened against the skull from pulling hoods of waxed cloth close, and continually wet with the indoor rain. She hears the world as a pressure and a weave of small sounds: the tick of a new drip that was not there at the last shift, the deep gurgle of a channel that is backing up, the minute change in the slip-slap rhythm of the chain-pumps that means a link has loosened. She carries a hollow copper tube tucked into her belt, and when she stops, she presses one end to a wall and the other to her ear, and listens to the veins of the catchment, the water singing through its mortar.

Her clothing is layers, all of them permeable, none of them entirely dry. She wears a short tunic of waxed-linen canvas, its front darkened by years of kneeling, and beneath it a woven shirt of some fiber made from rendered fat and roof-thatch, which holds the warmth without rotting. Her leggings are of boiled leather, greased at the seams, and they make no sound against the stone beyond a faint hush, like a hand dragged over a wet wall. On her feet are sandals of layered hide, the soles studded with little paddles of fired clay that grip the slick brick. Around her waist hang a coil of rope, a copper knife in a sheath of raw eel-skin, a leather pouch of powdered bloom mixed with brick-dust, and three lengths of notched bone, each one a record of a day's work. Her shoulders carry the permanent curve of someone who has spent years under low ceilings, and her hands are—above all—hands. The knuckles are enlarged, the fingernails black with water, the palms smooth as polished wood from lifting, scraping, shaping. One finger on her left hand is missing its last joint; the stump is capped with a small copper tube she uses to tap out signals on the pipes.

She is a water-carrier, but not only that. In the Low Catchment, everyone is a water-carrier; the water moves through the hands as much as through the channels. She is also a bloom-scraper in the early shifts, before the lamps are re-trimmed, when the walls sweat and the crust grows fastest. She is a mason's hawk when the bricks arrive still hot from the kiln, carrying wet mortar in a wooden tray. She is a child-listener's teacher, pairing her own hearing with the younger senses, pointing at silence until the silence becomes a shape. The work is not interchangeable; it is one work, shared, and the people of the Basin do not name themselves by what they do but by where they have been unfinished.

They are a small people, by necessity. The average inhabitant stands no higher than the doorways, which are cut low to keep the heat from rising and the water from spreading. Their skeletons are dense from the mineral dust, their chests deep from the perpetual humidity, and their blood, it is said, runs cooler than that of the upper galleries. They rarely run; there is no room to run, and a fall against wet brick can break a hip. Instead they develop an elasticity of movement, a way of compressing and extending, of stepping over rope-lines and under drip-panels without hesitation. The old ones move like water themselves, and eventually they stop moving, and are taken to the sumps, where the thread-eels coil in the silt and the white geckos pause with their translucent bellies pulsing. Their bodies are not buried—there is no soil—but set into the mortar of new walls, so that the building may have a little hearing, a little balance.

The children of the catchment are born silent. It is not that they cannot cry; it is that the water teaches them before the mother does. In the womb they hear the steady roar of the pumps, the hiss of steam, the tick of indoor rain, and they come out with ears already mapping. They walk early, but not on flat ground; their first steps are on the sloped brick, toward the sound of water. By the time they are two, they can tell the difference between a drip of condensation and a leak of waste-water by the note. They are given hollow tubes before they are given knives, and they spend their sixth or seventh years moving through the alleys with their ears pressed to the walls, finding the leaks before they become floods. The people call them the Listeners, and they are the most important beings in the catchment, for a leak that is not caught is a small failure, and ten thousand small failures would drown the world.

The adults do not have much talk. Words are used sparingly, for the noise of the catchment already carries so much. Instead, they communicate by tapping: a copper finger against a brick, a stone hammer against an iron rivet, the slap of a wet hand on a doorpost. There are rhythms for "water rising," "kiln cooling too fast," "child lost, follow the sound," "wall growing soft." They have names, but they are not spoken aloud except in ritual or extreme need. Each person carries a name-stone, a small shaped pebble of fired clay, incised with a pattern of dots and lines, worn around the wrist or neck. When two people meet, they put their hands together and feel each other's stones. That is enough.

Their bodies are marked by the catchment. The masons have shoulders that tilt from carrying bricks; the pump-tenders have forearms like hawsers from the wheel-cranks; the bloom-scrapers have hands that glow even in darkness, so that they can find each other in the deep sumps. The kiln-tenders have faces striped with soot and sweat, and their eyebrows are often singed away; they speak in the dialect of fire, which is short and crackling. The water-carriers have calluses on their inner thighs from the yokes, and their knees are always wet. The Listeners have ears that can move independently, a skill passed from parent to child, and they can hear the difference between a gecko hunting and a thread-eel spawning by the vibration in a damp stone.

The person who first emerges is not a hero; she is a woman of maybe twenty-nine years, in her third apprenticeship, because in the Low Catchment no one ever finishes learning the work. She carries water and scrapes bloom and teaches a child to listen, and at night, when the shift changes and the tallow lamps are re-trimmed, she goes to the small room she shares with four others, a room with no bed but a shelf of waxed cloth over a heat-vent from the kiln below. She sleeps in shifts, as everyone does, because the pumps never sleep and the walls never stop deciding. She dreams, when she dreams, of the upper galleries, which are lighter and colder, and of a world where the ground is soft, where water falls from the sky but does not need to be caught, where there is a green thing called a tree. The dream is not a hope; it is a curiosity, like hearing a sound that does not belong.

In the morning—there is no morning, but the work-gang changes—she rises, eats a handful of scraped bloom mixed with a paste of clay and fat, drinks a cup of water from the high cistern, and goes out into the indoor rain. She tightens the copper cap on her missing finger, checks the tension of the rope-lines, and presses her ear to the nearest wall. The catchment breathes around her, loud and wet, full of small sounds and small lights, and she catches a drip that has fallen on bare stone. It is a small failure, averted. She licked her finger, touched the drip, and it moved into the channel instead of the world. That is the only religion she has.

The Low Catchment is a country of made stone, and the people are made of the same materials: brick and water, bloom and fat, bone and rope. They are not separate from the landscape; they are its organs, its hands, its ears, its temporary mouth. They are building the world one wet brick at a time, and the world is building them in return. They do not look like people from above. They look like a city that forgot to leave room for the earth, and then remembered, and tried to become the earth itself.
