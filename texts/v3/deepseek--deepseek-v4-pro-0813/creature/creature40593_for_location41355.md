**The Listener**

At first you would say nothing lives in the Moss Bell. The drystone shelter is half-ruined and empty; the prayer wall is old; the copper bell has gone green. But the center is not empty. The swifts know it—that is why none of them crosses the hollow. The prayer wall knows it—that is why the scratched sides of the slate plaques face the rock, not the lake. The flat stone near the water knows it—that is why rain does not settle there.

It is older than the shelter, older than the prayer wall, older than the copper. It may be as old as the cirque. When the ice pulled back, it left the bowl of stone and water, and it left something else: a listener. The mountain’s ear. The shepherds do not name it except indirectly. They call the place Listening Hollow, and sometimes they call the place Moss Bell, but the bell is not only the bowl. The bell is also the one who rings.

Most of the time it has no body. It is the tall absence over the dark lake, a column of attention the size of the central volume. The air there is not empty; it is thicker, colder by half a breath. The swifts feel it as a standing wave, a pressure, an occupied place, and they circle along the walls. The mist follows it in a slow spiral. On hard clear days the light bends slightly around something that is not there. The hollow center seems to listen because it is listening.

When it wants to be seen, it gathers itself from the walls. It makes a body out of wet moss and shattered rock, old roots, bird down, the bones of frogs. It stands under the north waterfall and lets the water braid it into shape. It is roughly the size of a person, but not a person. Its shoulders are shelf stone. Its ribs are dwarf-tree branches. Its belly is a mat of black peat and pale lichen. Moss grows over it as deep as a hand. Water seeps from it continually. Its face is a hollow in the moss, a basin that drinks and never fills. Sometimes what looks like an eye is only a gap in the cliff where a swift nests, and the swift flies out of its head and returns at dusk.

It moves rarely. It does not walk so much as resolve elsewhere. It moves as mist, as a slow green flow down the headwall, as a sudden pressure in the ears. When it passes, the frogs chorus harder. The pikas fall silent, then scold. A fox at the shoreline lifts its head and watches it pass, not alarmed, only aware. It leaves no tracks, but it leaves dry patches on wet stone and a line of flattened moss that springs back by evening. The weasel sometimes winds between its legs as if they were only water.

It sits on the flat stone by the lake. That stone stays dry, even in rain, because the listener often sits there and drinks the rain before it lands. When it sits, the whole bowl comes into tune. The waterfalls, the frogs, the swifts, the wind, the distant clatter of the glacier—all of it balances for a moment. If you stand on the stone, you are standing in its seat. It moves aside, or it folds you into its attention. That is what the shepherds mean when they say the hollow listens.

Its work is listening. It listens to water going down and sound going around. It listens to the lake draining through the hidden notch under the moraine. It listens to the rockfalls and the fog pouring over the rim. It listens to the glacier freezing and thawing. It holds all these voices together, a little delayed, and gives them back as the echo that returns three times from three chutes. The third echo is higher because it is not only an echo; it is the listener repeating the sound in its own key. The mountain has a key, and the listener is the one turning it.

It also listens to people. That is why the prayer wall faces the lake but the scratched side faces the rock. The listener reads the prayers with stone hands from inside the cliff. The lake does not need to know what was asked. The blank side faces the water. When someone leaves a stone at the prayer wall, the listener takes it eventually. The wall settles and grows. The horn cup in the cairn, the one still holding a cup made of horn, is an old offering. The listener has drunk from it once, perhaps in some season before the path was marked.

It rings the copper bell sometimes. One clear note, when the fog is thick, and the note returns three times, the third higher than the first. Shepherds hear it and know the bowl is awake. The sound is the place’s weather. The listener is the weather’s ear.

It is not kind and not cruel. It is attentive. It keeps the rules: water goes down, sound goes around, and the center stays empty except for itself. It does not enter the drystone shelter; the shelter is for people. It keeps the roof from falling all the way, and sometimes a fox dens under the lintel. There is no iron in the bowl. The listener does not touch iron. Copper is different; copper sings. The green bell is its bell, and the mountain’s key is copper.

In winter, when the waterfalls thin and the frogs go silent, the listener retreats into the north wall. The blue glow of the lichen after sunset—the second daylight—is the listener sleeping in the stone, holding the last light. In spring, when the melt comes hard, it loosens and comes down the wall with the water, and the frogs know before anyone. The whole bowl begins to sing.

It is not a soul. It is not a god. It is what remains when a place is made to listen. The bowl is an ear of rock. The lake is the silence at the center of the ear. The waterfalls are the voice. The swifts are the heartbeat. The thing that lives in the Moss Bell is the listening itself, old as the ice, wearing moss and echo and waiting.
