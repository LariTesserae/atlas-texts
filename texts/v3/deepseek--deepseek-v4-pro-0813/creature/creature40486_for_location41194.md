The first thing that lives there is not an animal. It is a sheen, a bloom, a skin. In the shallows it colors the water rust-red and olive; at the hottest inflow it burns orange; on the wet concrete it spreads green-black, slick as oil. It is a living mat of bacteria and algae, a city too small to see except as color. It feeds on the mineral springs and the soda, on heat and water and stone. It is the first note of the place, and every other life there is built on it.

In the black mud along the pan edges, the alkali flies live in such numbers that the ground itself seems to shift. They are not a swarm but a layer, a gray carpet packed tight over the alkaline ooze. Their larvae graze the bacterial film in the warm, thick water. Their pupae dot the margin like seeds. The adults lift in a sheet when a shadow passes, hover with a tearing hiss, then settle back exactly where they were, as if the earth had breathed in and out. They eat the slime. They do not leave. The surrounding land is too harsh, too salt-crusted, too dry, and so their entire world is this one edge between wet and dry, brine and air. They live in a constant, trembling patience.

In the water itself, where the shallows cloud red, the brine shrimp drift. Each is a small comma of life, a red speck paddling through water too hot and too alkaline for most creatures. They feed on the same living scum, generation after generation, without seasons, without storms, without any change except the steady chemistry of the pans. They are eaten by few—some fish near the inlet, the flamingos, each other—and so they multiply into a dense, slow cloud, turning the water the color of rust. They are the flesh of the place, the middle of the food chain, billions of tiny hearts marking time in the warm brine.

At the cooler channel where lake water enters the plant, small silvery fish gather near the pipe outlet. They are no longer than a finger, and they live only here, in the thin ribbon of diluted water between the lake and the mineral works. Their whole lives are bounded by temperature and salt. They edge into the warmer current when the inflow slackens, then waver back into the cool. They turn in a loose, nervous school, feeding on midge larvae and fly cast-off, holding their narrow territory like a nerve in the body of the yard. They are the only fish, the only ones who can live in this one breathable margin, and they know its exact borders.

Out in the colored water stands the place’s grandest visible tenant: the flamingos. They are more numerous than seem possible in such a small yard, a long pink-and-white line reflected in the gray mirror of the pan. They feed with their heads down, bills swung upside down through the rust-colored water, filtering brine shrimp and fly larvae through fine, comb-like edges. Their legs are crusted white at the joints with soda. Their feathers are pale but warm, as if each bird carried its own small sun beneath the skin. They murmur constantly, a low barnyard sound a hundred voices wide, and that murmur is the voice of the pans—watchful, hungry, unalarmed. They do not nest on the bare black stone. They come because the food never stops, the water never changes, and the light stays the color of tarnished silver. They are the long enduring line that makes the place feel both wild and strangely domestic.

On the concrete walls, white snails cluster like rivets that have learned to move. They seal themselves inside their shells during the worst heat, then slide out slowly, scraping the green-black film from the wet concrete and leaving clean trails behind them. Hermit crabs drag borrowed shells from one wet crack to the next, patient as small householders in a house too hot to finish. Around the steam vent and the sodium lamps, midges whine in heavy clouds. Their larvae live in the same rich, marginal water, and the adults gather in the dim air as if the light itself had grown small wings. They do not bite. They are simply part of the air, a thin high note above the pump’s low thud.

In the one crack where rainwater dilutes the brine, a few glasswort plants have taken hold. They are small, jointed, fleshy, almost colorless in their green. They store the rare fresh water in their stems and do not spread beyond the seam of accidental sweetness. They are the only rooted green things, the only plants that can bear this ground, and they survive not by conquering the place but by fitting into its one small exception.

At the far side of the road, where the town leaks its own richness, gulls and crows come to feed on spilled grain, offal, garbage. They roost on the pipe trestle and the hopper, cross freely between the mineral shore and the human world. They carry the brine smell in their feathers and the town’s scraps in their bellies. They are opportunists, not natives, but they have learned to sit on hot steel and wait, and their cries cut across the steam’s white roar like arguments from another country.

And then there is a keeper. The yard does not need much human care, but it needs hands for the sluice gates, eyes for the pressure gauges, ears for the pump when its beat changes. The keeper does not sleep inside the fence—there is no bed among the pipes—but he belongs to the place as much as the flies or the flamingos. He walks the steel grating in the permanent twilight, boots white with soda. After years, he no longer hears the steam vent; it has become a kind of silence. He reads the pans by color and by feel: the rust of the brine shrimp, the lift of the fly mats, the temper of the flamingos at the edge. He notices if the water is a half-inch high or if the black patch of flies has shifted three feet, because in a place this steady, any change is an event. He is the one who sees the whole yard as a living thing, a machine made of water and heat and small, abundant lives.

All of these live together in the same held note. The slime and the glasswort, the flies and the shrimp, the fish and the flamingos, the snails and the hermit crabs, the midges and the gulls, the keeper and the crows. They do not compete so much as layer, each in its narrow band of tolerance, each fed by the one below. The water is too harsh for most, but those who live there have been shaped to the harshness. The place is not dead. It is saturated. Step inside the fence, and the mud lifts. The water clouds red. The air is full of small wings. The pink line at the pan edge sways and mutters. What lives there is not one creature but a community pressed into a small hot yard between the alkaline lake and the town—a dense, breathing tenantry, as stable as a chord, as alive as a skin.
