The people who live in the cut have bodies that remember the valley before the city. Not in the way of blood or story, but in the way stone remembers the river—by being worn along certain lines, by accumulating in certain bends. They are called the Gorge Folk, or sometimes the Moss-Backed, though not to their faces, and they are as much a part of the architecture as the cables and the roots.

They are not one kind. The city layers its inhabitants the way the gorge layers its walls, and each layer has learned a different way to breathe.

The High-Rim Watchers live on the plateau edge, in the old stone houses that huddle behind windbreaks of dwarf cloud-forest. Their houses are low, with roofs of thick turf and walls of the ancient planed stone, fitted without mortar. They tend the stone pans—the broad, sky-catching depressions that fill with dark water—and they are the ones who keep the mirrors. Each window on the rim has a mirror angled to catch the rare direct light and send it down into the gorge, a faint, moving coin of brightness that passes over lower streets at certain hours. The Watchers know the schedules of these lights the way sailors know tides. They have pale eyes, the color of the bright gray ceiling, and they wear coats of oiled wool with hoods that come to a point, so that from a distance they look like standing stones.

They do not speak much. Sound on the rim is different—thin, unabsorbed, traveling far over the tableland—and the Watchers have learned to listen more than talk. They note the movements of the fog, the arrival of rain, the state of the moss on their windbreaks. They keep the listeners’ huts supplied with tea and dried fish. In the evenings, they sit in their low doorways and watch the cloud-sea below the rim, and they murmur to each other in a dialect so compressed it sounds like the wind moving through heath.

The Gorge-Bound proper live in the stacked city itself, in the galleries and ledges and overhanging rooms. They are a compact, careful people, with the soft-footed gait of those who share walls and ceilings with others. Their clothes are dark and many-layered, because the damp gets into everything, and they wear household slippers with soles of braided fiber that grip the wet stone. Their faces are often lit from below by electric lamps or from the side by the pale reflected light of mirrors, giving them a fox-like, watchful look.

They live in vertical neighborhoods. The lower down the gorge, the deeper into the mist. The people of the lowest terraces are called the Moss-Dwellers. Their homes are wrapped in fog for most of the year, and they have adapted in small ways: they keep lamps burning with a bluish flame that parts the mist, they paint their walls with lime and powdered quartz to catch what light exists, and they grow food in floating racks on the river itself—cress and watercress and a kind of pale lettuce that needs no direct sun. They are fishermen of the cold shafts, lowering baited lines into the dark water for the blind char, which they eat smoked or fermented into a paste. Their skin is pale, almost translucent, and they have a custom of wearing small lights at their collars, so that in the dense fog they appear first as floating sparks.

Higher up, where the mist thins and the light is stronger, live the Machinists and the Craft-Workers. These are the people of the turbine galleries, the workshops, the glassworks, the copper-beating shed. They are practical, precise, and they have a culture of silent signals—small hand gestures used across noisy rooms or across the distance between ropeway platforms. They keep their hair short or bound under caps of waxed canvas. Their forearms are marked with small burns and scars, and they talk about machines the way farmers talk about weather: intimately, with patience, and with a certain resigned affection.

The Machinists are the ones who tune the fog-breaking frequency. They do not call it magic. But they will admit, over a cup of tea made from river water that has passed through six filters and a bed of moss, that some machines are more than their parts. They maintain the great penstocks that drop from the rim, and they are the only ones allowed to descend the ladders into the deepest turbine galleries, where the walls sweat with oil and condensation, and the roots of old trees have grown through the stonework. They say that when the turbines run at full speed, the fog lifts from the bridges, and the river’s roar becomes a low, steady note that holds the gorge together.

Above the Machinists, on the upper terraces and the rim, are the Keepers of the Rules. They are not rulers exactly, but more like stewards of the city’s agreements. They maintain the right of light, measuring shadows with instruments of brass and glass, making sure no new beam or balcony blocks a neighbor’s mirror. They oversee the replanting of trees—three for every one cut—and they keep the ledgers of water, the complicated accounting by which the city’s water belongs to no one until it has passed through a living thing or a machine. Their offices are narrow rooms lined with shelves of damp-resistant cedar, and they write with ink made from the soot of fish-oil lamps, in ledgers that smell of smoke and moss.

There are also the Bridge-Tenders, who live in small cabins at either end of the ropeways and footbridges. They are a solitary kind, half-watchful, half-dreaming, with the particular patience of people who spend their days waiting for other people to cross. They know the bridges by sound—the creak of a cable, the slight change in tension when a heavy cart passes, the hum of a frayed wire. They wrap the cables with waxed cloth when the frost comes hard, and they paint the ironwork with red lead to keep the rust at bay. It is said they can tell the weight of any crossing creature, and that some of them, in the deepest hours of the night, set out small dishes of milk for whatever crosses when no one is watching.

Then there are the Listeners.

They are the city’s ears, and they live in the small huts along the rim and in certain hollowed-out ledges in the gorge walls. They are chosen young, usually from the Moss-Dwellers, because the lower city breeds an alertness to subtle sound. They sit for hours with their eyes closed, a weighted brass bowl held to the rock or the water, and they map the river’s growl, the shifting of the stone, the slow grind of the gorge cutting deeper. They keep charts of rockfall frequency and river pitch, and they send reports down to the city via a system of colored flags and small lights. They are respected, and a little feared. They hear things others cannot. Some of them have grown so attuned that they can tell the difference between the sound of a turbine bearing wearing thin and the sound of a root pushing through a crack in the wall. A few, the old ones, no longer speak at all. They listen, and they write, and they watch the fog cast shadows where mist does not enter.

But there are others, too. The ones who leave the offerings.

They are not a separate group, exactly. They might be a Moss-Dweller, a Machinist, a Keeper, a Bridge-Tender. But they all know the niches by the river, the small dark ledges where salt and milk and bits of copper are left. They do not talk about it much. They simply add to the small shrines as they pass, a pinch of salt from a pocket, a drop of milk from a jar, a dull coin placed carefully on a wet stone. The offerings disappear. Whether the taker is an animal, a person, or something else, no one asks. The large tracks in the moss on the high rim are found after foggy nights, and they are measured but not discussed. The forest is quiet, but not empty. The people of the cut know this. They have always known it.

There are visitors, though they are rare. Travelers from beyond the plateau, or from the lower valleys, or from the cloud-sea itself—if the cloud-sea has shores. They come up the river or down the old stone roads, and they find the city strange: too vertical, too damp, too quiet, too dense with shadow and leaf. They comment on the moss, the mirrors, the lack of bells. The people of the city are polite, but they do not explain. They offer tea, dark bread, smoked fish. They show the visitors the guest houses, which are spare but dry, with little stoves that burn compressed peat. They answer questions with careful, patient sentences, and at night, when the visitors are asleep, they go back to their own lives—the small offerings, the silent signals, the listening at the rim.

And there are the ones who have gone deeper.

They are a mystery even to the Gorge Folk. The city does not end at the lowest terrace. Below it, past the floating gardens and the fish-lines, there are old stone steps cut into the living rock, worn almost smooth, descending into the mist and the river’s roar. No one uses them officially. But sometimes a person will leave a note—a small square of paper pinned to a door or left under a stone—and then they are gone. The note always says the same thing, more or less: that they have heard something in the river, something in the rock, and they are going down to listen more closely. The Listeners understand this, though they do not encourage it. The Bridge-Tenders sometimes see a figure on the lowest bridge in the fog, and then not see it again. The Moss-Dwellers find a pair of slippers left at the top of the descent, arranged neatly, toes pointing down. And that is all.

But the city continues. The fog rises and falls. The turbines hum. The moss grows over the empty doorways. And the people of the cut go about their quiet, continuous business, patient as the stone that holds them, waiting for the light that comes in shafts and coin-shaped reflections, living in the wound the river has made, and being slowly, slowly absorbed into it.
