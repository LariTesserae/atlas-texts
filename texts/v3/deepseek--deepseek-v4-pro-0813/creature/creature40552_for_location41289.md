The first thing that lives there is the mountain itself, but not in the way a stone lives. It lives as a slow, gray metabolism, as a million small mouths filtering the dark. But you are asking about the ones you would notice, the ones you would call people, if you stayed long enough to learn the shape of the water.

Begin with the smaller lives, because they are everywhere and nowhere. A curtain of glowing thread hangs over a balcony: a siphonophore colony, colonial and patient, each bell a tiny pulse too faint to feel. It catches whatever the current brings. It is not thinking. It is waiting. Around it, blind fish with long translucent fins hang in the galleries, facing into the current like sails without ships. They do not see you. They feel the disturbance you make, and they turn, not toward you but into the flow, waiting for whatever the water will bring. They are the mountain’s stillness.

Higher, where the towers open into the black, squid in schools of a hundred flash in unison, a sudden cloud of blue sparks, then vanish. Their light is fast and sharp, a counterpoint to the mountain’s long note. They live and die in a few years. They are the mountain’s quick thoughts.

In the hollow roots there are eel-like things, some no longer than a finger, others as long as a house. They come out only to hunt, and when they move, they are silent. Their bodies are smooth, white, almost transparent. They are the mountain’s appetite.

And then there are the ones you would call people, if people can be made of water and time. They are the Singers.

The first one is not so much seen as felt. The water ahead of you thickens. The long, low chord bends slightly, as if a second note has slipped beneath it. Then a shape detaches from the blackness. It is vast, soft, pale as a drowned moon. Its body is long and broad, not streamlined but billowing, like a sheet of living cloth slowly filling with water. It has no eyes. The front of it is mostly mouth: a wide, fringed opening that gathers the water and sieves it through plates of fine, horn-like fiber. Behind the mouth, the body stretches away, ten, twenty times your length, ending in a broad, flattened tail that moves in a continuous, wave-like rhythm.

Along its flanks, a pair of translucent fins run from just behind the jaw to the tail. They ripple in slow waves, not for propulsion but for balance. The creature is barely swimming; it is being carried by the current, and its fins hold it steady, angled so that every part of it is exposed to the water. Its skin is loose and thick, pale gray, with patches of faint luminescence. In places, the skin is grown over with the same maroon and amber veneer that covers the phytoid walls. Small isopods cling to the folds. The creature does not seem to mind. It is a landscape as much as an animal.

Then it sings. Not a cry, not a song with melody, but a long, low pressure-pulse that begins in the chest and lasts several minutes. It is almost, almost exactly, the note of the mountain. But it is not the same. The mountain’s note is steady, pure, a fundamental. The Singer’s note is a harmonic, slightly higher, with a slow pulse in it, a beat that feels like a heart. The two notes together create a third, a thrumming that seems to come from inside your own bones.

The pulse is not a word, but a name, or a statement of presence. It carries for kilometers. Somewhere far above, another Singer answers, and the water fills with a deep, slowly changing chord, not so much heard as felt. The conversation might last for hours, or for years. To them, there is no difference.

They are mostly solitary. Each Singer patrols a long circuit through the towers and galleries, its route so old that the water has worn a slight depression in the phytoid walls where its flank has brushed. It filters the water for the fine marine snow, for plankton, for the drifting spores of phytoids. Its gill-fibers are delicate, and they catch only the smallest particles; the rest passes through. In this way it is a gentle feeder, taking little and giving much, because its waste sinks and feeds the microbial mats on the lower slopes.

The Singers are not only feeding. As they move, their fins stir the water, carrying oxygen into the hollow galleries. They pause over the phytoid towers and exhale, a slow, warm cloud, and the warmth drifts down over the bark, encouraging the bacterial film to grow. They do not think of this as tending. They simply breathe, and the mountain breathes back.

They reproduce rarely. A female may give birth to a single calf after a pregnancy of many years, in a warm, still gallery deep in the mountain where the water is slightly heated by decay. The calf is born small—only the length of a house—and it is almost perfectly transparent. The mother sings to it, a different note, higher and rapid, and the calf answers with a pulsing that is still half the mountain’s chord. It will take decades before its voice deepens enough to join the long conversation.

There are gatherings. In the great amphitheaters, where the phytoid crowns form a ring of fluted chimneys, the Singers come together. Not often; perhaps once in a century. They arrange themselves in a circle, mouths outward, tails toward the center, and they sing. The mountain’s note is joined by many harmonics, until the water seems to become solid with sound. The squid schools flash in time. The blind fish face into the current. The eel-like creatures pause in their hollows. For a long while, the whole mountain is one voice. Then the Singers drift apart, and the chord returns to its single, steady note.

The oldest Singer is older than some of the towers. Its skin is almost completely overgrown with phytoid bark, so that it appears to be a moving ridge of the mountain. It no longer eats much; it filters the water slowly, and its gill-fibers are worn. It remembers, if memory is the right word, the songs of Singers long dead. Their names are still in the harmonics. It will die eventually, and when it does, the others will guide its body to the lower layers, where it will sink into the compressed stems and become part of the mountain’s slow decomposition. In that way, the Singers do not leave. They become the note.

They have no tools, no shelters, no fire. They do not need them. Their bodies are their tools; the mountain is their shelter; the cold dark is their fire. They are not the masters of the place, but they are not its children either. They are its voice, its memory, its slow, dreaming brain. The mountain grows and decays without them, but with them it sings.

So the place is inhabited by many things: by the phytoids, by the fish, the squid, the isopods, the siphonophores, the eel-like hunters. But the ones you would remember, the ones you would call a people, are the Singers. They are few. They are huge. They are as much water as flesh. And they will be there, singing the same harmonic under the mountain’s chord, until the ocean itself forgets how to be cold.
