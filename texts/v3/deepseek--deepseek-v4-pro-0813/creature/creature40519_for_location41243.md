The Dry Nave has one inhabitant, though it is not alive in the way you mean.

You will not see it at first. In the absolute dark, your eyes will fail in the ordinary way, and the only thing that seems present is the chord—that vast, slow respiration of the root, the note that began before you were born. But if you stay long enough for your own heartbeat to stop insisting on its rhythm, you may become aware of a place where the sound folds around something solid: a shape in the dark that is not a pillar, not a wall, not a sagging balcony. That is the inhabitant.

It is tall. Too tall for the old doorways, though it still passes through them, bending the way a slow vine bends. Its body is made of the same substances as the Dry Nave itself: wound fiber, bark plates, mineralized wood, resin, and the fine pollen dust that settles on everything but never germinates. It does not breathe. Its chest, if you can call it that, is still. The air does not enter it; the air only touches it, carrying the chord. It has no eyes. In the place where eyes might have been, there are two smooth depressions, not scars but places where a face never learned to grow sight. In the Dry Nave, there is nothing to see.

It sees by vibration. Its whole body is an ear. The plates of its skin are tuned to the note of the plant. It knows every room, every step, every sagging balcony by the way the sound returns. If you stand there, it knows you are there—not as a sight, but as a change in the density of the dark, a faint warmth in the cool dry air, a tiny interruption in the floor’s long note. It may turn its head toward you. It may take an hour to do so. That is not hesitation; it is its native tempo.

It has hands, though they are not animal hands. They are rootlets that have grown together into something like fingers, five or six or seven, each one capable of independent patience. With these hands it continues the city’s old motions. It sits at a spindle still wound with root-hair thread and turns it, not to make cloth but to keep the gesture of making. It collects dust in a pot of baked root-clay and pours it out again. It sharpens a blade of glassy mineral on a whetstone of dense plant-wood, though it cuts nothing. It mends the woven fiber of a sagging balcony with root-hair cord. These are not tasks of survival. They are tasks of memory.

It moves through the vertical city without light. It knows each doorway by the shape of its edge, each stair by the way the wear has softened it. It goes down to the far end, where the cold air pools, and stands there for years at a time, one hand flat against the floor. The cold is the only other presence it seems to acknowledge. It listens through the floor. It also speaks through the floor. If it answers you, it will not use a voice. It will press its palm to the wood, and the wood will hum under your feet. The vibration will rise through your bones and settle behind your sternum. It will not be words. It will be a pattern that means: *I know you are here. The dark is not empty. This is not a tomb.* Or perhaps: *You are too quick, too wet, too temporary. You will be gone before the next note ends.*

It does not eat. It does not drink. There is no water in the Dry Nave, and it has never known rain. Its body is fed by the clear, cold solvent that moves in the root, absorbed through the cracks in its bark. It is not animal. It has no bones, no blood, no droppings, no nest. It is not the plant, but it is of the plant—a limb the plant grew and then forgot to reabsorb, or a memory that became too solid to dissolve.

It sleeps, but its sleep is indistinguishable from the architecture. It will stand in a niche between two root-columns, and its feet will send threadlike roots into the floor. Its hair will become tangled with the wall fibers. Its skin will fuse with the bark over years. In that state, it dreams. Its dreams are the plant’s dreams. It dreams rooms that do not yet exist, stairways that spiral upward into the root, tools and baskets and spindles. When it wakes, after a decade or a century, the city has changed a little: a doorway has widened, a step has softened, a wall has grown a new shelf. It does not seem to notice. It steps out of the wall, leaving a shallow impression of its body in the wood, and resumes its slow rounds.

What is it? The Dry Nave has no name for it, and it has no need of one. It is not one of the original builders. The original builders are gone. No one knows if they died or left or became something else. But the plant remembers them. In the dry, dark, waterless chamber, memory does not decay. It accumulates. It thickens. It puts out roots. The plant had learned the shape of a resident: a body that stood upright, hands that could turn thread, a face that listened in the dark. It grew that shape from its own substance, not as an animal, but as an extension of itself. The inhabitant is the plant dreaming a person, and the person dreaming the plant. It is the last resident, the only listener, the one who stayed to keep the rooms from forgetting what they were for.

If you leave the Dry Nave and return a hundred years later, it will still be there. It may be standing in the same place, where the cold air pools and the sound folds around a shape that is not wall and not root. It will not have aged. It will not have eaten, drunk, slept, or died. It will simply be there, listening. And if you stand still enough, long enough, you may begin to hear the same thing it hears: the slow, dry breathing of the world.
