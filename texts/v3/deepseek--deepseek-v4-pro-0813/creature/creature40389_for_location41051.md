At first, you might think no one lives here. The High Condensery keeps its own company: the hiss of misters, the mineral drip, the blind white fish circling below the steel grating, the single pale moth whose orbit rounds the brass lamp. The plants are the only visible life, and they are so dense and wet that they seem not to breathe so much as to leak. If you stand on the lower walkway and listen, you hear no human sound—no voice, no footstep, no scrape of a tool. The quiet is complete except for the machine’s small, rhythmic talk.

But stay longer. Let the mist settle on your sleeves. Watch the ferns by the second-level rail. Eventually you notice a patch of shadow that does not sway with the circulation fans. A shape on a narrow cot of iron pipe and canvas, wrapped in a damp blanket. A pale hand resting on a fold of wool, fingers curled, nails short and rimmed with something white. It is a person, though the room has made them into something else.

The keeper—if that is the right word—lives in a niche behind a curtain of dripping fronds on the middle walkway. The space is no larger than a ship’s berth: a cot, a small table bolted to the wall, a shelf of strange objects. The table holds a logbook swollen with humidity, a pencil, a copper scraper, a tin cup, and a jar of dried leaves. The shelf holds what the keeper has saved: a piece of young travertine no bigger than a walnut, a shed wing of the pale moth, a glass vial of milky water, a root that grew in the shape of a hand before it was pruned. There is no mirror, no clock, no radio. The room itself keeps time.

The keeper is small and narrow, not frail but dense, like wood that has lain long in wet air and learned to hold its shape. Their skin is pale, almost the color of new stone, and it has a faint sheen even when dry. Their hair is long and colorless, often damp, hanging in strands like the aerial roots of the vines. Their eyes are pale too, and they blink slowly, as if unused to any light sharper than green-gold through glass and steam. Their mouth is quiet. Their hands are the most alive part of them: long, careful, the fingertips slightly swollen from years of mineral water, the knuckles stained with calcium. They go barefoot on the warm steel and stone. Their soles are tough and pale, and they know exactly where to step so as not to crush the freshest terraces.

They wear no uniform. Their clothes are simple canvas and wool, patched and stiff in places where mineral water has soaked them and dried. Over this, when they work near valves or boilers, they wear a rubber apron and old leather gloves—but mostly they do not. They say the stone should be felt, not just seen. They can tell by touch whether a new crust is soft enough to scrape or dense enough to leave.

The keeper does not keep ordinary hours. They wake when the first pressure release of the morning sighs through the pipes, a sound like a long breath held overnight and finally let go. They rise from the cot, pour water from a condensation line into the tin cup, and drink it slowly while sitting on the edge of the cot. The water tastes of hot metal and clean stone and something faint like ozone. They have grown to prefer it.

Then they walk. Not a patrol, not a survey—a slow movement through the room, from the lower sump to the upper walkway, touching the stone, listening. They stop at the same places each time: the north channel where the water has begun to cut a new lip; the east valve where the fig root is reaching for the pressure gauge; the south pool where the mineral film thickens. They scrape where the stone threatens to swallow a moving part, but they do it gently, with the copper tool, peeling back thin layers like pages. They prune a root only when it has found a moving part, and even then they speak to it, a low murmur that is not quite words, more like the click and hum of the pumps. *Not there. Not yet.*

They are not alone. The blind fish in the dark sump know their step on the grating and rise, pale as slivers of soap, when the keeper passes. The keeper drops a few crumbs of hard bread into the water and watches the white shapes circle. The moth returns each evening to the brass lamp, and the keeper sometimes leaves a drop of sugar water on a leaf nearby. They do not name these creatures. They are simply the fish, the moth. The room is full of other companions: the drip, the hiss, the creak of expanding metal, the slow bloom of a bromeliad, the frond unrolling over days. The keeper listens to all of them. They know the different sounds of water—the tick of condensation falling, the gurgle in the channels, the thin hiss of the misters, the deeper pulse of the sump pump. They can hear when a filter begins to clog or when a stream has shifted course overnight. They know the smell of water when the mineral content is too high: hot pennies and iodine. They know the smell of healthy growth: green and yeasty, like the inside of a wet greenhouse. They know when the boiler is running too cold because the stone grows slower and the air smells flat, less alive.

They keep the logbook on the little table. The entries are brief, written in a small hand, sometimes blurred by damp. They are not only readings. One entry reads: *The fig root has reached the second pressure gauge. The needle still moves. I will not cut it yet.* Another: *New pool by the north channel. Milky at the edges. Smells of rain and hot metal.* Another: *The moth came back. It always comes back.* The logbook is not for anyone else, though the workers glance at it sometimes. It is a way of keeping time in a place where time moves differently.

The workers come rarely. Every few weeks, they arrive on the lower landing with supplies: dried fruit, hard bread, tinned meat, soap, a new pencil, a new scraper. They do not stay long. The outside is thin and cold and wind-scoured, and the transition from the high mountain air to the warm, wet lung of the Condensery is a shock. The workers stand on the first walkway, their coats steaming, and call up to the keeper. *Stone’s fast on the east walk,* one of them might say. The keeper nods from the second level, half-hidden by ferns. *I’ll scrape it.* The workers leave the supplies and go back out. They think the keeper is strange but not dangerous. They say the Condensery has grown a soul, and that the soul has pale eyes and bare feet and speaks to the water. They do not say this unkindly. It is simpler than saying that a person has learned to live inside a machine-garden without being crushed or dissolved.

The keeper rarely goes outside. The outside air is too dry, too bright, too thin; it burns the lungs that have acclimated to steam. When they must, they wear a heavy coat and goggles, and they move quickly across the wind-scoured ledge to the supply hatch or the boiler shed. They do not look at the clouds below. They are not interested in the view. Their horizon is the wired glass roof, the fume of steam, the green-gold light. The outside world has become a rumor.

Over time, the room has begun to take the keeper into itself. There is a faint mineral smell to their skin, like wet limestone. Small patches of moss sometimes appear on the damp edge of their blanket or on the shoulders of their coat, and they do not brush them away. The cut on their forearm, from a steam burn long ago, healed with a pale, smooth scar that looks almost like travertine. They sometimes sit on the edge of the lower pool and let the milky water rise over their feet. They stay until the mineral film begins to form on their skin, a thin, living crust. They know they could scrape it off, but they usually don’t. The stone is patient. So are they.

What does it mean to live somewhere? The keeper is not sure. They have lived in the High Condensery for so long that the line between their body and the room’s body has blurred. Their breath syncs with the misters. Their pulse slows when the pumps slow. They dream of walking through endless terraces of soft white stone, and when they wake, their feet are wet. They are not unhappy. They are not happy in the way the outside world might measure happiness. They are in equilibrium, like the water, the stone, the plants, the metal. They are the animal that the room has allowed, or perhaps grown.

In the evening, when the misters have finished their last cycle and the light through the glass has gone from green-gold to grey-green, the keeper sits on a ledge by the fig tree. They rest one hand on the root that wraps the steam pipe. The root is warm. Water drips. The moth circles the lamp. Below, the blind fish turn in their dark tank. The keeper exhales, and the room seems to exhale with them, a long, wet breath in the glass lung. They are not the gardener, not the geologist, not the engineer. They are the one who stays. And in a place that is always growing, always accreting, always building itself one wet layer at a time, staying is the only thing that matters.
