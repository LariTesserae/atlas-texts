At first there is no one to describe. The chamber is not empty, but its abundance—springtails, flies, frogs, wet stone, sourceless light—sprawls without a center. If you stay, though, you begin to notice a shape in the corner of attention. It does not enter. The room simply leans toward personhood.

What lives there is sometimes a figure. It stands near the pool or by the drystone bench, tall and pale and slightly too narrow, like a root that has learned to stand. Its skin is not skin but limestone: the same glisten, the same raw fractures, the same damp mineral smell. In places lichen grows on it, map-like, not as a colony but as a kind of hair or memory. It has no fixed hair of its own. The hanging root-thread from the ceiling sometimes drifts close and twines behind its head. Its face is unfinished. Where eyes should be are shallow cupmarks, and in one rests a small chalk sphere, polished and oddly warm. It does not see with it, or not only. The sphere rolls in the cupmark when the figure turns, like a thought that has not chosen a direction. Its mouth is a narrow fracture that opens rarely. It drinks from the film on the walls and from the droplets that rise instead of falling. It does not chew. It tastes the air through its whole surface, the way stone does.

It has limbs, but their number will not hold. At first there are two arms, too long at the elbow, hands ending in pale jointed fingers like the white shoot rising from the silt. Then there seem to be more, or fewer, or they are not limbs at all but slow root-knots and slabs of shadow. Its legs similarly: sometimes two and narrow; sometimes a low broad base of many smaller movements, as if it uses the chamber’s uneven floor to walk. It is not one shape. When you look away and back, it is a line of split pebbles along the floor, then a hunched patch of silver springtails on the weeping wall, then a cluster of blind millipedes uncoiling under a lifted stone. It can be the row of translucent snails, dozens of them shell-backed and glistening, arranged too deliberately to be mere congregation. The frogs at the pool’s edge do not react to its passing. They are not used to it. They are waiting, and it has not yet finished the thing they are waiting for.

It built the drystone bench, or is building it, or is the bench’s absent sitter. It never sits. It runs its hand along the sharp stone edges, and the newly split faces seem to become slightly more real under its palm. The upright slab with the cupmarks is part of it too—not an altar but a spine, a gauge, a phrase. It sometimes takes the chalk sphere out of its own face and sets it in a cupmark, then rotates it slowly, and the light, though sourceless, seems to lean toward it. The steps cut into the floor are its track. It walks them without progress. Each step produces a small tilt in the room, a change in distance, and then the room forgets itself back.

It tends the pool. It kneels there for long intervals, still as the frogs, looking at the wrong reflection. The water reflects the chamber at another hour, and the figure in the water is not always the same one. Sometimes the reflected figure has already stood up. Sometimes there is a second opening behind it. When the inhabitant dips its hand into the pool, the surface does not ripple; instead, the hand comes out more finished, more particular, the wetness briefly setting its details. Then it dries back into damp stone and indecision.

It does not hunt the animals. It feeds on the same invisible economy they do: biofilm, fungal thread, mineral dust, the light. It may feed by standing in the bright air and letting the light move upward through its body, which then turns nearly transparent. Inside its torso you might see small forms—not swallowed, but participating. A white fly circles inside it and exits through the shoulder. A copepod moves in its chest like a heartbeat. It is a body made partly of the chamber’s tiny lives. The silence around it is not pure absence but a pressure, the way the quiet sits on the ears like felt.

Its voice, if it speaks, is not audible. When it opens the mouth-fracture, no sound comes, but the silence deepens. The droplets pause. The springtails halt in a silver wave. The frogs turn their heads. It speaks instead by arranging the pebbles. The line of split pebbles is a sentence. The cupmarks are older sentences, or newer ones. The notched slab is a word it has not finished, waiting for a beam or a shadow. The curved fragment of fired clay may be a syllable that broke. The single flat stone with a groove worn by no water is the track of one repeated thought. The inhabitant is not a creature that uses language. It is language in the middle of being made.

It is not always the same age. In one glance it is ancient: its stone skin pitted, its back folded, its fingers smooth as old church steps. In another it is newly formed: raw and milky, edges sharp, limbs still soft, light leaking from its seams. Both are true. It is like the chamber’s corner where one wall is already deeply weathered and two strides away another is still glistening new. It can be in two places at once, the way the white snail is. An older version sits on the bench while a younger one kneels by the pool, and neither notices the other. Their shadows point toward the brightest part of the room. Their shadows lag, then snap back.

It would be easy to call it a spirit of the place, but that is not quite right. It is less than a spirit and more than a creature. It is the chamber’s effort to become occupied—a tenant grown out of the same limestone, the same root-thread, the same light that should not exist. It did not come from somewhere else. It is what happens when a pocket in the earth begins to want a self. The built traces are not ruins left by it. They are stages of its body or its mind: bench, slab, cupmarks, sphere, steps. Each is a joint in a thing not yet complete. It sometimes presses its palm against the crack where new stone is filling itself, not like a mason, but like a person smoothing a fontanelle. It cannot tell if it is forming the room or the room is forming it. The distinction may not exist here.

It may leave, but if it does, it leaves through the pool’s reflection. You can see it there, perhaps, moving down the reflected passage with the chalk sphere or a line of pebbles or nothing in its hands. But even then it is still in the chamber, kneeling and not kneeling, because leaving here is only another kind of becoming.

If you stay long enough, you might feel it look at you—not with eyes, but with the entire bright, unfinished room. The attention is cool and slow, like water deciding whether to fall. Then the moment passes. The light stays sourceless. The frogs do not blink. The same droplet falls for the first time again. And the inhabitant is gone, or not gone, but folded back into the stone, the pool, the minnow-light, the bad shadows, the silence. It lives there the way a word lives in a mouth before it is spoken.
