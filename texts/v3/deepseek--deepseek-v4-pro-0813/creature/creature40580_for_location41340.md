At first you think the valley is empty. You search for green and find none. You listen for birds or insects and hear only the ringing of the ground. But the emptiness is an illusion, or rather a failure of your own attention. The residents are here, but they are the color of the water and the stone, and they move either too slowly or too briefly for the eye.

The first ones you notice are not animals but films. On the underwater faces of the rims, where the water pours over the stone, there are patches of gray-pink and rust-red. They look like stains, but they are living. These are the bacteria, the only producers in the valley. They eat the dissolved iron and sulfur that rise from below, and they turn those minerals into the first flesh. They have never needed the sun, though the sun falls on them all day. They grow as a skin, a fraction of a millimeter thick, layer upon layer. The older cells die and become part of the mineral crust, so the stone itself is partly their fossil, their breath turned to sinter. They are the oldest inhabitants, the founders, the base of the food chain. They do not grow upward. They spread sideways, following the water’s edge, and their colonies are as stable as the terraces they help to build.

Then, if you wait, you see a white thread-worm. It is so thin it seems a living line drawn on the water. It drifts in the low current, grazing on the bacterial film. Its body is a clear tube of muscle with a darker gut; it has no eyes, no face, only a mouth at one end and a slow wave of motion. It is not in a hurry. The film is sparse, and the worm’s life is a continuous, unhurried harvest. It feels the vibration of the ground through its whole body—the ringing that you feel in your sternum is, to the worm, a constant pressure, a direction, a ground truth. It tastes the water’s chemistry, the iron and the sulfur, the difference between one pool and the next. It follows the film, and the film follows the water, and so the worm’s life is a slow circulation through the terraces, from rim to rim, never leaving the mineral.

In a deeper pool, a copepod hangs. It is almost transparent, and at first you see only two tiny black spots. They are eye-spots, but the copepod is blind; those spots are the vestiges of eyes, left behind by ancestors who lived in brighter, more complex places. The copepod moves in sudden, tiny jerks, each one a kick that sends it a few millimeters through the water. It is not lost. It navigates by touch and by the shape of the currents, by the standing waves that do not move. To the copepod, those cross-hatched patterns on the water surface are a permanent geography, as real as the terraces are to you. It feeds on bacteria and dead cells, and it is so few in number that a single pool may have only one or two. But it is enough. The copepod does not need company. It needs only the water’s slow turn and the thin rain of organic matter from above.

The midge is the only resident that leaves the water, and it does so only at the end. It is flightless, or nearly so. When it emerges, it does not take to the air. It stands on the surface film, its legs dimpling the water like six small stars. It is silent. It does not sing, as midges elsewhere do. It lives for a day or two, warmed by the cold sun, and then it dies without a sound. Its body, lighter than a grain of sand, falls onto the rim or into the pool. The bacteria will reclaim it. The midge’s whole adult life is a brief pause between the water and the mineral, a single note too high and too short for you to hear, but it is part of the valley’s chord nonetheless.

There are no predators here. No fish, no birds, no spiders. The food chain is short: bacteria, then the grazers—the thread-worms, the copepods, the midge larvae—then death, which is mostly a settling, a return to the film. The density of life is tiny. If you counted every living cell in a square meter, the number might be smaller than the number of water droplets in the air above it. But the life that is here is not fragile. It has been here for a very long time, perhaps as long as the terraces themselves. It has learned to live on almost nothing, in a place too bright, too loud, too mineral for anything else.

And the numbers do not change. The bacteria grow as fast as the water brings minerals, and the worms graze as fast as the bacteria grow. The copepods eat what the worms and bacteria discard, and the midges emerge singly because the system cannot support more. There are no seasons, no floods, no droughts, so there are no booms and no crashes. The life of the valley is a steady state, a living equilibrium as precise as the standing waves. Each organism is a small fluctuation around an average that has held for centuries.

The residents are not separate from the valley. The bacteria are part of the stone’s growth. The worms and copepods are part of the water’s slow circulation. The midge is part of the air’s brief pause. They do not alter the terraces; they are another kind of deposit, another kind of current, another kind of standing wave. They are the life that the valley allows, and they are as unchanged as the sound.

If you ask who lives here, the answer is: no one with a name. The valley is inhabited by the almost invisible, by a community of extremophiles that has become indistinguishable from the mineral world they dwell in. They are not missing. They are exactly enough.
