No one lives here, and yet the place is full of life. The first answer to the question is: nothing. The second answer is: everything. The highland is not a habitat for separate beings; it is a single continuous body, and the lives within it are not individuals but processes, not citizens but organs. They move through the ember-dark with the patience of roots and the purpose of pistons, and they are as blind as the world itself.

If you stand long enough in one of the winding passages—if you let the heat press through the soles of your feet and the silence fill your ears until it becomes a sound—you begin to see them. They do not emerge from hiding. They are already there, their shapes inseparable from the walls until they move.

The harvesters are the first to become visible. They are low-slung, built of dense heartwood, their bodies all joint and no face. They move on root-stilts, each step a slow, deliberate flex, and their forelimbs end in rotary cutters of polished seed-stone. They are not eating in any animal sense; they are converting. They press their cutter-mouths to a wall of dead tissue, and the tissue comes away in long, fibrous curls. The pulp is drawn back through a hollow spine of reed, into a belly that is not a stomach but a small furnace. The harvester glows faintly as it works, a blush of heat visible through the seams in its bark. It does not stop. It does not look up. It is as blind as a root.

Then the pressers. They are huge and slow, and they do not travel. They are found where the mass is densest, where the passages widen into chambers. Their bodies are built of compressed lignite, dark and glossy, and they are shaped like inverted bells, or like the anvils of some underground smithy. They take the pulp from the harvesters—not by exchange but by a kind of shared flow, the pulp moving through channels that connect one body to the next—and they press it. Their pistons are thick as tree trunks, and they move so slowly that you could watch for an hour and see only a hand's width of progress. Heat pours from them. The air around them shimmers. They are the bones of the world being remade, one stroke at a time.

The weavers are smaller and quicker, though quick here means a movement visible over minutes, a limb uncoiling like a fern. They have many arms, each tipped with a spinneret of living sap. They crawl along the walls and ceilings of passages, laying new strands, weaving fresh growth over old wounds. They are the ones who close the tunnels that the harvesters open, who turn a collapsed ceiling into a new chamber. They leave behind them a faintly sticky lattice that hardens into wood within hours. Their bodies are slender, almost delicate, but they are made of the same heartwood and seed-stone, and they wear out just the same.

There are also the passage-shapers. They are long and serpentine, their bodies made of linked segments of hardened sap, and they bore through the mass with rotating heads of seed-stone teeth. They do not follow existing passages; they make new ones, leaving behind them a rough tunnel that the weavers soon line and the plants soon fill. They are the reason the corridors never stay the same, why the highland's interior is a labyrinth that rewrites itself.

Then there are the furnace-tenders, which are not separate from the furnaces but move around them, adjusting vents of woven fiber, feeding root-drawn heat into the hot cores. They glow the brightest, their bodies shot through with veins of ember-light. They have no hands, but their limbs end in heat-blackened hooks of dense charcoal. They hook into the furnace walls and pull, opening and closing the vents. They do not tend in the sense of care; they tend in the sense that a current tends downward, that a flame tends toward air. They are the most like what you might call workers, if the word could mean something without will.

And there are the root-drawers. They are the deepest inhabitants, if depth means anything in this woven place. They sink into the floor, their bodies half machine and half root, and they pull heat from the planet's core. They never move from their stations. Their only motion is a slow pulse, a drawing and releasing, a rhythm that matches the pulse of the whole highland. They are the reason the air is thin and hot, the reason the ember-glow never fades. They are not alive in the way an animal is alive; they are alive in the way a heartbeat is alive.

These are not separate species. They are the highland's limbs, its organs, its hands. A harvester that breaks down is harvested itself; its heartwood becomes a presser's beam; its vine-belts become a weaver's strands. A furnace-tender that grows too hot is torn open and its charcoal hooks are used to build another furnace. A root-drawer that slows is pruned back to the root and allowed to regrow. The distinction between plant and machine is not blurred—it was never there. They are all one body, one metabolism, one life.

But the plants themselves are also inhabitants. The tendrils that uncoil in the dark, the roots that creep through the woven ground, the leaves that unfurl without light—they are not a backdrop. They are the flesh of the world. They grow into the machines, through the machines, around the machines. A piston of hollow stem may put out leaves. A gear of dense heartwood may sprout a root. The weavers' sap-strands are alive; the pressers' lignite beams are dead tissue that the plants will reclaim. Even the floor is alive, a woven mat of roots and stems that flexes underfoot, knitting itself back together when torn. The whole mass is a single plant, a single machine, a single creature.

And if you ask who lives here, the truest answer is this: the highland itself lives. It is a single continuous organism, ancient and without boundary, built of plant and machine, burning without flame, growing and consuming and rebuilding. It has no eyes to see its own ember-light, no mind to know its own churn. It is alive the way weather is alive, the way a star is alive—a self-sustaining pattern that does not need to know itself to be.

But that is not the whole truth. Because the highland is not one thing; it is a slow-motion storm of things. The harvesters, pressers, weavers, passage-shapers, tenders, root-drawers—they are not just organs of a larger body; they are also, in their own way, lives. They have beginnings and endings, they have forms, they move, they do work. They are not aware, but they are not nothing. They are the highland's dream of itself, the shapes the world takes when it moves. They are the inhabitants of a place that has no inhabitants, the population of a solitude.

Maybe there is no one to name them, but names arise anyway. The harvesters are the eaters of the dead. The pressers are the makers of bone. The weavers are the closers of wounds. The passage-shapers are the openers of ways. The furnace-tenders are the keepers of the heat. The root-drawers are the hearers of the deep pulse. They are not people, not animals, not minds. But they live. They live in the only way this world allows: as parts of a whole, as waves on a sea, as embers in a banked fire.
