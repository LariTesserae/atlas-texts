What lives there is not obvious, because it is made from the place’s own grammar. At first you see only the small permanent residents: pale amphipods in the sump, a black water beetle rowing in a rusted bucket, a newt the colour of wet clay in the outlet channel. They are patient, nearly invisible. But they are not the resident. They are the fauna that a larger body supports.

The true resident is a body the basin has assembled out of its own ruins. It is broad and low, like a seal or a lungfish, but without fur or skin you could name. Its back is a line of old slabs and rust plate, crusted with calcite in pale, hair-like ridges. Its flanks are packed blue-grey clay, cracked in a net of fine fissures, but never dry. Water seeps from its seams just as it seeps from the weep holes in the retaining wall. Its spine is a run of buried pipe. The six standpipes in a row like organ pipes are its ribs or its voice. The toppled gantry is the roof of its den. The old wires run into it not as power but as nerve.

It has no clear head above water. Where the head should be, there is the split casing of the old pump, or the junction box that has become a mineral spring, or the dark mouth of a rusted standpipe. Its face is kept in the water. That is why the reflections are wrong. In one pool the pump house is reflected with its door whole, not hanging. The clouds move against the sky. That is the resident’s face, or its memory, or the inner surface of its body. The actual ruin is the outer hide; the reflection is the living underside.

It breathes. The buried dewatering system still cycles, but the pump is no longer the cause. The resident has taken the pipes into itself the way roots take a drain. Every seventeen or eighteen minutes—or sometimes not for an hour—it inhales. The water table rises. The main sump swells. A standpipe clears its throat and spits a fine spray. Every puddle shivers at once. Then it exhales, and the water sinks into the cinders and the joints and the split casing. The calcite crust cracks and reseals. The ground quivers. This is not a machine cycle; it is a slow wet breath, the kind a fish might take if the fish were the whole basin. The brass gauge still holds its needle at three o’clock—not because the gauge is stuck, but because that is the resident’s resting pulse.

It moves without walking. A mudbank slumps. A sinkhole opens and fills with black water and disappears when you look back. A plug of mineral scale pops from a pipe and floats away. The pool near the eastern wall drains backward into its own centre. These are the movements of a sleeper adjusting a blanket, of a body settling into its own shape.

Its sounds are the sounds you take for the place’s weather. The metal tick as it cools or warms is the click of its joints. The transformer hum on the leaning pole is the low edge of its blood. The water sliding over gravel with a sound like a page turned far away is its digestion. It feeds on iron, sulfur, the carbolic sweetness of old brick. It sheds calcite. The orange and white bacteria are the residue of its digestion. The mats of algae that bloom and vanish with the pump cycle are its gut-flora. The amphipods live in its folds. The black beetle rows in a bucket that may once have been its hand. The newt follows its exhalations down the outlet channel. The grey wagtail lands on the gantry, bobs exactly four seconds, and leaves—the length of the resident’s attention, the time it takes to notice something warm on its back.

Even the scarce vegetation belongs to it. The mare’s-tail grows where a cleaner seep rises from the base of the spoil—the resident’s breath at its purest. The black sedge is a patch of old leather where the body comes close to the surface. The moss on the north wall lives only where the resident’s sweat has the right salinity. The alder leans from the tailings, its roots wrapped around a porcelain insulator; one root is partly the resident’s finger, holding the insulator like the memory of a live wire.

It is not hostile, and it is not a ghost. A ghost is what remains of a person. This is what remains of a place after the place has been left long enough to start living. The low concrete wall, the steel sheet piling with rust holes like rotted lace, the spoil and broken brick—these are the creature’s edges. The water is its grammar. The dense metal thicket is its fur. The cool air near the puddles is its breath against your face.

If you stay too long, you may feel it notice you. Not as an intruder. As a stone. It will begin, very slowly, to round you, to coat you in calcite, to settle you into the ground. It might take years. It might take only until the next pulse. But the resident is patient. It lives at the bottom of the draw, breathing the basin in and out, digesting the old work site into the same wet shape, and keeping the door whole in the water, where no one can let it fall.
