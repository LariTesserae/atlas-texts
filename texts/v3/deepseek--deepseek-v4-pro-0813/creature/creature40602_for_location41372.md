What lives in Brack is not a person, not an animal, but the air itself. It is thick with condensation, sulfide, oil, and the breath of thousands of small bodies. Before you have seen a single creature, you have inhaled the town. The air is not empty; it is crowded. It hangs on your skin, runs down your hands in black lines, and fills your ears with a sound that is both mechanical and alive.

The ground below is covered by a purple-grey mat, a chemosynthetic skin that feeds on methane and sulfur rising through the mud. It is the first living thing, though it looks like mineral. Around some vents it has grown pale spires, not plants, not stone, but accretions of centuries. This mat is the base of everything. It smells of wet iron and sulfide. It regrows exactly as fast as it is eaten.

Then come the clinkers. They are small grey isopods, millions of them, black-segmented, moving over the mat so thickly that the mud appears to flow. They make a sound like pebbles rubbing. They do not flee from you; they part around your boots and close again. They are the grazers, the first animals. They feed without haste and are fed upon without alarm.

In the seep pools lie the whistle-eels, blind and white, half-buried. Every few seconds they lift their narrow heads and emit two notes, a low call and a higher answer. Multiplied by thousands, the notes merge into a glassy chorus that seems to come out of the ground. It is not a song. It is more like the pool’s own voice, tuning itself. The people no longer hear it as separate from the engine’s wheeze.

Along the banks stand the nodders. They are tall, grey-pink, featherless, eyeless. Their long legs are stilted and too thin, but they never tremble. Each has its own fixed place, its own worn hollow in the crust. They probe the mud with long black beaks that look iron-hard. They do not call. They only nod, once every few seconds, in a slow blind rhythm that matches the engine’s piston. They are not birds. They are something older and less concerned with flight.

The tunbacks move through the deeper pans. They are pig-sized but slower, their black and rust-colored plates overlapping like old armor. Their shells are crusted with mineral deposits and old iron fittings, and when the plates touch they ring faintly, a low metallic note that hangs in the wet air. They navigate by sound, following the same circuits around the town. They hum almost below hearing. You feel them before you see them, a vibration in the walkway, a pressure in the air. The people step aside for them; the tunbacks do not acknowledge the courtesy.

There are other lives hidden in the machinery: pale grubs in the pipe insulation, black flies that crawl in spirals instead of flying, blind eel-like things in the cooling troughs. No corner is sterile. There is no green thing here. The only green is the corrosion on the lamp glass, and even that is grey. The built world is sparse and the animal world is dense to the point of saturation. You can walk for several minutes and see no person, but you will see movement everywhere.

The people appear slowly. They wear grey wool and rubber aprons, and their movements have the same settled rhythm as the rest of the town. There are few of them, perhaps a dozen. They are all adults. There are no children, no elders, no one who seems young or old. Their faces have the same settled look as the rust and the mud.

One is the oiler. He is a man whose hands are black in a way that will never wash clean. He walks the engine house catwalks and touches each bearing with his palm, listening. He oils the same points every seven hours, not by a clock but by the two-part sound of the engine: dull clap, high wheeze. He does it not because the engine wears, but because the oiling is part of the engine’s staying. His body has learned that interval. His heart beats in it.

Another is the scraper, a woman with a long-handled blade. She walks the raised walkways and scrapes the mat and clinkers from the iron and timber. Her strokes are wide and slow, and the sound of her blade is a high metallic scrape that joins the engine’s hum. She does not scrape to remove the life, only to keep the walkways open. The scrapings fall into the pans, and the animals receive them.

There is a lamplighter. The lamps burn all day in the shadowless orange-grey light, but he still climbs each pole, wipes the green-grey corrosion from the housing, checks the filament, and climbs down. He is not keeping the lamps lit; they keep themselves lit. He is keeping them exactly themselves, the same quality of light, the same color of a sky made of unpolished tin.

There is a watcher. She sits on an iron balcony with a waxed board and a stylus. She counts the nodders, the tunbacks, the eel calls. The numbers are always the same. She writes them down anyway, because the counting is a kind of tending. If she stopped, she says, the numbers might forget themselves. No one asks what that means.

The others are more difficult to see. A foundryman tends the circular saw. A pump-house keeper checks the water in the concrete troughs. Cottage-dwellers boil the clinkers and press the mats into dark cakes. Their food comes from the same grey abundance: mat-cakes, clinker broth, the pale flesh of the blind eels. They eat sparingly, and the population of the pans never seems to diminish. They sleep in shifts, if sleep can be called that. They use the public bath, which is always warm and smells of mineral water. They do not speak much. When they do, their voices have a two-note cadence, as if the whistle-eels have taught them.

They do not seem to age or die. They do not remember arriving. If you ask them how long they have been there, they point to the older stone foundations sunk into the mud beneath the newer brick and say, “We have always been here, or the hands have.” They are not prisoners. They are not masters. They are the town’s hands, its memory, its one deliberate act of self-maintenance.

The whole of Brack is a closed loop. Warm seep gas rises; the mats consume it; the clinkers consume the mats; the eels consume the clinkers; the nodders and tunbacks consume the eels and clinkers; their waste sinks into the mud and feeds the mats. The engine removes the water that would drown the mats and returns it as condensation. The people oil the engine, scrape the walkways, mend the lamps, and watch the animals. They are another organ in the loop, not above it.

This is why the place is finished. It has settled into an equilibrium that does not change. The weather never changes. The puddles never grow. The rust has reached a stable color and thickness. The animals never multiply beyond their fixed number. The mats regrow exactly as fast as they are grazed. The same day repeats.

And yet within that fixed state, Brack is ferociously alive. The air is thick with bodies and calls. The ground is covered with movement. The water is full of blind white forms. You cannot tell where the machines end and the animals begin. You cannot tell where the animals end and the people begin.

When evening comes, the lamps glow a little brighter, and the animal calls rise as if answering. The people finish their rounds. The oiler makes his last circuit. The scraper hangs her blade. The watcher sets down her waxed board. The whistle-eels lift their heads and sing their two notes into the unchanged dark. The nodders nod. The tunbacks move in their old circuits, their shells ringing faintly.

Then the calls settle. The same day repeats. If you leave on the same road of crushed slag and brick, and look back, Brack is only a cluster of wet roofs and slow steam above a black pan. But you will know that you have not left a town. You have left a body with a thousand small voices, held in its own steady state, breathing the same breath over and over.
