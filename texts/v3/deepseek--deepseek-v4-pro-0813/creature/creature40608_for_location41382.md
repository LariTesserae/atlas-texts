You will not see them at first. Not because they hide, but because they are part of what the eye corrects. The room seems empty except for the small grazers and the moths, the slow dust and the warm walls. Then you notice that one of the wattled half-walls has a hand-shaped shadow that was not there before. The shadow flexes. A knot in the clay disc floor unties itself—not opens, but unties—and something small steps out of the pattern.

It is no taller than your lowest rib. It stands on four legs, or two, depending on what the task requires; its body is long and wraps around the wattle as if it were another strand. Its skin is the color of old linen, or maybe it wears old linen as skin: layer over layer of pressed fiber, living moss, plates of mica, all grown together. The hands are the thing you will remember. They have too many fingers, and each finger has too many joints, and each joint is tipped with a polished thorn of bone or a bead of dark resin. The fingers never stop. They move in the air as if braiding heat.

Others become visible only after you have learned the room’s quiet. One is broad and almost shapeless, a cushion of grey moss with a single long arm it uses to test the tightness of the dome. One is thin as a willow slip, its legs bound together with sinew, its arms trailing fibers that root into the floor as it walks. One has no head you can find; its face is a folding of skin where the neck should be, and its eyes are two pits of still amber that might be solidified sap. A fourth is only a long spine and many hands, moving along the ceiling like a piece of loose basketwork. If you try to count them, you lose count. They share edges. One steps behind a half-wall and emerges as two; two press together and the room has one less, or one more.

They are the Menders, the Folded, the Dry Green’s own hands. They are not a species, or not only a species. They are what the Fold does when it needs to touch itself. They are small in the way a key is small: not young, but shaped for turning something larger than themselves. Their bodies are dry and warm, like the walls. Their mouths—when visible—are not for eating words but for testing the moisture of the air. They never seem to breathe, but if you hold your own breath, you can hear them: a faint creaking, a rustle of dry membrane, the sound of a thread drawn through tough fabric. The air moves through them as if through a basket.

They do not use doors. They pass through knots in the wall that loosen as they approach, then tighten behind them, forgetting they were ever openings. They work without haste. One kneels by a wax-bulb, pressing its earless head to the warm surface, listening for the first inner crack. Another collects the mineral dust from a moth’s wings by brushing the moth as if petting it, then carries the dust on its palm to a crack in a horn panel and works it in with a fingertip. A third pinches crumbs of fired clay from the edge of a broken disc and fills a seam in the floor, then licks the seam with a long, clear-tongued mouth. The grazers climb over them as if over furniture. The many-jointed lizards pause to have their fibers tightened by a passing hand. The moths, when they are not being brushed, settle on the weavers’ shoulders and add their mineral powder to the living cloaks. Everything here grooms everything else.

Their speech is not sound. They converse by altering the room. A weaver will press its palm to the wattle and thrum a sinew strand; the message runs through the dome like a note through a spiderweb. A second weaver will answer by tapping a clay disc, or by twisting a bundle of reeds until it groans. The walls creak in response. Knots loosen or tighten. The whole Fold is their syntax. If they are angry, it does not shout; it leans inward a fraction, and the dust turns in a holding pattern. If they are content, the light through the horn panels seems to settle, the way a folded cloth settles. They speak through the walls because they are not separate enough to need voices.

What they eat is the room’s own leavings. They lick the clear resin from a cracked leaf. They chew shed fiber and old daub. They swallow the dust shed by the moths, the scraped shell, the dry pollen. Water is not a substance they understand, except as a darkening in wood, a memory of a coldness that has not been here for a long time. Their hunger is not for food but for order. They want the wall to be as tight as a held note. They want the floor to be exactly as it was, which is not the same as unchanged. It is kept, like a thought.

Some of the things you take for objects are also them. The ceramic bowl with its faint pulse is an old one, too old for movement. It has grown still and warm and full of listening. The bone needle quivering on the wall is a child, still dreaming of the shape it will take. The sleeping hollows in the wattle are not empty; in one of them a weaver is almost indistinguishable from the lichen, its body flattened into the wall, its fingers twitching as it weaves in its sleep. The difference between the made and the born has become so thin here that it is only a matter of time. Tools are cousins. Furniture is the slowest kind of animal.

They are born, when they are born, from the wall itself. In a niche that seemed empty, the wattle will thicken. Fibers will cross and recross. The daub will take on a pulse. Then a hand pushes out of the plaster, not breaking it but wearing it like a caul. The new weaver climbs out, dry as a newly opened pod, and begins to work. No one teaches it. The room is the teaching. When one of them grows too old, it does not die so much as slow. It sits against the wall and lets the moss cover its legs. The wall grows over it by degrees. Years later, if you look carefully, you can still see its shape in the wattle, a face in the fiber, one hand still holding a knot. The Fold remembers its dead not as absence but as extra thickness.

They have no names, or their names are not spoken. A name here is a pattern of knots on a particular stretch of wall, a sequence of taps. To remember someone, you touch the wall where they used to work. The wall is their memory. It is also their architecture of forgetting. There are places in the Fold where the knots are dense and dark, old as the oldest horn. The weavers do not speak of water, but they know the word as a texture, a coldness, a smoothness that is not resin. They know sky as a negative weight, something the ceiling must hold up. They know time as the slow growth of the walls, the splitting of a wax-bulb, the one loud event after which the whole room stills for days. They are patient because they are patience with fingers.

If you stay long enough, one of them will approach you. It will not look at your face. It will examine the hem of your sleeve, the seam of your shoe, the way your shadow lies against the undulant floor. With a bone needle and a thread pulled from its own forearm, it will mend any tear you did not know you had. It may offer you a warm wax-bulb and then take it back, as if deciding you are not ready. It may press a folded leaf to your lips, not to eat, but to smell: hot wax, nutmeg, crushed ants, old wood. Then it will step into the wall and become indistinguishable from it.

The inhabitants of the Fold are not its occupants. They are its ongoingness. They are the room’s breath given hands, the woven ribcage given a pulse. If you leave, the light will still be amber, the dust still turning slowly, the small hands still working in the wattle. And if you ever return, the Fold will have kept you, too, as a knot in the wall, a pattern of held breath, a mended edge where you once stood.
