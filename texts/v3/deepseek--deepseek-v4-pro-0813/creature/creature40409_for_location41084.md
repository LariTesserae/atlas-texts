The inhabitants of this place are not guests. They are the place, or the place is them—there is no clear line where black glass ends and living glass begins.

The first life is a rind. It covers every surface that can hold it: the floor plates, the walls, the lower curves of the roof. It is gray and yellow and rust-colored, never green. It is a mat of sulfur-oxidizing and metal-reducing microbes, a living skin that does not photosynthesize. It eats the difference between the hot reduced gases rising from below and the thin oxidizing air pressing down from the crack. It drinks no water; its drink is a gradient. It excretes tiny crystals of sulfur and gypsum, and those crystals fall to the floor and become the floor. The rind grows toward the vents, where the gases meet. It dies where the heat is too great or the air too thin. Its death is not rot. Its death is mineral deposition, a slow hardening into rock. It is the oldest inhabitant, but it has no age. It is the mortar between the mineral world and the animal world.

Above the rind rise the tube-dwellers. They are the most visible citizens, though they never move from where they settled. Their bodies are encased in tubes of their own secretion—some straight, some spiraled, packed so densely that they touch one another. The tubes are the color of old bone or blackened metal, but at the mouth of each tube, when the gas current is steady, there extends a crown of crimson or white filaments. These crowns are the animal’s whole face: feathery, branched, continuously waving in the sulfur-rich vapor. They are not plants, though they look like a garden of deep-sea worms. They filter the gas for sulfur compounds and microbial spores. Their crowns retract and extend over minutes, not seconds, so the wall seems to breathe in slow motion. The animal inside is little more than a soft, blind body filled with dense acid-salt fluid. It has no eyes, no limbs, no voice. It builds its tube by secreting minerals layer by layer, adding to the wall of the chamber with its own body. When it dies, the tube remains, and a new tube-dweller may settle on the rim. The colony grows upward and outward, a city of occupied and abandoned shells.

Between the tubes crawls a crust of flattened arthropods. They are pale and blind, their backs shiny as black mirrors, polished by constant contact with glass. They are no bigger than a thumbnail, but there are so many that they form a moving crust over the rock. They graze on the microbial rind, scraping it with hidden mouths, leaving behind a trail of tiny sulfur crystals. They move with the patience of a glacier; a single step may take a minute. They do not flee from the tube-dwellers’ crowns, because the crowns do not threaten them. They are the cattle of this place, but there is no herder. They are the first consumers, turning microbial film into animal flesh. Their bodies are filled with the same acid-salt fluids, so they do not need water; they need only the rind and the heat. When a spark lights the chamber, their backs reflect the blue-white flash, and the floor seems to be paved with moving mirrors.

The long centipede-like things are the hunters. They are black glass segmented, each segment a polished plate, and they move only when the gas pulse changes. When the vent drops pitch and the floor plates clack, they ripple through the tubes with surprising speed. They thread between the tube-dwellers, over the grazing crust, their legs lifting and falling in a wave. They hunt the grazers, seizing them with sharp jaws that can crack mineral casings. They also take the crowns of the tube-dwellers, nipping off a filament or two before the crown retracts. They are not fast by surface standards, but they are fast for this place. They are the only creatures that seem to have a sense of purpose, though it is not purpose—it is a response to the rhythm of the vent. They are the movement of the machine, the sudden ripple in a still world.

Near the static discharge points, where the air smells of ozone, cluster the wingless moths. Their bodies are dusted with white mineral powder, so they look like bits of ash or snow that have settled on the rock. They are not moths in any true sense, but they have a moth’s softness, a moth’s waiting. They feed on the electrical gradient itself, or on the reactive molecules the sparks create—ozone, nitrous oxides, radicals that exist only for an instant. They arrange themselves along the invisible lines of charge, their antennae held out like dowsing rods. When a blue-white spark snaps, the whole cluster flutters without wings, a ripple of pale bodies, as if the light itself has weight. They are the only inhabitants that seem to live at the speed of the spark, though they are not fast; they are merely close to the only fast thing in the room.

The air is thick with spores, sulfur droplets, and fine crystals. The spores are not pollen from flowers; they are heat-resistant cells, some with mineral coats, drifting in gas currents. They settle on warm walls, on the rind, on the backs of grazers. If the chemistry and the gas pulse are right, they germinate. The air is also full of the cast-off skins of grazers, the dust of spark moths, the microscopic crystals excreted by everything. To breathe here is to inhale life and geology at once. The gas vent’s roar carries these particles in a continuous slow storm, a dry blizzard of living and nonliving matter.

The whole community is shaped by the gas pulse. Every minute or two, the pitch drops and the floor plates clack against one another, like a great slow engine shifting gears. The tube-dwellers retract their crowns. The grazers pause. The centipedes ripple. The spark moths scatter slightly. Then the roar returns, and the crowns extend again, the grazers resume their grazing, the centipedes settle into stillness. This is the only clock the place knows. It is not a day or a season; it is the heartbeat of the vent. The inhabitants do not count it, but their bodies are tuned to it. They are born, they feed, they reproduce, they die—all in the intervals of the pulse.

The creatures are not separate from the machine; they are moving parts. Their waste minerals and body heat feed back into the chemistry. The sulfur they excrete becomes the crystals that line the walls. The gypsum they leave behind becomes the floor. The heat of their bodies, though tiny, adds to the heat of the chamber. The rind consumes the gases they stir. The predators eat the grazers, and their waste feeds the rind. The tube-dwellers filter the gas, and their casings become new substrate for the rind and new homes for the grazers. There is no outside. There is no waste. The place is a closed loop, a factory without a manufacturer. Its machinery is geology and evolution, its workers are blind and patient.

Death is not an ending here; it is a change of state. When a grazer dies, its mirror shell is overgrown by the rind. When a tube-dweller dies, its soft body dissolves into acid-salt fluid, and its tube remains as part of the wall. The fluid may be absorbed by the rind, or it may crystallize into gypsum. The centipede’s segments, when they fall, become little black plates that the grazers crawl over. The spark moths’ mineral powder burns away in the sparks, then settles again. The dead are not separate from the living; they are the ground. The places where the dead accumulate become new rock, new floor, new wall. The chamber grows by accretion, not erosion. It is still being born, and the dead are part of that birth.

If you ask who lives here, the answer is not a list. It is the crowd. It is the continuous, slow conversation between geology and metabolism. There are no individuals that matter, no faces, no names. The inhabitants have no eyes, no voices; they know each other by chemistry and charge, by the shape of a mineral edge, by the temperature of a shadow. They are all blind, all deaf to anything but the pulse. They are the place, and the place is them. The only resident is the chamber itself, a body with a vent for a heart, gas for blood, and a skin of black glass. It breathes in sulfur and breathes out mineral. It is a small, dark, crowded, roaring, hot, dry, high, unborn world—and it is alive.
