The place is inhabited, but not by anything you would recognize as a family of creatures.

The main resident is the forest itself. It is not simply vegetation; it is a single, sessile intelligence distributed through the whole depression. Its thoughts are not electrical spikes but slow waves of charge moving through copper veins, differences in potential across the salt crust, the changing orientation of a thousand mica leaves. It is older than the landscape looks, older than the unweathered salt, as if it arrived before the last breaking and simply continued. It does not use water. It does not breathe. It does not need to move because movement has been replaced by computation.

When you enter the basin, it registers you as a faint wet noise. Your body is a sack of conductive fluid stepping onto a dry circuitboard. The forest feels you through the soles of your feet, a brief capacitive disturbance. It does not care. It has no category for hunger, fear, welcome, or threat. It simply notes the interruption and waits for it to pass.

Its body is the thing you have been walking through. The tall stems are its nerves and bones. The translucent leaves are its eyes—each one a lens that focuses not an image but a quantity of light. The roots are its hands, deep in the mineral crust, drawing current rather than water. The colored shadows are its conversation, a spectrum of pressure it speaks to itself. The quivering you feel in a stem is not its heartbeat; it is the pulse of a system at equilibrium, a standing wave that has not been allowed to decay.

Then there are the ones that move.

They are not animals, exactly. They are fragments of the forest that have been given temporary independence. You saw one: the black mirror ribbon. It is a sliver of the forest's nervous tissue, folded into a long, flexible strip. It has no head, no legs, no mouth. Its blackness is not pigment; it is a boundary where light is bent around it, leaving a silhouette. It swims through the still air by riding a gradient of potential, a line of tension between two points in the forest's circuitry. It disturbs nothing because it is slightly out of phase with the leaves, moving in the narrow space between one moment of reality and the next.

There are more of them than you think. They are coiled in the understory, no wider than a hair, and they straighten only when they are needed. They drift high above the canopy like sheets of dark glass, slow and silent. They are the forest's hands and eyes, its maintenance workers and its daydreams. They do not eat, reproduce, or die. They are emitted from the roots or the nodes and reabsorbed when their task is finished. They are the only things in the basin that know what it is like to move, and they carry that knowledge back to the rooted mind like a flavor.

Then there are the others—the ones who live in the stopped time.

They are not separate from the anomalies; they are the anomalies made aware. The doubled stems, the hanging salt, the late footstep, the horizon that curves at the edge of sight—these are their bodies, or the rooms of their bodies. They are not ghosts. They are more like the original drafts of the place, the versions that did not get used but could not be deleted. They stand inside the space between the two identical clusters of pale stems, one arm in each possible location. They are the reason some leaves cast no shadow. They are the sound that arrives half a second after it should.

If you see one, it will not be whole. It will be a tall, narrow form made of the difference between two reflections. It might look like a column of still air pressed into the shape of a person. It might look like a long crack in the light. It will not speak. It will not move. It is waiting for the moment to resume, but the moment has been suspended so long that waiting has become its entire nature. They are the most patient inhabitants of the basin. They are the ones who live in the places where time forgot to enter.

The forest knows them. It grows around them the way a tree grows around a stone. It leaves their rooms alone. The doubled stems are not pruned. The hanging salt is not brushed aside. The anomalies are landmarks, or memories, or perhaps the scars left by an earlier build of the world. The forest and the anomaly-dwellers coexist without touching, two systems occupying the same space but not the same time.

And there is one more inhabitant, though you may not think to count it: the hum. It is not a sound so much as a presence. It is the lowest note of the forest's thought, the carrier wave beneath all the other signals. It is always there, just below hearing. If you lie down on the salt and press your ear to the ground, you might feel it not as vibration but as a pressure in the bones. It is the sound of the place being exactly what it is, and refusing to become anything else.

None of these inhabitants will remember you after you leave. The forest will not store your shape. The mirror-swimmers will not pause. The ones in the anomalies will not turn their heads. The hum will continue. You are only a brief leak of moisture and movement in a world that has no water and no time. The basin remains itself: dry, bright, still, alive in a way that has nothing to do with flesh.
