There is one who lives there.

You will not see it unless you have already stopped looking for a person. It is not a person. It is not the bird that calls once from the high trees, though the bird may know it. It is not the little black salamander that crosses the wet stone from pool to pool, though the salamander may be its smallest, most distant cousin. The one who lives there has been in the basin long enough that the place has shaped itself around its body, and its body has shaped itself around the place.

If you sit very still, with the water ticking and the hollow cups spilling their low notes, you may begin to notice that one of the shadows under the old tree roots is not a shadow. It is a body, folded into a long hollow in the stone, half-submerged in a pool of black tannin water. The pool is warm, warmer than the air, and a thin steam rises from it in the early greyness. The shadow breathes. One breath may take a minute, then another, and with each exhale a pale thread of mist lifts from the surface and tangles among the ferns.

Its body is long and low, perhaps nine feet from the blunt snout to the end of its tail, but so folded and still that it seems smaller. The skin is the color of wet basalt: black, but not pure black, streaked with mineral green and the rust-brown of old iron. It is not scaled. It is smooth and glandular, glistening with the same film of water that beads on the cliffs. Over its back and the outer curves of its flanks grows a dense mat of moss, not as a covering but as a part of its skin, rooted in the deep wrinkles of the hide. Small ferns have taken hold there, and a single pale orchid, waxy and impossibly patient, blooms from the ridge of its spine. When the creature breathes, the moss lifts and settles, and the orchid trembles.

Its limbs are long and folded. The forelimbs end in four soft, splayed toes, webbed at the base, with pads that grip the wet stone without leaving a mark. The hind limbs are heavier, built for slow surges through the pools as well as for pulling itself over the terraced rock. Its tail is flattened, a broad ribbon of dark leather, and when it moves from one pool to another, that tail drags behind, leaving a dark, glistening line that fades as the water closes over it.

Its head is broad and low, the mouth wide and soft. The eyes are set high and far apart, round and dark, with no white in them. They do not blink often. When they do, the lid slides sideways, and the eye becomes a wet pebble. There is no fear in that gaze, and no curiosity. There is only the same slow attention the grove gives to a falling drop.

It drinks by pressing its mouth to the surface of a pool and drawing the water in through the skin of its throat. The water level drops by a finger’s width. Sometimes it will lie at the edge of a pool for an hour, not drinking, simply letting the water move across its hands. The little black salamander may cross its wrist as if it were a stone, and the old one does not stir.

It is not a hunter. It feeds on what the water brings: the algae in the tannin pools, the waterlogged moss, the larvae of small insects drifting in the runnels, a snail on the wet stone. It does not need much. Its body is slow and cool, and the warmth of the stone keeps it through the night.

At long intervals, at dusk or just before the mist thickens, it makes a sound. It does not call from its mouth alone. It rises from its hollow, water pouring from its sides, and walks to one of the nearly closed stone cups, the ones that give out the low, resonant notes. It presses its throat to the stone lip and hums. The hollow cup takes the hum and gives it back deeper, richer, and the note rolls out across the basin and falls over the lip into the clouds. The bird’s single question may be an answer, or it may be only the bird. No one can say.

There is only one. It has no mate, no young, no rival. The little black salamanders may be of its line, but if so they have become small and brief while the old one stayed large and slow. It does not seem to mind being alone. Its mind is not like ours. It is made of drips and centuries. It knows the shape of every pool, the taste of every seep, the exact moment each stone cup will fill and spill. It does not think in words; it thinks in the slow accretion of mineral, the patience of moss, the weight of water.

It has not built anything, planted anything, or carved anything. But the grove has the feel of being arranged because the old one has been here so long that its body has become part of the stone. The hollow where it rests has been worn smooth by its weight. The channels in the rock are the paths it has used for centuries, and its tail has polished them. The moss on its back seeds the moss on the cliffs. The mist from its breath feeds the orchids. It is not a caretaker; it is a cause.

At night, when the light has failed and the fog rises in thin columns from the warm ground, you may see it move. It rises from the hollow, water streaming from its flanks, moss hanging from its back like a sodden cloak. It walks slowly, deliberately, a shadow across the wet stone, and it lowers its head to the largest of the stone cups. It drinks. Then it lifts its head and looks out over the open side, toward the green abyss below, and the mist rises around it until it is only a darker shape in the grey, a piece of the mountain that has learned to breathe.

Then it returns to its hollow under the root, and the water sounds close over it, and the grove seems empty again. But it is not empty. It is occupied by the oldest resident, the one who has been there so long that it no longer needs a name.
