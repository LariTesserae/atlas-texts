The one who lives here does not have a name, because names require someone else to say them. But if you forced the place to answer, if you held a shell to the water and listened until your ears bled, the sound might resolve into something like **Kern**.

Kern is not an animal. Kern is what happened when the Churn finally got around to inventing one.

He is made mostly of the same things the valley is made of, which is why he is so hard to see. His body is a long, low, segmented thing, like a root that decided to walk. He has too many limbs, but no two are the same. One is a black iron strut, scavenged from one of the drowned pump-heads, and the muscles that move it are braided ribbons of sulfur-yellow weed. Another is a pale, jointed thing that might be bone or might be ceramic insulator, and it clicks when he moves. His skin is not skin. It is a membrane of the same scar-tissue root mat that covers the valley floor, growing over a frame of bent rail and basalt. Where the membrane is thin, you can see light moving through him, the dull orange of a distant gas flare, the green-brown of the water. He smolders faintly at the seams.

His head—if it is a head—is a knot of gas bladders and coiled fronds, set low between his shoulders. He has no eyes, but he does not need them. He sees with his whole surface, the way a plant sees. The water tells him where the heat is, where the current is breaking, where the roots are pulling. He feels the iron singing in the transformer station from half a mile away. He knows the sound of every cracked flange, every failing seal, and he knows when one of them is about to go because he hears it break *before* it breaks, the same way you do. The sound comes to him as a pressure, a vibration in the root-mat of his chest, and he reads it the way you would read a map.

He lives in the boiler room. Not the one you are thinking of. It is a hollow in the root mat, under a shelf of black basalt, where a submerged steam pipe splits open and pours a constant column of hot water into a natural basin. The water there is almost too hot to touch, but Kern does not mind. He was born in that heat. The plants around his den grow faster than anywhere else in the valley, and he eats them the way you would eat bread: slowly, with a kind of absent devotion. He pulls the copper-black fronds down with one long limb, splits them open with the iron claws of another, and presses the soft inner pulp to the place where his face should be. The pulp steams. He absorbs it. The fibers become part of his skin.

He is not the only thing that lives here. But he is the only one that moves with purpose.

There are others, or there will be others. Kern has felt them stirring in the deep silt, in the warm muck under the drowned rail line. They are not yet finished. They are shapes held together by a single idea—an impulse, a twitch, a knot of heat-resistant weed trying to remember how to stand. Kern sometimes stops at the edge of the drowned channel and listens to them trying to breathe. He does not help them. He is not sure what helping would look like. But he leaves the remains of his food near their nests, the tough outer rinds and the cold iron scales he sheds. If they are going to become something, they will need material.

His days have no shape. The light does not change enough to call it day. He moves through the basin in a long, slow circuit, following the heat. He climbs the raw basalt ridges, his limbs finding holds in the unweathered cracks, and he sits for a while at the top, where the steam is thickest and the gas flares draw their orange columns against the murk. Up there, the rain does not fall so much as hang, and he can feel it settling on his back, warm and slightly thick, running down the folds of his root-skin. He opens the gas bladders on his head and lets them fill. For a moment, he is almost buoyant. Then the ridge trembles—a pump-head cycling somewhere in the deep—and he climbs back down.

He talks to the machines. Not in words. But he will rest a hand on the humming iron of the transformer station until the humming changes, until it matches something in his chest. He will lean against the gantry crane, his weight shifting with the vines, until the old hook twitches on its cable. The machines here are not dead. They are more like sleepwalkers, or like animals that have forgotten they are animals. Kern knows this. Sometimes he imagines the machines will recognize him as one of their own, will open some hidden hatch and let him sleep inside, where it is warm and dry and the sound is a lullaby instead of a pressure. They never do. But he keeps touching them.

There is one place he does not go. Near the center of the drowned channel, where the water is deepest and the narrow-gauge rail line disappears into the murk, there is a hole. Not a sinkhole, not a vent. A hole in the water itself, where the rain stops and the steam bends away. Kern has seen it once. He was following a new heat source, a bright line of warmth running through the root mat, and it led him to the edge of that hole. The water there was cold. That was the first thing. The water was cold, and it did not move. And when he looked into the hole, he saw a shape that was not him and not a machine and not one of the half-formed things in the silt. It was a shape of pure black, moving the way a throat moves, and it looked back at him. Kern turned and walked away. He has not gone back. But sometimes, when the sound drops into that one terrible second of silence, he can feel the hole still looking for him, the way a tongue feels for a missing tooth.

Mostly, though, he is not lonely. Loneliness requires the idea of another. Kern has no idea of another. He has only the valley, which is always making itself, and the machines, which are always humming, and the warmth, which is always somewhere. He eats. He sleeps half-submerged in the boiler basin, his limbs folded under him, his gas bladders rising and falling with the breathing of the root mat. He dreams in pressures and temperatures, in the groan of metal and the uncurling of fronds. When a frond brushes his back in his sleep, he does not flinch. He simply grows a little more of himself over the place where it touched.

The Churn is not a place for visitors. But if you stay long enough, if you learn the rules and unlearn the others, you might see him. You will not see him clearly. He will be a shape at the edge of the steam, a movement among the black kelp, a silence in the noise. He will be looking at you the way you look at a mirror that does not reflect your face. And then he will be gone, back into the heat and the sound, carrying his strange body through a landscape that is still deciding what it is.

He is what lives here. He is what the place made before it made animals. He is what you would become, if you stayed, if the roots wove themselves into your answering breath, if the water forgot to be wet and held you long enough, if the sound made a home inside your chest and refused to leave.

He is Kern. And he is home.
