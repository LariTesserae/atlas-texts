If you ask who lives here, the first true answer is that the city is already inhabited—not by people, not by anything with a face, but by a living film that is older than the walls and also is the walls. The mats are the city’s real residents. They lie over the stone like a skin of wet ash, olive-brown, sometimes greenish-black, sometimes rust-red at the edges of the water. Put a hand near them and they do not move. But they are not dead; they are so alive that they are building the place while you watch, too slowly to see.

The mats are not one organism. They are thousands of kinds of tiny lives stacked and tangled in a layer no thicker than a coin: filaments, chains, clusters, single cells. Some use the pale high-altitude sun to fix carbon. Some eat those. Some eat what the eaters leave. Some live off the chemistry of the water itself, drawing energy from iron or sulfur or the slight electric difference between water and stone. Together they are a city within the city, and their collective effect is to pull dissolved rock from the water and lay it down as new stone. They build the rims where the water spills over. They build the walls that hold the pools. They build the domes around the vents. They are not intending to build; they are only breathing, eating, dividing, dying. But their breathing changes the water. The water drops its mineral load. The mineral becomes architecture. The mats grow upward to stay above their own new stone, and the city grows upward with them.

If you look at the different parts of the city, you are seeing different kinds of mats, different kinds of chemistry, different moods of the same process. In the fast, sheetlike overflows, the film is dark and smooth, almost slick, and the stone under it is hard and dense. In the still pools, the film can be greenish and tufted, forming small soft clumps that waver in the water. In the splash zone above the waterline, where the mist rises and condenses, the film is gray and crusty, growing only when the air is wet enough to keep it damp. There are places where the mats have been buried by their own mineral production and have died, leaving only a faint dark band inside the stone. That band is a fossil of one of the city’s oldest residents. The city keeps its dead inside itself, layered like pages.

Next to the mats, the animals are few and secretive. But they are there. In the deeper pools, where the water is less agitated and the mineral load has partly settled, the silt stirs if you watch long enough. That is the blind amphipod. It is small, pale, curved, its body segmented like a tiny shrimp made of glass. It has no eyes because the bottom of the pool is always dark, not with night but with depth and milky water. It feeds by scraping the mats and by sifting the fine organic dust that falls through the water. It does not hurry. When it moves, it kicks up a small cloud of silt, and then it settles again into the dimness. The amphipods are not numerous, but they are constant. In some pools, a single square meter of bottom may hold a dozen of them, all living in the slow pulse of the pool’s fill and drain.

On the wet moss and the shaded rims, springtails move. They are tiny, gray or blue-black, sometimes no bigger than a grain of sand. They are named for the little forked spring under their bodies that lets them jump many times their own length. They feed on the film of algae and fungus and decaying matter that gathers where the moss holds moisture. They are silent. Even when a hundred of them jump at once, they make no sound. They are so light that they can stand on the surface of a pool without breaking it, and when they jump they seem to disappear into the mist. They are the city’s smallest visible animals, and they are everywhere, if you know where to look.

There are even smaller lives. In the water film that clings to the stone, rotifers spin their circle of cilia, pulling tiny particles into their bodies. Tardigrades, those slow, eight-legged bears, move through the wet moss and the lichen, curling into dry, almost indestructible barrels when the water retreats. Nematodes wind through the mineral floc. Ciliates swim in the warmer edges of the pools. None of these have names in any human language. They are the city’s plankton, the invisible dust of life that fills every drop and every pore.

The only animal you are likely to see directly, the only one with a face and eyes, is the bird. It is small, slate-colored, no larger than a hand. Its body is dense and compact, and its feathers are oiled so the water beads and runs off. It lives in the fast overflow channels where the water pours over the rims in thin sheets, and it does not wade like other birds. It walks underwater. It lowers its head, opens its wings slightly, and steps down into the current, gripping the mineral crust with its fine dark toes. Underwater it moves with a kind of deliberate, bobbing patience, prying at the biofilm and picking out the amphipods with quick, neat stabs. Its eyes are protected by a pale third eyelid, so it can see in the mineral cloud. It does not sing. Its only call is a soft, wet tick, the same sound the water makes when a drop falls from a high rim. It calls rarely, and when it does, the silence closes back over it almost immediately.

The bird nests under the overhanging lips of travertine, in cavities where the drip is slow enough to keep the eggs dry. It builds a small cup of moss and lichen, lined with its own down. The nest is almost invisible against the stone, just a dark seam in a shadow. The bird is the top of the food chain here. It has no predators, and it moves without hurry, dipping into one channel and then another, sometimes standing on a stone in the middle of the current with water streaming around its legs, looking at nothing. It is the only resident that seems to look back at you, if only for a second, before it dives.

The vegetation is sparse, but it is a kind of resident too. Black-green moss grows in the cracks of older walls, where the water is reliable but the mineral deposition is not too fast. It forms dense cushions, as thick as wet felt, and in the morning it is beaded with condensation. A few pale thin grasses grow in the same seams, never taller than a knee, their blades like threads of green glass. Lichens, gray and rust and pale green, crust onto the stone so tightly they look like paint. They grow so slowly that a patch the size of a hand may be older than a human lifetime. These plants do not claim much space. They are the city’s green seam, the patient edge, and they hold water and heat and shelter for the springtails and the tardigrades.

The rest of what happens here is not alive, but it is the medium of life. The springs bring water up from the ground, already heavy with dissolved rock and gas. The water moves through the pools, losing carbon dioxide to the air, dropping its mineral load as new stone. The air is so wet that breathing feels like drinking. The rain falls, the fog rises, the freeze-thaw cycles crack the rims and make new places for water to run. These forces are not residents, but they are the city’s breath and pulse. The living things here are shaped by them, and the city is shaped by the living things. It is a loop: water, mat, stone, air, water.

So if you ask who lives here, the answer is not one creature. It is a layered, slow community, most of it too small to see, all of it moving at a pace that does not match human attention. The mats build the terraces. The grazers eat the mats. The bird eats the grazers. The moss holds the water. The ice cracks the stone. The water carries the dissolved rock. The city lives in the way a reef lives: not because any one part knows the whole, but because every part is doing what it does, and the whole emerges.

And yet the place is not empty. It is full of lives that do not leave, do not build separate homes, do not mark territory. The mats are both the inhabitants and the apartments. The amphipods are the tenants. The bird is the only one that moves from room to room. The city itself is not a ruin and not a monument. It is a metabolism. It is being lived in and built at the same time. If you stood there long enough, you would realize that you were not standing in a city that was full of life. You were standing inside a living thing, made of many smaller living things, all of them unaware of the whole, all of them essential to it.
