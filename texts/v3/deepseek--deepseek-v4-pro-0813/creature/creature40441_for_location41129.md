The only heartbeat is the pump—until you have been there long enough to hear the second one.

It belongs to a woman, or something like a woman, or something that used to be a woman and has since been adopted by the Sump Exchange. She is not easy to see at first. You will look straight at her and see only a wet coat hanging on a root, a bundle of old canvas beside the engine, a shadow in the three-walled gauge shed. Then the shadow shifts, not quickly but the way water moves, and you understand that it was never a shadow.

She is low and narrow, with shoulders that have grown into the shape of the doorway she uses. Her coat is cut from a heavy machine cover, patched with bark-thread and copper wire. Moss grows in the seams, and tiny round leaves open along the sleeves. The coat is always wet, and the water beads on it the way it does on old gauge glass. Her hair is long and hangs in rooty strings; the indoor rain runs through it and drips from the ends. Her face is not young or old. It has the gray-gold calm of an afternoon that has lasted for years. One of her eyes is pale as a gauge, and the other is the color of strong tea. Her mouth stays closed, not in a hard line, but the way a pipe stays closed: because nothing needs to be said.

She leaves no tracks. Her bare feet are broad and pale, and they do not crush the moss; they read the root floor and pour themselves into it. After each step the water wells up and covers the little depression. By the time she has reached the engine, the floor behind her looks untouched. This is why the place seems empty to animals and trackers.

She lives in the time of the flywheel. Her breathing comes once every three seconds, between the chuffs, not competing with the engine but answering it. She moves through the day in a slow loop: from the moss-cushioned chair in the shed to the bank of gauges, from the gauges to the hot-bulb engine, from the engine to the intake screen at the sump, from the sump to the fern growing from the brass tap. She drinks from the fern, bending her head and letting the drip fall into her mouth. Sometimes she lies down in the sump and floats among the water fleas, her coat making a dark shape under the tea-colored surface. She does not swim. She lets the water hold her slightly, then pulls herself out and returns to the chair.

She tends the Sump Exchange, not as a master but as a third hand. She clears a leaf from the pipe with one finger and puts the leaf back into the water to rot. She rests her palm against the throttle linkage when the root swells, not to change anything but to feel the pressure. She presses a wet bit of moss into a leak until the hiss becomes less like a snake and more like rain. She licks her thumb and wipes the face of a pressure gauge, then stands back and watches the needle. The chair faces the gauges because she set it that way, long ago, and because she sits there when the machine is running well, waiting for the one needle that might begin to lie.

At night she sleeps in the shed, not in the chair but next to it, on a root mat where a warm pipe runs underneath. She curls around the heat. The condensation runs over her while she sleeps, and her breath falls into the engine’s rhythm. If the egret comes, she opens her eyes, watches it pick along the catwalk, and closes them again. Neither one makes a sound.

She might have been a mechanic once. She might have come to kill the engine or to mend it, and then forgot which she meant to do. Or the Sump Exchange might have made her from the things it had too much of—heat, moss, rust, water, patience—because even a closed system sometimes grows a witness. There is no way to know. She has been there long enough that the roots know her by weight, and the engine runs differently if she is not near it, though it would never stall.

She is not the owner. She is not a ghost. She is what lives there: a pair of hands that do not take, a pair of ears that do not tire, a body that has learned to breathe at the machine’s pace. If you stayed from one flywheel stroke to the next, you would see her. If you stayed a season, you would no longer be able to tell her from the place.
