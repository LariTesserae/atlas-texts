The one who lives here, if you need a face for the place, is an old black caiman in the deepest of the oxbow pools.

But you would not see it at first. It has learned, in the way an animal’s body learns over decades, to become terrain. Its back rises out of the water in a long, low ridge that could be a half-submerged log. Its snout rests on a root or a pad of wet clay, and the snout is the color of the clay. The scutes along its spine are dark and rough, and between them the skin is packed with silt, algae greening in the seams like moss on stone. A few pale leeches cling behind its hind legs. Its tail lies half-buried in leaf mold at the pool’s edge, still as a fallen branch. Only the eyes refuse to disappear entirely: two wet brown slits just above the waterline, holding the blue flame of the gas seep as a tiny steady reflection.

It is old. Not ancient in the way of the river, but old in the way of an animal that has watched the oxbow fill and stop flowing, watched the termite chimneys rise and soften and rise again, watched the canopy close and open in small deaths. It may have been here when the pool was still a bend in the river, when water moved through instead of merely standing. Now it lies in water that hardly moves at all, and it moves less than the water.

The caiman is not a serpent. It is a low, armored thing, broad across the belly, with a jaw built for waiting. Its teeth are yellowed and worn, some broken, some hidden by the lip. It does not chase much. It lets the pool bring things to it: small fish, frogs, a young bird fallen from the branches, the occasional catfish nosing through the dark. When it hunts, it does not thrash. It slides forward with the water, so slowly that the disturbance is no more than a change in the angle of the duckweed, and then the jaw closes with a sound like a thick branch snapping under mud. Afterward it returns to the same shelf, settles, and becomes a log again.

It breathes through raised nostrils, two dark commas that break the surface without a ripple. Sometimes it exhales and the air goes out in a low, wet hiss, almost the same pitch as the gas flame at the pool’s edge. The flame, small and blue and hand-sized, is the brightest thing in the understory. The caiman seems to share the pool with it without fear, the way an old tenant shares a room with a pilot light. At night the flame reflects in the water, and the caiman’s eye reflects the flame, and for a moment there are three blue lights in the black pool: the fire, its reflection, and the eye of the animal.

But the caiman is only the largest tenant of one pool. The place itself is not owned by any single creature.

What lives here is a layered, crowded occupancy. The leafcutter ants are a dark moving muscle through the litter, each one carrying a cut sail of green. The termites are the great quiet engineers, raising their lumpy chimneys from the mud, turning dead wood into heat, gas, and soil. The frogs are scattered through the dark like small wet pulses, one calling, then another, separated by long intervals. The bats move between the trees with high clicks. The electric fish in the shallows send out their weak pulses, so that the water near the bank can feel faintly charged, as if the pool itself were alive with a low, secret current. The fungi glow in dead wood with a cold green light no stronger than a memory of light.

If you ask who lives here, the truest answer is not one animal. The true inhabitant is the system itself: the rot and the roots, the ants and the caiman, the gas and the water and the endless slow digestion of leaves. The forest does not have a single occupant. It is occupied at every scale, in every surface, down to the film on a still pool.

But if you want a keeper of this particular oxbow, a resident with a face and a breath and a long body shaped by the dark—then it is the caiman. It has no name. It does not need one. It lies in the deepest water, near the small blue flame, and it waits, and it is not waiting for anything but the next slow thing the pool brings. It lives here. It has become part of the place, and the place has become part of it.
