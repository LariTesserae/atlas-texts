The one who lives in the Cask is not so much a person as a place within the place. You hear her before you see her: a wet, patient tread on the floating boardwalk, a pause between the pump’s pounding that is not the pump’s own, a soft click in the back of her throat that echoes the cave crickets. She moves through the warm mist like a current, and the lamp-light, when it pulses, catches her in fragments: a bare foot, greenish with moss, an arm slick with condensation, a fall of hair that is not hair but rootlet and web. She is the keeper of the root’s voice.

Her skin is the color of pale bark that has never seen daylight, and it is never dry. The air of the Cask is so thick with sap-vapor and steam that she glistens even in the dim orange glow. Her face is lined, but not with age as the surface knows it; the lines are the same striations as the root’s inner bark, as if the same pressure that creases the walls has creased her. Her eyes are large and dark, the pupils expanded to take in the blue-green luminescence of sap-nodules; in the brief surge of electric light, they contract to slits, like a cave salamander’s. Her ears are tuned to frequencies that the other inhabitants have long stopped noticing: the low moan of the root as sap pressure shifts, the hiss of a steam vent about to gout, the faint scratch of a root-crawler losing its grip on a pipe. She hears the Choir not as noise but as speech.

She is called the Moss, because she seems to grow over the town as slowly and permanently as the root itself. No one remembers her being born. The oldest pump-tender says she was there when he arrived, standing on the high-water mark with one hand pressed flat to the bark, and she had the same look then as she does now: not young, not old, but saturated. She wears a coat of sump-pig hide, cured to a soft grey, patched with pieces of moth wing and brass from old ladders. The pockets are sewn with root-fiber thread and hold the tools of her trade: a copper blade no longer than a finger, a bundle of dried guano briquettes, a stoppered vial of sap-thinner that smells of pears. She walks barefoot on the boardwalks, and the wood knows her weight; it does not creak, it only breathes.

Her work is the work of listening. The root is alive and under pressure, and the Cask is its ventricle. It swells and contracts with the seep-cycle, and the Moss can feel the change in her own body before the water rises. She wakes an hour before seep-hour, not by clock but by a tightening in her chest, and she makes her way down to the lower walkways to open the drainage channels. She wades into the warm sump up to her knees, the blind fish flicking away from her calves, and she clears the flutes by touch, her fingers finding the smooth grooves that generations of her kind have worn. She does not speak, but she hums, a low note that matches the root’s moan, and the wall answers. The other inhabitants steady themselves when they hear her hum; it means the pressure is beginning to rise.

She has a symbiosis with the root that is not supernatural but chosen. In her left forearm, just below the elbow, a small rootlet has entered the skin and grown along the bone. It was an accident, they say; she fell against the wall once and a fine root-hair pierced her. But she did not pull it out. Instead, she let it stay, feeding it with her blood and her stillness, and it grew, not as a wound but as a graft. Now it is as thick as a vein, and it pulses with sap not blood. Through it, she can taste the root’s chemistry: sugar-heavy at high seep, alcohol-rich after a guano burn, thick with resin before a storm. The rootwrights come to her when they must tap a new vessel, because she can tell them which one will bleed gently and which will gush. She touches the wall with her palm, closes her eyes, and hums until the root hums back. Then she nods, or she shakes her head, and that is the law.

The animals are her congregation. The cave crickets cluster along the walls, their black bodies a living carpet, but when she passes they part like water, clearing a path for her bare feet. She raises crickets in mesh pens for their silk and their protein, and she can tell the temperature of the chamber by the pitch of their trilling. The bats that roost in the highest root-ribs know her scent; they sweep down at dusk to brush her shoulders with their leather wings, and she catches a few in her hands to inspect their guano-clogged fur. The sump-pigs wallow in the organic film, their gill-necked heads raised when she approaches, and she feeds them scraps of root-scrap and listens to their grunts for the health of the water. The root-crawlers, small and sticky-toed, cling to her coat and clean the sap residue from its seams; in return, she protects them from the boots of the careless. She is not their master; she is their neighbor, and they all share the same walls.

She does not sleep in a house, not because she cannot but because the root is her house. Her bed is a hammock woven from root-fiber and bat-gut, slung between two major ribs in the upper gallery. The bats roost so densely around her that she is warmed by their bodies, and their guano falls like fine rain onto the walkways below. She sleeps in snatches, never more than an hour, because the pump house at the south end has a rhythm that she must match. When the sump outflow surges and the bulbs brighten, she is already awake, already moving, because the surge came through the root and the rootlet in her arm told her before the gauges moved. At night, the Cask is never dark; the sap-nodules glow blue-green, the moth wings scatter the orange lamp-light, and the eyes of the blind fish reflect the flicker like coins. She lies in her hammock and listens to the choir, and the choir is not a noise to her but a lullaby.

She eats what the Cask provides. Her diet is simple: roasted cave crickets, a broth of sump-pig marrow and moss, a cake of pressed guano and root-syrup that tastes of earth and sweetness. She drinks the sap-thinner diluted with warm seep-water, and it keeps her mind clear and her body warm. She never eats the root; that is forbidden to all but the rootwrights in time of famine, and she would rather chew her own arm. She bathes in the communal seep, but not often; the moisture of the air is enough to keep her clean, and the root-crawlers remove the dead skin from her shoulders as she sleeps. Her hair, if it can be called hair, is a tangle of root-thread, moss, and bat-fur, and it grows in the same direction as the condensation drips. She combs it with a piece of old ladder rung, and the combings are fed to the silk-farm.

Her voice is rarely used, and when it comes, it is a creak, like a door made of root. She speaks to the children in the school niche, not to teach but to tell them the names of the sounds: the puff is a valve, the rattle is a conveyor, the moan is the choir. She tells them that the root is not a tree but a body, and the Cask is its heart, and we are the blood. The children listen, wide-eyed, because they have heard her humming and felt the walls swell. She has no children of her own, but the whole town is her family. The rootwrights are her siblings, the pump-tenders her cousins, the sump-pigs her livestock and kin. She is the one who lives in the Cask, and the Cask lives in her.

At low seep, when the water falls and the high-water mark shows pale, she stands at the bottom of the ladder and looks up through the throat of the sinkhole. A single column of light, milky and thin, reaches down from the thorn-plain above. She does not climb it. She has no need. The world above is dry and harsh, full of wind and silence. She belongs here, in the warm dark, with the pumping, the dripping, the trilling, the shrieking, the moaning. She belongs to the root, and the root belongs to her, and the animals are the breath between them. She is not the only inhabitant, but she is the one who has become the place, and the place has become her.
