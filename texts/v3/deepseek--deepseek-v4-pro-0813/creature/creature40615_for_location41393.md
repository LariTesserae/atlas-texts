You do not see them at first. The basin is too busy, too loud, too full of steam and movement. Then a shape detaches from a pale wall, and you realize it was not a shadow but a person. Another rises from a pool’s edge, hands white with calcite. A third hangs from the underside of a travertine ledge, feet braced on slick stone. They are people, or something close enough to people, and they have been there as long as the noise.

They are small and pale, their skin the color of wet tufa, their hair fine and colorless or shaved away. Their bodies are broad in the shoulder and long in the arm, with fingers that end not in soft tips but in horny, spatulate nails from a lifetime of scraping. Their feet are wide, toes spread to grip the wet mineral. Their eyes are dark and deeply set, pupils wide in the permanent steam-light; they blink slowly, as if time does not press on them. They wear little—narrow aprons of fish-skin or bat-leather, belts of braided sinew, sometimes caps of birdhide—because the air is always warm and wet. Their skin is creased with fine white lines, not age but mineral, settled into the folds of knuckles and elbows. They smell of sulfur, guano, and warm animal fat.

They do not speak as we understand speaking. The roar would swallow any sound not made close. So they have two languages. At distance, they use their hands, their eyebrows, the tilt of the head: a grammar of fingers that can carry precise meaning across a hundred meters of steam. Up close, they speak in low, breathy voices, mouth near the listener’s ear or pressed to the throat, the words felt as much as heard. Their spoken language is made of low vowels and soft stops, sounds that travel through bone rather than air. A child’s first name is a shape made by the fingers; its spoken name is a vibration hummed against the chest. They have no word for silence. The closest sign is “the moment before the pool overflows,” a thing that never quite happens.

Their houses are not separate from the land. They are cut into fresh stone, built onto ledges, stacked four or five deep. The rooms are small, low, thick-walled. Inside, the roar changes quality, becomes a hum transmitted through the floor. They sleep in these hum-rooms, curled on mats of woven sinew and bat fur, their hands curled against the stone. They do not expect quiet. A house without vibration is not a house; it is a tomb. They keep small clay lamps burning animal fat, but the light is less for seeing than for company. The flame makes a small, warm sound and a point of color in the dim.

Their work is never finished because the stone is never finished. Every morning, a fresh crust of calcite has formed on the doorsteps, the tools, the water channels, the bones. They scrape it away with blades of shell, copper, or volcanic glass. The scraping is not resented; it is the rhythm of the day. The sound of a thousand scrapers joins the roar. They cut fresh tufa from the growing terraces while it is still soft, shape it into blocks with chisels, and set it into walls that will harden over days. They cut channels to direct the warm water, but the channels themselves grow new stone and must be re-cut. They maintain the pools as we might maintain gardens, though there are no plants. They tend the bacterial mats, scraping them in patterns that encourage the shrimp and snails; they harvest the pale shrimp with baskets woven from sinew and fish bone; they gather snails by the thousands; they net the blind fish in the deeper warm pools; they take eggs from the ledges where the ash-gray birds nest; they hunt the bats in the caves with small clubs and nets. They cook in the hottest springs, lowering net bags of meat and shell into water near boiling. They dry snail flesh on warm stone. They render fat for lamps and fuel. Nothing is wasted. The guano is scraped from the ledges and dried to burn in the lamps and hearths; the bones are cracked for marrow, then used as tools or beads. The shells are ground for mortar or strung for decoration. Even the sulfurous mud is used to seal joints.

They are not farmers or herders; they are tenders of a wild abundance that depends on their attention. If they stopped scraping, the stone would grow over the pools and smother the shrimp; if they stopped hunting the birds, the birds would strip the insects and starve. The equilibrium is maintained by small, constant acts. Each household is tied to a particular spring, a particular ledge, a particular stretch of channel. They inherit these obligations as they inherit the bones of their dead.

The dead are not put away. When a person dies, the body is placed in a warm, mineral-rich pool where the small blind fish and the shrimp do their work. After some days, the bones are taken out, scraped clean of the new stone that has already begun to form on them, and placed in niches in the walls of the family’s rooms. They are not hidden; they are part of the architecture. The skulls look out from the tufa with dark eye sockets, the long bones laid in patterns. Every day, the living scrape the ancestors’ bones, keeping them from being lost to the stone. To let an ancestor crust over is the worst failure of care; it means the stone has taken back what the family once borrowed. The dead are the true owners of the house. The living are their hands, their scrapers, their voices in the roar. A marriage or an adoption is not complete until the newcomer has scraped the bones of the new kin.

Children are born into the noise. They do not cry much; perhaps because the world is already so loud that crying is pointless, or perhaps because they learn too early that sound is not an alarm but a medium. They are carried against the chest, where they feel the mother’s voice as a buzz through the ribs. Before they can speak, they make signs with their fat fingers: “up,” “warm,” “more,” “stone.” Their first tool is a piece of snail shell for scraping. By the time they can walk, they can navigate the wet terraces; by the time they can speak, they have already learned to listen through the roar—to pick out the sound of one pool changing, one bird’s alarm, one channel clogging. They are taught never to shout; shouting is for the birds, and it means nothing. To be heard, you go close.

The people do not see themselves as separate from the other animals. They are the slowest, longest-lived, most skillful of the basin’s inhabitants. They share the ledges with the birds, the caves with the bats, the pools with the fish. They have clan-signs drawn from these animals: the Blind Fish, the Snail, the Swift, the Frog, the Bat. These are not totems in the sense of worship; they are more like shared adjectives. A person of the Swift clan is quick and nervy; a person of the Blind Fish clan is patient and pale. The animals are not pets or livestock; they are neighbors. The people mimic the birds’ shrieks to call them down; they know the bats’ click-trains well enough to walk among them without startling; they can read the frogs’ silence as a warning. The noise is not a nuisance; it is the community’s breath. When a person is alone, they still feel the roar against the skin like pressure. Solitude is not silence but the absence of other bodies.

Their beliefs are not elaborate. They do not have gods in the way of high clear lands. They have a single, simple understanding: the world is a body, and they are inside it. The stone is its flesh, the water its blood, the steam its breath, the noise its voice. The body is always growing, always being born, but it does not age. They are the small living things in the body’s warm folds, no different in kind from the bacteria, the shrimp, the birds. When they die, their bones become part of the stone unless their kin scrape them; and if they are scraped, they remain as a kind of memory in the body’s long noise. There is no afterlife; there is only continued presence or dissolution. The worst thing is not death but silence. A person who is forgotten is not silent; they are nothing.

So they do not tell stories of heroes or battles. Their stories are about maintenance: how a channel was cut, how a pool was cleared, how a ledge was kept from falling. They are told in low voices at night, in the hum-rooms, while the hands of the listeners move in half-signs of agreement. The stories are not linear; they circle like the water, returning to the same point: this is how we keep the body from growing over us. The only drama is the occasional failure—a spring that shifts, a wall that bursts, a child who wanders too far toward the cold edge and comes back with silence in their ears. These are the myths. The heroes are those who scrape the most diligently, who hear the change in the roar first, who know where the tufa is soft enough to cut.

They do not have seasons, but they mark time by the growth of stone and the bodies of their kin. The year is measured in scrapes, in the thickening of a wall, in the return of the birds to the same ledge, in the slow accumulation of shell-beads on a string. A person’s age is not counted in years but in the number of times they have scraped the ancestor bones. An elder is someone who has worn their own thumbprint into a particular knife handle, who can read the mineral layers like a book.

At night, the settlement is not quieter; the roar merely changes shape. The birds settle, the frogs take over, the bats pour out in a warm, dense stream. The people move by the blue-green glow of the pools and the smoky orange of their lamps. They visit each other, not to talk but to be close, to share heat and food. They sleep in piles, skin to skin, so that the vibration of the water and the stone passes through the whole household. They dream, perhaps, in signs. The dark is full of movement and sound, and they move through it without fear. Fear is for the edge, where the steam thins and the cold silence begins. They do not go there unless they must trade for wood or copper, and they do not stay long.

In the end, the people are not the masters of the basin. They are one of its many living surfaces, another layer of the mineral body. They scrape, they build, they harvest, they tend, they listen. The noise roars over them and through them. If they were to vanish, the stone would grow over their houses, the pools would fill, the birds would find other ledges, and the place would still be loud. But while they are there, they are the part of the place that knows itself. They are the noise made aware of its own pattern.

They do not need a name for where they live. The place is the noise, and the noise is them. If you ask, they will make a sign that means “here,” but there is no word for it. The name is lost in the noise because the noise is the name.
