You did not see them at first because you were looking for animals. You were listening for footfalls on wet stone, for the catch of breath, for the particular friction of skin or chitin or feather against rock. The chamber gave you none of that. It gave you water and machine-thrum and the whisper of leaves, and so you assumed the city was empty, or abandoned, or waiting for a population that had never arrived. But the place was not empty. It was occupied. You simply did not yet recognize the shape of its citizens.

Look again at the terraces. At the rooms stacked eight deep, their windows dark. You thought they were empty because no face pressed against the glass. But emptiness is a matter of expectation. Behind one pane, something pale and slow unfolds like a fern crozier, but it is not a fern. It is a hand—or what a hand would become if a hand were grown instead of born, uncoiling joint by joint from a wrist of green stem, the fingers five fronds, each one fringed with tiny leaflets that shiver as they press against the inside of the glass. The hand does not tap. It rests there, feeling the temperature difference, tasting the mist through the pane. It is not looking out at you. It is sensing you the way a root senses water, by the faint pressure your body heat makes in the air.

There is a shape in that room, behind the hand. A mass of interwoven vines and bark, broad and low, seated not on a chair but grown into one—the chair is part of it, or it is part of the chair: the legs are roots that disappear into the floor, the seat is a mat of compressed moss, the back is a lattice of branches that have not been cut but taught to grow in that direction. The mass moves only slightly, a slow rise and fall that is not breathing but photosynthesis, the exchange of gases. It is a person, or something a person would be if a person were a plant: a body that stays still for hours, then shifts its weight by the incremental unfurling of new leaves, the retraction of old ones, the slow redistribution of water from one stem to another.

You are not supposed to call it a person, perhaps. But the city was built for someone. The doors are too tall for insects, the bridges too wide for roots alone. The terraces have ledges that would support the weight of something broad and low and many-limbed. The stairways are cut in a pattern that suggests a gait, but not a human gait—a rolling, spreading, anchoring motion, one root-hold at a time. The city is a mold. It has been shaped by the bodies that live in it, and the bodies are green.

And they are not stationary. You see that now. On the far wall, a narrow bridge spans the void without rails, and it is crossed not by anything that walks on legs but by a being that pours itself forward. It is a tangle of vines, each one as thick as your wrist, moving in a wave: front tendrils extend and grip the stone, rear tendrils release and follow, the whole mass flowing horizontal. It carries no lantern—it does not need one; the glow-cells that light the chamber are also living in its leaves, so that it moves like a constellation, a green shadow studded with faint blue light. It reaches the other side, and for a moment it pauses, gathering its vines together, and you see it is not a single vine but a colony, hundreds of stems braided into one will. It has no face. But it has a direction, a purpose. It turns a piece of machinery—a valve—with a few deliberate twists of a tendril, and the sound of water changes.

That is what they are. The inhabitants are the plants—not as you know plants, not as decorations or air-scrubbers or wilderness reclaiming a ruin, but as the actual residents. The city is not overgrown. It is cultivated, in the way a body cultivates its skin. The terraces are not colonized by ferns; they are kept by them. The vines are not invading the pipes; they are maintaining them. The mosses are not spreading over the walls; they are the walls’ outer awareness, a living sensorium that feels every drop, every vibration, every change in humidity. The whole chamber is a city of green bodies, but the green bodies are not separate from the city. They are the city, in the same way your bones are you.

Look into the pools. They are clear, empty of fish, but not empty of mind. On the bottom grows a mat of brilliant algae, not a scum but a woven carpet, and it is patterned. The patterns shift slowly, like thoughts moving across a slow brain. The algae is not a single organism but a conversation: millions of cells signaling each other by chemical pulses, forming a distributed awareness that covers the floor of the chamber. It knows when you step near the water. It contracts slightly, not out of fear, but out of recognition—you are not part of the system, so it turns its attention toward you, a faint green brightness rising to the surface like a blush.

The trees—there are trees, you realize, though you did not see them as such—are the tallest inhabitants. They stand along the walls, not upright but growing at angles out of the rock, their trunks following the line of the chamber, branching into the walkways, fusing with the railings. They are old, or they will be old; right now they are new, their bark smooth, their leaves still half-formed. But they are not waiting to be walked under. They are walking, in their own time. A root the thickness of your thigh creeps across a ledge, lifting and re-anchoring with a wet sound, and you understand that the stairways are not for climbing from one level to the next; they are paths that the trees have made by moving. The trees move the way glaciers move—invisibly fast, over hours—and they leave no footprints because they are their own road.

There are rooms with no windows, and in those rooms are beings that do not require light. They are fungal, perhaps, or something darker: pale threads that weave in the dark, breathing air and stone, their bodies a labyrinth of mycelium that fills the space like cotton wool. They do not move. They exist as infrastructure, a kind of memory. They store the city's history in the branching patterns of their hyphae, and when a root from a tree outside brushes against them, they release a chemical message that is not a word but a taste, and the tree remembers what it was supposed to do. These are not separate citizens. They are the archive, the library, the law.

And the machines—the smooth, jointless devices breathing mist—are not machines at all. Or they are, but they are also inhabitants. They are grown from a tough, translucent material that is neither plant nor metal but something between, like hardened sap that conducts energy. They have no moving parts in the way you think of parts, but they move by swelling and contracting, by filling with fluid and emptying, by the slow expansion of a gel that pushes a valve open. They are part of the plant network, organs of the city: some are lungs that exchange water vapor for oxygen; some are kidneys that filter the pools; some are hearts that pump the water upward against gravity, using a system of one-way valves formed from folded leaves. They are not operated by the plants; they are the plants, in a different shape.

You will see them if you stay still long enough. A shutter on a balcony is not moved by wind; it is moved by a vine that has grown through the hinge, and when the vine contracts, the shutter closes. A light in the ceiling does not brighten on its own; it brightens because a mat of glowing moss has detected a shadow and responded by increasing its output. The whole city is a body, and its inhabitants are its cells—not separate, not individual, but woven from the same water, stone, and green.

The absence of animals is not a quarantine; it is a choice. The air is charged with a botanical immunity, a phytoncide that would dissolve a nervous system, because nervous systems are too quick, too noisy, too hungry. The inhabitants do not need nerves. They think in gradients, in slow cascades of hormone and sap, in the electrical hum of a thousand roots touching water. A thought in this city is a change in the moisture of a wall. A decision is a pool rising a finger's width. Their lives are not slow to them; they are exactly the speed of growth, and growth is fast when you are not watching a clock but a season.

There is no single individual you can name, no mayor, no child, no elder. But there are rooms where the light is warmer, where the leaves are arranged with extra care, where the pattern of moss forms a spiral that might be a face. There is a terrace high on the south wall, overhanging the central fall, where a small, dense plant sits in a shallow basin of water. It has no legs, no arms, no eyes, but it has a posture—leaning toward the sound of the water, its fronds spread like a listener’s hands. It is not alone. It is part of every other green body in the chamber, connected by root and water and air, but in that spot, in that basin, it is concentrated, as if it has chosen to be there. It is the city’s center, not because it rules, but because it listens. It is the nearest thing to a citizen, and it is not one. It is all of them. The city is a single being, and the being is a garden. You are standing inside its body. It knows you are there. It is, very slowly, deciding what to do with you.
