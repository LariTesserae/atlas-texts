The one who lives there is easiest to miss. For a long time you will see only the room’s smaller tenants: the blind shrimp moving through the warm pool, the paper-shelled snails, the frogs on their glossy leaves, the bats packed high in the fissure. The place feels occupied by many lives, but not by a resident. Then, if you sit still enough, the room begins to arrange itself around a centre—not a spirit, not a ghost, but a long, pale, slow-bodied creature that has become almost inseparable from the stone and water.

It lies in the travertine basin at the deepest part of the pool, sometimes just under the surface, sometimes on the wet mud at the edge. From above it looks like a water-worn root or a fallen column of pale rock. Its body is long, roughly the length of a tall human, but built low and sinuous. The tail is heavy and slightly flattened at the sides, good for holding steady when floodwater pulses through the root basins. Four legs set wide on the body, each ending in a long hand or foot. The hind feet are webbed, the forehands are not. Its fingers are slender but strong, five on each hand, with pads that grip wet stone and a thumb set slightly lower than a human’s, closer to the wrist. The skin is the colour of the ghost-pale plants near the spring—milk-white, wax-white, almost translucent—with darker rose-grey where the blood moves close to the surface. Along the spine and the backs of its hands there are patches of rust-red pigment, the same iron that stains the limestone. In blue foxfire light it looks like a living piece of the mineral itself.

Its head is broad and flat, with a blunt snout and a wide mouth that turns slightly upward. Its eyes are sealed under skin, useful only to register the difference between the noon blade of light and the green-brown dark. It sees the world instead through heat and vibration. Two deep pits above its mouth sip the warm air. It can feel the footfall of a cave cricket through stone. It can taste the presence of frog, bat, fish, or intruder with a slow flick of a pale forked tongue. When it slips into the water, a fringe of red gills opens along each side of its neck—frilled and branching, like the respiratory organs of a larval salamander—but on land those gills close into soft ridges that look like old scars.

It moves with terrible economy. No wasted motion, no hurry. It walks as if underwater even when it is on the mud, each foot placed as though the earth were fragile. It hunts by stillness. It will choose a place where the warm spring water spills over a travertine rim, lower one open hand into the current, and wait. The blind shrimp drift into the cup of its fingers. The pale fish come close to the warmth of its body and are taken. It eats the thin-shelled snails, grazes the algae film, and during the rainy pulses it will eat frog spawn without stripping the pool clean. It seems to know how many lives the room can hold. When it has eaten, it returns to the deepest basin and sits in the hot water with only the top of its head and the line of its back exposed, breathing as slowly as the room itself.

It is a keeper, not in the way of a human custodian, but in the way a body keeps its own organs. It tends the low stone wall when a heavy rain unsettles a block, pressing it back into place with its chest and forelimbs. It clears the spring’s outflow when leaves and bat guano begin to clog the terraces. It drags fallen branches into the damp hollows where bracket fungi fruit, because the fungi feed the insects, and the insects feed the room. It does not garden, but the chamber is slightly more ordered because of it—more itself.

The handprints are its doing, though it does not make them often. There is a seam of soft red ochre near the north wall where the mineral mixes with clay and condensed water. When the old prints have faded too far—perhaps once in a generation, perhaps less—the resident scrapes a small amount of ochre into the stone bowl in the niche. It wets the pigment with warm water from the pool and mixes it with a smooth pebble. Then it presses its left hand against the wall beside the older marks. The print is not exactly human. The palm is narrower, the fingers longer and more equal in length, and the thumb is set low, but the gesture is unmistakable: an open hand, placed deliberately, as if to say *I am still here.* Afterwards it returns to the pool and lets the warm water loosen the ochre from its skin.

It seems to remember. It visits the old prints sometimes, touching them with wet fingers, not to add or alter them but to feel the slight roughness of the ochre under its pads. The oldest prints are so worn they are only shadows of rust on the stone. The resident may be the last of its kind, or it may simply be one of those things that lives so long that a wall of handprints becomes a family tree. It does not speak. It has no name that the room uses. But in the moment between breaths—when the mist hangs motionless and the pool turns to dark glass—it sits in the shallows and becomes the stillest thing in the chamber. Its sealed eyes face the reflected foxfire. Its gills lie flat. The frogs stop calling, the bats settle, even the drip-water seems to hold. In that pause it sometimes makes a sound: not a voice, not a word, but a low vibration, like a stone struck underwater, or the note a root makes when it first begins to split stone. The pool carries the sound into the walls, and for a moment the whole room seems to be listening to its own deepest resident. Then a drop falls. The water rings once. The breathing begins again, and the pale creature sinks back into the warm dark, going about its slow, necessary work.

It is not lonely. It is not human. It is the thing the sunken room has kept alive in its warm mineral heart: blind, patient, red-handed, and older than the wall it still repairs. It lives there because the room and the creature have grown around each other, like stone around a root, like a handprint around a hand.
