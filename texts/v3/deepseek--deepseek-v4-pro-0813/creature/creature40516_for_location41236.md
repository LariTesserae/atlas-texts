They are not the first thing you notice. The first thing is the heat, then the wet, then the green, then the low machinery. Only after all that do you see them: a figure on the upper gallery, not moving, watching your hands.

They are not large. Generations in the narrow rift have made them compact, narrow-shouldered, with the careful economy of people who have learned to turn sideways through a world of pipes. Their skin is pale, but not a cold pale—the warm pale of a root kept too long in the dark, or of old paper steeped in weak tea. In the amber lamplight they look like figures in a painting that has been left too near the damp; their edges are soft. Their hair is cut close, singed at the ends so no strand can catch in a belt or flywheel. The old ones let their hair grow long and fine, the color of the blue-green fungus panels, and they wear it loose, because they no longer work the lower galleries.

Their eyes are their most obvious adaptation. They are wide, slow-blinking, with irises that seem to drink the light and give back whatever color is offered: copper, moss-green, fungal blue. They do not meet your gaze for long. In this place, a direct stare is a kind of shout. They look at your hands, or at the hollow of your throat, or at the space just to the left of your ear. This is not shyness; it is courtesy. The eyes are for seeing, not for signaling.

They dress in cloth woven from the same tough leaf fiber that feeds them. It is never quite dry. They wrap their feet in layers of it and bind them with copper wire, leaving the toes free to grip the grating. Some wear gloves with the fingers cut away; the fingertips are always bare, because the first language of this place is heat and texture. A Tender can read a pipe’s mood by laying two fingers against its lagging, and that requires skin.

They have no single name for themselves. If pressed, they might say “the ones who stay,” or “the keepers of the Breath,” but usually they say nothing. Names are for things that need to be distinguished from other things. They are not separate from the place in that way. They call each other by work-names: Sumpstone, Coil-whisper, Loom-shuttle, Root-hand, Pre-echo, Two-Drips. A child is not named until it has seen its first heat-moth alone. Until then, it is called “Small,” or “New,” or “the one who cries.”

They number fewer than two hundred. No one is sure of the exact count, because the count changes in small ways—a birth, a death, a child who goes up to the surface and does not return, an old one who goes down to the root crypt and becomes part of the light. They do not keep a census. They keep a ledger, yes, but it is a ledger of water and moths and geckos and root growth, not of people. People are not counted; they are known.

Their work is the work of attention. The pump-tenders live at the bottom, near the sump and the steam engine. They are the widest and slowest, with voices like the low hum of the dynamo. They know the exact moment a valve will open by the way the floor vibrates a half-drip before. The splicers are the surgeons. They carry copper needles and sinew thread and a curved blade of volcanic glass. When a root enters a junction box, a splicer does not cut it. They open the box, speak to the root, and braid it into the cable. The worst insult in the rift is to say of someone, “He cut what should have been braided.”

The loom-workers sit for long hours, their backs curved, humming at a frequency that does not disturb the threads. The gardeners are the only ones who sing, but their songs are low and wordless, and they sing to the vines because the vines grow toward the sound. A gardener never pulls fruit. They wait until the stem yields with a twist no firmer than a handshake.

Children are rare—three or four in a generation. They learn to walk on the grating before they learn to speak. Their first task is to carry a cup of water from the upper cistern to the sleeping gallery without spilling and without hurrying. If they hurry, the water spills, and the task is repeated. The lesson is not about obedience; it is about water. Patience is a form of water. They are taught to touch the walls only with the backs of their hands, and only after testing the air for wetness. A child who sees a gecko on a particular pipe will sit and watch for an hour, then report the sighting with the exactness of a surveyor: “On the seventh pipe above the loom gallery, near the valve with the copper collar, at the time when the mist was thinning.” The gecko is then entered into the ledger, and the child may choose a name for it—something like Soot, or Once-warm, or Little Valve-eye.

Their relationships are not loud. They greet by touching the backs of their hands, because the palms are tuned to heat and must remain sensitive. They say, “The mist is thinning,” or “The walls are sweating,” instead of hello. They do not say goodbye. They say, “May your roots find water,” or “I will see you when the leaves are dry.” Conflict is rare and slow. When two people disagree, they sit at opposite ends of a gallery and tell the story of the disagreement from the beginning, without interruption, until the story becomes so long and slow that the disagreement seems small inside it. The others do not take sides. They bring water and leave it between the two, because an argument must not be resolved by thirst.

They have no priests, but they have a deep attention that resembles reverence. They believe the rift is a body—not metaphorically, but actually. The mist is its breath; the sump is its bladder; the fissure glow is its low fever. They are not its masters. They are its microbiome, or its hands. They do not ask why they are here. Asking why is a kind of waste, like shouting in the lower gallery. Instead they ask, “What does the place need next?”

Their technology is not dead to them. The dynamo is called the Old Woman, and the pump-tenders speak to her before opening her casing. The flywheels are said to have moods. A machine that runs smoothly is “content.” A machine that hisses too often is “restless.” They do not oil a bearing; they “give it a drink.” They know the old brass plaques are writing, and some can read them, but the meanings have become less important than the sounds. A young person learning the dynamo must recite the sequence of its sixty-three bearings, not as a spell but as a map.

They have no day and night in the surface sense. They sleep in short pulses, like the machines, so that the settlement is never fully unconscious. Someone is always awake, listening. The condensation cycle is their true clock: mist, drip, dry, repeat, over about three days. They call one full cycle “a Breath.” They do not count years. A person is not seventy years old here; they are “old enough to have seen the Clock stop twice,” or “so young they have not yet seen a heat-moth alone.” Age is measured in attention, not time.

They eat after the mist-tide, when the leaves are too wet to touch and the air is heavy. The meal is a stew of leaf-pith, fungus, and the occasional blind cricket, which is a delicacy. They eat slowly, with their hands. The loudest sound is the scrape of a clay spoon. They drink the sweet upper drip, never the sump water. Water is never wasted. A visitor who pours out a cup of water has committed an unforgivable act. They do not say “forgiveness” easily.

When someone dies, the body is carried to the root crypt behind the sump, where the fungus panels grow thickest. It is laid on a bed of wet moss. Within a few cycles, the body feeds the fungus. The panels glow brighter for a time. The Tenders say the dead are learning to shine. The family plants a cutting from the dead person’s favorite plant in the upper garden. Each death is a new planting. The garden grows.

The geckos are the most honored of the non-human residents. They are the color of old ivory, with bulbous eyes that do not blink. They move only when the temperature changes, so they seem to teleport. Each gecko has a territory of one warm pipe. They are counted but never owned. To see a gecko in a new place is a communal event, whispered through the galleries like a prayer. The blind crickets are the voice of the leaf litter. Their stridulation is a low, dry ticking, like tiny gears. The loom-workers time their shuttles to it. If the crickets stop, everyone pauses, because the air has changed. The heat-moths come from the fissure, pale as ash, and circle the upper gallery once before dying. Each one is tallied. They say a moth is a question the heat asks.

The root-cables are the slowest residents. They grow through the rock and into the conduits, and after enough time, the plant and the cable become a single being—a braid. A splicer can spend a full cycle listening to one, feeling its growth with two fingers. They call these braids “the thinking roots.” They do not know if the roots think, but they behave as if they might.

The Clock is the oldest resident. It is the turbine that turns in the fissure’s updraft, too slow to mark any hour. It has worn its own bearing into a kind of life. When it stops, even for a moment, the whole settlement knows, and a hush falls. They do not say it is broken. They say it is thinking.

If you stay long enough, you will stop seeing them as inhabitants. They are the soft-footed, low-voiced, damp-skinned hands of the rift. They do not live there; they are lived by it. The place breathes, and they are the breath’s shape. When you leave, you will carry the hush in your ears. You will walk too slowly for the surface, and the dry air will feel like a scold.
