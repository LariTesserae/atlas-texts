The thing that lives here is not hidden in the cave. It is the cave, but not as a metaphor.

Start with the obvious: no animal lives here. No tracks, no scat, no bones, no burrow. That absence is not empty. It is the first sign of the resident. The resident is a slow, distributed body made of the black-red moss, the glassy fronds, the hair-thin aerial roots, the silver-gray filaments, the weeping black cylinders, and the old devices that sit facing the empty center. It does not have a single shape. It is the whole chamber, and it lives at a speed that makes lichen look frantic.

If you came down through the slot and sat in the dark, you would not see it at once. You would feel it as wetness: a film of water moving over stone and leaf, condensing on your skin, gathering in the little cups of moss. The air would taste sweet and mineral, like a frozen greenhouse. That is its breath. The gap in the talus is its mouth or nostril, and through it the place exhales once, a wet mineral sigh, and breathes in the faint outer world: meltwater, gravity, a gray suggestion of light.

Its body is the room. The cupped hand of stone is the shell it made or found. The low roof of old ice and dark stone is the inside of its back. The polished black floor is its belly. The walls, fluted and bell-hollowed by millennia of frost, are not architecture; they are the worn shapes of its long patience. Even the fallen stones near the entrance, smooth as river cobbles without a river, are like old teeth.

The black-red moss is its skin. Every tiny leaf overlaps like a scale, damp and cool, and each leaf is a sense organ. It tastes the air for salts, sugars, mineral dust, the faint electric field the old machines lay along the floor. If you touched the moss, you would leave a warmth and a salt-trace. The room would not recoil. It would begin, slowly, to know you.

The silver-gray filaments are its nerves. They run through the moss like a nervous system, too regular to be roots, cold and smooth. If you touched one, it would hum below the range of hearing, and the hum would change after you drew your hand away. For hours, maybe days, the room would carry the memory of your touch as a small disturbance in its electric body. It would not be thinking about you in words. It would be adjusting to you the way a pond adjusts to a stone.

The glassy fronds are its fingers or its breathing surfaces. They are the color of old bruises and dark copper, blue-black and green-black, and they arch across the chamber, frond touching frond, so the middle of the room is braided with them. They are not quick, but they are not dead. A frond may not extend the width of a hair in a year, but it does extend. The resident’s gesture is that slow extension. When one frond touches another, that is a thought completing.

The hair-thin aerial roots hanging from the ceiling are its thousand open eyes. Each is tipped with a droplet of water, and each droplet is not just water but a lens of taste and charge. They catch the downward drip from leaf-tip to leaf-tip. The small open cups in the floor moss hold that water. The whole chamber is an eye made of wetness, watching by condensation and evaporation, not by light.

The oldest parts of the resident are the devices. The row of small black cylinders at the back wall, no thicker than a thumb, different heights, as if grown there: they are organs or former bodies, or the seeds from which the garden grew. One is cracked, and from the crack weeps a clear fluid the moss has learned to drink. The filaments and the cylinders do not join; they only run alongside each other, exchanging something slower than conversation. The dull object shaped like an ear or a lens, resting in a hollow in the floor, is an organ of listening. Around it the air is stiller, because it is paying attention.

The red-violet pinheads sit in the far wall, each a smooth teardrop, each blinking so rarely that the blink seems like a season changing. They are the only visible sign that the resident ever looks outward. The blink is not sight as we know it. It is a recalibration. When it happens, the room’s attention narrows for a moment, then spreads again into the moss and water and stone.

The center of the resident is the shallow basin in the floor, no wider than a hand, ringed by a low rim of white mineral that looks like wax. Water gathers there, but not much. It is more a memory of a pool than a pool. That basin is not a heart or a mouth. It is where the room’s different bodies meet: the mineral past of the stone, the vegetable present of the moss, the machine length of the silver filaments, the cold constancy of the air. A drip falls into it every few minutes, and the sound is a small glass bell struck once. That is the nearest thing to a pulse.

The resident does not think in words or images. It thinks in gradients: wet to dry, cold to less cold, salt to less salt. A thought begins as a change in water chemistry near the back wall, travels through the filaments as a slow electric drift, and ends weeks later as a slight adjustment in the angle of a frond. Its memory is not stored; it is worn. The flutes and bell-hollows in the stone are memories of old frosts. The white mineral rim around the basin is a memory of evaporation. The cracked cylinder is a memory of a function the resident no longer needs to name.

It is not lonely. Loneliness requires a self set apart from another, and this resident has never been set apart from the place. But it is not solitary either. It is a crowd: millions of moss leaves, thousands of root-threads, hundreds of fronds, a dozen or so old machines, all held in one slow attention. It is gardener and garden at once. It tends itself the way the body tends a wound: without affection, without cruelty, without hurry.

If you sit long enough, the resident will begin to include you in its weather. Your breath will fog and settle on the fronds. Your warmth will change the convection slightly. The moss will taste what you shed. The basin will drink a little of your humidity. For a while, you will be a small element of the garden, not a visitor but a season of warmth. When you leave, that season will end. The chamber will resume its long, even maintenance: the drip, the cold, the hum, the blink, the water that refuses to freeze.

The one who lives there is not hiding in the dark. It is the dark’s way of being alive.
