The first lives are not human. They are the microbial mats—the rust-red, sulfur-yellow, teal, milk-white films that coat the wet rock and eat the heat. They are the oldest residents, older than the terraces themselves, older than the pipes and pools. The place would be dead without them. They make the rock slick, give it color, and feed everything else.

Then the animals. The scalebacks graze the mats in slow, pale herds, their silica shells like fitted plates, their undersides soft as wet brushes. The ribbon-fish move through the hot canals like strips of white cloth that have learned to drift. The steam skimmers ride the thermals overhead, broad and translucent, casting shadows like hands. The needle-herons stand at the blue pool edges, still as surveying rods, beaks thin as needles. And the sulfur ticks gather at the fumaroles in dense orange clusters, armored in the very mineral they breathe.

Then, only then, the people.

They are not easy to notice at first. They stand so still beside the separation tanks and along the pipe galleries that the eye passes over them, as if they are part of the machinery or the stone. They wear loose clothes the color of sinter and salt: cream, rust, pale grey, the green of copper, the white of old mineral. The cloth is never dry. It clings at the elbows and knees, and salt crusts along the seams. Their skin is damp and pinked from the constant steam, and many of them have fine white lines of salt in the creases of their hands, around their mouths, at their hairline. They have the permanent squint of people who live in bright haze.

They move carefully. They do not run. A running child can crack a terrace rim, can send a pulse through the wet rock that scatters the scalebacks and confuses the ribbon-fish. So the children learn to walk by learning to listen through the soles of their feet. They learn the signs before they learn to speak: small hand shapes for *hot*, *overflow*, *fish moving*, *dam cracking*, *tank full*, *go slow*. Spoken words are few and quiet. The town has a rule: do not shout. Loud sound is dangerous here. It travels as vibration through the stone, and the animals feel it before anyone sees it. A dropped hammer can make the scalebacks scatter. A sharp cry can send a mineral dam into failure. So the people hum low to each other, or click their tongues, or simply sign.

Their work is water work. Some tend the upper springs, where the brine is hottest and the air is thick enough to drink. They wear stiffened suits and move only in short shifts, pulling the mineral crust from wellheads, replacing valves that have become stone. Others run the separation tanks, reading the layers of brine and salt the way a farmer reads soil. Others walk the evaporation ponds, raking white and pink salt into windrows, guiding the slow mechanical arms that turn almost without sound. Others mend the boardwalks and the pipe galleries, because the mineral is always trying to swallow them. A pipe left too long becomes a ridge. A boardwalk left too long becomes a wall. Maintenance here is not repair; it is resistance against solidification.

There are not many people. The settlement is sparse, stretched across the drier lobes of sinter, its buildings far apart and connected by raised boardwalks. Some of the people are seasonal workers, come for the harvest of lithium and boron and rare salts. Some have been there so long their skin has a mineral sheen and their boots have grown thick white soles of deposited silica. They do not talk much about where they came from. It does not seem to matter. What matters is whether the terraces are stable, whether the ponds are filling, whether the brine is rich.

The people do not own the animals. They live alongside them. They read the animals the way sailors read weather. If the scalebacks bunch and move downhill, the heat is shifting. If the needle-herons leave a pool, the fish have gone. If the steam skimmers rise high, the air is about to change. The animals are older residents, and the people treat them with the cautious respect of neighbors whose house was there first.

The fumarole workers are the strangest of the people. They are short and broad-shouldered, burned by the gas, their clothing crusted yellow. They move among the sulfur ticks without disturbing them. Some have learned to sit so still that the ticks crawl over their boots and hands, orange and slow, tasting the heat gradient. The workers do not brush them off. They say the ticks are cleaning them.

At night, which is not really night but a dimming of the white haze, the people go inside their thick-walled homes of quarried tuff, cut sinter, and ceramic tile. There is no wood. The roofs are angled to shed condensation, and small distillation lines drip fresh water into stone basins. Inside it is cooler, but still wet. They sleep in hammocks or on shelves of fitted stone. They sleep lightly, because the ground never stops moving. A deep thrum means a geyser is venting upstream. A sharp crack means a dam has shifted. Silence is not peace. Silence is the world listening.

The dead, if they stay here, are not buried. There is no soil. They are laid in quiet mineral pools where the water is hot but not too hot. Over time the water takes them and gives them back as part of the terrace. Their families visit the places where they have become stone. They touch the new sinter, warm and white, and they do not cry loudly. Loud grief could break a rim. Then they go back to the pipes.

So the place is not a town in the old sense. It is a mineral reef with people growing inside it. The people are alive, but they are also being incorporated into the landscape, salt by salt, cell by cell. They know this. They do not fight it. They keep the water moving, and they move with it, and they try not to stay still for too long—because whatever stays still long enough becomes stone.
