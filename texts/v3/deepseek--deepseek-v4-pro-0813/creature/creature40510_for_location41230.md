The one who lives there is a woman in the way the moss-covered penstock is a woman: she has a form, and a function, and the place has taken her into itself. She came down the ladder years ago wearing a pack with a set of wrenches and a wool blanket. The blanket is still there, folded into the warm root cage above the steaming soil, but it has become part felt, part moss. She did not intend to stay. The first night she slept beside the turbine to listen to the bearing knock, and when she woke, the mist had soaked her clothes until they smelled like crushed fern. After that, leaving seemed less urgent than staying.

She is small and flat-chested, with a spine that shows when she bends, and her hair is cut short with a utility knife, growing out in dark irregular tufts that the steam curls. Her hands are wrapped at the wrists with strips of rubber from an old drive belt, not for warmth but because the basalt is sharp and she has already given enough skin to the rock. The rest of her is pale, marked with mineral white around the nails and in the creases of her knuckles, as if she has been dipped in the pool up to her hands. Her boots are heavy, lace-to-the-toe, and around the left one she has wound copper wire to keep the sole from peeling away. She moves like someone who has learned the exact distance between one sharp edge and the next.

She sleeps in the hollow where the tree roots lifted from the hot ground, beneath the penstock. It is not a bed, only a depression in the warm moss and black earth, lined with the remains of a canvas tarp. The roots make a roof low enough that she has to crawl in sideways. The heat comes up through the soil, and so she sleeps without cover, smelling the mineral steam and the hot oil from the pipe. She does not dream often; when she does, the dreams are of small round mouths of water opening and closing in the pool, and the mechanical whine instead of a voice.

Her days are not divided by hours but by the pitch of the water. She rises when the light turns gray-gold and the midges begin their slow vertical column over the pool. She drinks from an enamel cup she keeps hooked on the penstock bracket, catching water from the bypass line. The water leaves a white rim on the cup and a sweet, bitter, sulfurous taste on her tongue. She eats little—a handful of pale tubers she digs from the wet margins, the inner bark of a wind-trained tree, once in a long while a few frog eggs from the bromeliad cups. She has become used to the feeling of being warm and hollow at the same time.

Then she walks her circuit. She touches the penstock first, resting her palm against the black steel until she can feel the water inside it. She knows the machine by vibration more than by sight. She climbs the catwalk with her boots ringing on the metal, and she stops at the white-faced pressure gauge. She wipes its face with a rag she keeps tucked in the waistband of her trousers. The moss is not allowed there. It grows overnight in small dots, like a rash, and she removes it with a fingernail. She watches the needle flicker in its nervous arc and does not think of numbers; to her the needle is the expression of the mountain, a quick thin muscle telling her the water is working. She taps the gauge’s brass rim twice with a knuckle, then moves on.

At the turbine housing she crouches and listens. The whine is clean today, under it the dull knock of a bearing that needs replacing, but not yet. Soon is another winter, or the winter after. She has learned to know the difference between the knock of a loose bearing and the sound of a stone falling on the pipe. The pipe rings long and silver; the bearing knock is a soft wooden sound, like a knock on a door far inside the rock. She calls it the door. Sometimes when it stops, she feels a loneliness she won’t admit.

She carries a small brass drift and a stub of pencil in her jacket. With the drift she taps the turbine housing to loosen the mineral scale. With the pencil she writes nothing anymore; it has been worn down to a black nub, and the paper she once had has gone to mold. Instead she makes marks on the housing with a piece of basalt: small white scratches in a row. The rows have grown into a lattice that only she can read, a record of the days the water pressure spiked and the door knocked.

The animals in the notch treat her as part of the weather. The pale frogs do not jump when her shadow falls over them; they sit on wet leaves with their throat sacs fluttering. The wren works the moss pads near the ladder and sometimes follows her for a few steps, waiting for her to turn over a stone and expose the small pale things beneath. The black snake, when it unrolls from the warm pipe, drops into the ferns without any urgency, as if it has an understanding with her. Once she found the snake coiled in her root hollow, and she waited outside until it left. They share the warm places.

She is hard of hearing in the middle frequencies. The waterfall has taken them, or the whine has. She cannot hear her own name spoken from across the notch, but she can hear the change in the pitch of the turbine from her sleep. She knows when a new trickle has begun on the back wall by the sound it makes on the basalt. She feels sounds in her teeth and in the palms of her hands. The place is loud enough to empty out all but the edges of hearing, and she has grown accustomed to living in those edges. She does not miss birdcalls because she has forgotten what they sound like. The rush and whine and drip are like weather to her, and silence would be a kind of death.

She tends the way water tends. She does not clear the moss except where it would swallow the gauge, the valve handle, the governor. She lets green grow over the rockfalls and the pipe seams. She has learned which roots to cut and which to leave; the ones that wrap the penstock she leaves because they have become part of the pipe’s support. She believes the place is one body made of rock, water, metal, and moss, and that she is only the part of it that moves with hands.

Sometimes she climbs the ladder to the rim, not all the way out, and sits in the slot where the two shear planes meet. She watches the cloud run through the gap. The thin black wire goes up past her and disappears. She has never seen anyone at the other end. At first she thought it was a telephone line, or a resistor, or a way for someone to know if the turbine stopped. Now she thinks of it as the notch’s antenna, a single hair growing into the sky. She sometimes touches it, and it is as warm as a living thing.

Her name is not spoken in the notch. The place does not use names. If a visitor came down the ladder—and visitors are rare, maybe one every few seasons—they would find a woman standing next to the turbine, holding a rag, her head turned slightly to one side to hear better. They might think she was the caretaker. She would not correct them. She would point to the gauge, which is always clear, and then to the door inside the rock, which is still knocking. She would tell them, in a voice that sounds like steam escaping, that everything is fine.

But she is more than the caretaker. She is the one who sleeps in the root cage and drinks from the bypass line and listens to the water change. She is the reason the moss has not covered the window of the machine. She is the soft thing the notch has allowed to stay, in the same way it allows the wren and the snake and the white-faced gauge. And if she ever leaves, the place will not mourn; it will simply grow over the places where she slept, and the moss will eventually cover the gauge face, and the turbine will knock alone until the door opens.
