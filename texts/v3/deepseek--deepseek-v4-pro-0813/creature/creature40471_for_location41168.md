They live there in layers, all of them mineral.

The first layer is the rooted growths—the sulfurfans and gypsum-blades, the spires of arsenic red. They are not plants in any watery sense. They are slower than breath and faster than geology. Each cluster begins as a condensation of hot vapor at the mouth of the fissure, a lattice of sulfur and silica that builds itself outward like frost on a window, except the frost is alive. They feed on the gas, on the heat, on the dry exhalation of the earth. Their leaves are not leaves but blades and fans and needles that would shatter if you breathed too hard. They do not tilt toward light. They lean toward the note, toward the place where the gas rises. They grow in pulses that last for centuries, each pulse exactly matched to the appetites of the things that eat them.

The second layer is the grazers.

They are the shape of beetles and trilobites, but they are made of flint and agate and obsidian and basalt. Their bodies are segmented, carapaced, fitted together with the precision of knapped stone. They are small—some the size of a thumb, some as large as a hand—and they move with a patience that makes watching them feel like watching a minute-hand or a shadow cross a floor. Their legs are rods of chalcedony. Their joints are thin films of iron oxide. They have no eyes, only small pits along their heads and shells that sense heat and vibration, so the whole room is visible to them as a slow map of warmth. They breathe through those pits, not air exactly, but the sulfurous vapor itself. The note is their weather.

They graze the mineral forest. Their mouthparts are quartz blades, chalcedony rasps, corundum grinding plates. They shear the yellow needles, scrape the soft bloom of sulfur, leave behind a polished scar that shines with an oily sheen. Then smaller, flatter grazers arrive—flat as old coins, slow as lichen—and take the fallen flakes, rasping them into fine dust that settles and becomes new floor. There is no waste. There is no overgrowth. The plants grow exactly as fast as the grazers eat, and the grazers die exactly as often as new ones coalesce.

When a grazer dies, it does not rot. It stops. Its shell stays where it was, a perfect mineral architecture, and over years the sulfur bushes grow through it. Tiny crystals line the seams between its plates. The shell becomes a seedbed. Eventually a new grazer precipitates there, a new collection of quartz and pyrite and dark stone, not born so much as accumulated. The population never rises and never falls. The territory of each one is remembered in the floor, in faint polished paths that only they can feel.

Then there is the third layer. The one who left the handprint.

It is no longer possible to see it, because it no longer keeps a body. But once it did. It was small, smaller than a human adult but not a child. Its shape was upright and long-armed, with a back that curved slightly, as if from years of leaning into low places. Its skin was dark and porous, like vesicular basalt, so the dry heat did not parch it. Its joints were not flesh but interlocking planes of obsidian that moved with a soundless, fluid patience. Its face was narrow, almost featureless, with two black flint eyes that reflected the orange and blue of the room. It had no mouth for speaking, only a hard seam that opened when it needed to taste the sulfur fume. Its hands had four fingers and a thumb. The palms were fine-grained and slightly soft, like pressed talc or dried clay, and their creases were shallow cracks. When it pressed its hand to the wall, those cracks left a perfect print in red.

It came from somewhere else—the upper cave, or deeper, or from one of the old passages that no longer open. It brought the skill of flaking obsidian. It carved the bowl with its own shed plates and a patience that did not need to mark time. It gathered the fallen sulfur and the red iron oxide from the wall and ground them together in the bowl, mixing the powder with the fine mineral oil from its own skin. Then it pressed its hand to the basalt. Not painted. Printed. A signature. A seal. A way of saying: *I am here, and I am not separate from this place.*

It built the half-circle of stones not as a fire-ring, because there was no fire, and not as a shelter against any wind that has ever blown. It built it as a shape of itself, a small boundary in a room that otherwise has no boundary except the walls. It slept there, curled with its back to the low stones and its face toward the fissure. The floor is polished from that weight, not quickly, not over years, but over the kind of time the room knows.

The tally on the wall is not a count of days. The room has no days. It may be a count of molts, because the creature that slept there did not age the way flesh ages. It shed its stone skin seven times. Then came a long interval, a long mark. Then two marks at once, perhaps the time when it split, or the time when it tried to leave and came back twice. The marks are shallow, but they have not faded. Nothing in the room fades.

It did not die in the way meat dies. It returned. Its body slowed, softened, crumbled at the edges, and over a long span it was taken up by the same mineral cycle that consumes the dead grazers. But the room kept the memory of it. The handprint holds the fine detail of its palm. The bowl holds the shape of its hunger. The blades hold the edge of its last work. The polished hollow in the stone holds the shape of its rest. And the note—the single endless note rising from the fissure—holds the sound of its staying.

So if you ask what lives here, the answer is not one thing. It is a small, closed world of mineral grazers and crystalline groves, and it is also the lingering person-shaped animal that became the room. The grazers keep the garden. The garden keeps the air. The air keeps the note. And the note keeps the handprint from disappearing, because in a place where nothing changes, even a mark can become a life.

When you leave, the grazers do not notice. The bowl stays empty. The tally stays counted. The handprint stays red. And the note sounds exactly as it has always sounded—long, dry, warm—a single held breath in the body of the mountain.
