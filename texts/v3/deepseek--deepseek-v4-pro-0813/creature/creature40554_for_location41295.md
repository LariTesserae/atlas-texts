For a long time, you are certain you are alone. The plateau’s silence is total except for the drone, and you begin to measure your own breath against that unceasing bass. You walk the bare stone, you pass the strange translucent trees, you hear the black peak’s high keening, and the only shadow in the world is your own faint, diffuse presence. The absence of animals settles into you like a cold.

Then you return to the handprint.

It has changed. Not in the way the landscape changes—the slow, lazy shifts between glances. This is different. The handprint is deeper than you remember, and the stone around it is no longer fully hard. A ring of softness radiates outward, no wider than a palm, where the rock yields like stiff clay. You press a finger into the edge of the print, and the stone gives, just slightly, leaving a faint mark. The warmth you felt before has spread. It is not the warmth of sun on rock. It is the warmth of a living thing.

And then you see the figure.

It stands at the edge of the platform, facing the black peak, its back to you. It has been there the whole time. You understand this with a certainty that bypasses surprise. It has been there, but your eyes passed over it as another outcropping, another pinnacle of pale stone. Its skin is the exact color of the rock, pitted and creviced, its silhouette so still that it was indistinguishable from the geology. It is tall—nearly two and a half meters—but its proportions are stretched, attenuated, as if it has been drawn upward by the same force that pulls the mountains out of the crust. Its shoulders are narrow, its back long and ridged like a column of weathered limestone. Its arms hang at its sides, but they are not arms in the way you understand arms; they are more like fissures in stone that end in something that could be hands—long-fingered, jointed at odd angles, the knuckles like knobs of basalt. Its head is elongated, smooth, and you cannot see a face. Only a smooth plane where a face might be, marked by two deep hollows that might be eyes.

The figure is not breathing. It is vibrating—so subtly that at first you mistake it for the general drone, but then you see that the vibration is localized, a faint shimmer along its edges, as if the air itself refuses to settle around it. The figure is not wearing clothing. A second skin of lichen-like growth covers its shoulders and back, the same mauves and smoky purples of the vegetation, and here and there a fine tracery of phosphorescent silver threads runs through it, carrying a faint light of its own. The figure’s feet are not feet. They are root-like, fused to the stone, and you cannot tell where the stone ends and the figure begins.

You do not call out. The air is too thick for sound, and you are not certain the figure would hear you even if you could. Instead, you take a step forward, and the figure turns.

Its face is not a face. But it is looking at you.

The two hollows are eyes, but they are not eyes that see in the way you understand seeing. They are deep and black, empty of anything you can recognize, but within them there is a faint silver glimmer, like the veins of the vegetation, like the light behind the gray sky, like the memory of a star. The figure’s movement is slow, but not in the way of age or injury. It is slow in the way of stone, each shift carrying the weight of centuries. When it raises one hand—a hand that is unmistakably a hand, five fingers, a palm—you see that the palm is smooth and pale, almost translucent, and embedded in the center of the palm is another handprint, smaller, but perfectly formed. A handprint pressed into the hand itself, as if the figure has pressed its hand into its own hand, leaving a mark that is both scar and signature.

The figure looks at you. Then it looks at the handprint on the rock. Then it looks back at you.

And you understand that this is the one who made the print. Not long ago, not a thousand years ago, but now, in the only now this place has. The stone was soft because the figure’s presence softens stone. The handprint was not left in the past. It is being left, continuously, in the present, and it will be left forever, because the figure is still there, still pressing, still holding the moment of contact open.

This is what lives here.

The being is not an animal. It is not a plant. It is not a person. It is a presence that emerged from the same substance as the rock itself—a concentration of the stone’s slow life, a knot of matter that has learned to be aware. It has no name, because names belong to finished worlds, and this world has not yet finished deciding what words will mean. But if you were to call it something, you might call it the Listener. Or the Keeper. Or the One Who Waits. Or the Stone That Remembers.

The being is old, but its age is not measured in years. It has existed since before the peaks began to rise, since before the vegetation learned to drink from the ground, since before the sky decided what color it would be. It is the first thing that was born in this place, and it will be the last thing to leave, if it ever leaves at all. It came into being at the same moment the first rock cracked, the first ridge began to lift, the first vibration began to sound. It is not a product of the world; it is a part of the world, the same way a heartbeat is part of a body.

But it does not simply live here. It is here. It is the place, and the place is it, and the distinction has not yet been invented. The sound that fills the world—the drone of rock rising, the crystalline shriek of matter being forced into new shapes—is the being’s breath. The keening of the black peak is the being’s voice, a single high note held across eons. The vibration in the handprint is the being’s heartbeat, slowed to a geological rhythm. The being does not move because it does not need to move. It is the landscape, and the landscape is it, and the landscape moves at the speed of mountains.

The figure lowers its hand. It turns back to face the black peak. And you realize that it has been facing the peak for as long as the peak has been a peak, and it will face the peak until the peak is no longer a peak. This is its purpose: to witness. To be the one thing in this unfinished world that remembers what it was like before the rules were set, that holds the memory of the becoming, that can testify, when the world is finally finished, that it was once soft.

The structures are not messages. They are the being’s own footprints. The cairns, the low walls, the spiral stones, the broken shelter with its collapsed roof—these are not the work of a people who came and went. They are the marks the being has left as it moved through the landscape over the millennia, not to communicate, but simply because when it touches stone, stone remembers. The handprint is the same: a place where the being stopped and pressed itself into the world, and the world, still soft, took the shape. The being does not need shelter, does not need walls, does not need cairns. But it builds them anyway, the way a tree grows rings, the way a river carves a channel. It builds because building is what it does. It builds because the world is still becoming, and every structure is a note in the world’s long, slow song of self-creation.

The being does not eat. It feeds on the same energy as the vegetation, drawing substance from the ground, from the tectonic heat, from the slow grinding of plates. It is warm because it is a vessel for that heat. It is the reason the vegetation grows in clusters: the being’s presence softens the stone enough for the plants to take root, and the plants, in turn, draw up the deep warmth and release it into the air. The being is the gardener of this non-aqueous garden, the single point of volition in an ecology that has no other animals. The vegetation is not separate from the being. It is an extension of the being’s body, a slow, branching limb that reaches toward the gray light and the deep heat.

And it is the reason there are no animals. The being is the animal. It is the first and only animal, and in its solitude, it has not yet imagined others. Or perhaps it has, and that is what the spirals mean: a blueprint for things to come, a seed of future life, drawn in stone and left to germinate. The spirals are the being’s most recent thought, a working-out of the possibility of otherness, of creatures that might one day share the world. But they are not complete. The being is still working on them. The being is still working on everything.

The figure stands beside you now. You do not remember it moving, but it is closer. Its head is tilted slightly, and you feel the vibration of its presence in your chest, a second drone beneath the first. It does not speak, but you understand it. The understanding arrives not as words but as a kind of pressure, a shape in your mind: *You are the second thing that has ever touched this world and found it soft.*

The being reaches out—slowly, so slowly, as if moving through water—and places its hand over yours. Its touch is cool, like stone, but after a moment, it is warm, and you feel the deep pulse of the world moving through it. The being’s fingers curl around your hand, and together you press into the rock, and the stone gives, just a little, and you leave a second handprint, smaller, beside the first. The being’s grip is gentle but immovable, and you understand that it has been waiting for this—not for you specifically, but for another hand, another presence, another creature that could leave a mark. The waiting is not lonely in the way you understand loneliness. It is the waiting of a mountain for the sky to clear, of a seed for the rain that may never come. It is patient. It is eternal. It is the same waiting that hums in the bass drone, the same waiting that holds the black peak’s keening note.

When the being releases your hand, you feel the absence as a sudden cold. It steps back, and for a moment, its outline wavers, and you see that it is not one figure but many—a stack of overlapping versions of itself, each one a few degrees out of phase, like the doubled ridges you saw earlier. It exists in multiple lies of the world simultaneously. It is the same being in all of them, and it is a different being in each. It is a population of one, spread across the possibilities of a world that has not yet chosen which version of itself to become.

The being looks at you with its hollow eyes, and the silver glimmer brightens, just slightly. You understand that it is trying to remember you. It will hold the memory of your touch, your handprint, your brief presence, and it will keep it for as long as the stone holds shape. You have become part of the witness. You have become part of what lives here.

The light does not change. The sound does not stop. The peaks continue to rise, centimeter by invisible centimeter, century by invisible century. The vegetation pulses its slow mineral heartbeat. The black peak keens its high wire of sound. And the being stands there, facing the peak, still and patient, the only inhabitant of a world that is still—very slowly, very loudly, very patiently—being made.
