At first the valley seems empty. Then a hatch opens in a retaining wall, or a figure steps out of a doorway no wider than a ladder, and you understand that the valley has been inhabited all along—not by strangers, but by people who have become part of the wet green machinery.

They are not ghosts and not machines, though they share qualities of both. They wear the valley the way moss wears a wall. Waxed wool, oiled canvas, rubber boots, hoods of greased felt. Their clothing is patched so often it has become a kind of sediment. Moss grows on their shoulders and hat brims. Their gloves are dark green at the knuckles. They carry small brass hammers, curved knives, coils of wire, and keys for the many gates and lockers of the terraces. They move with the slow, deliberate pace of water finding a new path.

Their faces are narrow and pale, their eyes adjusted to the gray-green light. They have the look of people who have lived under a wet leaf. They speak little, and when they do their voices are low and unhurried, with many words for the different kinds of wetness: seep, weep, run, drip, sweat, gush, sluice, hush. They do not shout. The valley muffles sound, and there is no need; the work crews use hand signals, taps on stone, and certain whistles that sound like water over a lip.

They call themselves the keep, or the crews, or simply “the ones who stay.” They are not one family, though after generations nearly everyone is some kind of cousin. They are descended from the old builders, from engineers, failed farmers, canal workers, apprentices who came up with a work gang and never found a reason to leave. The valley took them and put them to work, and they stayed because the work is never done, and because after a while the outside world seems too dry, too bright, too quiet.

Their lives are organized by terraces. The Moss Terrace grows and harvests thick mats of moss for padding, insulation, and pressed blocks. The Wheel Terrace holds the great turbines in their stone halls. The Rope Terrace grows fiber plants and rets them in long wet troughs. The Hanging Terrace receives the cableway from the lower valley, winching goods down through the mist. The Root Terrace is a series of sunken glass beds for roots, herbs, and tubers. And then there are the unnamed places: Leak Wall, Gantry Row, the Slump, the Railing Stair, the Dripping Court.

Children are apprenticed to the valley almost as soon as they can walk. A child of five is given a length of gutter to keep clear with a brass scraper. By twelve, they can walk a wet coping, tie a knot in a slick rope, and identify the sound of a blocked drain from a distance. By sixteen, they join a crew: menders, drainers, planters, weavers, pipe fitters, plank layers, grease-and-bearing keepers. They learn to read the valley as a living text. A bulge in a wall is a sentence. A patch of too-bright moss is a warning. A crack that opens after a cold night is a story they must finish.

The old do not retire. They do the fine work—repointing stone, splicing belts, grading moss, teaching the young to listen. They squat on their haunches by a sluice with one palm flat on the stone, feeling for the vibration of a poorly seated block. They speak about the structures as if about old animals. They say a wall “wants to sit down,” a flume “is thinking about wandering.” It is not fancy. A wall that wants to sit down bulges at the knee. A flume that is thinking about wandering undercuts its own footings. They have watched it happen. They have caught it in time, or not.

There is no single ruler. Each terrace sends a delegate to the council in the Steam Hall, a long room warm with condenser heat and loud with mist. They stand in the dimness and speak in turn, their voices barely rising above the hiss. Votes are taken by dropping polished stones into one of two channels; the water carries the stones to a tray, and the count is known by weight. It is a slow parliament, and no decision is made quickly. Even emergencies have a kind of awful slowness—the way a wall gives way over three days, sighing a little before it falls.

They live in the terrace walls themselves. The houses are narrow and deep, set into the stone, with doors on different levels. Inside there is a stove that burns pressed moss and plant blocks, a bed of heather and dry moss, a shelf of tools, a single electric lamp. The walls are limewashed but green at the corners. A door on the lower floor opens onto a path; a door on the upper floor opens onto the next terrace. They move through the valley in three dimensions—through houses, under catwalks, along gutters, down ladders. A person can cross two hundred vertical meters without ever setting foot on a main way.

Their bodies are shaped by this. Their feet are wide and silent. Their hands are cracked but precise, used to tiny adjustments. They stand with one hip higher, always leaning slightly into the hill. They sleep lightly, listening for changes in the night water. They dream of drains, they say, not of fields or sky. They dream of the sound a pipe makes just before it bursts.

They eat what the terraces give. Dense dark bread from root flour, stews of mushrooms and leeks, pickled stems, herbs, moss cakes. What little meat or dried fish they have comes up on the tram from the lower valleys, for feasts and for the sick. They drink hot infusions of steeped herbs, tasting always of the mineral bite of the valley’s water. In the wet and the cold, food is not just fuel; it is a way to remember that the valley can feed as well as wear them down.

Their dead are not buried in the wet ground. The ground is too full of water, and it would not hold them. Instead, the dead are walled into the terraces they maintained, and moss is planted over the niche. A small pipe drips water onto the stone for a season, as if the valley is crying or simply doing what it does. The living say the dead are still working, only slower. It is not a metaphor. In the great walls, some stones are warmer than others in winter, and the crews know not to move them.

There is a woman on the High Flume who has kept the same four hundred meters of channel for forty-one years. She knows each stone by the feel of its wetness. She can tell if a leaf has fallen into the flow a hundred meters away by the change in the note. She has rebuilt the same section of wall nine times, each time a little differently, so the wall seems to be breathing. She wears an old coat, patched with felt, and her boots are tied with wire. The moss on her left shoulder is as thick as a pelt. She speaks rarely, and when she does, the words come out like stones dropping into a well.

There is a boy on the Root Terrace who is not yet eight. He has been given a length of gutter and a small brass rake. Every morning he goes out before the first lamps dim and clears the leaves and moss plugs. He works with total concentration. He has found that if he hums a certain tone, the water in the gutter carries a twig smoothly around the bend. He does not know that the tone matches the frequency of a pump somewhere below. He thinks the gutter likes the song. Perhaps it does.

There is an old man in the Wheel Terrace powerhouse who sits in a wooden chair beside the great turbine. He has a stethoscope with a rubber tube and a brass bell, and he listens to the bearing the way a doctor listens to a chest. He says the turbine is in a bad mood when the mist is thin, and he feeds it graphite and talks to it in a low voice. The turbine has not failed in seventy years. The old man is not its only keeper, but he is its favorite.

These are the ones who live there. They are not many—a few hundred scattered across the terraces like seeds in a wet sponge. They are quiet, persistent, and saturated. They have made themselves into a part of the valley’s process, caught between the water and the stone, tending the green that tends them back.

If you stand still long enough on a catwalk or a stair, you may see one. A figure in dark wool and moss, moving with the slow care of someone who has all the time in the world because the water does. They will not greet you at first. They will stop and listen, and then they will lift one hand, palm open, as if to say: I am here. The valley is here. We are both still holding.
