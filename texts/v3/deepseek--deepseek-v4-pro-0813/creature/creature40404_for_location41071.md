What lives there is a people, but they are hard to see at first. They are the place where the mist thickens, the movement behind the spray. They have the coloring of the stone: pink-white, gray-white, the color of the mineral film on wet rock. Their skin is slick with the same briny water that weeps from the walls. Their hair is the color of wet slate and lichen, worn close and oiled flat. Their eyes are pale, set deep under brows made for squinting, and they squint always—not because the light is dim, but because it comes from too many directions and a wide-open eye would be useless. Their mouths are small, closed, as if permanently tasting the mineral air.

They are not tall. The low doorways and narrow paths have shaped them, and they move with a forward lean, like water finding its grade. Their hands are large and long, the knuckles prominent from generations of fitting stone and lifting wet rock. The fingertips are flattened, almost spatulate, from reading walls. Their feet are broad and splayed, the soles thick and gray, gripping the wet stone like the pads of a mountain animal. They walk on the balls and outer edges of their feet, rolling each step so softly that a footfall is a press, not a sound. They smear their soles with a paste of mineral film and animal fat, and they leave no trace except a slight darkening of the wet rock.

They breathe through their noses, shallow and slow, because the cold is a liquid and a deep breath would fill them.

They dress in the hides of the six-legged grazers, fur turned inward, stitched with sinew and fish-gut. Over this they wear long coats of fish-skin or eel-skin, oiled and overlapping like shingles. Nothing flaps. Everything is tied close. Hoods frame their pale faces like dark stone around a pool. Their tools are obsidian and stone and splinters of antler, carried in pouches against the chest. They use no wood, except the rare twisted shrubs, and those are too precious to burn. They burn dried dung and fish oil and a slow moss from the deep cracks, but mostly they do not burn. They warm themselves with bodies—their own, each other’s, the animals’—and with the dense wet breath of the rooms.

They communicate in two ways. The first is a low speech, words formed in the back of the mouth, pitched below the sound of water. The second is a language of hands, spoken against the chest, the belly, the rock. A question is a tilt of the chin. A warning is a spread of fingers. A debt is a tap over the heart. They can hold a conversation across a crowded room without a single sound, and they do, because the mountain is always listening. They do not shout. They do not sing. They have no songs, no drums, no horns. Music is a memory from before the mountain, and they do not trust it. The loudest thing is the inside of one’s own head, and they have learned to quiet even that.

The work is the work of water. The hollow is always changing, and the people change with it. They are film-keepers, who tend the pink-white crust on the rock, scraping it into cakes or feeding it to the fish. They are water-keepers, who adjust the channels with stone lips and bone splints. They are animal-keepers, who climb to the high ledges to take eggs and down from the silent white birds. They are bone-workers, who turn every dead thing into tools and roofs and thread. They are stone-fitters, who rebuild walls in the night, carrying away the pieces of what the cold has split. They work without haste but without pause. The settlement is an anthill with the sound deleted.

They live so close that privacy is not a room but a posture. They turn their shoulders, lower their eyes, draw their hoods. A person can be alone three inches from another, and yet not be seen. They sleep in stacks with the animals, because a lone body in the wet cold will not keep itself warm. Small furred things burrow into their clothes, and the people allow it. Warmth is shared like food. The six-legged grazers are kept in roof pens, and their heat rises through the floors. Fish are so many in the channels that they are gathered rather than caught. Nothing is wasted: bones become tools, hides become roofs, guts become thread, eyes become beads, blood is mixed with mineral paste to make a red-brown mortar. Even the breath of the animals is kept inside the rooms, a warm wet fog that holds the cold at bay.

The children are born pale and silent. A newborn is wrapped in bird-down and grazer hide and held against the mother’s chest, and if it cries, the mother puts her mouth over the child’s mouth, not to smother but to teach it: sound is a thing that can shake the hollow. By the time they can walk, they know the signs for water, stone, quiet, fall. They learn to read the walls before they learn to speak. A child can tell a safe ledge from a rotten one by the way the cold moves through it. They are given a small scraper and a pouch of mineral paste, and they join the work.

The old are the ones who remember the terraces. They sit on damp ledges with their hands in the water, feeling the changes. They can tell which basin will overflow by the way the water tugs at their fingers. When they die, they are placed in high cracks where the cold dries them slowly, and over years their bones are taken down to become handles, needles, roof-pins. A person’s bones may end up holding a neighbor’s roof, and this is considered a great honor. The dead do not leave; they become the place.

They do not name their gods. They have only the mountain, which listens, and the water, which remembers. They say the water remembers every stone it has touched, and the mountain remembers every word it has heard. So they speak carefully. They make shrines of small bones in the cracks and leave offerings of fish and mineral cake, not to any god but to the place itself, as if feeding a great, slow animal. They do not pray aloud. They press their palms to the wet rock and wait, and if the rock seems to press back, that is enough.

There is no real night in the hollow. The sun is pale and huge, and then it goes away, and the mineral film glows faintly on the walls. The people sleep in shifts, because the water never sleeps. Someone is always awake, walking the terraces, listening with their hands. They do not use torches; fire is precious and the light would only blind them. They navigate by touch and heat, like the blind white birds. In the dark, the settlement is a warm, breathing pile of bodies and stone, and the only sign of them is a slight movement in the mist.

If you watched them long enough, you might think they are not separate from the hollow. They are the hollow’s hands, its listening, its silence made flesh. They carry the rock away and bring it back. They keep the water moving. They do not sing, so the mountain will not answer. And if the mountain ever does answer, they will be the first to feel it—through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands—and they will be ready.

For now, they are only a change in the density of the mist, a place where the spray has shoulders. They are the people who live in the hole in the shoulder of the mountain, and they have been there a long time, and they are not finished yet.
