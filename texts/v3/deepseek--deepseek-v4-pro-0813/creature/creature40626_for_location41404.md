The people are not easy to see at first. The city is so dense, so folded into the rock, that a human figure reads as another piece of moving infrastructure: a shadow between gratings, a hand on a valve, the brief glint of goggles. They are small against the verticality, and they move without hurry, because hurry is dangerous on new rock.

They wear layers, but not for cold. The layers are insulation and armor: a close inner garment of woven mineral fiber that wicks sweat and salt; a middle layer of padded cloth with cooling tubes sewn into the seams; an outer shell of stiffened hide or hammered metal scales, scarred from brushing against fresh basalt. Their hands are gloved in soft leather with copper fingertips. Their boots have soles of braided wire, so they can feel the temperature through their feet without burning. Around their eyes, narrow goggles with lenses ground from first-crystal quartz; the frames fit so closely that the skin beneath is pale and unwrinkled, while the rest of the face is dusted with salt.

Every person carries at least three things: a valve key, a water flask, and a little probe of copper or crystal. The valve key is not just a tool; it is a passport, showing which vent faction they belong to by its shape and wear. The water flask is never empty, but also never full; to offer a drink is a formal act, and to refuse one is not insult but prudence. The probe is for reading, not fighting—they touch it to pipes, to rock, to their own lips to test salt.

Their bodies have become attuned in small ways. They are lean, with long calves from stair-climbing and a particular stoop from moving under low pipe bundles. Their skin is pale beneath the mineral dust, but their cheeks and the backs of their necks are flushed from ambient heat. Many have a fine network of salt lines in the creases of their skin, like maps of dried brine. Their teeth are worn, not from food but from habit—they test mineral samples by mouth. They speak softly, not because of secrecy but because the hiss of steam would eat loud words. They have a sign language of taps on nearby pipes, a kind of second speech that travels farther than voices and leaves a vibration in the wall.

The vent factions are the basic unit of life. A faction is not a family, though families often belong to one for generations. It is a guild, a legal body, a shared identity, a maintenance crew. The names are chemical: House Sulfur, House Halite, House Low-pH, House Bitter Condensate. Each controls a cluster of condenser towers and the terraces below them. They keep water accounts not in ledgers but in ledges of mineral paper, or in knots on cords that can be read by touch in the dark.

Within a faction, roles are fluid but respected. There are the condensers, who climb the towers at dawn; the gardeners, who shape the mineral gardens; the pipe-singers, who listen to the walls; the strata-mappers, who descend into buried districts; the valve-keepers, who control heat. No one is only one of these. A gardener may be a pipe-singer in the evening; a child may be a valve-keeper's apprentice and a drip-mouse herder.

Morning begins not with light but with the first change in the steam's pitch. People wake in shifts. The condensers go up while the air is still silver; they collect the night's yield from the drip pans. Water is gathered into bladders and carried down to the common cisterns, which are not pools but arrays of sealed flasks in cool shadows. The gardeners come next, adjusting the flow to the mineral beds. They use tiny picks, not to carve the crystals, but to scrape away the wrong kind of growth. They talk to the stone in low, rhythmic phrases, not because they believe the stone hears, but because the rhythm steadies their hands.

Take, for instance, a woman named Halite, a gardener of the third terrace. She is small and lean, with goggles pushed up into hair cropped close and dusted with salt. Her forearms are a map of old steam burns, and she moves along the mineral beds like someone reading a text. She carries a tiny copper wand and a flask of water that she never drinks. Her garden is a shelf of black rock where the steam leaks from a hairline fissure. She has shaped the drips into a line of calcite spires, some as tall as her hand, some barely nubs. Every morning she visits them. She wets her finger and holds it near the steam to measure the heat. She scrapes a flake of salt from the edge and tastes it. She whispers to the spires, not words, just a low tone that keeps them growing straight. When a new spire appears overnight, she marks the day in a small notebook made of mineral paper. This is her life, and she does not find it small.

By midday, the terraces are too bright and too hot for most work. People retreat into the shaded canyons or into the rock. They eat small meals—dark wafers pressed from the microbial mats that grow in hot sumps, salt-cured meat brought up by traders from the lower valleys, water that has been cycled through the rock so many times it tastes of old copper. They sleep in shifts, not all at once, because the valves must be watched even in the heat. The city is quietest then, though the heat makes it feel loud: a pressure behind the eyes. In the afternoon, the first shadows return, and the children come out to practice moving silently on the gratings.

Children are rare. The environment does not tolerate large populations. A child is raised by the whole terrace, not because of ideology but because a single misstep can kill. They learn to walk on gratings before they learn to walk on stone. They are taught the three rules: never block a valve, never trust a cold shadow, never drink water you have not tasted twice. Their first toy is often a small gear or a lump of pumice. A child is not given a permanent name until they have shown a temperament. Until then they are called by a simple word: Drip, Clank, Hiss, Thermo. Later they may choose a mineral name—Feldspar, Gypsum, Halite—or keep the sound-name if it fits.

The pipe-singers walk the alleys at dusk, when the steam banks roll through and the light turns silver. They carry small hammers and tap the pipes, listening for changes. Some pipes are so old they have mineralized into stone; those are tapped too, and they ring like bells. The singers can tell if a buried district is still breathing by the echo. The strata-mappers are the only ones who regularly go below. They carry coils of luminous wire and compasses that point not north but toward heat. They descend through tunnels where the walls are warm as skin and the air tastes of copper. They map the city's buried layers: an old factory now filled with basalt, a street that became a ceiling, a courtyard that is now a chamber. They leave marks on the walls—not paint, but small inlays of different minerals, so future mappers can read the strata by touch. Some of them stay too long below and come back with pale eyes and a habit of speaking to the dark as if it were a person.

The valve-keepers are the most powerful and the most burdened. They sit in small rooms where the heat of the city is diagrammed in pipes and dials. They do not command the heat; they negotiate with it. A closed valve can raise a terrace by twenty degrees; an open one can waste water. They keep logs of temperature and pressure, and they dream of imbalances. Their hands are always slightly burned.

Marriage is a contract of shared maintenance. Couples do not exchange rings; they exchange valve keys or small crystals grown in their own gardens. To be married is to be responsible for each other's water accounts and cooling schedules. Love is expressed by checking someone's goggle seal, by saving the coolest corner of the room for them, by leaving a bowl of water out where the steam will condense.

They do not have gods in the usual sense. If they have a religion, it is a religion of patience. The mountain is still rising; the city is still being built; the crystals are still growing. They say, "The mountain is not angry; it is only becoming." They measure time by the thickness of mineral crust on a pipe. An elder's age is known by the number of layers in a crystal they planted as a child. Death is not an ending but a return to the mineral body. The dead are placed in quiet sumps where their bodies slowly calcify, or in crevices where the steam will carry their water back to the sky. There is no mourning except the closing of valves for a day, a silence in the hiss.

The other lives are few but present. The sump-creatures are called drip-mice, though they are not mice. They are pale, many-legged, smaller than a fingernail. They live in the water that gathers at the bottom of cool pipes. Children keep them in small glass vials with a drop of water, but they release them before the water evaporates. To see a drip-mouse is a sign that the water is clean enough to drink. The mites are called crust-folk. They live in the mineral gardens and emerge when a pipe cools enough to gather moisture. They are not pests; the gardeners watch for them, because their presence means the chemistry is right. A garden with no crust-folk is a garden that is dying. The black-winged scavengers are rare. They ride thermals up from the lower valleys, and they never land, only circle. The people call them sky-hunger and consider them beautiful but untrustworthy. A child who sees one is told to remember the shape of its wings, because the bird carries news of the lower world.

In a way, the city itself is the largest living thing. The people are its sense organs. The pipes are its veins. The mineral gardens are its skin. The buried factories are its memory. The people do not live in the city; they live as part of it. When a new lava tongue spills into a courtyard, they do not flee; they watch, and when the rock cools enough, they build on it. That is their deepest belief: that they are not separate from the unfinished world. They are the first mark on a fresh page, but they are also the hand that holds the pen, and they know the page is still being written.
