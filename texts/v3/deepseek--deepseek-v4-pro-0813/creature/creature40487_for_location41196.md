It does not enter the chamber. It is already there, and has been there long enough that the word *lives* begins to mean the way heat lives in a stone.

You would not see it if you stepped behind the curtain. The dark is total; the eye never adjusts because there is nothing to adjust to. But you might feel it before you thought to name it—a displacement in the warm air, a small pressure on the eardrums, a change in the pattern of drips as something moves among the hanging lobes of pale vegetation. The frogs fall silent in a wave, not from alarm but from a kind of acknowledgment. The blind crickets stop their antennae mid-sweep. For a moment the chamber’s sound narrows to the waterfall’s roar and the low hum that seems to come from the rock itself.

It is not large in the way a bear or a human is large, but it is the largest thing in this room by an order that matters. In the blackness, size is told by heat and by the way the air parts. It moves like a raft of warm mist drifting along the wall, never upright, never hurried. Its body is long and low, many-limbed, and folded into itself so that it can press against the gneiss without leaving a gap. The skin—if skin is the word—is not smooth but covered in fine, overlapping pleats, each one no wider than a finger, soft as wet moss and just as absorbent. The color, though no one sees it, would be the gray-white of the mineral terraces, or the violet-white of the lightless plants, or the ivory of the flatworms. It is the color of things that have never needed pigment because they have never been seen.

Its hands are not hands. They are flattened, broad, with seven or eight long digits that end not in claws but in dense pads of sensory tissue, each pad able to read temperature, vibration, and the faint electrical charge of moving water. It uses them the way another creature might use eyes, moving them slowly over the wall, the floor, the surface of the pool, reading the room in a constant, patient touch. The fingers are jointed in more places than bone would allow, and they curl around the flutes and potholes of the stone as if they are remembering them. The creature has no face in any conventional sense—no eyes, no external ears, no mouth that opens in a single place. Instead, the front of its body is a soft, folded mass of olfactory and auditory tissue, covered in the same wet pleats as the rest of it, and it breathes through a dozen small pores along its flanks that open and close with a rhythm too slow for human patience. When it exhales, the air around it warms by a fraction of a degree; when it inhales, the nearest droplets in the mist bend toward it.

It is a creature of thresholds. It lives not in the center of the pool, where the water is still and empty, nor in the deepest darkness of the back wall, but along the margins where water touches stone and life condenses. It is found most often near the three dark rods.

The rods are the only things in the chamber it treats with a different kind of attention. It does not rest against them, does not climb on them, does not drink from them directly. It kneels—or the equivalent of kneeling, lowering its low body into a shape that is almost triangular—and holds its many-fingered hands in the air a hand’s breadth away. It can feel the wrong temperatures the rods make: the cool to the left of one, the warm to the right of another, the subtle flow of heat through the mineral scale. It stays there for hours, sometimes for days, its breathing slowing until it is barely distinguishable from the seeping water. The tip of one long digit may tap the air in a rhythm that matches the drip from the ceiling, or it may trace the air around the weeping rod as if following a line only it can feel. It appears to be listening to something the rods are doing—not a sound, but a change in the way warm water moves, a signal encoded in temperature and mineral taste. It is not worship. It is more like the way a gardener listens to a taproot.

What it eats is obscure. It does not hunt the pale amphipods or the transparent frogs. It does not scrape the vegetation from the walls. Instead, its folded underside, which it presses into the wet mineral silt at the pool’s edge, is lined with thin, vascular ridges that take up dissolved sulfur and iron directly from the water. The same bacteria that feed the food web feed it, but it has cut out the middle steps. It absorbs warmth and chemistry the way a lung absorbs air. It can go for months without moving from a chosen seep, its body becoming a temporary terrace, its pleats slowly accumulating a film of mineral scale that it sheds in thin, flexible sheets once a year. The shed skin lies for a while among the liverwort-like plants, and the amphipods graze on it, and so the creature feeds the room even as the room feeds it.

It has a voice, though it rarely uses it. When it does, it is not a cry or a song but a low, almost subsonic vibration that begins in its chest and passes through the water film on the walls. The frogs answer it with their high piping. The blind crickets drum their legs. The sound does not echo, exactly; it is absorbed by the hanging vegetation and the porous stone, and it lingers in the mist like a warmth. It is the same note as the chamber’s own low hum, or a harmonic of it. Sometimes, when the geothermal spring changes its output and the waterfall outside thickens, the creature exhales for a long, slow count and the whole room seems to breathe with it.

Its life is seasonal, but the seasons here are not of light. They are of water temperature, of mineral concentration, of the subtle rise and fall of the pool. In the warm months, when the spring runs hotter, the creature moves less, spending whole weeks pressed into the back wall, its body almost indistinguishable from a fold of stone. In the cooler months, it becomes more active, gliding slowly along the perimeter, its many-fingered hands exploring every crack, every pothole, every inch of the old groove that runs at hip height. It repairs the mineral terraces, not by building but by nudging the finest silt into place with its finger pads. It clears the small channels where water flows over the edge. It is a gardener, but its garden is the room itself.

It reproduces—if the word still applies—in a way that no one has seen. There may be only one of it, and has been only one for as long as the rods have been doing their patient work. Or there may be two: one that stays near the rods, and one that sometimes curls into the deepest part of the pool, where the water is warmest and the black silt is thick, and remains there for a year, two years, ten years, while something inside it divides and re-forms. The frogs and crickets do not fear it. The flatworms crawl over its folded skin. The springtails skate on the surface film of its eyes—not that it has eyes, but it has blind, smooth patches on the front of its face where the moisture gathers, and the springtails drink from those patches the way they drink from every wet surface. The creature does not flinch. It is not above the food web; it is the web’s slowest, warmest stone.

There is a story the place might tell, if the rods were ever read by a machine from the forgotten time. It is a story about a being that was not born but left—a caretaker, a watchman, a piece of living infrastructure designed to tend a device that draws water from the deep rock. The rods are the only visible part of that device. The creature is the invisible part, the part made of flesh because flesh can adapt and self-repair and wait for longer than a machine could. It has forgotten its own name, if it ever had one. It does not know that it is old. It only knows the warmth, the sound, the taste of sulfur, the way the rods make the air feel wrong in just the right way. It knows the room’s rules by heart: light does not enter; water moves in thin sheets; heat comes from below; nothing hurries.

And so it lives there, not as a ghost and not as a god, but as the room’s slowest heart. It does not seek to leave. It does not wonder about the outside world beyond the waterfall. The roar of falling water is its sky; the dark is its weather; the mineral terraces are its calendar. It is becoming nothing. It has already become what it is.
