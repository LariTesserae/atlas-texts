There is no single answer to what lives here. The lake lives here, breathing on its own slow schedule. The city lives here, shifting its walls like something asleep. The green islands live here, their pale threads carrying a faint pulse of light from root to leaf. But the ones you mean—the ones who walk the paths and wear wide hats and stand at the edge of the water with their hands in their pockets—are quieter than all the rest.

At first they are hard to see, not because they hide, but because they belong to the place the way reeds belong to a seepage channel. You might mistake one for a young tree, or a shadow, until she steps onto a path and the path glows faintly at her feet. They are tall and narrow, or maybe they only seem tall because the basin is so wide and the buildings so strange. Their clothes hang loose, in colors of the place: clay-gray, ochre, the pale turquoise of the mineral streaks, the off-white of dried salt. The cloth is not exactly cloth. It is a fine, close-woven mesh that beads with moisture. Their wide-brimmed hats are not for shade. Each one is a small fog-collector. When the mist rolls from the lake, the brims catch it, channel it down hidden seams, and carry it to a reservoir at the collar. They drink as they walk, without stopping, without breaking the silence.

Their skin is the color of the silt flats after the fog lifts: a gray that holds a little gold, or a gold that holds a little gray. In the dim light it seems to absorb the glow of the paths. Their faces are narrow, their cheekbones high, their mouths still. Their eyes are large and dark and wet, set wide, and they blink slowly, as if light is a currency they must spend carefully. There is a faint sheen on their skin in certain angles, like the bloom on a plum, or like the bioluminescence at the bottom of the green islands when the dimness is deepest. Their hands are long and careful, with fingertips calloused from touching stone and leaf and mesh. They touch the world the way someone might touch a beloved animal: with attention, with restraint, with the knowledge that the world is listening.

They move slowly, but not from weakness. Each step is placed, not because the ground is dangerous in any obvious way, but because the ground is still forming and you do not step on a face without asking. They walk along the paths with their lights held low or not at all. At night, if they carry anything, it is a small pearl of bioluminescence cupped in one palm, less to see by than to show others where they are. Their feet are bare or wrapped in soft soles that let them feel the temperature of the earth. They pause at cracks in the mud, crouch, listen. Sometimes they lay a palm flat on the ground and wait. The building behind them may shift its weight, and they nod without turning, as if the building had spoken.

One of them, a woman whose hat is beginning to fray at the brim, walks toward the lake. She stops where the mud has cracked into polygons, steps into the crack, feels the water well up around her foot, then moves on. At the water’s edge she kneels and rinses her hands. A clay-colored lizard watches from a reed. She says something too low to hear, and the lizard tilts its head, then goes back to its stillness.

Their work is not loud. They tend the green islands the way a nurse tends a wound: they clear salt from the leaves, uncurl a fern that has not quite opened, run a finger along the pale threads that run between plants. Where the thread’s light is weak, they dampen the ground above it. Where it is strong, they leave a small marker—a twist of grass, a pile of stones. They open panels on the buildings by pressing a palm to the wall. The wall softens and makes an opening like a mouth, and they step inside. There, in the warm translucent dimness, they do what is needed: a hand pressed to a surface that is too warm or too cool, a channel adjusted, a reservoir of captured fog tilted so the water flows cleanly. They do not repair the buildings. They converse with them.

The few animals—the clay-colored lizard, the slow insects—do not fear them. The lizard clings to its broad leaf and watches a person pass with the same calm with which it watches the fog. It has learned that these people are not predators; they are another part of the water. The inhabitants do not feed the animals or call to them. They simply share the dimness, in the same way the bare flats share the fog with the green islands.

They live in small groups, not exactly families. A cluster of buildings might hold three or five or nine of them, and they share the work of water and green. They sit together in the deep settling hours, not speaking, each with a hand on the ground or on the wall, as if listening to a common pulse. Touch is their more fluent language. A hand on a shoulder can carry more than a sentence. When they do speak, their voices are low and rustling, like reeds in a light wind. The language has no word for “mine” or “forever.” It has many words for different kinds of wet: the wet of a cupped leaf, the wet of seepage, the wet of fog that does not fall but hangs. It has a word for the particular silence before the fog thickens, and a word for the sound a building makes when it settles in the dark.

They do not measure time in hours. They measure it in fogs and breaths. A day is not dawn to dusk but from one rising of the fog to the next. They speak of “first white,” when the mist begins to climb from the lake, and “deep settle,” when the ground creaks and the buildings adjust. They know by the color of the gray-gold light whether the long breathing dark is near. They sleep in short stretches, not all at once, in alcoves that open in the walls when the body wants rest. The buildings know their rhythms, or perhaps the rhythms are the buildings’ own.

The young are few, and they are treated less as children than as new roots. They are allowed to wander, but always near the green islands, always within sight of a path. They learn the rules by watching: how to step on cracking mud, how to cup water without disturbing the silt, how to be quiet. When a child laughs, the sound is startling and bright, like a birdcall, and the whole basin seems to pause, then fold the sound away gently into the fog.

The lake is their clock and their chapel. They go to it often, alone or in pairs. At the edge they stop where the last green gives way to open mudflat, and they stand with their hands in their pockets, looking at the milky water. The water rises and falls by a few inches, breathing in and out. They read the breathing. When it quickens, they move back. When it slows, they may kneel and touch the water with their fingertips, then touch their lips. They do not drink the lake water; it is too mineral, too close to the blood of the basin. But they keep its taste on their skin.

The small platform on slender legs is their meeting place. They sit under the mesh roof while the fog condenses and runs down the sides. Sometimes two or three are there for an hour without speaking, just watching the fog roll across the flats. This is not idleness. It is the closest thing they have to reading the sky. They have no stars to steer by, so they look down instead: at the seepage channels, the cracks, the pale threads, the path’s faint glow.

They experience joy, but it is a low, slow joy—the joy of a new green shoot, or a building that has stopped shifting, or a fog that comes thick and clean. They grieve by sitting very still, allowing the mist to settle on them until they are nearly indistinguishable from the ground. Anger is rare and is taken out into the open flats and walked off, into the empty space between the green islands, until the person returns with the anger worn smooth. They do not raise their voices. The basin would not know what to do with a shout.

Their bodies are adapted, or perhaps they have simply become more themselves in this place. Their hearts beat slowly. Their body heat is low. They eat little: some fruit from the thickets, the tender inside of a reed, a pale root pulled carefully from the mud. They drink from their own hats. Their blood, if you were to see it, is not bright red but a deep, cloudy rust, like the minerals on the basin walls. In the deepest dimness, a faint light moves under their skin, along their veins, in the same slow pulse as the threads in the green islands. They are not separate from the place. They are one of its organs.

There are few of them, and they know this without regret. The city is too large, and they move through it like a breath through a body. They do not fill the empty rooms. They tend them. Some buildings have not been entered in years, and when they are, the walls seem to wake slowly, unhurried, like a sleeper recognizing an old friend. The people speak of the empty places as if they are waiting, not abandoned. “Still arriving,” they might say, with a gesture at the bare flats, the raw scarps, the water that is still retreating.

They are not finished. That is the defining thing. They are not finished any more than the basin is. They are the tenders of an unfinished world, the quiet ones who live by its rules: watch the ground, gather the water, spend the light sparingly. They would not call themselves its masters. They would not call themselves its children. They might not call themselves anything at all. They would simply stand at the edge of the lake, hands in their pockets, and watch the fog rise, and wait.
