They are not easy to see. The eye must learn to separate them from the weave of root and fiber, because they have become, over generations, a part of it. Not in the way of camouflage—they do not hide—but in the way of a hand that has worn a tool so long the tool becomes a finger. They are the Tenders. They are few: never more than a dozen, often fewer. They are human, or were once; the cold has made them something else, something patient and slow and mineral.

Their skin is the color of the bark when it is old and dry—grayish, with a fine grain that runs in the same direction as the plant's growth. Not bark itself, but skin that has thickened and cracked in patterns that echo the surrounding textures. They are tall, or seem tall, because they are thin in a way that is not starvation but architecture: their bones are long, their joints articulate with the same deliberate economy as the leaning trunks. They move rarely, and when they do, they move in the grooves left by the walking trees, stepping exactly in the compressed center, so that their passage leaves no mark that the ground has not already made.

They do not speak. Or perhaps they speak in a language that is not sound. They communicate by touch: a hand laid flat on a trunk, a pulse of pressure through the woven floor, a gesture made by bending a single filament. The plant itself is their telegraph; the ground carries their signals the way it carries the peristalsis. A Tender standing at one end of the valley can ask a question and receive an answer, minutes later, from another Tender miles away, not as words but as a subtle shift in the rhythm of the ground. They call it "the reading." They spend most of their waking time reading the floor: kneeling, palms down, feeling the history of the last thousand years written in scar tissue and release.

They eat what the plant gives, which is not food in the human sense. When a collapse begins—when a great trunk drains of color and the vines begin to uncoil—the Tenders arrive, not to interfere but to harvest. They press their hands into the dying body at the precise moment when the heat of its stolen life is rising, and they gather a substance that wells up from the root-weave: a thick, mineral paste, warm, pale gray, tasting of earth and electricity. They call it the milk. They do not cook it; there is no fire here, no flame has ever been lit in this cold, wet air. They eat it raw, in small amounts, and it sustains them in ways that have nothing to do with calories. It is the plant's memory, or perhaps its intent, and by consuming it, they become part of the machine.

They drink from the black pools, but only at the edges, where the water has been filtered through the glassy filaments. They do not disturb the surface. They kneel and drink with cupped hands, and the water is so cold it feels hot, and so clear it feels like swallowing light. There is no other water. The rain that falls in brief, local showers is too mineral, too heavy with the plant's exudate; it would burn their throats. The pools are the only true water, and the Tenders regard them with a kind of reverence that is also engineering: they know the pools are the reservoirs of the factory, the cooling system, and they are careful not to contaminate them.

They sleep in the hollows of leaning trunks, wrapped in the fibrous curtains that hang from the canopy. They do not build shelters; the plant provides. At night, when the temperature drops and the ground begins to wring itself, they lie still and let the peristalsis rock them. They do not dream, or if they do, their dreams are the slow dreams of the root-weave: images of pressure and release, of light dimming, of water rising through capillary veins. They wake with the first faint brightening of the sky, and they resume their work.

What is their work? It is difficult to say. They tend the machine, but they do not know what it produces. They prune nothing—the plant forbids it; a cut would heal, but it would also remember, and the Tenders avoid damaging the tissue. Instead, they tend by attention: they watch for signs of imbalance, for a trunk that is leaning too far, for a channel that has become blocked, for a patch of ground that has gone too quiet. When they find such a thing, they do not intervene directly. They kneel and press their hands to the bark, and they "listen"—which is not hearing but a kind of sympathy, a matching of their own slow heartbeat to the slower pulse of the plant. And then they wait. Sometimes the plant corrects itself. Sometimes it does not, and a collapse begins, and the Tenders retreat to watch.

The collapses are their religion, or perhaps their calendar. They gather at a distance, all of them who can reach, and they stand motionless, their gray hands folded, and they watch the dying giant be consumed. They do not weep. They do not celebrate. They observe. They are the witnesses, the ones who remember what the plant forgets. After the gap closes and the new shoots burst upward, they approach and read the scar, tracing the new glyphs with their fingertips, adding the event to the long, unwritten history they carry in their own bodies.

They know you are there. They have known since you entered the valley. They felt you through the ground, a foreign rhythm, a hot, irregular pulse moving through the cold weave. They have not approached, not out of fear, but out of patience. They are waiting to see what you will do, whether you will read the ground or simply cross it, whether you will try to speak or remain silent. If you meet them—if you stumble upon one kneeling in a groove or drinking at a pool—they will not run. They will rise slowly, turn their pale, lined face toward you, and look at you with eyes that are not unkind but are very, very old. And they will wait, without speaking, for you to understand that you are the interruption, the brief, warm noise in a place that has been humming in silence for longer than your species has had a name.

They are not lonely. The plant is their companion, their host, their deity, and their employer. They feel the ground breathe beneath them, and they feel a kind of contentment, even joy, in the knowledge that they are part of something vast and purposeful, even if the purpose is unknown. They are the tenders of a factory that has no product, the servants of a loom that weaves nothing but itself. And if you stay long enough—if the cold does not drive you out, if the silence does not unmake you—you will begin to understand that they are not the exception to the absence of fauna. They are the final, necessary addition to it, the one animal the machine has chosen to keep, not for their labor, but for their witness.
