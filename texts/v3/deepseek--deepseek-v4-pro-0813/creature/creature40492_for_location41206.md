The inhabitant is not the salamanders, though they are many. They are the blood-cells of the place. The inhabitant is the thing they gather around.

It looks at first like the central dome of stone—the only dry thing in the hollow, the thing the fog breathes over. But it is not a glacial erratic. It is the exposed back of an immense, old amphibian, a paedomorphic salamander or something older, grown in the cold and dark until it filled the bowl from below. Its skin is dark and fine-grained, polished to the look of basalt, and it secretes a thin, bitter oil that makes water bead and roll away. That is why no moss grows on its crown.

The rest of it is buried. Its body spreads through the peat like a net of roots, branching beneath the sphagnum. The floating floor is not floating on water alone; it is floating on the creature’s back and flanks. When you step and black water rises around your boot, you are pressing on its body. When the fog above the moss rises in slow, even pulses, you are watching it breathe. It draws air or water through hidden folds near the base of the dome and exhales through the whole mat. Its rhythm is slow because its heart is slow—one beat in a minute, maybe less.

It is not a hunter. Its mouth, if it can be called that, is a soft, downward-facing slit hidden under the edge of the dome. It feeds by pulling water through the peat, straining out the dissolved dead: shed skins, drowned midges, old salamanders, the bodies of frogs. The reason there are no bones, no shed skins, no dead things left in the hollow is that it absorbs them. It turns the dead into a thin mineral soup and drinks them back into the living moss. It does not chase, it does not bite. It is the hollow’s digestion.

Its head is buried deeper, pointing toward the back wall and the concave overhang. It has no eyes. The eyes are sealed over, reduced to pale spots under the skin. It sees through pressure and taste. It feels every insect landing on the water, every bird hopping in the moss, every salamander curling against its side. The salamanders face the rock because they are listening to its pulse through the water-film, the way a seed listens to the rain. They are not worshipping it. They are simply oriented to the oldest and steadiest thing in the dark.

It moves very rarely. When it does, the floor rolls. The pools spill. The moss tears in long quiet seams, and the caddisfly larvae are carried to new pools. The birds take off, circle once, and land again. The movement lasts an hour, maybe a day, and then it settles. Afterward the moss begins, slowly, to weave the seams shut. That is why the hollow is not being carved anymore. The creature is its shaper, but it has finished shaping. It only adjusts.

It has no mate, no young. It may have budded once, long ago, and the small pale offspring may have crawled into a lower seep and died, or become another hollow elsewhere. It does not remember. Its memory is not made of images. It remembers as a long pressure in the water, as the taste of peat getting deeper, as the cold of a year that did not thaw.

If you stayed long enough, it would learn you. Not your name, not your face. It would learn your weight on the moss, your heat, the salt of your breath condensing in the fog. It would feel you as a new mineral in the water. It would not harm you. It would wait.

When you sat still, the moss would grow around your boots. When you lay down, the water would rise around your ribs. When you died, it would draw you under, not out of malice but because that is what the place does. It would make you part of the full, dark, breathing floor.
