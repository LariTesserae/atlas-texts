You do not see it at first because it is too large to see, and because you are looking for something with a face. The absence of animals reads as emptiness. But the green is not a blanket. It has a nap, a direction. The moss lies smoothed toward the far end, where the water hammers. The ferns lean not toward the gray light but toward the deepest thump. The roots hanging from the rim are not limp. They are tensioned, every one of them, like cables under load. Where they cross, they have grafted. Where they touch the wall, they have sent fine white threads into the basalt cracks. You are not looking at plants. You are looking at a single connected fabric.

The first thing that moves is the water. It is not separate from the living surface. It runs through the moss, along the root cables, into the liverwort mats. When a gust spills down through the slot, the whole wall pulses. The moss does not just drip; it exhales. The leaves do not just flutter; they turn. The green is breathing. It takes in the wet wind, swells, then relaxes. The movement is too slow for the eye, but your skin knows it: the air pressure changes, the mist thickens, your sleeve grows wet without rain.

You follow the grain inward. The floor shifts under your boots. A mat of roots and moss detaches from a slab and slides a hand’s width down the rock. It does not fall. It reattaches, like a limpet. Another slab, the size of a door, has already dropped and shattered. The pieces are not dead. They are already fuzzing with new green. The body does not mourn its broken parts; it uses them.

At the far end, the root cables converge. The braid is thick, thicker than a torso, wrapped around the lip of the siphon. It is not a nest. It is a knot of living and dead tissue, old roots as scaffolding, new roots pushing through, moss filling every gap. The water slams into the pothole and forces itself through the braid. The mass clenches. The siphon shifts pitch. Thump, suck, thump. The braid shudders in time, not because it is shaken, but because it is pumping. It has become the engine’s soft heart. It does not have muscles; it has cells swollen with water. The piston is the stream, and the braid is the cylinder.

Now you see the whole body. It is not spread over the rock; it is the rock’s skin. The walls are its ribs. The fresh slabs are its shed shell. The stream is its circulation. The wind is its lungs. The mist is its breath. It eats the mountain slowly, sending root hairs into the sharp basalt, pulling minerals from the fresh fractures. It does not need to hunt, because there is nothing else to compete with. It is the top, the bottom, and the middle. It is the only animal-like thing here, but it is not an animal. It is a plant that has stopped being a collection of plants and become a single body.

It breaks itself down. The dead leaves do not rot; they are reabsorbed. The root hairs grow into their own fallen debris and pull the minerals back out. When a branch tears away, it does not become waste. It becomes a cutting. It rides the stream to a new crack, roots before your eyes. The body does not reproduce by seed or spore alone; it reproduces by injury. Every rockfall is a birth. Every torn mat is a new colony that remains part of the same whole.

It senses you. Not with eyes or nerves, but with the water film that covers everything. Your body heat makes the air around you warmer and wetter. The moss near your boot grows a shade brighter. A rootlet uncurls from a crack behind your head. It is not reaching for you in anger; it is following your breath, the carbon dioxide, the salt, the heat. You are a disturbance in its skin, a small weather event. It turns toward you the way a plant turns toward a seep.

It cannot think. It does not want. It simply is what the Throat has made: a green machine in a stone engine, a resident so complete that it leaves no room for anything else. The place was never unoccupied. It was occupied by something so large and continuous that you mistook it for the world.

When you leave, the ringing in your ears is not only the noise. It is the afterimage of a body that has no outline, a living system that idles and grows at the edge of collapse, using the collapse as its own renewal. The Throat keeps thumping behind you, and you know now that the thump is not just geology. It is a heartbeat, but not one you will ever see.
