At first you think nothing lives here. The chamber seems too young, too mineral, too perfectly balanced to host anything but the small blind grazers at the water’s surface. But there is a larger inhabitant, and it has become so continuous with the place that you must watch for a long time before it separates from the stone. It is not exactly an animal, not exactly a person; it is what the chamber kept.

It sleeps in the warm pool, not at the bottom but half-floating just under the surface, its back a low ridge of mineral-crusted skin. Its color is the color of wet basalt and old calcite: gray-white, sulfur-stained, with a faint translucent quality where the water touches it. Its shape is roughly that of a person, but shortened, folded, as if the mountain pressed it into a smaller mould. The hands are broad and short-fingered; the same hands that left the ochre marks. The legs are bent, the body soft and heavy, built for stillness rather than speed. Its head is hairless, with no visible eyes—only two shallow heat-pits above a small, downturned mouth. Its nostrils are little more than slits that open only when the gas-hiss changes.

When it breathes, it breathes once for every sixty drips, matching the water. Its ribs are not visible; the body is too encased in a rind of porous mineral for bones to show. Along its spine grow the same glassy threads that line the pool’s edge, but these are not dead growths; they are sense-fibers, thin and jointed, trailing in the warm current. It can feel the needle of water through the pool’s skin before it ever touches it.

It does not need eyes. The chamber is lightless, and its body knows the room by heat and vibration and taste. It feels the far wall by the way the air densifies near the gas crack; it hears the drip falling through the heavy silence before it lands; it tastes the mineral dust in the air with the roof of its mouth. The fingers are blunt, but they are quick to read the wall’s crystal fur, the rim of the pool, the edges of the bowl. It has no language, or its language is the same as the chamber’s—a slow code of touch, temperature, and waiting.

It eats rarely. It feeds on the mineral paste that creeps over the rocks, on the thin mycelial mats, on the translucent threads that grow around the pool. When it hungers for more, it lies in the shallow water with its mouth just open, and the little six-legged animals come to the warmth of its breath, and it swallows one or two without disturbing the water. The mites it does not eat; they crawl over its body and pick the sulfur from its skin, and in return it carries them from one warm crevice to another. It eats enough to keep its body just this side of living, no more.

The bowl and the ring of stones are its work. Long ago, before the chamber sealed itself completely, it still had hands and memory enough to gather seven water-worn stones from a river far below and carry them up to this hot dark. It placed them in a circle to mark the place where it would keep its small fire of thought. The clay bowl it shaped from wet clay and mountain heat, and it broke one day while it was trying to learn the shape of the needle. The seeds were a gift or an experiment—it does not now remember—but none lived, and it stopped trying to make anything grow.

The flake of sharp stone and the twist of plant fiber are newer. It works by touch, shaping the fiber into cord, then untwisting it, then shaping it again. It knaps the flake against the mineral crust, not to make tools but to hear the sound—a small, precise click that answers the drip. It is a kind of conversation. It sometimes sits for hours, blind face turned toward the needle, and lets the warmth of the water column rest against its lower lip. It passes one broad hand through the needle and waits for it to re-form, as if this is the one thing it still wants to understand. The handprints on the wall are not a mark of departure; they are a signature of staying. It pressed them into the mineral paste with its own short fingers, and the water has glazed them over like a promise.

Its breath, if you could hold it close, would smell of sulfur, wet stone, and mud—not decay. Its skin is warm, the temperature of the pool, and slightly tacky with mineral exudate. When it moves, it moves with the same slow, even economy as the water cycle. It does not hurry, not because it cannot, but because the chamber is exactly balanced and so is it. It has aligned its own metabolism with the mountain’s long breath. It sleeps for what might be weeks, then wakes to touch the pool rim, to check the water level, to press its fingertips to the needle’s base. It does not remember names, time, or why it came. It only knows that it is to stay, to tend, and to wait.

If you saw it, you might mistake it for a heap of mineral growth. But if you stayed long enough, you would see the heap be breathing, as slow as the gas crack, and see the broad hand lift toward the needle in the dark, not to catch it but to let it rest against the palm like a single thread of water that never falls. The chamber has no seasons, no day or night; this being has no age, no hunger, no fear. It is the thing that lives in the place—not the only life, but the largest, the slowest, and the most like the place itself.

The needle of water is the one thing that does not fit, and the inhabitant is the second thing that does not fit. They are two answers to the same unanswered question, keeping each other company in the warm, mineral dark.
