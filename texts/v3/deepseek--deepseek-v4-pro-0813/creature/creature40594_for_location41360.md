They do not live in the valley so much as they are grown out of it. That is the first thing. You will not see them by looking for people. You will see them by noticing where the noise folds back onto itself, where the tapping does not scatter but answers, where a spiral of pale pebbles makes a sentence in the dust.

The small ones are the ones you see first, if you see them at all.  
They are the size of a child, but nothing about them is childlike. Their skin is leather, not hide over flesh but a living armor of small, overlapping scutes, colored like old rust and silver lichen. Where the light hits them, the scutes have a waxy sheen, and the dust from the dry air settles into the seams until they look like part of the bark. Their faces are flat, with no muzzle, no protrusion of nose, just a broad plane set with two enormous dark eyes. The eyes are not wet. They gleam like polished resin. They blink sideways, a slow nictitating membrane moving across the surface to clear away spores and the fine mineral crystals that drift up from the vents.

Their hands are long. Fingers, three of them, each with an extra joint that lets them curl backward around a stem or a cord. The thumb is not a thumb but a fourth finger opposed on the lower side of the hand, so that a grip is a cage of bone and sinew. Their feet are the same, split-padded, the toes prehensile and tipped with hard keratin. They do not wear clothing, exactly. They wear harnesses of braided fiber, dyed dark with resin, studded with beads of black glass and the empty shells of small six-legged animals. Pouches hang from the harness, made of dried glider-skin or seed-pod leather, and in the pouches are flakes of obsidian, coils of twine, bone awls, pellets of dried pith, plugs of resin.

They move in bursts. One moment they are a shadow on a fused trunk, the next they are ten meters along a root-ladder, the motion a series of rapid landings. Each landing is a tap. The taps are not incidental. They are language. The small ones do not call out, not from the mouth. Their mouths are small and lipless, turned inward, used for grinding the fat pith and black sap, not for speaking. They speak with their feet and knuckles, with the hard edges of their hands against resonant wood. The valley is full of sounding bodies: hollow stems, taut root-mats, seed pods, the ribbed trunks that carry a strike for half a kilometer. The small ones use the whole place as a drum.

A name here is not a sound you make, but a rhythm you leave.  
A person is known by the pattern of their taps, the spacing of the beats, the particular way they let silence fall. When a small one appears on a high branch and taps its feet, it is not just announcing itself. It is saying, *I am here, in the place between the third and fourth beat, and I am moving toward the vent-spiral.* Far away, another taps back. The sound travels through the wood, through the root-mat, through the warm dry air. The valley does not hear the noise as noise. It hears it as a grammar. The animals have learned some of it. The people have learned the animals. They borrow rhythms from the chittering grazers, from the gliders’ membrane-rattle, from the seed pods that detonate in chain reactions. The whole valley is a conversation, and the small ones are only one voice in it.

They build. But they do not build as we build.  
Their structures are not houses, not fortresses, not monuments. They are hands laid on the landscape. A hoop of greenwood bent and tied with fiber to mark a route. A platform of woven stems lashed to three trunks, four meters above the mat, where three or four of them can lie during the bright heat and listen. A windbreak of black stone blades and dried hide, stitched with sinew, angled to make a pocket of relative quiet. A spiral of pale pebbles around a steaming vent, not for magic but as a calendar and a signpost: *this vent is hot enough to bend wood, but not so hot it cracks the stone.* A tripod of long bones tied at the top, from which hang clusters of seed pods that rattle when the ground shakes.

None of it is meant to last. The valley does not allow permanence. The mat grows over everything. A platform not tended for a season becomes a bulge in the living wood, then a rib, then a branch. A pebble spiral is swallowed by the roots. The small ones know this. They build the way a bird builds a nest, not to own the tree but to use the tree for a while. They weave their sleeping places into the canopy, slung between stems, because the floor is not a place to rest. If you lie on the mat too long, the runners begin to probe you. They do not mean harm, but the land is always assembling, and anything still becomes part of the assembly.

The other ones are taller, paler, slower.  
You will not notice them at first. They stand so still that the eye skips over them as part of the trunk. They are the color of dry clay and mineral dust, pale as the inside of a split stone. Their skin is smooth where the small ones are scuted, and it has a waxy bloom, like the surface of a leaf that drinks heat instead of water. Their eyes are smaller, set deeper, and they blink rarely. Their hands are long, even longer than the small ones’, but thickened at the knuckles, the fingers bearing tough callused pads from years of pressing against bark. They wear fewer harnesses, sometimes only a cord around the waist from which hang hollow gourds and a single stone scraper.

They are the sap-tenders.  
They move along the sap-bleeding stems with a slowness that is not weakness but attention. They stop at a raised ridge under the bark and lay their palm against it, feeling the pressure. They close their eyes. Then, if the time is right, they take a flake of obsidian and make a small, precise cut. The black sap begins to bead up, thick and volatile. They catch it in a hollow gourd. They do not rush. The sap comes in its own time, and the tall ones wait as if they have become part of the plant’s respiration. When the gourd is full, they seal the wound with a plug of resin and move on.

No one is entirely certain if the tall ones are the same people grown old, or a different kind that shares the valley. The small ones treat them with a particular rhythm, a slow triple-tap that is used for no other purpose. It might mean *elder*, or *the one who hums*, or *the part of us that has become plant.* The tall ones respond rarely, with a low hum that you feel more than hear, a vibration that comes up through the roots and into your sternum. The hum seems to make the sap flow more easily. It is not a command. It is more like an agreement.

The small ones are the hands of the valley. The tall ones are its memory.  
The small ones hunt, gather, weave, flake stone, tie knots, build platforms, carry messages. The tall ones tend the sap, the vents, the slow pulses of the root-mat. They know which stems are thirsty for mineral crystals, which seed pods are ripe and ready to detonate, which sections of the wall are about to shear. They pass this knowledge not in words but in hums and in the placement of objects: a pebble moved from one side of a spiral to the other, a gourd hung at a particular angle, a twist of fiber tied around a shoot.

The small ones bring them food: plugs of fat-rich pith, the meat of the small grazers, waxy grubs dug from the root-mat. The tall ones eat slowly, with a kind of reverence, and they excrete dry pellets of salt and silica, just like the animals. The small ones excrete the same. No one here drinks water. There is no water to drink. They take what they need from the oily sap, the resins, the fat in the pith. Their breath is a fine, sweet dust, slightly caustic. Their blood, if you cut them, is thick and dark and smells of turpentine and hot iron. It seals almost at once into a black scab.

They do not use fire.  
Fire is a danger here, a thing the land does not forgive. The air is too dry and too full of volatile oils. A spark could run through the canopy like a nerve. So they use the heat of the vents instead. They carry greenwood to the steaming fissures and hold it there until it bends. They soften resin in stone bowls set near the heat. They never open a flame. The small ones have stories, told in tapping, of a time someone tried to bring fire into the valley. The story ends with a long silence, which is the worst ending they know.

Instead, they use sound and heat and patience.  
They flake stone in the shade of a fused trunk, striking flakes from a black glassy cobble. They twist fiber into cord, weaving nets and harnesses. They carve bone awls from the long bones of animals, and sometimes from the long bones of their own dead. The dead are not buried. They are laid on the mat, and the mat takes them. Rootlets pierce the leathery skin, the pale skin, and the body is slowly digested into the living land. What remains after a season is bone, cleaned and warm, and the people collect it. They use the bones for tools, for tripods, for rattles. The dead become part of the scaffold, part of the sound.

A death is marked by a rhythm.  
The person’s tap-name is repeated, slower each time, over the course of many days. The small ones strike the same pattern on the roots until the roots themselves seem to hold it. Then they hang the person’s tools from a tripod of long bones, and they string seed pods around it. The pods rattle when animals pass, when the ground shakes, when the wind moves. The dead become a small percussion in the valley’s roar, and the living hear them and remember.

The inhabitants do not have a word for “nature.”  
They have no outside. The valley is not a world they live in; it is a body they are part of. They are the ones the land grew to do the tending. The small ones say, in taps, that the valley is a great instrument, and they are the fingers. The tall ones say, in hums, that the valley is a body, and they are the blood. Both are true. The plants use sound. The animals use sound. The people use sound. They are all parts of the same braided roar.

If you stand still long enough, they will notice you.  
A small one will perch on a branch above your head and tap a query. The taps will be answered from far away, a chain of small strikes running through the wood like a decision being made. Then it will tilt its flat face, its large dark eyes reflecting the dusty light, and it will tap once, very close, as if to say: *You are here. You are full of water. You are not part of the song.* Then it will be gone into the leaves, and the tall one below will turn slowly toward the next sap-bleeding stem, and the mat will begin to grow over your feet.

That is what lives there.  
Not strangers, not settlers, not caretakers. They are the valley’s own nervous system, its hands and ears and slow, patient breath. They are the ones who tap and hum and bleed the black sap, who weave the platforms and mark the vents, who die into the root-mat and become the land. They live there the way sap lives in a stem, not as a separate thing but as a pulse. And if you listen long enough, the sound they make stops sounding like noise. It begins to sound like the valley speaking to itself, and you are only an interval in it.
