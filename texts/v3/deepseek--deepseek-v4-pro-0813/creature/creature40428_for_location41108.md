At first there is no one. The plateau seems empty in the way a fever dream is empty: full of heat, shifting stone, and a dark that has texture. Then, if you stand still long enough—longer than is comfortable—you begin to notice that some of the upright forms are not quite as fixed as they should be. A black basalt spire ticks as it cools. A white salt-crusted shape near a sulfur vent has a faint interior rhythm, a slow flexing, like a hand opening and closing underwater. In this place, the inhabitants do not arrive. They emerge from the mineral world itself.

They are not a species. They are a condition of the land when the land becomes aware of its own pressure.

What lives here is made of the same inventory as the high broken plateau: black basalt, reddish scoria, anhydrite, gypsum, crystalline salts, sulfur, obsidian. They have no water in them. Their bodies are dry, anhydrous, sharp-edged, unweathered. They are built of plates and columns, of crystal faces and conchoidal fractures. Some of them are tall and narrow, with limbs like bundles of columnar basalt that unlock joint by joint when they move. Some are low and broad, huddled like cooling lava, and when they shift they leave behind a fan of white salt needles as if the ground had sweated them. Some are nothing but a standing shadow with a doorway-shaped hole through the chest, and through that hole the dark seems warmer, more inhabited.

Their faces, if you can call them faces, are mostly mineral planes. One might have a flat obsidian tablet for a head, with a spiral groove where a mouth should be, the groove grown rather than carved, like a stress fracture that forgot to stop. Another has a rhomb of translucent gypsum where a skull should be, and inside it burns a low, blue sulfur flame that never flickers in the still air. They do not breathe as we understand breathing. They exchange heat. When they stop moving, the stone under their feet cools slightly, and a white bloom of anhydrite spreads outward in a ring.

They are few. You can cross the heaving stone for long stretches and see nothing but the land itself. Then a “standing slab” you passed an hour ago has changed its angle. That is their way of looking at you—not with eyes, but by canting their whole body toward a source of heat or a new crack. They feel the ground through the soles of their feet. Sounds here arrive too late or too early, so they listen with their bones to the deep creak of the plateau adjusting its weight. They speak, if they speak at all, by tapping the rock in patterned sequences that travel for kilometers through the brittle crust. Sometimes they use a ring of fitted basalt columns on a promontory as a resonator, and the whole headland hums with a tone too low for ears, but you feel it in your jaw.

The structures scattered across the plateau are theirs, but not as houses or temples. They build the way a fever builds: to bring the ground’s pressure to a point and let it pass through cleanly. The low wall crossing the saddle between two spires is not a boundary. It is a stitch across a fault, meant to hold two crustal blocks together until the land decides whether to tear. The stairway cut into warm scoria goes down five steps and then is buried by new salt, because they stopped building it when the ground changed its mind. They do not mourn unfinished work. To finish a structure here would be to argue with the world, and they are not here to argue. They are here to listen, to flex, to guide the enormous forces without being crushed by them.

They tend the cracks. When a fissure opens with its ripping sigh and exhales warm, struck-flint air, they gather at the lip. They do not look down into the orange heat. They crouch, and one of them places a hand—a slab of anhydrite—against the new rim. The rock stops trembling for a moment. The fissure’s glow steadies. Then the being steps back, and its palm is filmed with a crust of new crystal. It feeds on that, not by eating but by incorporating the mineral into its own dry body, growing another layer of scoria, another facet of obsidian.

They are the ones who leave the marks on the rock faces. The long parallel grooves that turn at right angles are not messages. They are the tracks of their thinking. When one of them works on a stone, its fingers—slender plates of gypsum or salt—drag across the surface, leaving those grooves. A spiral is the record of a decision that doubled back on itself. Rows of shallow pits are where a hand rested while it listened to a distant collapse. They write by pressure and subtraction. They never add paint or cord or metal; they only remove or align. In this anhydrous world, addition is a kind of violence. They work by taking away.

They navigate by the unreliability of distance. A feature that looked close is a kilometer away; a distant spire seems to stand just at your shoulder. The inhabitants know this. They do not measure distance in meters. They measure it in how long a sound takes to arrive through the stone, or how far a shadow leans the wrong way. Their own shadows do not obey the light here. Sometimes a shadow points toward the only glowing fissure; sometimes toward no light at all; sometimes it detaches and lies on the ground like a dark salt stain while the being walks on without it. They follow their shadows as often as their shadows follow them, and sometimes the two trade places.

The doorway-shaped opening in the basalt wall—the one too smooth to be a natural fracture, with no chisel marks and no rubble—is theirs. They did not carve it. They stood before the rock and folded a piece of themselves into absence, and the doorway formed as a negative crystal, a vacancy in the mineral. They do not all enter. Some of them stand before it for long intervals, stone against stone, and the dark beyond the doorway becomes, for a while, occupied by something that is not quite them. It is the world’s unfinished side, the space where the next layer of creation is still cooling.

There are no animals here, no cries. The inhabitants are the only audience for the plateau’s few performances: the slow swelling of a salt pan into blisters, the burst of white needles, the fall of a pinnacle with its low, late sound arriving through the ground. They stand at the edge, not looking at the collapse but into their own mineral bodies, where the same forces are at work. They are not separate from the land. They are the land when it becomes legible, the plateau’s pressure given a spine and a task.

At the far edge of the high country, where the tableland drops vertically into blackness, one of them sometimes stands. It does not look down into the unseen. It looks out into the blackness that is not empty but unfinished. Its body is a column of black basalt and reddish scoria, banded with salt. Its head is a rhomb of gypsum, glowing faintly from within. It raises one arm—a limb of interlocked crystal plates—and for a moment its hand opens, and a thin white salt flower blooms from the palm. That is the only gesture that looks like prayer. Then the ground shifts. A fissure behind it glows with slow red heat. The figure is gone, not walked away but reabsorbed into the mineral dark, as if it had been only a fold in the rock returning to the place where the world is still being made.
