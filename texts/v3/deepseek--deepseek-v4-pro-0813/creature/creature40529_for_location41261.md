The ones who live in that basin are not named by a word. They are named by a sound: the click of two stones at a particular distance, returned by the rock in a particular shape. If you ask them what they are, they may tap a slow phrase against the bedrock—a low strike, a high strike, a pause. The echo means something close to *the ones who listen through the stone*.

They are low and broad. Their bodies have been shaped by the same subtraction as the land. There is no softness to them except around the eyes and throat. Their skin is the color of wet basalt, fish-belly white in the pools, and it is rarely clean: rust-red stains on forearms from the bacterial mats, sulfur-yellow dust around nostrils, black mineral lines in the cracked soles of their feet. They are hairless over most of the body; what hair remains is pale and fine, kept braided or cut close, so the wind cannot use it to pull at them. Their eyes are large, set a little too far forward, dark as the potholes; they blink slowly, as if the eyelid is reluctant to close off the light. Their ears lie flat, small, and can shut like the mouth of a shell. Their hands are wide, the nails worn to nothing, the knuckles thickened from knocking stone. But their feet are the truest part of them. The soles are thick, gray, crossed with cracks that do not bleed; they can tell whether the tide is turning by the way the cold moves through the rock. When they stand still, they are not stopping. They are reading.

They dress in what the place provides: long-neck hide scraped and oiled until it repels water; eel-skin leggings stitched with sinew, shining like wet rubber; vests made from overlapping shell-plates; capes of pale fish-skin that glisten in the dimness. They carry no metal. Their tools are bone hooks, stone hammers, knapped basalt knives, needles of split fishbone, cord braided from sinew and long-neck tendon. They wear small stones around the neck, drilled through by years of current in a pothole. Those stones are not decoration; they are names.

Their shelters are not so much built as kept. They choose places where the roar drops into a pocket of quiet—behind a rock wall, under a low overhang, in a defile between two bedrock ridges—and then they add to what is already there. The walls are stacked slabs fitted so tightly that wind-driven water cannot enter. The roof is a frame of whale-rib or water-worn wood, covered with layers of hide. From outside, a shelter looks like a rockfall that has learned to breathe; smoke seeps from a vent no wider than a hand, and the only sign of recent use is a fresh hide pegged over the doorway. Inside, there is no furniture, only a stone lamp burning rendered animal fat, a bed of eel-skin stuffed with dry shells and matted hair, and racks of hooks, hammers, and cord. They do not stand inside. They crouch, sleep, and eat close to the floor, because the floor tells true things.

They speak two languages. One is a close language of clicks, throat-sounds, and short hisses, used at arm's length in the quiet pockets. It is enough for hunger, danger, laughter, grief. The other language is the stone. Each person carries two stones, one dark and one light. The light one is tapped against the bedrock in patterns; the dark one is held to the ear or pressed against the mastoid bone. The echo comes back changed, and that change is meaning. A phrase is not the tap; a phrase is the return. They can send a message across a mile of channels in a few seconds, if they know the stone. They can also tell from the echo where the listener stands, whether a pothole is about to drain, and whether the tide is pushing the channels backward. Children learn to read the stone before they learn to walk. A mother taps a pattern on the floor beside her sleeping infant, and the infant's hand is placed on the rock so the vibration enters the body. By the time they are old enough to fish, they can tap their own name and hear it return.

Their food is all animal, all mineral, none of it green. They are fishers and gatherers, not farmers. They set fish weirs across the channels: low spirals of fitted stone that turn the current back on itself, funneling the eels into traps woven from eel-skin and sinew. During the eel runs, when the channels run silver-black, they work in silence, lifting the traps and splitting the catch on windward rocks. The wind and salt air dry the flesh; they do not build fires for that. Some of the catch is fermented in sealed potholes, pressed down with stones, left until the taste goes sharp and mineral. They gather the shelled things from the clicking fields, but only when the shellfield falls silent. They do not cross a shellfield when it is singing. They do not say why in words anyone outside can hear. It is one of the rules.

They fish the deeper pools with bone hooks and braided sinew lines. They take the pale fish, but they cook them before eating; raw, the flesh is too cold and does something to the gut. They render fat from the long-necked animals, though they rarely hunt them. They follow the long-necks at a distance, because the long-necks break channels open and stir the eels into motion. When an old long-neck separates from the herd, the people may take it, using a harpoon and a line, and they say a low chest-sound to it so it will not be startled. They use every part: hide for roofs, bones for frames, sinew for cord, meat for days, tallow for lamps.

The other predators—the black ribbon-shaped things that strike from the pools—are given their distance. The people have no spoken name for them. They tap a warning: two sharp strikes and a long silence. When the black ribbons are running, the people pull their weirs and wait. The bass bellowing of the long-necks is felt through the ground, not only heard; the people feel it as a change in the stone's pressure. Sometimes they answer it with their own low hum, not to call the animals, but to let them know a person is near, so neither will be startled.

There are no gods here, in the sense of beings to be worshipped. There are instructions. The people are keepers of instructions. The rules are not written; they are tapped. Never trust a still pool. If a pothole drains, step back from the rim. Do not cross a shellfield when it is singing. If a standing slab hums, move inland and wait. The tide does not follow the moon; it follows the basin's own long breathing. They do not think of the basin as a machine. They think of it as a body. The potholes are mouths. The channels are veins. The slabs are ears or teeth, depending on the wind. To live there is to be inside a body that is still breathing.

The standing slabs, polished on their water-facing sides, are tended. They are not cleaned, exactly; they are listened to. When a slab hums, the people stop, turn inland, and wait. They say the slab is speaking to the tide, and a person should not interrupt. The fog-shaped things, the too-tall too-narrow shapes that hold for a few seconds in the lee of the rock, are a different matter. The people do not say they are alive. They do not say they are not. They teach the children: if the echo comes back too clean, with no softening at the edges, do not go that way. A clean echo means the fog has folded over something. The children do not ask what. They learn to hear the difference before they learn to explain it.

They are not many. A cluster may be four, seven, twelve people, seldom more than the channels can feed without stripping them. They are mostly mothers and daughters, an old man or two who mind the weirs, and children too young to read the stone. There is no word for belonging in the close language. There is a word for sharing a lamp. People come, stay, leave; the shelters are old, and no one owns them except by use. A child is born behind the whale-rib walls, and the first sound it is given is not a spoken name but a tapped pattern on the floor beside its body. If the child lives long enough, that pattern is what the echo answers.

Death is simple. The dead are not buried; there is no soil. They are carried to a pothole that opens downward, one that drains not into the channels but into a deeper place. The body is lowered with a final tap, and the name-stone is dropped after it. Then the people listen until the echo changes. They say the basin remembers them as a pattern in the current.

They are not exactly human anymore, or not only human. They are the result of the place, the same process that smoothed the bedrock and wore away every unnecessary edge. They are what was left after a long subtraction. They do not fight the roar. They listen through it. They mend the weirs, not because the weirs are broken, but because mending is how they stay in conversation with the tide.

And then there are the other lives, the ones that are not people but are no less at home. The eels, small and silver-black, run in thousands, a single ribbon of muscle and intention. The shelled things, tiny helms, shift by the million, and their rising and falling click is like rain on a metal roof. The pale fish circle in the deep pools, their spines visible through their bodies. The long-necked animals step through the fog on broad padded feet, bellowing at a frequency that is felt as much as heard, their calls passing through the mist in patterns too regular to be coincidence. The black ribbon predators strike and vanish. The bacterial mats, rust-red and sulfur-yellow, spread across the stone, eating minerals and dissolved rock. All of them, together, make the place a living body. The people are only one of its senses.
