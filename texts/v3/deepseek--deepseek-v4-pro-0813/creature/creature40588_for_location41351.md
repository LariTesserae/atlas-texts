The first thing to understand is that the place is not inhabited. It is itself an inhabitant, too old and too large to be a creature, but alive in the way a slow fever is alive. And yet there are others—or there is one other, repeated many times.

The mats are the first mind. They are the oldest living thing: a film one cell thick, breathing in slow blisters, covering every stone and puddle and salt crust. They do not think in thoughts; they think in minerals, in the bloom of rust and sulfur, in the way light bends through a tear in the world. But where the noise is damped by one of the old stone stitches, a mat can thicken. It folds over itself. It begins to hum. That is where the Keepers come from.

Call them the Keepers, or the Stitch-Walkers. They have no name of their own. If you asked them, they would not understand the question. Their nearest word is a low pressure wave that means something like *the ones who hold the line*. They are long, soft, and almost transparent. They move through the mud like eels, but they are not eels. They are more like canals of water that have learned to remember a shape. Their skin is the same film as the mats, folded many times over. They have no eyes, because light here is a lie. They have no ears, because sound is everywhere, a physical pressure that never stops. Instead they have a row of small bell-shaped organs along their undersides, each tuned to a different frequency of the world’s roar. These bells are their senses, their voice, and their hands.

A Keeper begins when a patch of mat, perhaps after a storm or the bursting of a mud dome, finds itself wrapped around a place where reality has thinned too far. The mat does not die. It thickens. It draws iron and sulfur and salt from the water. It begins to pump, not blood, but warm mud. After many years it has become a pale, mouthless grub. Then it lengthens. The bells form, one by one, along its belly. It learns to sing the mud into carrying it.

Their movement is not walking or swimming. They sing the ground into loosening ahead and firming behind, so they are always a kind of current. They leave no tracks, but the mud remembers them for a few seconds: a long, graceful absence. When they move, the water nearby runs slightly more downhill. The distance between one stone and another becomes a little more reliable. That is not magic. It is memory. A Keeper’s body is full of remembered rules. It carries a small patch of the world where things still make sense.

The stone stitches are what happens when a Keeper has grown old. After centuries, the bells fall silent one by one. The body stiffens. The skin turns to that pale, fine-grained, cold stone that comes from no local rock. The Keeper chooses a place—usually a place where the horizon folds and the rain rises upward—and settles. It does not die. It becomes a line, a circle, a pillar. Its body straightens or curls, and its memory becomes a local law. That is why the air is quieter near the stones. That is why the water runs downhill more often. Someone is remembering the rules there. The standing pillar of black glass is not a monument. It is an ancestor.

The Keepers are rare, but they are not lonely. They meet in the troughs between waves of sound, where silence is only relative. They do not speak in words. They exchange pressure-memories: a long, vibrating account of a storm that passed through twice, or the taste of a new mud dome, or the shape of a horizon that bent. They may stand side by side for a season, facing nothing, while their bells ring in slow counterpoint. Then they separate, each carrying a copy of the other’s memory. They do not have families. They do not even have individuals, not the way we do. Each one is a fold in the same old film, a finger of the same mind that has been thinking since the mountains were ground down.

The eel-things in the mud are not separate animals. They are the Keepers’ arms, or their children, or their dreams. A Keeper may send out a dozen of them to taste the edges of a frayed place, to measure how far the world has forgotten itself. The pale frog-like creature that sits on a stone for a whole season is a young Keeper learning the first rule: to be still in a world that will not be still. It pulses its throat until its rhythm matches the steam vent. When it has learned, it sinks into the mud and begins to grow the bells.

The crustaceans are the Keepers’ food, but only when the water is warm enough. They rise from the sediment in small clouds and are absorbed through the skin, their shells dissolving into the Keeper’s body like sugar in water. The black reeds are not eaten; they are used as sounding sticks. A Keeper may tap a reed against a stone, and the air around it will fold into a softer shape, a small pocket of quiet where a thought can last longer than a breath.

The bell that rings every thirteen seconds is the pulse of an old Keeper whose body is no longer entirely present. It is a stitch so old that only its sound remains. The Keepers use it as a lighthouse. When it rings, the mud holds still for a moment. The horizon straightens. The rain remembers to fall down. Then the sound passes, and the world frays again.

If you meet one, you will not see it at first. You will feel the noise begin to organize. The mud under you will firm. A shape will rise not out of the ground but as a change in the ground: a long, blind, translucent thing, like a root made of water, with a row of bell-teeth that do not bite. It will turn its whole body toward you, and your bones will hum. It is not hostile. It is simply reading you. It may wait for you to speak, but your words will be too quick and too small. It will lean close, and the air will smell of sulfur and overripe fruit. Then it will pass through you, not unkindly, and you will know it has tasted the shape of your life and found it very brief. After it leaves, the ground where you stand will be slightly more solid for a few hours. That is the only gift it can give.

There are not many of them. A few hundred, maybe fewer. But because distance is unreliable, they are never far. They are the last living things in a place that has outlived its own geography, and they are the reason it has not completely come apart. They do not know this as a duty. It is just what they are. The mats are their flesh. The stone stitches are their elders. The sound is their breath. The place is not empty. It is full of them.
