After a time, you realize the Sumpworks is not as empty as it first seemed. Not because you hear a voice or see a face, but because the space has been shaped by something that moves through it.

There are signs. A cleared patch on a catwalk where the black iron is polished by repeated touch. A small cup made from a halved valve, hanging from a pipe by a twist of copper wire, still beaded with condensation. A set of tools laid out in order on a folded square of oiled cloth—hammer, thin chisel, brass brush, a glass vial of oil. A narrow track on the iron floor plates, worn smooth by a route walked thousands of times. The yellow moss is not random. In some places it has been trimmed back from a valve wheel with careful cuts. In others it has been encouraged to grow along a weeping pipe, its bright color forming a deliberate border.

Then you see the one who lives here.

At first you mistake it for a bundle of pipe lagging, or a shadow cast by a tank. It stands at the edge of the central channel, very still, watching the dark water slide past. It is tall and thin, bent forward at the neck, with arms that hang too long. Its skin is the color of wet clay, and its back and shoulders are covered with a short, dense growth of yellow moss, like a porous hide. White root-threads run from its scalp and elbows and knees, trailing in the steam, some of them anchoring into cracks in the wall. Its face is narrow, the cheeks hollow, the eyes large and dark with a faint orange reflection from the furnace mouths. It wears no clothes except a harness of black iron and copper, riveted at the joints, with loops holding its tools. One arm is not flesh; it is a brass piston with a three-fingered clamp at the end. The other hand is wrapped in cloth to the knuckles, and the cloth is soaked through with mineral water.

It moves.

The movement is slow, deliberate. It places each bare foot flat on the iron plates, as if listening for a change in the ringing. It steps over the central cut without looking, knowing exactly where the sharp banks are. It reaches up to a pipe, lays its hand on it, and cocks its head. The piston arm gives a soft hiss, then relaxes. It moves on.

It tends.

It goes to the fern growing from a cracked ceramic insulator and picks off a yellowed frond. It chews it slowly, then spits a paste of green and white root-fiber into the crack around the insulator, pressing it in with its fingers. The fern shudders, as if pleased. It opens the door of a small hopper a hand’s breadth, peers inside, and adds a few pellets of mineral scale from a pouch at its belt. It wipes the glass of a pressure gauge with its moss-covered sleeve, then taps the gauge twice with a knuckle, listening. The needle trembles. It does not smile; it simply nods to itself.

It collects condensation.

At a place where a pipe elbow weeps, it holds the copper cup under the drip and waits. The drip comes every few seconds. It stands there for a long time, patient as the rock, until the cup is a quarter full. Then it drinks, not greedily, but in small sips, closing its eyes. The water runs down its chin and into the moss on its neck. It uses the last drops to moisten the white root-threads at its wrist.

It prays.

In the chapel-like alcove, it pauses before the pressure gauge mounted as an icon. It does not kneel; it simply stands and places its forehead against the warm glass. Its lips move, but no sound comes out except a faint tick, tick, like a gear settling. Perhaps it is speaking to the city. Perhaps it is reading the pressure of its own blood. The gauge reads 3.1 bar, and the needle does not move. The word **MAIN** is stenciled on the bulkhead beside the alcove, and the Keeper—if that is its name—touches the letters with the back of its moss-covered hand, as if they were the name of a god, or perhaps its own.

It sleeps.

At the north end, near the turbine pit, there is a locker with the door slightly ajar. Inside is not spare valves but a bed of mineral wool and yellow moss. The Keeper folds itself into the locker, knees drawn up, the piston arm folded across its chest. The steam pipe that runs through the locker gives off a low, steady heat. The moss on its back rises and falls with its breathing. When the rain cycle begins, the Keeper does not wake. The drizzle lands on its bare feet, which stick out of the locker, and trickles down to the floor. The locker is not locked. It is simply a place to be small in a place that is too loud with its own heartbeat.

It is not alone, but it is the only animal in the Sumpworks—and even it is not entirely an animal anymore. The plants are rooted; the machines are bound. The Keeper is the one thing that moves between them, the soft connective tissue of the city. It does not control the Sumpworks; it is the Sumpworks’ sense of touch, its quiet repair, its memory of having hands. It may have been human once, or it may be what the city grew in the damp dark when no one was looking. It does not matter.

What matters is that it is there, and it will be there when the next rain comes. It will stand in the open, face tilted up, mouth open, letting the warm drizzle wash the mineral dust from its eyes. The moss on its shoulders will brighten to the color of a warning lamp. The white root-threads will uncurl and drink. For four minutes, it will look less like a person and more like a plant, or a pipe, or a piece of the city that has learned to breathe. Then the rain will stop, and it will fold back into the steam, and you will not see it again unless you know where to look—for the one patch of yellow moss that moves, for the ticking that is not a gear but a heartbeat, for the narrow track on the iron floor that leads to a locker in the north end, where the steam pipe enters and the city keeps its only living voice.
