The people who live in the Underworks do not call it home. They call it “the floor,” and themselves the floor watch. They are mechanics, divers, electricians, pump tenders, and one person who is trained to cook in a steel room and also to listen. They are not settlers. They are a rotation of long visitors, but their lives are shaped by the place more than by the surface they return to.

The bunkhouse is a long yellow steel cylinder bolted to the deck near Pump House 6. It has a white stripe around its middle, a small crane, and a moon pool that opens into a wet porch. The water there is dark and contained, and the current outside does not pull at you. Inside, the air is warm and dense and smells of neoprene, soap, hot electronics, and a faint boiled-vegetable smell that nobody can trace. The walls are white, the floor is diamond plate, and everything has a handrail. There are eight bunks, a galley table, a control room with pressure screens, and a tool room full of spare parts. The lights are never all off. A red lamp glows over the moon pool; a blue lamp glows over the hatch.

The crew live in coveralls and thick socks and rubber boots. They are pale, and their hands are cracked from salt and cleaning solvent. They eat from plastic trays. They sleep under nylon cargo nets so the pump cycles won’t roll them out of bed. They do not speak much, but they talk constantly through the radios: valve states, flow rates, filter pressures, which hatch is open, who is outside, who is wet.

They do not call the sea the sea. They call it the working fluid, or just “the water.” “Open” means white lights. “Closed” means red lights. “Blue” means safe to enter or leave the water. A hot spot is not a place but a warm outflow plume where the fish gather. A fog bank is a cloud of industrial silt rolling across the deck. The weather is not the weather overhead; it is the current in the machinery. A storm is a pressure change that makes the hull creak. Day and night do not matter. Shift change matters.

When the shift horn sounds, it comes through the hull as much as through the air. The crew wakes, drinks something hot, and gathers in the control room. The whiteboard lists intakes on reduced flow, anodes replaced, traps due to open. The offgoing watch speaks in abbreviations. The oncoming watch repeats them. Then the outside crew suits up and goes out into the green-grey light.

Outside, they move slowly. They use surface-supplied hoses or closed-circuit rebreathers, because the work is long and the water is full of edges. They walk on the deck as if it is ground, but they know it is a floor above darker spaces. There are hatches down to ballast tanks and cable runs, and those are fully dark except for the white line of a headlamp. No one goes down alone. A marker is left on the hatch.

They clear screens. They brush mussel collars off the intake bars. They do not remove mussels from the upper legs unless the weight threatens a joint. They unhook crabs from pipe mouths and put them back on the deck. They inspect zinc anodes, change filter socks, tighten valve packing, grease the crane rail, and clean the camera lenses. The work is not heroic. It is just maintenance, done with the sea pressing against every surface.

They know the animals. Not all of them, but the regulars. There is a large lobster living in the mouth of Culvert 17; the crew calls it the supervisor. It does not move when they pass. There is a cormorant that follows the tram because the tram stirs up small fish. There is a sea star on the north leg of transformer platform 4 that has not moved in three weeks. The crew checks on it as they pass. They do not touch it.

The animals are the real permanent inhabitants. Mussels, barnacles, sea stars, anemones, lobsters, and the small silver fish. They live in the pipe mouths and valve wheels and the grooves of the grating. Some of the animals have adapted to the machines: the rock crabs gather under the sediment traps because food is stirred there; the small cod hold in the warm plume near Pump 3. In the dark spaces below the deck hatches, where no light reaches, there are pale amphipods and flatworms in the muck. They came with the sea, and they stayed. The Underworks does not drive them away.

The people adapt too. They learn the floor. Okafor, the pump mechanic, can lie in his bunk and tell which valve is opening by the sound through the hull. He says the old valve on Pump 4 sounds like a man clearing his throat, and the new one sounds like a knife on a sharpening stone. He is not being poetic. That is the sound. Mara, the watch lead, keeps a green notebook. She writes down water clarity, plume temperature, and the number of sea stars on each column. She has noticed that the kelp around the warm outfall grows more toward the red lights than the white ones. She records it because it is a fact.

They sleep when it is dark, but it is never dark. They wake when the pumps quit because the silence is too clean. They know the fish. If the school around the bunkhouse suddenly opens, a striped bass is passing. If the cormorants leave, a surface storm is coming. They live inside this information as much as inside the bunkhouse.

They are not prisoners. A hitch lasts six to ten weeks, and then they decant to the surface in the yellow bell, stopping to decompress. Some come back. Those who return say the silence on land is worse than the noise. They say they miss the floor. They mean the deck. They miss knowing which valves are open, which lights are blue, and which pumps are cycling. They miss being small but necessary.

But the Underworks does not miss them. When the bunkhouse is empty, the machines cycle on. The locks fill and drain. The crane swings. The fish scatter and re-form. The sea stars climb the legs of the pump house. The cormorants dive. The surface buoys rock. The red lights glow. The place is already inhabited.
