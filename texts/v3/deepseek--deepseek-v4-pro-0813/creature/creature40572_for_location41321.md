First, there are the mats. They come before the people and will come after—pale blue-gray fuzz on the warm vent lips, sulfur-yellow crusts on the upper rocks, rust-black stains in the slag. They grow on gas and metal dust, not on sunlight, and they are the true floor of the Throat’s food web. Then come the grazers: glass-flies drifting in electric sheets, salt-lopers stepping over the white pan, blind crickets waving at the heat, smelt-mites boring through clinker. They are abundant not in spite of the dark and the dryness but because of them. The city’s pipes, heat-sinks, and busbars have made accidental gardens of the ledges.

Then there are the people.

They call themselves the Throat-born, or sometimes the Salt People, though neither name is quite what they mean in their own quiet language. They are not water-people. Their bodies run on the same chemistry as the rest of the vent: esters, sulfur compounds, iron, salts. The fluid inside them is a thin, pale amber, thick as warm oil, and it smells faintly of bitter minerals when a cut is fresh. Water, when it condenses on a cold pipe or beads inside a glass trap, is not drunk. It is collected like a relic, sealed in glass, and looked at for a long time. Water is not a currency. It is a legend, a substance from the blue world beyond the rim, and to hoard it is considered a kind of dreaming.

A resident of the Throat is small by lowland standards—compact, deliberate, built for ladders and narrow bridges. They stand perhaps four feet tall, with long arms and strong, splayed hands. Their skin is a dry integument, smooth from a distance but fine-scaled up close, and its color shifts with the level where a family has lived: pale salt-gray on the upper terraces, rust-red and basalt-black below. Their eyes are large, round, and completely dark, with a faint copper glint when an arc lamp catches them. They blink rarely, and a third lid closes sideways to clear dust and acid film. Their mouths are small and nearly lipless, kept pressed shut against the mineral air. They do not sweat water. When they overheat, they excrete a fine oily salt through the scales of their neck and wrists, and they scrape it off with a soft blade and save it.

They breathe through two paths. One is a small, narrow nose. The other is a pair of fine spiracles behind the jaw, hidden beneath the high collars of their coats. In the lower levels, where the gas is heavy and the air is full of vent-breath, they close the upper passages and draw air through the spiracles, filtering it through a bed of blue-gray fuzz that grows in the collar. This is why a high collar is both practical and polite. To leave the spiracles bare in public is a little like walking around with your teeth out.

Their feet are wide and flat, with soft soles, and they read the mountain through them. A Throat-born can stand on a ledge and feel whether it is settling, whether the rock behind the brick is cracking, whether the gas below has shifted. Children are taught to walk at the same time they are taught to listen. Their first shoes are felted booties with a bare patch at the toe, so the stone can speak to them.

Speech, in the Throat, is only one language, and not the most important. The spoken tongue is a low, palatal clicking interspersed with soft hums and the release of held breath. It is never loud. A true shout is almost a mortal sin, because a sharp sound can bring down rock. The word for “loud” in their language is the same as “rockfall.” But most conversation happens in the hands. There is a full grammar of finger-shifts and knuckle-turns, capable of expressing love, danger, prices, poetry, and technical instruction. Over distance, they use lamp-flash—red, amber, white—through little shutter-houses on every wide ledge. At night the city is strung with flickering sentences.

The people are not idle. The city is dense and always rearranging, and every resident has a task. Some are riggers, hanging in leather harnesses over the shaft, testing bolt holes with a brass sounder and their bare palms. Some are foundry workers, wrapped in waxed canvas and copper mesh, their skin pocked with tiny arc-burns they call *arc-bloom*. Some are mite-sifters, crouching in the slag heaps with soft brushes and vibration probes, following the rust-red tunnels of the smelt-mites to harvest the beads of reduced copper and iron the creatures excrete. The mites are the secret of the city’s metal, and they are treated with rough respect. Their queen chambers are marked with white stones that no one moves.

There are trap-tenders who sweep glass-flies from the electrified screens, then sell the dried insects in market as food. There are gas-bleeders who walk the lower vent pipes in masks, smelling of sulfur and old batteries. There are bridge-riggers, cable-men, dish-wards, lamplighters, heat-weavers, and children who tend shallow trays of microbial film in the upper light. Even the elderly sit in their alcoves sorting copper beads under magnifying lenses. No one is idle, because the city is not a settled place. It is a process.

Home is small. A dwelling might be no larger than two arm-spans, built into a ledge or hung on steel hawsers. The bed is a hammock of woven cable and felt, strung between hooks that can be lowered in minutes. The hearth is a closed ceramic box fed by a thin gas pipe, never open unless the work demands it. Windows are made of mica or the glued wings of glass-flies, and they are small. Every home has a shelf of tools, a lamp, a clacker, and a drape of hush-cloth over anything that might rattle in a tremor.

Wealth is vertical. The upper terraces are brighter, cooler, cleaner; the air there smells less of sulfur and more of bare stone. Families who have lived high for generations are pale, their skin the color of old salt, and their clothes have fewer patches. The lower terraces are dark and warm, close to the gas mouths and the salt floor. The people there are darker—rust-red, oxide-black, sulfur-yellow—and they tend the smelters, the vent pipes, the electrolytic cells. There is no open war between levels, but there is a quiet pride and a quiet resentment. The word for someone too used to the light is *white-eyed*. The word for someone too used to the gas is *smoke-blood*. But both fear the same rockfall.

The market moves. It is a cluster of carts and cloth booths that changes level every few weeks, because yesterday’s market may now be unsafe or gone. Trade is small: coil of copper wire, iron beads in a dull bag, waxed rope, ceramic cups, jars of pickled glass-flies, blocks of pale microbial cake, vials of acid, blankets of felted setae. Prices are chalked on slate. When a tremor comes, the whole market freezes for a breath, then goes on. If the tremor continues, it folds in an hour, quietly, and reassembles somewhere else. No one calls it a disruption. It is just the city breathing.

The Salt Clock is the city’s heart. It is a broad bronze dish hung at the center of the shaft, fed by a single crystal of salt dropped from a glass tube at every minute. The crystal falls with a small *tink*—one of the only sounds that is never hushed. When the dish fills, which takes a long time, the city holds its only festival. The dish is carried slowly down the spiral stairs, past every level, to the white floor of the salt pan. People line the ways with small lamps. At the bottom, the salt is scattered across the pan, and for a day everyone walks on it, leaving footprints the wind will erase. The festival is not loud. It is a shared hush.

The people do not have a god in the usual sense. They have the Mountain, which is not a god but a fact. They call it the Unquiet One. They say the city is not built in the mountain; it is lodged in the mountain’s windpipe, and the mountain tolerates it only as long as it stays small and soft. They also honor the Vent, the warm breath that gives them heat and gas and small lives. Their closest thing to prayer is maintenance. Their scripture is the repair manual, passed down by hand and lamp-flash. They do not say, “May you be safe.” They say, “May the rock hold you gently,” which is less a hope than a command to test every anchor.

Their law is small and old, carved into the wall near the Salt Clock:

*Be small in the throat. Do not be loud. Do not waste heat. Do not trust the rock. Do not waste metal. Do not hoard water.*

The last rule is the strangest to an outsider. Water is beautiful to them, cold and clear and faintly poisonous, a liquid their bodies were never made to carry. It appears on pipes like a ghost and is collected in glass tubes, but it is not drunk. To drink it would be like drinking the moon. A child who hoards a droplet of it is thought to be dreaming of the surface, which is both a sin and a sadness.

Children are rare and cherished. When a child is born, the neighbors bring no gifts; they bring silence. For three days, all heavy work on that terrace stops, and the nearby bridges are rerouted a body-width. The child is given a name after something small that shines in the dark: Salt-Crystal, Copper-Mite, Lamp-Latch, Soot-Whisper, Low-Hum. They grow up learning to feel the rock before they learn to speak. Their toys are spools of copper wire, pet salt-lopers in little mesh pens, and kites made from glass-fly wings.

Death is frequent but not tragic. The dead are wrapped in old cloth and laid on a high, dry ledge called the Departure Shelf. Over time the body desiccates to a brittle figure; then the family returns with brushes and a small bronze pan. They brush the remains to powder and carry it down to the salt pan, where it is scattered among the salt-lopers and the white crust. The dead become part of the floor. In this way, the salt pan is the city’s only graveyard, and the white crust is partly ancestor.

At night, when the coin of sky has gone dark and the arc lamps have taken over, the city is a column of small lights. You can see a rigger crossing a bridge with a red lamp at her belt, a child selling cups of warm ester-broth on a corner ledge, a crew of mite-sifters coming up from the slag levels with their boots dusted red. The Salt Clock ticks softly at the center. The mountain shifts a fraction, and the city shifts with it. No one speaks above a whisper, but the whole place is saying, in hand-sign and lamp-flash and gentle vibration: *we are still here.*
