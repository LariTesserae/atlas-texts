The first thing to understand is that the Cold Nursery is not empty. It is sparsely inhabited, but the sparseness is a kind of fullness. The plants are the most numerous lives, but they are not residents; they are the architecture. The animals are rare, pale, and secretive. But there is another order of life here, the one that leaves the caches and cuts the notches and builds the small domes. They are the reason the place feels watched, and the reason the caches are not yet swallowed.

They do not live there the way a person lives in a house. They live the way a hand lives inside a glove, or the way a nerve lives inside a body. You can walk for weeks and not see one, but you will see the work of their hands, and the work is recent.

The ladder of notches in the vertical root is clean. No frost has formed in the cuts. The cord left coiled in the fiber dome is still damp from being used. The tallies on the wall have grown by one. The place is not abandoned. It is attended, lightly, between long intervals.

If you see one, it will first be a vertical mark against the glowing green—a root that is too straight, a stem that bends the wrong way. Then it will turn, and you will understand that the hand-carving with too many fingers was not a mistake.

They are tall, thin to the point of being architectural. Their bodies are like the place itself: pale, translucent, cold. Their skin is the color of old ice over new leaf, and there is light inside them, a slow green-white pulse that moves under the surface like sap. They have no hair. Their heads are long and smooth. Their eyes are wide, dark, lidless; they do not blink. Their mouths are seams. Their noses are small, and their ears are only small openings; they hear more through their palms and feet than through their heads. Their fingers are long and too many—seven on each hand—and each one bends backward at the last joint, which may be why they do not leave fingerprints, only comb-like marks where they have gripped a root.

They wear very little, but what they wear is functional: a harness of woven ice-silk and plant fiber, a few pouches, a blade of black chert at the hip, a coil of cord. The cord is not for binding; it is for crossing, for measuring, for leaving as a signal. Their feet are long, with toes that grip like roots.

When they move, they move slowly and exactly. They test every foothold before they trust it. They never put their full weight on dead material; the dead stuff is brittle and sings before it breaks, and they have learned to hear that song. They can climb a vertical root as easily as they walk. They can cross a gap by paying out line and letting the weighted end catch in the living growth on the far side. They can wait for a tendril to grow, and they do not seem to mind waiting. Sometimes you will see one motionless for hours, not sleeping, just listening with its palms pressed to the wood.

This is what they do: they tend. They are the keepers of the lattice. They check the caches placed where the plants will not overgrow them for a long time. They sharpen the chert blades. They re-tie the cords. They cut away brittle growth with a sound like clean breaking. They drink from leaf-pools. They eat sparingly—a handful of glowing lichen-scrape, a fish from a root-river where the water runs clear and cold. They use the small domes as way-stations, places to wait out a freezing fog or a spore-snow. They have fire-pits but do not light them; fire would be a wound here, and they have no need for heat. Their breath barely smokes.

They do not fight the growth. They are the growth’s own restraint, the part of the nursery that keeps it from tearing itself apart.

They are not numerous. They may be a few dozen in the whole sub-surface country, spread across hundreds of miles of root and air. They communicate over distance by tapping on the roots, low tones that travel through the living fibers. They do not speak the way a surface animal speaks. The carvings are their speech, or their memory. The spirals are maps of the three-dimensional net. The tallies count collapses and safe spans. The hand with seven fingers is not a portrait; it is a unit of measure, a signature of the body that made it.

And they are grown, not born. The big bladders the size of houses are not all empty. Some hold sleepers. When the time comes, a bladder splits, and one of them steps out, wet and glowing, already the size of an adult, with seven fingers pressing at the membrane. It already knows the task. It does not know the current state of the bridges, so it reads the tallies. The caches are for these new ones, and for the old ones who have begun to forget.

When one grows too old or too slow, it climbs down to a quiet place where the roots are dense. It sits. It does not die in the usual way. The roots recognize it; they grow over its legs, then its chest, then its hands. The light inside it passes into the wood. That is why the living growth sometimes forms domes and arches that look intentional, and why the carvings on living wood seem to grow. The ones who became part of the plants are still there, remembering.

If you meet one, it will not be afraid. It will look at you with its wide black eyes and see you as a temporary animal. It may leave you a gift—a fishhook, a coil of cord, a cut of edible new growth. It may lead you along the living ways, never the dead. If you die in the Cold Nursery, it will not be cruel. It will move your body to a place where the roots can use it. That is not a threat. It is just the rule of the place.

The Cold Nursery is not empty. It is full of life, spread thin over an enormous dark. The grazers, the blind fish, the pale moths are the loose thoughts of the nursery. The keepers are its hands. And the place is still growing them.
