No one lives here as a name. The place is inhabited the way a body is inhabited by cells—too many to count, too constant to notice as separate lives. If you wait, the inhabitants come into focus not as individuals but as a single living surface, a pulse laid over the stone.

The main lives are the frogs.

On the wet walls where seep water slides down in a glassy film, they sit so close that their bodies nearly touch. Each is no bigger than a thumb joint. Their skin is translucent, grey-white, faintly pearled with moisture. In the dim blue-grey light you can see the small dark knot of the heart, the pale loop of the gut, the shadow of the spine. Their eyes are black pinpricks, almost lost in the soft flesh. They do not leap. A frog that falls from the wall does not climb back. But falls are rare. Their toes grip the stone with a kind of exhausted certainty.

The call is not a song. It is one high, metallic note, thin as a wire drawn through wet glass. Each frog’s note is slightly different, so the chorus is not a chord but a dense, bright edge of sound. It never stops. It has no rhythm, no surge, no rest. The frogs call not for a season or a mate but to hold their territories. The wall is an array of territories, each the size of a spread hand, each occupied. A silent frog is a frog losing its place. A juvenile that cannot claim a territory waits in a damp crevice, listening for a gap in the ringing.

Above the pools, small pale flies hover in stationary loops. They are wax-colored, delicate, their wings clear as water. They do not seem to feed. They emerge from the water, split their old skins, rise into the cloud, and spend their brief lives suspended in the same small circle. The cloud never disperses because the flies are always being replaced. Mating, dropping eggs back into the pool, dying, and being taken by something waiting at the edge—it all happens so steadily that the cloud itself becomes a kind of animal.

On the silt, the ground shimmers. It is not water. It is millions of springtails moving by millimeters, too small to see one at a time, but together making the floor appear alive, as if the dust itself has a pulse. Among them move tiny white beetles, glossy as teeth, and other pale things without names. They feed on the dead and the dying, on shed skins and fallen flies, on the small white flakes that drift down from the roof.

The crickets sit on the boulders. They are long-legged and thin, with antennae finer than hair and longer than their bodies. They do not sing in rhythm. They scrape their legs together to make a dry hiss, like sand falling on stone. It is a patient sound, almost not a voice. They sit motionless for hours, then move once, then are still again. They wedge grass stems into crevices to pry out grubs, and when the stem breaks, they leave it where it falls.

The centipedes are pale and slow, as long as a hand. They ripple through the moss, each leg moving in a wave from head to tail. They are not in a hurry because there is no hurry. They feed on the frogs sometimes, or on the white beetles, or on the soft bodies of flies that have fallen from the clouds. They are fed upon in turn by larger, blinder things that lie at the edges of the fly pools.

Those larger things have no fixed shape. They are pale, soft, the color of old candle wax. Their mouths stay open. They do not chase. The world comes to them. When a fly cloud drifts too low, they suck in a few flies with a movement so small it is almost not a movement. Their skin is loose and wet. They have no eyes, only small dark pits where eyes might once have been. They lie in the wet moss or at the edge of a clear shaft and wait. They are not dangerous to anything larger than a fly. They are simply part of the stillness, the open mouths of the place.

In the pools, eyeless shrimp hang in the clear water. They are translucent, and you can see the tiny stones inside them. They carry pebbles to anchor their burrows, then drop the pebbles and carry another, endlessly, as if the act of carrying is the point. Flat fish, pale as silt, lie on the bottom. They twitch only when a drip hits the surface. They do not swim unless touched.

There are no built structures. No wall, no path, no fire, no tool left behind. A cricket uses a grass stem and drops it. A frog hammers a snail against a stone and leaves the shell. A shrimp carries a pebble and lets it fall. The tools return to the stone. Nothing accumulates. The place is untouched by human hands, but not untouched by life. It is full of life that leaves no record of itself.

The deepest thing is that nothing changes. The light is constant. The temperature is constant. The seeps and drips fall at the same rate. The animals do not migrate or hibernate or breed in bursts. They breed, feed, and die in a continuous, steady pulse. The same number of frogs sits on the same wall because each territory is always occupied. When one dies, the springtails find it within minutes. They strip it to a small pale skeleton, and the skeleton dissolves into the wet stone. A juvenile frog climbs from the nearest damp crevice, takes the empty patch of wall, inflates its throat, and begins the same note. The chorus does not waver.

This is not a place of beginnings and endings. It is a place of replacement.

No one lives here who could tell you about it. The inhabitants are not people. They are flesh, chitin, wet skin, and the constant ringing call. They are the population of one long note. To stand among them is to feel like the only variable—your breathing, your movement, your heartbeat the only changes. The frogs call on. The drips fall. The eyeless things open their mouths. The place has found its one note and will not stop sounding it.
