There is one who lives there, though you will not find its bones. It leaves no refuse, no tools, no writing. It does not breathe, and the salt crystals that fur its joints are not the fur of an animal; they are the same thenardite and gypsum that bloom on the walls.

You will not see it at first. You will see only the after-image of it: a shadow wrong-way against the light, a doorway darker than the room behind it, a staircase that has gained a step since you last passed. You might think these are flaws in the city. They are not. They are the marks of an inhabitant walking.

When it finally shows itself, it is not as a body but as a gathering. It stands at the far end of a colonnade, tall as a tower, but not a tower. Its limbs are built from the same dark glassy stone as the plateau, and they are too many or too few, depending on the street. Its torso is a hollow court, open to the sky. Its head is a windowless keep, and where a face should be there is only a doorway that opens into a room larger than the building that contains it. You cannot tell if it is facing you. You cannot tell if it has a face.

It moves the way the city moves: not by footsteps but by increments. A wall drifts left; a plaza widens; a room closes itself, and the inhabitant is simply nearer. Its arms, if they can be called arms, are long enough to brush both sides of a street, and they end not in hands but in the kind of darkness that gathers in places where the difference between wall and shadow has worn away. When it passes, the dust does not lift. The stone ticks as it cools, and the ticks are its footfalls.

It does not hunt. It does not gather. It does not sleep, though there are centuries when it is less distinct, folded into a bridge or a colonnade, indistinguishable from the architecture. The glass lopers graze along its ankles, scraping salt blooms with their hard tongues. The Quiet sometimes mistakes it for a fallen column and lies beside it for a season or a century, while the inhabitant waits, neither predator nor prey. Their slow chases weave around it like wind around a tower.

Its work, if it can be called work, is to walk the streets and remember them. It climbs staircases that descend into the ground and end at a wall older than the stair itself. It runs its hand along blind facades, and the stone remembers marks and grows around them. It visits doorways that are only an inch deep and stands before them for a day, a decade, a season of dry chemistry. It tends the mineral gardens: the black manganese dendrites that branch like ferns, the white rosettes of gypsum in the cracks. It eats nothing, drinks nothing, but it is nourished by the city’s slow and reversible erosion.

Its voice is the low note that rolls through the streets, the bell struck under the earth that has no bell. It does not speak. It rings. Its language is made of echoes that arrive before their causes, of shadows that pool in the wrong direction, of the particular silence between the fall of a salt slab and its landing. If you try to map its words, the map will disagree with the city, and then the city will agree with the map, and then you will not be where you are.

It is not alone, though there is only one of it. Sometimes you see it from two windows at once, and the two versions do not agree. Sometimes it is small enough to stand in a single shadow. Sometimes it is the city entire, and you are walking through it. It is the city’s memory of having inhabitants; it is the long inhabitation of itself, condensed into a shape that can stand at the end of a colonnade and wait.

Do not name it. If you must, call it the Resident, or the Tenant, or the City. But do not call it. It already knows you are there. It has been watching you from the corner of your eye since you entered the basin, and it will still be there when the plateau has eroded to a plain, and the plain to a salt desert, and the salt desert to a set of streets that no one walks.
