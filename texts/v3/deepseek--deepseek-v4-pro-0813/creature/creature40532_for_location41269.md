You have been in the room for a long time. Long enough that the cold has stopped feeling like cold and become simply the texture of the air, the way the dark is the texture of space. Long enough that the sound has stopped being sound and become a pressure that holds you upright, a second spine. Your breath hangs in front of you, a small ghost that does not rise or fall, only dissipates into the mist with a patience that is almost animal.

At first you saw only the fauna, because the fauna is everywhere and because your eye was trained to count living things. The white mites on the valves looked like a mineral crust, the isopods like fur between the iron plates, the blind crickets like a woven mat on the back wall, their legs interlocked in a single still choreography. The bats on the ceiling formed a false insulation, water dripping through them in slow, faithful beads. Under the grating, the pale worms lay so thick in the black water that they seemed to be the floor of the pool rather than its inhabitants. The midges hung in the air, suspended in the mist, and you thought: this is a room of one season, a place where life has been sieved to a fine, white sediment.

But then, as you stood there, you began to notice that the shadows were not empty. Not because of the midges or the bats. The shadows had a different weight. In the corner where the half-sprayed concrete ended in rough lobes, where a row of uncapped pipe stubs protruded and went nowhere, there was a shape that you had taken for part of the wall. It was seated, or perhaps leaning, or perhaps growing out of the concrete itself. It did not move, and that was why you missed it—but then, as your eyes adjusted to the amber dimness, you saw that the shape was breathing.

The breath was almost imperceptible. The chest did not rise and fall in the way a human chest would. Instead, the surface of the shape—which at first looked like more grey-green lichen, like the same pale moss that grew in the corner—expanded and contracted so slowly that the condensation on its skin did not run. It simply thickened and thinned, a film of moisture that behaved like a membrane. The shape was not sitting. It was lodged, half-emerged from the concrete, as if the wall had been poured around it, or as if it had grown from the wall itself, a limestone deposit that had dreamed itself into a spine.

You crouched, though the grating bit into your knees, and you looked closer. The figure—for it was a figure, though not a human one—was pale, the color of wet plaster, its skin the same texture as the unsprayed concrete behind it. Its arms were folded across its chest, but the elbows were glued to the wall, the fingers splayed, each one ending in a thin, root-like extension that disappeared into the roughness of the formwork grain. Its legs were bent, the shins fused to the floor grating, the toes long and flattened, spreading into the gaps like poured lead. Its head was bowed, but not in sleep; the neck was too rigid, the jaw too set. The eyes were open.

They were small, round, and the same amber as the caged bulbs. They had no pupils, or rather, the entire eye was pupil—a polished, unblinking surface that reflected the room back at itself. The mist hung in front of them, but it did not condense on them. The eyes were dry, though the rest of the body was wet. They were not looking at anything. They were not looking at you. They were in a state of attention that had no object, a vigilance that had become identical to the room's stillness.

The mouth was closed, but not sealed. It was a thin line, a seam in the plaster-skin, and from it, very slowly, a thread of vapor rose. Not breath—it was too steady, too fine, and it did not pulse. It was as if the mouth were a leak, a place where the room's saturated air found a way out, or in. The vapor mingled with the fog and disappeared, and you realized that the figure had been doing this for so long that it had become part of the room's water cycle. It was not breathing the air; it was exchanging it. It was a valve.

You looked at its hands again. The root-like fingers were not roots. They were the same pale material as the worms in the black water, but dried, mummified, or perhaps simply in a state of suspension. They had the tight, waxy look of hibernation. And you saw that along the arms, along the ribs where the skin was thin, the white mites had formed a living sleeve. They were not feeding. They were not moving. They were simply there, a second skin, a colony that had settled into the figure's stillness like sediment into a riverbed. The isopods lay in the creases of the elbows, in the hollow of the collarbone, packed so densely that the figure appeared to be wearing a garment of translucent scales. A single blind cricket sat on the figure’s shoulder, its antennae resting against the figure’s ear, as if listening for something that would never come.

The figure was not the room’s inhabitant. The figure was the room, or at least the room’s seed. You understood this slowly, the way you understand the weight of water when you have been standing in it for too long. The room was unfinished—the concrete half-sprayed, the pipes uncapped, the gauges blank. And this figure was the room’s unfinishedness given a body. It was the form of the arrested moment, the “before” that had been caught and held. The room was embryonic, and this was the embryo: not a creature that would grow into something else, but a creature that had been frozen at the instant of its own conception. The room’s stasis was not a law imposed from outside; it was the figure’s own condition, radiating outward. The temperature stayed the same because the figure’s skin was the same temperature as the air. The water condensing on every surface was the figure’s own transpiration, slowed to a geological pace. The sound—the continuous chord of forced draft and condenser ring and electrical hum—was the sound of the figure’s heart, or its equivalent, a single note held without vibrato.

You looked up at the ceiling, at the white bats hanging in clusters. They were not separate animals. They were the figure’s dream of flight, shed like spores, each bat a fragment of the figure’s own stillness, multiplied. The worms below were the figure’s thought of depth, of sinking. The midges in the air were the figure’s exhalations, each one a tiny, winged stillness that had forgotten how to fly. The lichen on the copper, the moss in the corner, the white fungal body pushing through the cracked gasket—these were not flora. They were the figure’s slow, half-formed attempts at remembering what green was, what growth was. The room was a single organism, and the figure at its center was the nucleus, the part that had not yet divided.

You stood up. The figure did not move. The mites on its arms did not move. The water dripped through the bats, and the pool did not rise or fall. The sound pressed into you, and after a while it became a kind of silence, and in that silence you understood what lived here.

It was not the figure, exactly. It was the waiting. The figure was waiting for something, but not for you, not for rescue, not for time to resume. It was waiting for its own completion, for the moment when the wall would finish spraying itself, when the pipes would connect to something, when the needles would find their numbers. But that moment would never come, because the moment was already here, and here was a place without progression. The figure was the inhabitant of a landscape that had no future, and so it lived in the only way possible: by being exactly what it was, fully, without change. Its life was not a process; it was a state. The room said: nothing changes. The figure said: I am that nothing, and I am full.

You looked once more at the eyes. Amber, unblinking, reflecting the caged bulbs. They did not see you, but they saw everything, because there was nothing to see except the room, and the room was the figure’s own body. You were a temporary disturbance, a ripple in a still pool, and the ripple would pass. The room would return to its equilibrium. The figure would continue to breathe its slow vapor, to hold its pose, to dream its white, still fauna. The sound would continue. The water would continue. The cold would continue. And the figure would remain, half-emerged from the concrete, not waiting, not sleeping, not dead, but living in the only way this place allowed: as a permanent, patient, unformed thing.

That is what lives here. Not the mites, not the bats, not the worms. Not the lichen or the fungus. The thing that lives here is the one that is becoming the room and has not yet decided to stop. It is the room’s own future, held in amber. It is the moment before the first breath, drawn out into forever. You will not see it move, but it is alive. It is the most alive thing in the room, because it is the reason the room is alive at all. And when you finally turn and climb back through the serviceways, down into the machine-mountain, away from the cold and the sound and the dense white stillness, you will carry the image of those open, amber eyes with you—not as a memory, but as a question that has no answer, because the answer is still forming, and always will be.
