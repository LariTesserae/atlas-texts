**The Hollow Folk**

They are not the only life in the Hollow Country. There are the white-throated birds, the thumbnail frogs, the bats that spiral out of sinkholes at dusk. But the Hollow Folk are the only human presence that can be called resident—and even they are more like a season than a fixture.

They do not call themselves by that name. In their own sign, they are “the ones who keep the gaps,” or sometimes “those who sleep with the drum.” Travelers call them the Hollow Folk, the Bellowing People, the Stone-Listeners. They are human, but a human from the lowlands might not recognize them at first.

They are lean and long-limbed, not from hunger but from a life of moving through slots and over chasms. Their hands and feet are broad and calloused, the toes flexing for grip on slick stone. The soles of their feet are cracked and dark as the rock itself, and they walk with the whole foot placed flat, feeling the sound through the bone before the ear hears it. Their skin is weathered to the color of wet limestone—grey-brown, sometimes pale where the sun has not reached. Their hair is cut short or bound with braided fiber so the wind cannot whip it into their eyes. They wear almost nothing: a vest woven from the inner bark of dwarf trees, a wrap of fiber cloth, sometimes a belt of braided sinew. They go barefoot even on the sharpest solution blades, because they say a shoe would make them deaf.

Their faces are narrow, with high cheekbones and small, close-set ears. The ears are not large, but they are always in motion—not turning, because the outer ear cannot turn, but the whole head tilts and the body shifts, as though the person is listening with the collarbone, the sternum, the diaphragm. Their eyes are half-closed against the glare of cloud and wet stone. They do not make eye contact when speaking; they look at the other person’s chest or hands, because that is where meaning lives in a country where words are lost in the wind.

They live in the green seams, but not as villagers. A Hollow Folk shelter is easy to miss: a low dry-laid wall under an overhang, roofed with fern mats pressed to black felt. The doorway is always angled away from the prevailing gust, and sometimes it is just a gap between two stones. Inside, the floor is swept bare. There is a hearth of blackened pebbles, but they burn only dry moss and the wood of dwarf trees, and only at night or in deep slots where smoke can be lost in fog. During the day, they use no fire; the sun and the warm earth are enough, and smoke would mark them to the wind.

They are not nomadic in the sense of wandering. They move in a slow circuit between known seams, following the frog calls and the mist. Each band—rarely more than six or eight people—has a territory of a dozen green pockets and the bare rock between them. They know every seep line, every temporary rill, every cup of water held by a bromeliad. They drink by dipping a wooden cup into moss or by pressing their lips to a carved cistern. They eat the fiddleheads of ferns, the tubers of certain orchids, a sour red berry that grows only where fog pours over a ledge, and a pale root that sends up one leaf in the dimmest part of a slot. They tend the green seams as careful gardeners, transplanting cushion moss, clearing dead leaves from bromeliad cups, loosening soil in cracks for orchids. They sometimes eat the thumbnail frogs, but only in the rainy season, and only after asking the frog’s permission by tapping a stone three times. Mostly they leave the frogs alone, because the frogs are weather-changers.

Their technology is of stone, bone, wood, and fiber. They do not use metal. Metal, they say, has a voice too sharp; it cuts the land’s song and draws lightning. Their knives are flakes of stone, their awls are bone, their rope is braided vine or sinew. The rope bridge across the chasm is theirs—they re-braid it every few seasons, using wooden wedges and no metal. They are the ones who carved the steps descending into the water-dripping slot, though they would not say they carved them; they would say they only encouraged the stone to open a path.

Their language is mostly silent. The roar of the plateau makes speech useless for much of the day, so they speak with their hands and with small taps on the body. A whole argument can happen in the space between two gusts, in gestures too fast for an outsider to follow. They have a spoken language too, but it is low and chesty, used only in lulls and inside shelters. Their names are not words but rhythms: a sequence of knuckles on stone, a double-tap on the sternum, a pause, a single tap. To name a person, you strike their rhythm on the nearest resonant rock. They recognize each other by footstep tremor at a hundred paces.

They are the builders of the cairns, but they do not think of them as monuments. The cairns are sound-marks. Each one is placed where the roar is loudest, where the land speaks with unusual clarity. The stones are chosen for resonance, balanced so that the wind sets up a faint vibration. The Hollow Folk can feel the vibration through their feet and read from it the weather, the time of day, the movement of fog. The cairns are not aligned with stars; they are aligned with the strongest sound, exactly as travelers say. When a tower falls or an arch fails and the sound changes, the Hollow Folk dismantle the nearest cairn and rebuild it in a new alignment. They say they do not build the cairns; the land does, and they only arrange what the land has already chosen.

Their inner life is built around the sound. They call the plateau “the old body” or “the bellowing one.” The sound is not noise to them; it is a music, a weather, a compass, a scripture. They say the land breathes through the holes, and the circular pits that never fill are its mouths. They never approach those pits. Around each one the moss grows in a dense ring and then stops, and the Hollow Folk mark the boundary with small pebbles. They say that to stand too close is to hear your own death in the note, and the metallic smell is the land’s blood. They sometimes leave a single stone on the edge of the ring as a listener—a stone that will vibrate and carry a question into the pit. Later they return and place their palm on the stone; if it is warm or humming, the answer is yes. If it is cold and still, the answer is no. This is their only divination.

They also know the tall solution spires that are not human, the upright stones shaped like figures. They call them “the standing voices.” When the wind reaches a certain pitch, these spires vibrate and sing, and the Hollow Folk avoid them then. They say the standing voices are the land talking to itself, and it is rude to listen too closely.

The pictograph panel under the rock shelter is theirs, or their ancestors’. The red ochre figures with long arms, all facing a hole, are not hunting scenes. The Hollow Folk say those are the first people, who had no mouths. They listened with their whole bodies, and the hole taught them to speak by resonance. The long arms are not arms; they are the lines of sound entering the body. The red ochre is renewed after heavy rain, mixed with spit and oil. No one adds new figures; to add a new figure would be to say the old story was incomplete.

They are a small people. Maybe four or five bands remain, scattered across the Bellowing Shelf. They do not have a leader; in lulls they gather and make decisions by sign. The oldest among them is not the one who speaks most, but the one who can feel the subtlest vibration in the stone—the Bone-Ear, they call that person. The Bone-Ear knows when a slab is about to flex, when a green pocket is about to slump, when the wind will turn. They teach the children to place their palms on the rock and listen for the plateau’s pulse before they learn any word.

When a Hollow Folk person dies, they are carried to a place where the rock sounds hollow underfoot. The body is laid in a natural solution slot, not buried. The family taps a rhythm on the stone—the person’s name—and then walks away without looking back. The land takes the body through water and time.

They are shy, but not hostile. If you cross their territory, they will know long before you see them. They may watch from a ledge, their bodies flattened against the fluted stone, their breathing matched to the gusts. They will not speak to you unless you first put a hand on your sternum and then on the rock. That is the greeting: *I feel the same vibration.* If you do this, one of them may step out and offer you a cup of water from a carved wooden vessel. They will not smile, because a smile is for the still air. But they will look at your chest and hands, and perhaps they will tap a short rhythm on a stone, and wait to see if you understand.

If you do not understand, they will vanish back into the seam. Later you may find a small gift left on the trail: a polished stone that hums in the wind, a braided cord, a handful of edible fern shoots. Or you may find nothing, only a new cairn where the roar seems loudest, its stones already singing.

They do not try to tame the Hollow Country. They do not build walls against the wind or roofs that resist collapse. They know that any shelter may be taken by the land in a season. They carry their homes as knowledge, not as walls. They are a people of gaps and lulls, of secret green pockets and vibrating stone. They are the ones who live there, but they would say they do not live there; they listen there. The land is alive, and they are a small part of its long, roaring breath.
