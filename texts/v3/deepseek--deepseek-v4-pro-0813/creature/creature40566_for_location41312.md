There is one who lives there, if “one” is the right word for something that is also many, or part of many. You will not see it at first. You will see a spire that was not there before, or a low wall that has moved a hand’s width since you turned away. It does not announce itself. It is the same color as the terrace rims, the same wetness as the stone.

It stands no taller than your chest, sometimes less, because it does not straighten. Its back is a slope of flanges and knobs, like a miniature of the spire forest. Its skin is not skin but a crust of travertine and tufa, white and gray, stained at the joints with rust-red and sulfur-yellow. In places the crust is smooth; in places it is sharp as a fresh rim. It does not wear anything. Its body is its shelter.

Its head is the hardest part to hold in the mind. It is not a face but a cluster of black holes and stone ears, a sponge of cavities. There are two dark pits that could be eyes, but they do not blink; they only deepen. There is a mouth that never closes, a long horizontal gap in the lower part of the head, and it is not for eating. It is for hearing. The whole head is an ear. The whole body is an ear. The chest is hollow, ribbed with flanges like the spires that look like shoulder blades. When the water runs over them, the chest vibrates with a low note, and the note joins the basin’s music.

It moves the way the terraces grow. You cannot watch it move. You look down at a pool, and when you look back it is closer or farther or kneeling. It flows, the stone of its legs softening just enough to bend, then hardening again. It leaves a trail of fresh white sinter, a bright line that will darken and build. Some of the low walls in the shelf are not ruins. They are the paths it has taken, pressed into the ground over a long time. That is why they stop at pools and cracks: because it stopped there, or passed over, and the stone remembers the stopping.

The rings of stones around dark springs are its work. It arranges them not for shelter, not for ownership, but to focus the water’s note. It sits by a black spring for a long time, not moving, and when it rises there is a circle of stones, each one placed so the note comes clear. The cairns are tuning forks. It stacks flat stones at places where the sound is different, where a note can be heard alone, so it can find that note again when the mist thickens. The shelters, three slabs and a roof slab, too low to stand in—those are not houses. They are sleeping places, or listening places. The dweller folds itself into them the way a crab folds into a crevice. It does not sleep. It becomes still, and the mineral water seeps over it, and the stone grows around its edges. After a long rest it breaks itself out, leaving a fresh crust on the walls. That is why the shelters are wet and black. They are casts of its stillness.

It is at work even when it appears to do nothing. It kneels by a pool and lowers its face to the water. It blows, not air but a moist exhalation full of dissolved limestone. The film of stone on the rim thickens, smooths, repairs. It can seal a crack or open a new runnel by holding its tube-fingers to the ground and letting the water thread through them, laying down mineral in a guided line. That is how the terraces keep their crisp edges. They are not made only by water and chemistry. They are made by this one, this tender, this stone gardener.

It tests the pools the way a caretaker tests bells. In a line of round pools whose overflow tones rise in a scale, you may see it tap the rim of one with a stone finger. The water shivers. The note rolls to the next pool, and the next. It is not playing music for anyone. It is checking the pitch, the shape of the stone, the flow. If the note is dull, it will spend a long time at the rim, breathing on it, adding a finger-width of stone. A finger-width in a human lifetime is nothing to it.

The blind fish and the transparent shrimp and the white threadworms do not fear it. They drift through the water it disturbs. It does not eat them. It does not eat anything in the way we understand. It takes in water through the cracks in its feet, draws out the dissolved stone, and gives back the white grit that coats everything. Its body is a slow chemical cycle, the same cycle that builds the spires. The spires are its slow relatives. It may have begun as one of them, a vent or a mineral pipe that over centuries grew a nervous system of water channels and resonant cavities, until it began to move. Or it may be the last of many. The others now stand as spires, their bodies still ringing but no longer listening.

It has no name, or its name is a sound: two low notes and a single drip. If you ask, it will not answer. It will turn its stone head, orient its holes toward you, and you will hear your own heartbeat coming back from inside its chest, as if the stone has swallowed the sound and returned it. That is as close as it comes to speech.

Do not call it a ghost. It is not dead. It is the only thing in the Weeping Shelf that moves with intent, the only thing that keeps time by adding stone. The rest of the place is a process. This is a process that knows itself. It lives there because it is the place, in a shape that can stand and walk and stack a stone. It will continue to weep, to build, to hum.
