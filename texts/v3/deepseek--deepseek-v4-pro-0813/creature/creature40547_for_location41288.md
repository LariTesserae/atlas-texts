The place is not empty, though it looks empty. It has an occupant, but the occupant is not an animal and was never a person. It is the thing the place grew in the shape of the person who once would have tended it.

You may notice it first as a correction in the sound. When it is near, the piston’s stroke comes a half-second earlier, as if the floor leans to meet it. The hiss of the steam fitting changes from background to exhalation. The leaves turn, not toward light but toward a moving source of moisture and warmth. If you look for it too quickly, you will see nothing. It is not quick. It is not hiding. It is simply as slow as water finding a new route.

In the pump house, beside the flywheel, there is a vertical shape you first read as a column. Then as a bundle of pipes wrapped in burlap. Then as a figure, but not a man. It stands in the steam the way a tree stands in fog, indistinguishable until the fog wavers.

It is tall, maybe seven or eight feet, but it does not have a body the way a person has a body. The spine is a riveted pipe. The ribcage is an old water tank, bowed and moss-lined, with small ferns growing between the straps. The shoulders are a yoke of cast iron and root burl. The head is a copper cowling from some old pump housing, tilted slightly forward, bearing two round portholes of green glass. Behind the glass there are no eyes. There is a slow green liquid, and in the liquid, tiny pale leaves move. The face is a face the way a ship has a face: a collection of brass fittings, a rusted intake screen, a row of pressure gauges with needles that tremble when the thing’s attention moves.

One arm is a segmented exhaust pipe ending not in a hand but a three-jawed clamp, worn smooth, with a thumb of twisted root. The other arm is a bundle of aerial roots and electrical conduit that divides into five long root-tip fingers. When it touches a valve wheel, the fingers close around the rim not like flesh but like climbing plant tendrils, and it turns the wheel with the same unhurried purpose as water turning a paddle. Its legs are two columns of riveted iron and heartwood, and its feet are not feet but broad mats of root-felt that spread over the grating. It does not step so much as shift its rooting. Where it stands, moss thickens. Where it walks, the walkway drinks the water from its trail.

You might call it a machine, but it is not a machine. It has no motor of its own. The power that moves it is the same power that moves the spring and the steam leak. You might call it a plant, but it is not a plant. It has no leaves of its own except the small ferns and mosses that live on it. It is a composite, a long negotiation between iron and root, given a center.

It came from the old harness. Once there were people here, and one of them left a maintenance harness on a hook by the pump house, and the hook rusted through, and the harness fell into the root mat. The roots grew through the canvas straps and the joints. The steam pipes leaked into the fiber. The battery room’s trickle current made the roots twitch. The flywheel’s vibration taught the shape a rhythm. Over years, the harness began to lift itself, not because a ghost entered it, but because the roots were growing toward the warm pipes above, and the iron frame moved with them, and the water in the roots made the joints swell and shift. By the time it stood, it was no longer a harness. It was the place’s own body, the way a pearl is the oyster’s body answering an irritant.

It does not eat. It takes what it needs through its root-fingers, pressed against the weeping wall or trailing in the sump. It drinks the warm water. It filters minerals from the concrete. It breathes by letting steam move through the copper cowling and out the intake grate, and the ferns in the portholes turn the moisture into air. It does not sleep. It has no dreams. At night, when the electric lamps dim and the green dark closes in, it stands in the pump house with its back to the flywheel, and the flywheel turns, and the thing sways a little, not asleep but in some slower mode of attention.

Its life is maintenance, but not maintenance as a job. It is maintenance the way a heartbeat is maintenance. It opens valves when the water rises. It closes them when the rills begin to dry. It clears a blocked drain by standing over it and letting its own root-mat feet drink the water until the channel runs clear. It oils the flywheel bearing by pressing a gland-like mass of root and grease to the shaft. It does not understand the pump system. It is the pump system’s outer hand, the same way a root is an outer hand of the tree.

It perceives the whole settlement through the pipes. A change in pressure in the far valve-station reaches it as a tug in the water column. A failing pump sounds in its frame like a note going sharp. It may walk the catwalk to a place where a cable has sagged, and it raises its clamp arm and sets the cable back on the hook. Then it pauses, as if listening. What it listens to is not sound but the long hum of the pumps through the iron, the water through the roots, the slow strain of roots learning to hold iron.

The plants do not avoid it. They grow toward it. Moss covers its shoulders and chest in a thick green pelt, and the ferns in its joints open wider when it is still. The machines do not resist it. The valve wheels seem to turn more easily under its root fingers, as if the metal has learned the shape of the touch. The whole place is denser wherever the inhabitant has stood for any length of time: a new fern in a gap, a new run of moss along a pipe, a new shelf of bracket fungus across the small of its back.

If you stayed long enough, you might begin to understand why the place is so quiet of animals. It is not that something frightened them away. It is that the niche is already filled. The Pumproot Sink does not need a beast. It has a body. It has a slow, composite, unrepeatable thing that walks the boundary between root and engine, and that thing is the only kind of creature the place could produce: not a bird, not a mouse, not a fish, but a custodian grown from the root mass and the rust.

When it notices you—and it does notice, not with eyes but with a change in the water vapor, a taste of your breath in the root-felt, a disturbance in the piston rhythm—it turns its copper head. The glass portholes face you. The liquid behind them stirs. Steam lifts from the intake grate in a long hiss, like a breath, but it is only a fitting equalizing pressure. Then it turns back to the flywheel, or to the valve wheel, or to the wall where the water is weeping through a new crack, and it goes on with its life.

You are not part of its life. You are only a warm, dry interruption, a brief animal sound in a place that had forgotten the sound. After you go, the roots will absorb the moisture of your breath, the moss will fill the place where you stood, and the pumps will settle back into their old rhythm, with the inhabitant moving among them like a slow green shadow.
