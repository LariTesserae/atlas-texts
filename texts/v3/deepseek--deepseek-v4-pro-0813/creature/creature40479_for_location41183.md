You do not see them at first. The room is too full—pots between you and the far glow, steam folding every shape into the next—and the heat makes your eyes want to close. You hear them before you see them: a palm beating a pot’s side, the suck of a foot lifting from warm mud, a low chant that might be words and might be the spring’s own rhythm. Then the steam shifts a little, and what you took for a slumped clay pot straightens and becomes a person.

They are not quite a family and not quite a crew. They are the keepers, or the builders, or simply the ones who stayed after the mountain first opened its mouth. There are perhaps three of them, perhaps four; the number resists you because they take turns being wall and worker. They sit so still between tasks that they grow a skin of condensation, and the moss at their feet creeps up their ankles. Their skin is clay-colored—ochre and umber and the grey-pink of wet terra-cotta. Their hair, where they have it, is lank and dark and often slicked back with mineral water. Their eyes are small in the red-brown light, pupils wide and slow, but they do not rely on seeing. They read the room by the sound of water and by the give of the floor.

Their hands are the first thing you truly notice. The fingers are long—not elegant, but made long by use, each joint capable of finding a crack or a bubble before the eye could. Some of them have extra fingers. Not all, and not always on the same hand. A few have six, one has a thumb that bends backward. They do not consider this a deformity. It is what happens when you work clay in a place that is still dreaming itself. The hand learns to be more. The prints they leave are not always five-fingered.

They wear almost nothing: a beaten bark apron, a twist of sinew at the waist, a bone needle in the hair. Tools hang from their necks—a rib, a smoothed spatula, a lump of raw clay. Their feet are broad and splayed, toes spread to grip the mud. The heat has made their skin loose and damp, and the mineral water leaves white and orange rinds on their shoulders. They do not wipe these off. To wipe off would be to remove. They do not remove. They only add.

There is the woman who beats the pot. She is the one you heard first. She does not beat it to shape it, or not only. She beats it to keep time with the thump behind the north wall—the deep pulse of steam building and releasing, the room’s too-large heart. Her palm hits the clay, and the clay hits back, and the sound goes into the floor and up the vine. She knows a pot is finished not by sight but by the note it makes when it agrees with the room.

There is the one who feeds the newt. This one is older, or seems older, though age is hard to read. They keep the lidded pot of grubs at their hip. They lower one grub at a time into the central pool, not dropping it but placing it on the surface as if introducing it to the water. The pale newt rises like a thought from the clay. The keeper makes a low click in the throat and then takes a little mud from the pool’s rim and presses it into the wall. If the newt refuses the grub, all work stops. The keepers say the newt is part of the foundation. If it sickens, the room sickens. If it eats, the day may proceed.

There is the one who prunes the vine. This one is smaller, lighter, and moves in a crouch along the load-bearing cable of the dark glossy vine. They do not cut it with a blade. There are no blades. They pinch new growth between thumbnail and forefinger, or bend it until it decides to separate. Then they press the wound into a ball of wet clay and bind it with a strip of bark. The vine tolerates this. It grows through their hands. New shoots come in the color of wet copper, and the keeper’s fingers are stained with the same green-black sap.

And there may be a fourth. It is not always visible. It stands in the steam near the far fracture, where the daylight glows like an opened kiln. It is tall, or seems tall, and it leaves handprints in the soft places of the wall—handprints too long for any of the others, with too many fingers. Its shadow sometimes falls toward the light instead of away from it. The keepers do not look at it directly. They leave a patch of wall unfinished for it. They mix extra clay and leave it in an open pot. In the morning—if there is morning—the pot is sometimes empty, and the wall has grown a little, and the new handprints are warm.

You might ask who or what the fourth is. The keepers would not answer in words. They would tap the wall, then tap their own chests, then open their hands to the steam. The gesture is not a name. It is a relationship. The place is still dreaming itself, and the fourth is part of that dream. It may be the mountain’s own body learning to use hands. It may be someone who once lived here and went into the wall. It may be the room itself, pressing back.

Their work is endless but not frantic. They knead clay with their feet in the shallow basins. They carry water from the spring to the terraces not in pots but in cupped hands, letting it spill through their fingers in beads. They coax the wrong-moving water up the open clay channel bead by bead, then upside down into the leaf mats overhead, and they say the water is learning to climb. They mend the ladders with green wood and sinew. They smear fresh clay into any crack, not to hide it but to feed it. They collect the mineral rinds that form where the walls weep, grind them into powder, and stir the powder back into the clay. What the mountain secretes, the building reabsorbs. Nothing is discarded. Nothing is scraped away. If a pot cracks, it is not thrown out. It is broken down—by hand, by water—and mixed into the next pot. If a beam sags, it is not replaced. It is reinforced with new cane and bound until the sag becomes part of the line. They do not chase the pots that move from one shelf to another while still warm. They say the room rearranges itself, and they adjust their paths accordingly.

They eat little. A paste of clay and mineral water in the morning, if there is morning. A few fern shoots. A blind moth from the louvers, eaten still warm. The grubs are for the newt. The heat does not let them want much. They drink from the air as much as from the spring: when the chamber takes its breath and the hot condensation falls like fine rain, they stand with their mouths open and their arms lifted. They do not swallow so much as let the water enter through the skin, the eyes, the open mouth. They call it the mountain’s exhale. All work stops for it. Even the pot-beating stops. Even the vine seems to wait.

Their chanting is not language. It is a rhythm made of the room’s own noises: the spring’s hiss, the drip from the ceiling, the thump behind the north wall, the slap of clay. They fit their voices into the gaps. A low note when the steam blooms. A click when a pot settles. A long, high sound when the vine curls. It is work-chant, but also a kind of listening made audible. You would not need to understand it. It would teach your pulse to match.

They sleep—if sleep is the word—in upright niches in the clay wall, standing, with their hands still touching the wet surface. They do not lie down. Lying down would take up too much room, and the room is not for resting; it is for building. They close their eyes and let their bodies sink a little into the soft clay. When the heat spikes, they wake. They wake not with a start but with an inhalation, and their first act is to press a handprint into the wall. It is the same act every time. It means: I am still here, and I am still adding.

The too-long handprints are never wiped away. They are the place’s own script. The keepers trace them sometimes, but they do not try to match them. They know that a hand is not a signature. A hand is a tool, and the room has its own tools. They say the place is not finished. They say the place is not quite a place. It is a body in the act of becoming, and they are its skin, its breath, its hands—though not all the hands are theirs.

When an elder dies—or, as they say, goes into the wall—they do not carry the body out. There is no out. They let the heat and the mineral water do their work. Then they grind the bones to powder. The powder goes into the next batch of clay. The teeth are set into the wall as small white pebbles. The handprints remain. The person does not vanish; they thicken the room. This is not grief. It is the rule: what is removed must be mixed back in. A body is not removed from the place. It is added.

You would not stay long, probably. The heat would become too much. The air would fill your lungs like water. The walls would seem to lean toward you, and the handprints would seem to move. But if you did stay, you would begin to understand that the keepers are not exactly inhabitants. They are not separate from the place. They are the place’s way of having hands, just as the spring is its way of having a voice, and the vine is its way of holding itself up, and the newt is its way of being alive in the center. They do not live in the cleft. They are the cleft, still dreaming, still adding, still beating the pot to the rhythm of the too-large heart behind the north wall.
