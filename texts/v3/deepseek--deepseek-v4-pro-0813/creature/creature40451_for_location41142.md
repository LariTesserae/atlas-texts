What lives there is not a person. There are no structures, no tools, no made things, and no one to use them. What lives there is a blind eel.

She is not easy to see. She is long, perhaps the length of a forearm, but so thin and so pale that she might be only a seam in the water, a place where two layers do not quite meet. Her body is not a single color. It changes as she crosses the warm and cool boundaries of the High Fog Sea, because she is built in densities that match the water’s own layers. When she drifts from one thermocline to the next, her outline seems to break and rejoin, as if she were drawn in pieces. The water does this to the glass-reeds too, making them look severed at the boundary between warm and cool. But she is whole. She is only more legible in some layers than in others.

She has no eyes. They would do her little good in that green-gray light, under the fog, under the leaf mats, under the surface where the sun arrives only as a memory. She feels the water instead, through a line of fine pits along her jaw and down her long, nearly invisible sides. Through those pits she tastes the rock’s slow dissolution, the faint sugar at the base of reed stems, the tiny silica trail of a glass snail after it has passed. She senses the rise of gas from the basalt fissures not as sound but as pressure, a low hiss that is like the water breathing. She knows the basin not as a place of edges and distances, but as a stack of still planes—cool, warm, cool—and she lives in the spaces between them.

She eats seldom. Sometimes she presses her small, round mouth to the algae felt that covers the sharp black stone and grazes a pale line across it, a track that closes slowly after she has gone. Sometimes she finds a glass snail, no bigger than a thumbnail, and takes it whole. The snail’s shell is made of nearly the same silica as the water, and it passes through her almost unchanged, as if she had swallowed a small piece of the sea and then returned it. She does not hunt in any urgent way. Urgency does not exist here. She comes across food and, over the course of hours, decides to take it.

Most of her life is not eating. It is hanging. She hangs in the middle layer, where the water is warmest and stillest, and lets the water move through her skin. Her gills are nearly internal, only a faint red shadow beneath her translucent sides. She breathes the water, and the water breathes her, exchanging dissolved minerals, carbon dioxide, and something else—not food, not waste, but a kind of patience. She can remain almost motionless for days, undulating just enough to hold her place in a current that is barely there.

She grows slowly. She may be older than the high-water mark on the emergent trunks, older than the cracks in the floating leaf mats. Her skin keeps records of the basin’s long, slow rise and fall—not as rings around a shell, but as faint lines along the narrow fin that runs beneath her body, one line for each cycle of the water’s thickening and thinning. When the water level drops by a few centimeters, she moves down into a deeper layer, following a gradient only she can taste. When it rises again, she returns. She has done this so many times that the movement has worn a groove in her, a habit carried in the blood.

She is not the only one of her kind. There are others, but they are very few, and they keep to different layers and distant parts of the basin. Seeing one is an event, though there is no one here to see it. Once every few decades, when the water chemistry shifts and the layers fold into one another, two blind eels may cross in a blur of cool and warm. They do not fight, and they do not call. They touch briefly, skin to skin, and each learns the other’s age and the taste of its particular water. Then they part. The meeting is stored not in any brain but in the skin, as a faint change in density, a line of light that slowly fades.

She lays eggs only when the basin is rising, when the water climbs into newly wetted fissures and the leaf mats crack along old lines. She lays them in the undecayed shed leaves at the bottom, the thin dark mat that is the only soil this place has. The eggs are clear, no larger than seeds, and they look like water droplets resting in the dark. When they hatch, the young are not yet eels. They are wisps, threads of life that drift into the reed forest and begin, slowly, to learn the layers.

If you stayed in the High Fog Sea long enough, still enough, you might see her. First you would notice a slight misalignment in the water, a place where the stacked panes of warm and cool do not quite match. Then she would drift, and you would think a reed stem had broken, but there would be no stem. Then she would turn, and you would see the line of her body, the faint internal shadow of her spine, the blind head tasting the water for your warmth. She would not be afraid. There is no reason for fear here. She might stay a moment, swaying at the edge of a layer, and then move back into the place where you cannot see her. The water would close over the sight of her like a leaf closing over a pool.

That is what lives there. A blind eel, nearly colorless, built of the same layered stillness as the water itself. She is the only large thing that moves with anything like will, and even her will is slow, patient, folded into the breath of the sea. She is not the garden’s gardener. She is only the garden’s ghost, drifting through the glass-reeds, eating almost nothing, waiting for the water to rise or fall, and carrying the place with her in the lines of her skin.
