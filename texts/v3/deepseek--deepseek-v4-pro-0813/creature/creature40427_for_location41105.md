First there is no one. The city is so good at looking empty that you could walk a mile along the cracked pavement and see nothing but crabs parting around your boots and mussels holding their shells shut. But emptiness here is not absence. If you stand still long enough—past the point where your own sweat stops being a discomfort and becomes just another damp surface—you might see the one who lives there.

She comes from the direction of the old worker housing blocks, or maybe from the canal itself. At first she is only a shape, low and slow, like a heap of rags that has learned to walk. Then the shape becomes a person, though it takes time to believe it. She is not walking so much as following the waterline, the way a crab follows it, not planning a route but reading the wet edge.

She is small, or maybe just stooped. Her coat is cut from a rubber conveyor belt, the same black as the wet street, and it shines in the haze. Her hood is made from a torn tarp bleached to the color of old salt. Her boots are two different sizes, laced with copper wire and strips of mussel shell. She carries a net bag over one shoulder, made from a cargo sling, and in her other hand a flattened iron bar.

Her face is not old and not young. The skin is dark from heat and salt, cracked at the corners of the mouth, with a white crust along the hairline. Her eyes are pale, almost colorless, like two shallow pools of bilge water. Her hair is a stiff rope of brown and grey, threaded with fishing line and tiny white shell fragments. When she moves, it makes a faint clicking sound, like the crab mats at low tide.

She does not hurry. The heat will not allow it. She moves at the speed of the tide, which is to say she is still moving even when she seems to be standing. Her feet know the pavement the way the water knows the drains. She pauses at a storm drain, crouches, and waits. The crabs around her do not scatter. They climb over her boots, investigating the salt and the oil in the leather. She lets them. Then she reaches down, not grabbing but sliding her hand under the largest one, and lifts it into her bag. The motion is so smooth it looks like the crab has decided to go with her.

At the old dock pilings she stops. The pilings are not wood anymore; they are columns of shell, growing outward like a slow explosion. She takes the iron bar and works it under a cluster of mussels, twisting at the hinge. They come away with a sound like tearing cloth, and she drops them into the bag. She does not take many. There are thousands. The gap she leaves will close in a week. She knows this the way you know your own breath.

Her home is a room on the fourth floor of a worker block, but you would not call it a home. The lower floors are drowned; the water in the stairwell rises and falls with the tide, and the walls are dark with the waterline. A rope made of braided electrical cable hangs from a hole in the ceiling. She climbs it slowly, her weight making it swing, and the water below her stirs. A long dark shape moves under the surface. It is an eel, thick as her arm, maybe longer. It does not startle. It circles once and settles back to the bottom. She does not look down. They have known each other for years.

The room is small and bare. The floor is concrete, but she has covered it with a layer of dried mussel shells, which crunch underfoot. The window has no glass, only a sheet of clear plastic, stained brown and bellied inward by the wind. There is no bed, only a pile of rags in the corner, but it is not dry. Nothing here is dry. She sleeps curled around the damp as if it were a blanket.

She eats her catch raw. She pries open the mussels with the iron bar and swallows them from the shell, the way you would drink from a cup. The crabs she cracks with a stone, picking out the meat with her fingers. She also scrapes the bacterial mats from the warm outfall pipes, using a flat piece of plastic. The mats are sulfur-yellow and rust-red, and they stain her fingers. She dries them on the roof into a paste that tastes like low tide and iron. She eats it sparingly, as if it were medicine.

Water is the hard thing. The city is full of it, but it is all bad. The rain is hot and salty, the canals are brackish, the puddles are warm with things she does not name. She has a solar still on the roof—a sheet of plastic over a steel drum—but the sun is weak behind the haze. On good days it gives her a cup of water. On bad days she drinks from the old cistern, and the water is warm and greenish, but it is water. She has learned to drink slowly, letting the heat settle in her throat.

She was born here. She thinks she was. There is no one to ask. She has a memory of being small, of the water being lower, of a woman’s voice that might have been her mother. But the memory is like the sun behind the haze: there, but without edges. She knows there were others. She has seen their bones in the dry rooms, their names written on walls in grease. She does not read them. The words have become just another kind of salt crust. She knows the city was built by people who are gone. She does not miss them. You cannot miss what you never had.

The crabs do not run from her. The mussels do not snap shut when she passes. The eel in the stairwell rises to the surface when she drops the crab scraps into the water. She sits on the edge of the flooded stairs, her boots in the water, and watches it feed. The eel moves like a ribbon of dark oil, and she makes a small clicking sound with her tongue, like the sound of mussels closing. The eel does not answer. It does not need to. They are both quiet things.

In the late afternoon, when the rain comes, she sits on the roof and lets it wash over her. The whole city exhales vapor, and for an hour the streets are shallow hot rivers. She watches the tide move up the canals, carrying the crab mats with it. The red lights on the stacks blink on. The pump station throbs below, more vibration than sound. She eats a mussel, slowly, and drops the shell onto the pile. The eel moves in the flooded stairwell. The city is full, though it looks empty. She is not a survivor. She is not the last of anything. She is just the one who lives here, moving at the speed of rust and shell growth, part of the water’s slow allegiance.
