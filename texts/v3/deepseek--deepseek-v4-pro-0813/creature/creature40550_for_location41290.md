It does not announce itself. For a long time, you assume the room is only what it seems: a drowned nave, crowded with small blind lives, the stone finished and patient. Then the water changes—not in temperature, not in current, but in mass. Something has shifted by a hand’s width. Something has been listening to your heartbeat as a pressure change.

What you took for a seam in the central pillar is not a seam. It is a fold of skin. What you took for a fallen lintel is a shoulder. What you took for mineral bloom along the floor is the ridge of a spine.

The inhabitant is not separate from the room. It has lain here so long that the room has grown over it, or it has grown into the room. Its body follows the ledges: a long, soft, pale form, blunt at the head and tapering to a tail that curls loosely around the central bowl. It is longer than a human is tall, but so still that the eye refuses to assemble it. The skin is the colour of wet stone, grey-white and faintly translucent, and across its back the same pale tube-worms have taken hold in a crust so thick that the animal seems furred in mineral wool. Along its flanks run rows of small pits, like the thumbprint pits on the walls, but these are alive: they quiver with the water’s chemistry, tasting it.

It has limbs—not legs, but arms, or perhaps they were once arms. They are folded now against its chest, each hand tucked into the stone niches at the sides of the room. The fingers are long, jointed in too many places, and the thumb is broad and flattened. You understand the wall-pits then: each is the shape of that thumb. The creature pressed them there, one by one, when the stone was young. Or its ancestors did. Or it has been pressing them for so long that no distinction remains.

Its head is broad and flat, with a mouth that is not a slit but a soft oval, slightly open. Inside, rows of teeth catch the faint blue-gray light—tiny, transparent, shark-like, but not made for tearing. They are for scraping the bacterial film from the stones, or for catching the occasional drifting crustacean. They are also its calendar. The teeth fall out, one by one, over years or decades. When one loosens, the creature rouses itself. It swims with a slowness that is almost not movement, feels along the row of pits with its fingertips, and presses the shed tooth into an empty socket in the stone. It has done this so often that some pits hold a dozen teeth, stacked like rice grains; others are worn smooth and empty. The newest pits are at the far end, neat and sharp. The oldest have softened into dimples.

It breathes through the water itself. On either side of its neck are three pairs of gills, branching and colorless, so like the tube-worm crowns that you could not see them until they opened. They open very slowly, once every few minutes, and when they close they pull a faint current across your legs. The creature’s heart is as slow as the room’s cycle. You might feel it if you placed your hand flat against its side, but you do not. The warmth from the floor passes through it, so its body is the same temperature as the water, and your touch would not know where stone ends and animal begins.

It is blind. It has no eyes, no sockets, only a broad smooth brow. But it is not unaware. The water is its light. Every ripple, every bubble from your mask, every twitch of a fish’s spine arrives at its skin and its lateral line as a small disturbance. It can taste the metal of your tank, the oils in your glove, the heartbeat that you cannot quiet. When you move too quickly, its head turns a fraction, not to look but to listen with the whole length of its body. The fish do not flee from it. They school around it as if it were part of the architecture, and perhaps it is. Some of them rest against its gills, picking at the film there. Shrimp walk over its folded hands and antennae-tap the pits of its flanks.

The creature is not a god, though the room has the feel of a shrine. It is not a guardian, though it never leaves. It is the last of the ones who built this place, or perhaps the only one who remains, or perhaps it has forgotten the difference. It no longer works the stone. The doorways have rounded, the steps have hollowed, and the blocks have shifted. It lets them shift. But it keeps the pits. That is the one act it has not forgotten: when a tooth loosens, it finds the wall, measures the row with its thumb, and presses the tooth into place. Sometimes it touches the old pits for a long time afterward, one by one, as if reading them by touch. The water around its hand goes very still.

When it swims, its body makes the shape of the oval doorways. The steps were carved for the way it rests, the wide ledges for the weight of its folded arms. The handholds are at the height of its reach when it moves along the walls. It does not need them anymore. But it fits them, still, as if the temple had been built not for a people but for this single, slow, remembering animal.

It may not want anything in the way a mind wants. It may only be the slow answer the room gives to the question of what lives here. It lives as the water lives, as the mineral crust lives: by patience, by repetition, by occupying a shape until the shape becomes a fact. The chamber is not a ruin. It is a body asleep, and the inhabitant is its dream of hands.

When you leave, it does not watch you go. It has no eyes. But the water closes behind you with the faintest possible pull, and behind that, long after your bubbles have gone, you might imagine a sound: not a click, not a breath, but the soft, wet push of a tooth being fitted into stone.
