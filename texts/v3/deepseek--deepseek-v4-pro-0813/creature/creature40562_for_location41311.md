At first the land seems empty, but that is only because its inhabitants have become mineral, or fog, or patience.

The first ones you might notice are the grazers. They are broad and low, their backs covered in overlapping plates of basalt, sinter, and black salt. The plates grow in pale rings, each ring a season, a year, a long slow history. They move in loose lines through the microbial mats and sparse shrubs, their breath steaming into the damp air. They have no eyes. Heat-pits pock the heavy fronts of their heads, and a fringe of sensitive cilia reads the air for moisture and sulfur. When their shells brush together, they tick, and sometimes a small blue spark jumps between them and lights the mist for half a second.

Around their legs move the skitterers. They are pale, many-legged, blind, their bodies almost translucent except for a thin thread of nerve-light running the length of them. They live on the grazers’ parasites and spilled blood, picking at the soft places between the armor plates. In return they keep watch—not by screaming, but by going still. When the grazers stop cropping and the skitterers vanish, the silence itself is a warning.

Then there are the Low Ones. They are predators that have learned to be stones. You can pass within arm’s reach of one and not know it until it shifts, and by then it is too late for something else. They press soft sensory pads against the rock and listen to the world as vibration. When they move, it is a single uncoiling of wet darkness, a quick crackle of static, and then the quiet closes again. They leave no blood on the ground; the land drinks it.

On the stillest parts of the dark, the kites come. They are not exactly birds, though they fly. Their wings are ribbons of dried membrane, and they ride the electric fields around the steam vents, drifting more than flapping. Their wingtips glow faint blue, and their bodies are long and pale. They feed on the small insects that cloud the fumaroles and on the ozone that gathers along the rock edges. Their calls are single clicks, spaced far apart, like hot stone cracking in water. Because the dark is so open, a kite a kilometer away can sound as if it is perched on your shoulder.

The tubular growths that rise near the warmest ground are not plants, or not only plants. They are living chimneys. In their early lives they crawl on a foot of mucus, pale and blind, away from the vent that gave them heat. Later they root. Their bodies harden into mineral pipes, and they spend their long adulthood drawing warmth through their hollow cores. Inside them, mats of bacteria fix sulfur and iron, and the pipe-things grow slowly outward, layer upon layer, like a breath turned slowly to stone.

But all of these are only the shallow life. The deeper inhabitants are the reason the ground hums.

There are circles of bare ground where nothing grows, and in the center of each circle the earth is warm and hums. The animals avoid these circles. They are not empty places. They are the breathing places of the old ones.

The old ones—if you needed a name, you might call them the Sintered—are the largest and slowest things on the plateau. They are not easy to separate from the landscape because they are made of the landscape. Each is a low mound, many meters wide, with a knobby shell of basalt, pumice, and salt crust. Soft folds of dark, damp skin show only where the plates do not meet, skin the color of wet ash and just as warm. They have limbs, but they are thick and slow, more like the roots of mountains than legs. Their heads are not heads; they are raised platforms where clusters of heat-pits and vibration-organs open, each one a tiny crater, each one breathing.

They do not see. They do not hear. They feel the world through the rock and the heat, through the slow movement of water deep below, through the electric taste of the fog. They are not pure animal and not pure stone. They are colonies: a hot core of living tissue, fed by thermosynthetic bacteria, armored by their own mineral excretion. They eat the earth’s own chemistry—sulfur, iron, the deep heat—and they give back rock. The sinter terraces, the salt crusts, the rounded swells of cinder: some are natural, and some are the Sintered, grown over centuries until no one can tell them apart.

They have no tools. They have no walls or roads or artifacts. Why would they? Their bodies are their houses. The land is their road. Their history is written in the rings of mineral on their shells, each ring a year, or a century, or a thermal cycle, legible to another of their kind pressed close in the dark. They have a language, but it is not sound. It is vibration, sent through the basalt, felt by the soft pads on their undersides. A conversation between two Sintered may last for months. To a human standing on the ground, it would feel like a low hum in the chest, a tingling in the teeth, a faint ringing from a nearby fluted rock. The fluted rocks are not played by the wind; the wind is only the medium. The songs are already there, moving under the ground.

The young are born in the humming circles. The bare ground there is not dead; it is a nursery. The adults gather, and their combined heat cracks the salt. A new Sintered emerges not as a child but as a soft, pale, glowing thing the size of a hand, wet and blind and already humming. It crawls toward a vent, and over decades it grows its first plates. It will take centuries to look like a boulder, millennia to look like a hill. The animals avoid the circles because the young are fragile, because the adults’ electric fields are uncomfortable, and because the ground there is not safe for small quick things. It is ground in the process of becoming someone.

When a Sintered moves, it is not something you would notice in a day. The ground cracks softly. Salt falls from its sides in thin sheets. The fog thickens over it. It lifts itself, a few centimeters at a time, and shifts, following a deep change in the geothermal gradient. It may travel a kilometer in a season, and when it settles, it does not sit; it merges. The land around it softens and then hardens, and after a year there is no trace that it moved at all—only a new ridge, a new terrace, a new hollow where the mist will gather in the mornings.

This is why the place feels industrial. The Sintered are not using the earth’s machinery; they are the earth’s machinery, or at least a part of it. The geothermal fields act like engines, and the Sintered are their slow-moving pistons. The fog is thickened by their breath. The static that crackles along the rocks is the discharge of their long, slow movements. The terraces and cones that look like factory stacks are old ones, still warm, still humming. The whole plateau is not a machine with a builder; it is a machine that lives.

So the dark plateau is not empty. It is inhabited by grazers and skitterers and patient predators and electric kites and stone chimneys, and by the Sintered beneath them, the old ones who are both creature and land. They do not welcome and they do not threaten. They simply are, like the heat, like the fog, like the long vibration that runs through the rock and makes the whole world hum.
