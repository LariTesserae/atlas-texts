They are not seen all at once. At first the Throat-House seems inhabited only by sound and moisture, by the irregular thump of the water drum and the creak of baskets. Then a shape detaches from the floor near the blue-glazed channel, and you see the child.

The child crouches where the water slows into a shallow curve. She is small, seven or eight, with hair cut uneven and stuck to her damp forehead. Her legs are folded under her, knees wide, bare feet flattened against the tile. She scrapes the channel with a broken sherd of pottery, not in long strokes but in small patient arcs that follow the glaze. The green algae comes away in thin ropes, and she drops each rope into a wooden bowl beside her. She doesn’t hurry. She is learning to read the water by the thickness of the algae, by the way the silver fish school or scatter, by the faint vibration that travels up through the clay pipe in the east wall. They call her Silt, though the word also means “the one who listens to the channel.” She has no fear of the deeper shadows. Her arms are thin but strong, and the skin just above her wrists is crosshatched with tiny white marks from the sherd’s edge.

Near the western edge, the woman at the hearth kneels on a mat of braided hide. She is broader than the child, her shoulders round from years of leaning over coals. Her hair is wrapped under a cloth stained with grease and ash, and her forearms are bare, pitted with small burn scars like the surface of a very old brick. She feeds the charcoal bed one piece at a time, using two smoothed sticks instead of her hands. She listens to the coals as much as she watches them. When they settle, she adjusts them with a rake made of fire-hardened wood. When they hiss, she adds a few drops of water from a clay cup, and the room breathes a little easier. She is the keeper of warmth, but also of dryness and damp. The others call her by a name that sounds like the word for “bottom of the pot,” where the heat is most patient. She rarely speaks. At night she keeps one ember alive under a cap of ash, and if she wakes and the air tastes too dry, she feeds it water and whispers to the fire in a language that is mostly made of small clicks and spit.

The old man works near the western wall, a little way from the hearth. He is bent so far forward that his face is often level with his chest. He wears a sleeveless coat made of many patches, and his hands are pale with dried clay that never quite comes off. He is the repairer. He carries a wooden bowl, a stick, and a bundle of coarse animal hair. The walls of the Throat-House are old, and they move with heat and water, so new cracks appear every few days. The old man finds them by smell and by touch, pressing his ear against the plaster and waiting. When he finds a crack, he fills it not all at once but in small coils. He mixes the clay with water and sometimes with his own spit, rolls it between his palms, and presses it in with the heel of his thumb. Then he listens again. If the wall gives a low, satisfied sound, he moves on. If it stays silent, he digs the clay out and begins again. His eyes are grey as the clay, almost white in the dim light. He has not left the chamber in longer than anyone can remember. He says the walls are not broken; they are breathing, and he is only reminding them to close their mouths.

The girl on the ladder is faster than the others. She climbs the notched pole with bare feet and one hand, the other hand holding a small pouch of woven fiber. She is twelve, or maybe fourteen; it is hard to tell because she moves like an animal and never stays still long enough to be counted. A single grey bird feather is tied into the end of her dark braid. The grey birds with black masks live high on the north wall, and they know her. When she climbs, their alarm calls soften into a rough cooing, like a door being opened slowly. She gathers eggs from the nests, but she never empties a nest. She holds each egg up to the pale light that falls through the oiled hide and mica screen. Some she places in the pouch. Others she returns, still warm, to the nest. She knows the birds as individuals—the one with the chipped beak, the one that nests too far out, the one that always lays twice. She is the only human the birds allow so close. Her arms are covered in fine scratches from twigs, and she smells of down and warm dust. The others call her Nestling, or simply “the one who climbs.”

Then there is the listener. She is the most hidden of them all, and the most important. She sits near the water drum at the center of the room, where the carved stone tongue drips onto the hide stretched over the clay pot. Her legs are folded under her, and she is so still that pale geckos climb over her feet. Her eyes are clear and brown but fixed on nothing; she has learned to see with her ears. The drumbeat is the house’s pulse, and the listener reads it the way a healer reads a wrist. When the beat slows, she taps a small bone against the rim of the drum. When it quickens, she tilts her head toward the pipes. She hears the weather changing above long before the sky shows it. She knows when the cistern is low by the sound the water makes as it leaves the north wall. She is the one who decides when the plants are watered, when the shaft is unshuttered, when the compost is turned. She has no name. They call her the Ear of the House. When she dies, another will be chosen from the child who reads the channel.

But the human people are only one part of the living treaty. The grey birds are the room’s only true commuters. They leave each morning through the light shaft and return at dusk with seeds from the thorny plain above. Their droppings feed the moss on the made stone. Their feathers drift down into the cracks and are gathered by the old man, who mixes them with clay. They are not pets; they are tenants, with their own high rooms and their own loud arguments. The girl says there are never as many as there seem, because the room’s echoes double their sound.

The two weasel-like creatures are called slip-ribbons. They are thin as straps, with bronze-brown fur and white throats. They patrol the ledges and ladders, swift and fearless. They raid the birds’ nests, take the eggs that were left for them, catch geckos, and then vanish behind the storage jars. The people do not hunt them. They are the house’s sharp teeth, keeping the room from growing too full and too soft. Sometimes one will run along a rope stretched over the cistern, and for a second the whole room seems to hold its breath.

The pale geckos are everywhere, waiting near the lamps and along the plaster. Their toes click faintly, a sound like tiny knuckles cracking. They eat flies and moths that fall down the shaft. Some have lost tails. Some have grown two tails, thick and misshapen, and the child speaks to these ones with clicking sounds. The geckos are not intelligent in any human way, but they are part of the room’s attention. Their green-black eyes catch the lamplight like chips of the old blue glaze.

In the cistern, the silver fish school like a single thought. They are not caught, not even when the plain above is dry and food is scarce. They keep the water clean. Their movement tells the listener whether the channel is clear or clogged. Below the floor, the longer pale thing moves in the dark channels. It is the Under-Throat, a creature like a snake or an eel, white as old ivory. It feeds on fish and drowned beetles and whatever falls through the cracks. It is rarely seen, but its cool movement can be felt through the tiles. The people leave a lump of suet and a fishbone at the place where the water disappears under the north wall. They do not call it a god. They call it the house’s gut.

The crickets and beetles are the smallest inhabitants, but not the least. The crickets sing from behind the storage jars, and their song is a kind of temperature. When it stops, the woman at the hearth knows the room has cooled too much. The beetles in the compost corner work with a patience older than the walls, turning old straw and bird castings into black soil for the three green plants. No one steps into the compost corner. It is marked with a border of white lime, and even the weasels avoid it.

At night the people sleep in low alcoves lined with old hides, but one of them is always awake. Sometimes it is the listener, sitting near the drum. Sometimes the woman at the hearth, watching the ember under its ash. Sometimes the old man, pressing his ear to a new crack. The Throat-House never quiets. The water drum continues its slow, irregular beat. The pipes moan softly. The crickets take over where the birds left off. The geckos call from the walls. And the people, half in sleep, breathe in the same rhythm as the house.

They are not its owners. They are its breath, its fingers, its ears. To live there is not to occupy the Throat-House. It is to be part of a body made of clay and sound, a small wet organ beating beneath the dry plain.
