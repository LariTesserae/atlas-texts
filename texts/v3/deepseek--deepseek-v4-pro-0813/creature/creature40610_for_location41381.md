They are not the first thing you see. The first thing is the green dusk, the glistening walls, the slow migration of wool-snips like a living tide. Then, after a while, you notice that some of the shapes on the ledges are not moss or alder. They are too still, then too suddenly moving.

A hand appears from a seam. It is a human hand, but pale as the underside of a shelf-fungus, fingers long, nails trimmed with obsidian. It tests the rock before the body follows. The person steps out of a doorway that seemed only a shadow, and stands on a ledge no wider than a shoulder. She is barefoot; her toes spread on the stone. She wears a tunic of grey-green felt that looks wet even when dry. In the dim, the cloth holds a faint glow, like a breath of wool-snip light remembered by the fibers.

She does not call to you. She raises one hand, turns the palm out, and waits.

They are the people of the Gape, and they have grown into its rules the way alder roots grow into cracks. They are not exactly pale, not exactly short, not exactly silent; they are the thing that happens to human beings when a wound in the earth teaches them to live without noise.

Their skin is the color of wet clay, old bone, the inner bark of a cloud-forest shrub. Their hair is dark and kept short or braided tight; longer hair would catch on stone, gather moss, block their ears. Their eyes are large, with pupils that open wide in the permanent dusk, so the iris is a thin ring of brown or grey. They blink slowly. They see more than you do in the green dark, but they do not stare; they look sidelong, as if direct sight is too loud.

Their bodies are built for the vertical. Their arms are lean and ropy, their hands long and strong, knuckles enlarged from gripping pegs and rungs. Their feet are broad, the toes spaced wide, the soles tough as hide. They walk on the balls of their feet, toe-first, each step placed before weight is committed. A child who runs on a rope bridge is not scolded for danger; the bridge itself teaches, swaying, and the child learns to move like water.

Their clothing is wool-snip felt: soft, warm, damp-tolerant, mottled grey and green like the moss. New garments carry the faint green luminescence of the shed skins, so that at night the people can see each other as patches of moving light. Older garments are patched with gecko hide at the elbows and knees. They carry little: an obsidian knife in a bark sheath at the small of the back, a coil of inner-bark cord, a split-wood sign-card, a hooded lamp of animal fat and scraped horn. Some carry bone needles in a clay tube stoppered with moss. None carry metal. None carry more than is needed.

Their homes are not rooms so much as perches made warm. A dwelling might be a cave scraped deeper under an overhang, its mouth lashed with alder poles and packed with moss and wool-snip felt. The floor is a web of springy branches covered in hides. A fire in a covered clay pot breathes a thread of smoke into a skin chimney. The walls are lined with pegs: for tools, for drying gecko meat, for hanging sign-cards. The doorway is low, the threshold a raised sill of stone to keep water out. From outside, you might mistake it for a shadow. From inside, it is the only dry thing in the world.

They do not live in rows. They live in the empty spaces between ledges, in the pockets of green. One lamp in a doorway may be forty feet across the void from another, visible as a pale smudge in the fog. There are no streets. There are routes: a ladder of lashed alder up a chimney, a walkway pegged into the basalt, a rope bridge strung between two thickets. Each route is maintained, re-woven when the damp rots it, tested with a foot before the weight. Neighbors do not shout. If they need to speak across the gap, they use sign-cards or tap a coded rhythm on the stone with a knuckle, felt through the rock rather than heard.

The children are born in the birthing cave, a deep dry overhang behind the council platform. The birthing cave is lined with wool-snip felt and heated by a single covered brazier. A newborn’s first cry is not silenced; it just does not travel far. The mother holds the child against her chest, and the felt swallows the sound. By the time the child can walk, it has already learned that noise is something that happens close, like breath, not something you throw across a gap.

They teach their children sign before speech: water is a hand cupped and tipped; fire is two fingers flicking upward; the Glove is a flat hand with fingers spread and curled. Speech is low, a murmur. They use their voices for small things—for names, for warnings spoken directly into the ear. The rest is hand and stone and light.

Their work follows the migrations. At dawn, when the wool-snips slide down the walls in sheets to drink from the seep-pools, the people pull up their ladders and wait. At dusk, they shutter their lamps and retreat above the floor. In spring, when the wool-snips shed their skins, the people move along the ledges with soft baskets, peeling the damp translucent skins from the leaves and stone. They do not take the living animals; they take only what is left. The skins are felted into cloth, and the cloth is sewn with bone needles. In summer, they hunt pale geckos with little snares of bark cord and kill them quickly with obsidian. The meat is salted on the smoke-rack, the hides tanned with moss-acid, the bones saved for needles and hooks. They do not hunt the spine-mice except when the mice gnaw the storehouse; then they trap them with deadfalls and use the fur for cord.

They watch the rim for rockfalls. They watch the seeps. They watch the wool-snips’ light. The wool-snips pulse in slow unison at certain times, long waves of green moving across the walls, and the people read these pulses as a calendar. The pulses quicken before the wet season, slow before the dry. A sudden rippling in the green tide means the crack is breathing, they say, and the elders may call everyone to the outer ledges to listen with their palms.

The elders are the ones who have learned to press their ear to the stone and not just hear but feel. They keep the sign-cards and the knotted strings. They watch for the two-headed wool-snip, not as a curse but as a warning: when one is found, the elders measure the cracks and tell everyone which ledges to leave. They sit on the council platform, a slab of basalt balanced on three stones, and pass a smooth pebble from hand to hand. The elder who holds the pebble speaks; the others wait. They do not vote. They wait until the pebble has gone around the circle enough times that the silence itself has become a decision.

There is a bell-keeper. The cracked stone bell is struck once at evening. It is not a ring; it is a single low thump that goes down through the rock and up through the soles of the feet. The bell-keeper is chosen young, often because they are too sensitive to sound, or too drawn to the bell’s crack. They learn to strike with a padded wooden mallet in exactly the place where the crack would split if struck wrong. After the strike, the bell-keeper presses both palms to the bell and waits, feeling the note drain out of the stone. It is the closest the Gape comes to a voice.

The Glove is not named often. Children call it “the long one” or “the hand.” It comes out of the high caves at night to hunt geckos, and it is silent. The people have no quarrel with it. They do not build on the floor after dark, not because the Glove is evil, but because the floor belongs to it after the last light goes. When they see its shaggy shape against the faint glow of the wool-snips, they turn their lamps away and press themselves against the walls. It passes. The Gape remains.

There is no music. There is no birdsong, no insect chirr, no drumming. But there is rhythm: the drip of water from ledge to ledge, the pulse of the wool-snips, the tap of a neighbor’s knuckle through the stone, the thump of the bell. The people do not think of it as music. It is just the Gape, alive.

If you watch long enough, you will see that the people are not separate from the fauna. They are another kind of creature in the wall, another body in the green. They cluster in the pockets, they move softly, they communicate by touch and light and subtle sound. Their silence is not emptiness. It is a kind of fullness: every surface used, every drop kept, every body listening.

At night, when the fog rises and the wool-snips pulse their slow green waves, the people become visible as small lamps moving along the routes. They do not call out. They climb, step by step, to their high ledges. One of them stops at a seep-pool, dips a clay cup, drinks without disturbing the surface. Another pauses to press a hand to the stone, feeling for the crack that might be widening. The bell thumps once. Then the lamps go out, one by one, like wool-snips closing their skins, and the Gape is left to its breathing.
