What lives here is not a population, exactly. It is more like a conversation that has been going on so long the speakers have begun to grow into one another. The first one you notice is a woman poling a flat-bottomed boat across the open pool where the market gathers. She moves without appearing to hurry, but the boat cuts cleanly through the amber water, leaving a wake that is swallowed by the next swell. Her name is Miren. She is not young, not old. Her hands are broad and ridged with callus, the knuckles slightly swollen from years of ropework. Her skin, where the tunic leaves her arms bare, has a faint greenish cast in that light, but it is not an illness; it is the stain of the place, the mineral bloom that comes from living in water that has been filtering through root and stone for longer than memory.

She poles with a length of gray wood that is not wood at all, but a living branch cut from her family’s stack and cured without killing it. The branch oozes a clear sap when it is left too long in the sun, and Miren will sometimes lift it to her mouth and drink a little of the bruise, though the taste is bitter. It steadies her. The poling is not work; it is how she thinks. The whole basin is a body of water that has no rest, and she has learned to think in its rhythm.

In the bow of her boat there is a child, Osi, seven years old or maybe eight. She is lying on her stomach with one arm trailing over the side, her fingers opening and closing in the water as if she is trying to catch something that isn't there. The glass-fish scatter and regroup around her hand. Osi does not speak much. When she does, her voice is pitched low, almost underwater, as though she learned words from the gurgle of the channels rather than from people. She has a mark on her shoulder—a patch of skin that gleams faintly in the dark, shaped like a leaf or a map. Her mother says the stack gave it to her. Whether that is a story or a fact is not the kind of question the people here ask.

There are perhaps forty people in the basin. The families are not large. Miren's family is seven. Across the water, in the stack called Root-Gray, a family of boatmakers grows and tends the trade. In the low stone structures on the eastern rim, a solitary woman named Sill keeps the gate to the upper path, though no one has come up from the high country in a generation. She does not guard it so much as keep it clear of vines. There is a family of net-weavers in the middle stack, and a pair of brothers who tend the grazer herd, and a cluster of elders who live mostly in the open-roofed common at the center, where the rain is allowed to fall into a shallow cistern. There are no soldiers. There is no council. But there is an agreement, older than the oldest person, that the place will be shared: the water, the stone, the stacks, the light.

The stacks themselves are counted among the people. Not as gods, not as beasts, but as persons whose time is longer and whose thoughts are wider. They have names, and the names are used in stories, in songs, in arguments. *She Who Leans at the Still Hour* is one. *Old Root-Gray* is another. The youngest is called *The One Who Woke Late*, because it only began to glow in the lifetimes of the current elders. It is smaller, but it grows fast, and the children like it best because it opens playful little hollows in its base and makes a sound like laughter when the water runs through them. The stacks do not speak in words, but they answer. If you ask them a question—not with your mouth, but with your whole attention—they will answer in a way that takes days or years to understand. The people accept this. They are patient.

Miren's family has lived inside *She Who Leans at the Still Hour*, or Maru-Il in the old local mouth, for nine generations. The chambers run upward through the living tissue, some carved long ago, some only recently opened by the stack itself. The walls are not flat. They swell and dip, and in the older rooms the floor has grown up to meet the ceiling in places, so that the house is slowly becoming a cave. The family adapts. They sleep in hammocks slung from the room's own shelves, and they eat at a table that is a living root-buttress, and they keep their belongings in little round niches that the stack has grown like pockets. The rooms glow from within at dusk, not brightly, but enough to see by, and the light does not flicker; it breathes.

The stack does not speak in words. But it listens. You can tell when it is paying attention, because the air in the chamber gets a little warmer and the walls bead with moisture more heavily. Osi sits by the wall and tells it things—small things, the way children do with an animal or a grandparent—and the wall answers with a slow thrum that only she seems to feel. Sometimes the chamber grows a new shelf while she is telling. Sometimes the water in the basin rises.

There are others. Old Tem, the listener, lives in the lowest root-heart of the North Stack, where the sound of water is so loud it becomes silence. He is half-blind, and his ears are stopped with wax he makes from root-sap, because the voices would deafen him otherwise. He wears a harness of straps and copper loops, and he taps the roots with a bone hammer in patterns no one else remembers. Tem can tell when a stack is about to shift before the roots move. He can tell which way the water will run tomorrow. He can tell when a chamber is going to open, and he lets the family know so they can move their belongings before the wall seals around them.

He is not a leader. No one leads. The people make decisions by drift, the way water finds its level. But Tem is listened to. When he speaks, his voice is so soft that people stop their boats to hear it. He has lived here longer than anyone; his mother, they say, was not born in a stack but in the open water, and she gave birth to him in a stone hollow while the water was rising, so he came into the world half-drowned and has never been dry since. His skin is patched with lichen, not as an affliction but as a kind of companionship. He eats almost nothing. He drinks a great deal. At night he sleeps standing, braced between two roots, and the roots grow slowly around his back. Some of the children think he is already becoming part of the place. He might be.

The animals are not separate from the community. The six-limbed climbers, called *slickbacks* or *handwalkers*, live in the walls and the roof-crowns. They are not pets, exactly; they are more like second neighbors. Some are tame enough to enter the chambers and steal food from the table, and the people allow it because the slickbacks keep the walls clean of certain soft-bodied pests. One of them, a gray old male with a scarred muzzle, follows Miren from room to room. His name is Chirk, and he understands perhaps a hundred words, though he pretends not to. When Osi swims in the channel, Chirk runs along the overhead root-bridges and chitters if he sees a snake-otter moving below. He has saved her twice, or so the family says.

The air is full of the leather-winged fliers, fist-sized and iridescent, and the children sometimes net them gently, just to watch the colors move under their skin, then let them go. At dusk their chattering is so loud it replaces thought.

The snake-otters are another kind of inhabitant. They are rarely seen by daylight, but their presence is written on the water. They leave long, slow ripples that move against the current. They have a smell like cut iron and wet fur. They are not hunted, because the people here say that everything with a pulse in the channels is part of the same body, and to take one piece too much would loosen the whole. But they are respected, and the children are taught to recognize their spraint, and to stay out of the deep green pools where the water is cold enough to numb the hands.

The grazers, the soft-backed amphibious ones, are kept just enough to be called livestock. They are not herded with fences but with sound. The children sing to them in the shallows, a low, wordless crooning, and the grazers turn their broad heads and follow the sound as if it were a current. They are milked, sometimes, though the milk is thin and faintly luminescent and tastes of stone. They are shorn, though their fleece is not hair but a kind of wet moss that is dried and woven into matting. They are killed only when the settlement agrees, which is rare, and then the death is done in the water, quickly, and every part is used. The people say thank you to the water, to the grazer, to the stack that will take the bones.

The dead are not buried. The stone is too hard, and the water is too present. Instead, the body is placed in a shallow depression in the bedrock, a natural basin that fills with water from the weeping rock. The body is weighted with porous stones, and over time the water takes it apart. That is the word they use: *takes it apart*. It is not a sorrowful thing, or not only sorrowful. It is a return. The living wade into the pool and sing a series of high, wavering notes that rise and fall like the water level. Then they go home and the stack grows a small shelf for the dead, and on that shelf they place a cup, a knot, a child's tooth, something small that belonged. The shelf remains for years, sometimes for generations, until the wall slowly encloses it. They say the dead become part of the stack's dreaming.

A birth is a more dangerous event. The mother is taken to a low, warm chamber that the stack opens near its base, where the water can be heard but not felt. The stack grows a cup-shaped depression in the floor, and the baby is born into that living hollow. The walls sweat more heavily, and the light in the room quickens. The whole settlement seems to know without being told. The water in the market pool rises a finger's width. The six-limbs gather on the outer walls. The mother drinks a thick sap that the stack exudes for the occasion, and she is not afraid, or if she is afraid, she does not say so. The baby is given to the water first, before it is given to the mother—a quick immersion, a baptism of the living pool—and then placed in a sling woven from grazer-moss. The child's first cry echoes off the stone and is answered by a hundred small splashings, as if the basin itself is applauding.

The people themselves are not beautiful in the way of dry-country people. Their skin is damp, often slicked with a film of mineral oil they rub on to keep the water from softening it too much. Their hair is worn long and braided tight against the head, to keep it out of the way. They have wide, dark eyes, many of them, and their pupils contract rarely in the low light. Their feet are broad and almost prehensile; they can grip the woven walkways with their toes. Their chests are deep, their breathing slow; they can hold their breath long enough to free a tangled rope underwater, though not so long as they would like. Their voices carry, but they do not shout often. They have a way of speaking in the middle of a sentence and then pausing while a boat passes or a root groans, as if those sounds are part of what they meant to say.

They wear little, because cloth rots quickly in the wet, and because the air is warm. What they do wear is mostly woven root-fiber, soft and pale, or cured grazer hide, which is supple and nearly waterproof. Some are draped in netting, worn loose over the shoulders, with wooden hooks hanging from it for carrying things. Their tools are simple but old: knives of sharpened stone or boiled bone, awls made from the spines of glass-fish, hooks of copper that they say comes from nowhere, from before. The copper is pitted and green, but it never breaks. It is inherited.

The things they use are not all made. Some are grown, some are found, some are remembered. A cooking pot here is a stone that the stack has opened into a bowl, or a gourd of hardened root-cavity. A heated stone sits in the bottom of the boat in the morning, keeping a bundle of food warm. A piece of wall, when touched in a certain sequence, lights up with a pattern like water, and the trader can read it to know what the weather will do, or whether the south channel is passable. These are not machines in any sense the dry world would recognize. They are more like organs. They belong to the place.

The boat Miren poles has no keel made by human hands. It was grown from a single root that the stack pushed up into the shallows, light and buoyant, and Miren's grandfather taught it to float. The boat still has a kind of memory. It leans into the current when it wants to, and when Miren is not paying attention, it sometimes finds its own way home. Osi says the boat knows its name. Its name is *Silt*.

When the North Stack shifts, it is not a single event. It begins as a low, prolonged moan that comes up through the rock and the water and the soles of the feet. Then the water level begins to change—not quickly, but visibly. The market boats pull to higher ground. People stop what they are doing and press their hands flat to the nearest wall or stone, listening. Old Tem taps his bone hammer against a root, and the tapping is answered by a series of long, slow cracks from deep below. A walkway snaps. Somewhere a basket falls and floats away. The children shout, but not with terror. It is more like excitement, the same energy as a storm.

Miren steers Silt toward the family chamber. Osi sits up, water running from her fingers. "She is moving," Osi says. Miren doesn't ask who "she" is. She knows. The water rises half a meter in the time it takes to cross the pool, and then it stops, as if the whole basin has taken a breath and held it. The air goes very still. Then the water drops, and the sound of dripping returns, and the people laugh, and someone calls out a joke, and the boats begin to drift back to their places. It is not a disaster. It is just the place turning over in its sleep.

That night, the new chamber opens. It is a small one, high in Maru-Il's southern face, just above the waterline. Miren climbs the wet stairs to look. The walls are still glossy with fresh growth, and the light inside pulses slowly. There is a shelf already present, and a hollow for a bed, and a round window that looks out over the flood pool. Miren stands there for a long time, breathing the smell of warm sap. She knows what it means. The stack has decided there will be another child in the house. Whether that child will be born to her, or to Osi, or to someone not yet met, the stack does not say. It simply opens the room and waits.

And beneath all of them, the stone lives in its own way. Not alive, but not dead. The people sometimes say the stone is the oldest listener. It does not grow, but it holds the water; it does not speak, but it gives the water its voice. The stone is what the stacks rooted into, what the people walk on, what the dead return to. It is the floor of the world, and the whole place is a conversation between stone and water, with the living things as the words that arise in between.
