At first, you would say nothing lives here. No birds, no insects, no voices. But that is only because you are looking for animals. The inhabitants do not move as animals move; they grow, swell, twist, collapse. They are the builders and the buildings, the demolition crew and the city itself.

The first resident you learn to see is the pale bonewood. It erupts from the root-mat as a white spear, hollow and fast. In a day it can add a storey, rising into the steam like a splinter of sunlight. It is not a tree in the slow sense; it is scaffolding, a temporary tower. Its bark cracks as it grows, and steam hisses from the fissures. It is brittle, clean-edged, and it lives fast. By the time the sun sets, it may already be inhabited by its companions.

Those companions are the tendon-vines. They are dark, rubbery, and wet. When the humidity rises, they swell and shorten; when it falls, they relax. They are the muscles of the plateau. You will see them wrapped around the bonewood, not tied but twined, and over hours they contract, pulling the spires into arches and holding them there. A bridge is not built by hands; it is tightened, day by day, as the vines remember the shape of stress. The bonewood provides the skeleton, but the tendon-vine provides the grip.

In the gaps between the towers, the ground cups itself into rosettes: the bladderleaves. They are thick-skinned and translucent at the edges, and they collect hot water. They grow swollen, heavy, buoyant. Their weight anchors the root-mat; their buoyancy lifts plates of tissue. Some are as broad as houses, and inside them the water is not still—it ferments, grows green with algae, and the gas pushes against their walls. They are the reservoirs, the counterweights, the quiet bellies of the world.

But a city that only builds would choke itself. So there are the dissolvers. They are not plants so much as their pale and hungry kin: fungi and parasitic creepers that find the fissures in the bonewood. They digest the cellulose, soften the joints, and eat the scaffolding from inside. They are the demolition crew. Without them, the plateau would lock itself into a solid mass. They ensure that every tower falls before it can become permanent. Their work is hidden, almost silent, until a long tearing sound announces that another tower has become a cloud of yellow spores.

Then there are the regulators, the valve-membranes. You find them at the edges of water channels, leaves the size of doors that curl and uncurl as the water level changes. They have no nerves, no muscles, but their upper and lower surfaces swell at different rates, and so they move. They watch the water with their whole bodies. When the level rises, they bend to spill overflow into a different channel; when it falls, they close. They are the gates, the sluices, the slow bureaucracy of the plateau.

And everywhere, the resin-sealers. Where a stem breaks, a wound opens, and from it weeps a resin that never stops. The resin seals the crack, hardens into a dark scar, and then is overgrown by the next shoot. The whole surface of the plateau is a history of wounds and seals, each one a line in the living record.

The air is full of their reproduction. Seedpods burst with sharp pops. Spores drift in the steam. Some are wind-borne, some water-borne. Reproduction is not a season; it is continuous. A fallen limb roots itself before it has finished dying. A fragment of leaf floats in a hot pond and begins to grow. There is no childhood or old age, only growth and decay overlapping.

Each day is a storm of construction and demolition. At dawn, new bonewood shoots rise from the wreckage of yesterday’s towers, pale and eager. Tendon-vines, already wet with condensation, twine around them and begin to contract in the morning heat. Bladderleaves fill with the previous night’s moisture. By noon, the steam is thick, the towers groan, and somewhere a valve-membrane curls open to release a spill of hot water. By evening, the dissolvers have done their work; a tower tears itself down in a long, wet sound. Yellow spores bloom. By dawn, the wreckage is already being replaced.

So who lives here? Not a single creature, but a society of forms. The bonewood is the pioneer, the tendon-vine the builder, the bladderleaf the reservoir, the dissolver the demolition, the valve-membrane the regulator. They are not separate species; they are roles in a single living process. The place is not a wilderness, not a city, but a machine made of plants, and the residents are the machine’s moving parts. They do not think, but they act. They do not speak, but they are loud. They do not build with hands, but the building never stops.
