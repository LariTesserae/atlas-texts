The place is full of inhabitants, but they are not visitors; they are events of long mineral time.

The rooted lives are the most obvious. Each colony is not an individual in the human sense. It is a slow city of fronds—some for light, some for salt-sweating, some for spore release, some for grinding rock. They rise from a central mound of cemented mineral crust. The tissue is dense with oil-solvent, not sap. If you press your hand near a frond, you will feel the slow exhalation of pores: not breath, but a controlled leak of solvent vapor. The fronds are warm. They are chemical furnaces burning at the temperature of a sleeping mammal. Their colors are not fixed; the pigments are arranged in branching channels that darken and lighten with the angle of the light, so the whole mound seems to turn its face.

Each colony has a root system that is not for water but for stone. The roots are pale, acid-tipped, branching feelers. They weave through the polygonal cracks, dissolving mineral crystals one molecule at a time. They leave behind a fine white pellet of waste salt. This is why the ground around a colony is lighter, as if the stone has been dusted with powdered bone. The colony is slowly sinking, not because it is heavy, but because it is eating its foundation.

At dawn, the colonies release their spores. The spore stalks stand up from the center; they are brittle, glassy, and they uncoil with a sound like a tiny ceramic hinge. The spores are not wet seeds; they are flat, radiation-resistant cases with a wing of translucent fiber. They rise in the first heat and go sideways on the wind. You may walk for a day and see dozens of them lodged in the fine dust, waiting. Only a few will send down the first acid filament.

Between the colonies, under the stone, is another kind of life. The **glassroot**, the deep web, is an underland of oil-filled threads that runs through the fractures. It is not one organism but a linked network of colonial clones and fungal-like symbiotes. It feeds on mineral films; it transfers rare ions from one colony's waste to another's hunger. The ground's slow breathing is the movement of this web as it contracts and relaxes in response to temperature. The hollow rocks are its resonators: when the wind passes over them, the web's fluids move in slow eddies, and the sound is the voice of the underland.

The animals you will see—if you see them—are the **stone-pilgrims**. They are not large. A full-grown one fits in your two hands, though the oldest can be as broad as a tortoise’s shell. Their bodies are flat and oval, made of overlapping black plates, each plate rimmed with a faint cobalt or bronze patina. They have eight legs, but they move them in such a slow, wave-like order that you may not notice the legs are moving until you count. Each foot ends in a brush of mineral-fiber setae that clings to the rock by tiny electrostatic forces. They leave no footprint, only a faint polished track where their weight has pressed the dust.

The shell is not just armor; it is a thermal instrument. The dorsal plates can be lifted or laid flat. In the morning, the stone-pilgrim tilts them toward the rising sun, and the black surface drinks heat. In the middle of the day, it presses itself into the shade of a stone or the flank of a colony and closes the plates, becoming a dark, flat stone. Its head is small and retractable. It has four eye-pits, each with a bead of mineral crystal that reads polarized light. It has a mouth composed of two curved rasps, like a small geological file. It feeds on the living rind of the colonies, but it never takes more than a few mouthsful from one edge. The colony responds by thickening its tissue at the wound, and the stone-pilgrim moves on.

They do not seem to have a daily purpose. They walk along routes that have been worn into the dust over centuries. These paths are shallow at first, then deeper, until they are grooves in the stone. They connect colonies, hollow rocks, salt pans, and the sheltered places where dust accumulates. A stone-pilgrim will travel one of these paths for a year, then turn and follow another. The paths are not random; they form a loose net over the plateau, and at certain intersections there are small piles of smooth stones, not made by the pilgrims but by the wind, which they seem to use as markers.

Their reproduction is so rare that no one has likely seen it. The females lay one or two pearlescent eggs in the dust beneath a colony's rim. The young are smaller, paler, and slower even than the adults. They spend their first years inside the shelter of the colony's mineral crust, feeding on the loose salt scales. When they emerge, they are like small black buttons with legs. They grow by molting, but only when the ground temperature stays within a certain narrow band for a full season. The molt is a dangerous time; the new shell is soft and white for a few hours, and the individual must hide in a hollow rock.

There are few other mobile lives. **Spore-flits** are tiny, short-lived creatures with wings of iridescent film; they appear only around colonies at dawn, when the spore-release begins. They live for a day, maybe two, and their bodies are so light that they are carried upward by the slightest thermal. They do not fly so much as float, their wings held open to catch the rising heat. Their larvae develop inside the spore cases themselves, eating the inner oil and leaving a hollow shell. You might mistake them for a flicker of light, a brief rainbow at the corner of your eye.

There are **salt-mites** too small to see, that live in the crystalline frost; they are no larger than dust grains, and they feed on the chemical salts. Their populations bloom at dawn, when the frost sublimates, and by midday they have returned to the ground. The whole plateau’s dust is alive with them in a dispersed, almost invisible way.

If the place has a personhood, it is not in the colonies or the stone-pilgrims alone. It is in the way these things are connected: the rooted ones passing rare elements through the glassroot, the stone-pilgrims pruning their edges and carrying spores on their shell margins, the hollow rocks singing, the dust rising and settling. The lives here are not busy; they are woven into the long intervals. They live the way the light lives—in pulses of bright and cool, in patient store-and-release, in distances measured by the slow curve of the world.

If you ask who is the person here, who is the mind, do not look for a face. The stone-pilgrim has a deliberation that looks like thought. The colony has a responsiveness that looks like memory. The glassroot has a net of signals that looks like a nervous system. But none are persons in the way you mean. They are inhabitants of a place where being is not a story but a slow fact.
