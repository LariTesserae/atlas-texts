The Drowned Lattice is not empty. The water lies about emptiness the way it lies about distance and light. The inhabitants are there, but they are so slow and so woven into the garden that you have to learn to see them as part of it. They are not animals. They are not ghosts. They are the Tenders.

The first time you see one, you have already been looking at it for hours. It is a dark bundle of root and wattle at the corner of a terrace wall, all elbows and knots, so still that a mat of moss has grown over its back. You take it for a collapsed trellis. Then, over a time you cannot mark, it leans. Not a sudden movement—only the long, underwater tilt of something settling. A limb unbraids from its chest, pale new root-fingers opening like a slow bloom. The fingers close around a length of kelp cord. They begin to tie a knot. You watch for a while. The knot will not be finished while you are there. It might take a year.

Call them the Tenders. They are built of the same materials as the landscape: black root, split wood, woven wattle, stone blades, kelp fiber. Their bodies are not flesh. They have no blood, no muscle, no bone in the animal sense. Their limbs are bundles of fiber and xylem that bend when water pressure changes inside them. They move by growing. A foot extends as a pale root, presses into a joint in the pavement, thickens, darkens, becomes wood. The old foot behind it softens and lets go. One step may take a season. Raising an arm may take a month. They are not slow out of weakness. They are slow because they are plants that have learned to walk.

Their heads are not heads. They are dense knots of fine filaments, each one drifting in the current, tasting the water. What you might take for eyes are two deep pits where the glowing moss grows thickest; the moss catches the faint blue-gray light and gives back a cold green stare. They do not see the way you do. They read changes in light and shadow, the chemical taste of mineral seeps, the pressure of water moving through a channel. The whole garden is their sense organ. A wall slumping a finger's width sends a shiver through the paving stones, and a Tender a mile away tastes the change and begins, very slowly, to turn.

The work of the Tenders is the work of the place. They are why the terraces have not collapsed, why the kelp rows still run straight, why the sluice gates still rise and fall. They do not work in shifts. There is no day. A Tender may stand at a sluice gate for a hundred years, its body grown into the stone lintel, its root-fingers resting on the counterweight basket. When the water level changes, when the cold current shifts, it will move the gate. Not by pulling a lever. It will grow a new root into the basket, heavy with mineral, and the gate will rise. Then the Tender will root there again and wait another century.

In the kelp fields, the row-tenders are tall and spindly. They move along the planted lines on stilts of hardened root, bending at the waist to tie a slipping frond or cut away a leaf that has gone black. They use flint blades, knapped from the same gray stone as the walls, and they hold them in sockets of braided wood. The blades are not sharp in the way of metal. They are sharp enough, and they are replaced when the water wears them smooth. A row-tender may spend a decade on a single blade, resharpening it with careful pressure against a whetstone, the sound a low, rhythmic creak in the dark.

The wall-keepers are broader, older, more rooted. They do not walk. They have given up walking. They sit or kneel at the bases of walls, their backs grown into the stone, their legs sent out as a net of roots through the joints. When a wall slumps, the wall-keeper feels it as a change in its own body. It grows new root-joists into the crack, swelling slowly with water and minerals, pushing the stones back into place. Over centuries, the wall-keeper becomes part of the wall. Its upper body may still move—an arm to brush away silt, a head to taste the water—but the lower half is indistinguishable from the architecture. This is not a fate they fear. It is a promotion. A wall-keeper who becomes a wall is not dead. It is simply holding the garden more completely.

The channel-clearers are the opposite. They are thin, almost skeletal, with long trailing filaments that braid and unbraid in the current. They lie in the channels with their bodies arched over the water, and their fingers, finer than hair, sift the fine silt. They do not move the silt by force; they grow through it, binding it into mats, then the current carries the mats away. When a channel is blocked, a channel-clearer may spend twenty years lying in the same ditch, its back forming a bridge over the water. The moss on its back glows a little brighter when the water runs clear.

They communicate, after a fashion. Not with sound. They make sounds, but the sounds are only byproducts: the creak of fiber, the clink of stone, the hiss of trapped bubbles. They talk by grafting. When two Tenders meet, they do not look at each other. They stand close, sometimes for years, and their root-filaments grow into one another. They exchange strands. When they part, each carries a few roots of the other. Those roots remember. A Tender may have a dozen foreign strands woven into its body, each one a record of a different meeting, a different wall, a different season. The garden itself is a network of such grafts. The Tenders are not individuals in the way you are. They are a slow, rooted society, connected under the pavement by threads of shared root.

They do not eat. There is no eating here. They draw their substance from the mineral seeps and the cold water, through root-hairs on their feet and the moss on their shoulders. They do not sleep. They rest by rooting, by letting the water move through them. They do not breathe. The only breath they have is the slow exchange of gases in their fibers, a process so close to the stone's own patience that it is hard to call it life.

And yet they live. They grow. They reproduce—not by seed or birth, but by shaping. There is a nursery on a shallow terrace, a place where the water is faintly warmer and the moss glows a little brighter. Here, young Tenders are grown. They begin as bundles of root, no larger than a small chest. The old ones tie them to wooden forms, bending the still-soft limbs into the shape of their future work. A future row-tender is stretched tall; a future wall-keeper is braced low and broad. The forms themselves are old, polished by centuries of young roots. The young Tenders do not learn. They are trained, the way an espaliered tree is trained, by pressure and time. Knowledge is in the shape. When a young Tender's roots harden into the right angles, it is ready.

Sometimes a Tender grows too old. It does not die so much as settle. It chooses a place—a gate, a corner, a broken arch—and it roots for the last time. Its filaments go still. Its moss grows over its face. Its body becomes a trellis, then a wall, then a hill. The other Tenders do not mourn. They may graft a piece of the old one into the nursery, so that the new grow with a strand of the old. Memory is not lost. It is shared out like seed.

There are no animals, and there never were. The garden does not miss them. The Tenders are not a substitute. They are the oldest fact of the place, older than the walls. They built the walls. Not with machines, not with metal. They split the stone by growing roots into the cracks and waiting. They cut the blocks with flint and shaped them with stone hammers. They hauled the stones by growing their own bodies under them, lifting a little at a time over decades. The whole country is handmade because they made it with their hands—slow, root-fingered, patient hands that were never in a hurry.

If you stay long enough, you begin to see the garden for what it is: not a ruin, but a working landscape. The Tenders are still building. A new terrace is rising at the far edge of the basin, where the silt is deep. It will take a thousand years. The wall will rise one stone at a time, and each stone will be set by a Tender that will root beside it for a century before moving to the next. The Drowned Lattice is not abandoned. It is not frozen in time. It is only moving at the speed of its own life.

And when you finally leave, if you leave, you carry with you the texture of a place that is neither dead nor empty, but simply too patient to be seen. The moss glows. The kelp sways. A Tender at the gate raises its head, and the water shifts, and you understand that you were never alone in the dark.
