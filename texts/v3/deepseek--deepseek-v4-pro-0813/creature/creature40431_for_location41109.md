In the Bell there lives a woman, or something that remembers being one. She is not large. She is the size of the room in the way a shadow is the size of a wall. At first you might mistake her for a pile of broken basalt, because she sits so still on the highest ledge and her hair is a black, glassy tangle like obsidian that has not yet cooled. Then she breathes, and the pile becomes a body.

Her skin is the color of wet basalt. In places it has cracked like columnar jointing, and in those cracks there is a fine black dust and sometimes the same white mineral pigment she uses on the wall. Her face looks unfinished—not old, not young—as if the sea and the rock have been carving it in their spare moments. Her eyes are deeper than they should be. One is clouded, the gray-blue of the light through the roof opening. The other is darker, clearer, and moves less than it should, as if it has already seen the water’s two surfaces meet and does not need to look again.

She wears the place. A mat of storm-petrel feathers and sinew covers her shoulders. Around her wrists and ankles are cords of braided sinew, ash-colored and stiff with salt. Her feet are wrapped in hide, though the black sand has cut through them many times. Her hands are the oldest thing about her. They are hard-backed and split-knuckled, with flint nodules where fingernails used to be. With those hands she can cling to the wall when the mountain shrugs. She can stack seven blocks of basalt into a tower on the highest ledge. She can scratch water-level marks into the wall with a flint point, and she does, every time the pool rises or falls enough to matter. Some of the marks are faint; some are deep. She does not know if she made the faint ones a long time ago or if someone else did. The Bell has not told her.

She has no boat, no rope, no obvious way in or out except the sea-throat and the rent in the roof. She may have come through either one. She may have been left by the first flood that drowned the plateau, or condensed out of the spray after a long storm. When the water is calm and the mountain is not grinding too loudly, she sometimes sits with her back to the wall and runs a finger along the old scratches, as if reading a story in a language she is forgetting. The pictograph below them—two descending lines, like rain, or a person dissolving into sea—is hers. She made it with white mineral pigment, using her thumb and the edge of the flint scraper. She does not explain it. She only adds a third line when the upper water and the lower water lie unusually still against each other, and then she sits back and watches the pool as if she has said enough.

She eats almost nothing. She licks salt from the walls and scrapes the green-black cyanobacteria film with the flint, chewing slowly. She eats the blind amphipods one by one, taking them from their crevice like small, cold beads. She drinks from the fresh meltwater that bleeds down from the roof, never from the heavier brine below. She can see the glimmering skin between the two waters, and she watches it the way a landsman watches weather. She does not seem to need fire. The old hearth is there, but she rarely lights it—only when the roof-vent is clear and the air has gone thin and quiet. Then she makes a small flame and lets it throw her shadow up onto the wall, where the shadow joins the marks and the pictograph. She never sleeps on the lowest ledge. She sleeps on the high ledge, curled loosely, with one hand touching the stack of seven stones. In sleep she still seems to be listening. Her breathing changes with the surge, slowing just before the water seals the entrance and the hidden vent begins to whistle.

When the big waves come, she does not flinch. She opens her mouth. The air compresses, the vent whistles like a flute made of bone, and the note passes through her skull and out between her teeth. It is not a cry. It is a high, clear tone, almost tuneful, as if she has become the Bell’s clapper. Afterward, the deep bass of water hammering rock comes up through the floor, and she closes her mouth and listens to the mountain continuing to grow. She has learned the rhythm. She knows the water is still a breaker, not an eroder. She knows the rock is not yet soil. She knows that by evening a fissure may split the wall and by morning a slab may fall into the pool with a boom that shakes the bones. She is not afraid of it. She is more like the fissure than the slab.

There is a strangeness in her that matches the strangeness of the room. Sometimes she is not solid. When the light comes through the roof opening and strikes the spray, there is a moment—if you blink—when the ledge seems empty except for a deposit of black sand and a length of ash-colored sinew. Then the pool breathes, and she is there again, wet to the waist, or drawing air, or pressing a new scratch into the wall. She is not drowning. She is not staying. She is becoming. The two descending lines are not only a picture. They are her posture toward the world: a person entering, a person dissolving, a rain that falls into a sea and does not end.

The Bell is unfinished, and so is she. That is why she lives there. The room of air is not a refuge; it is a pause between one form and another. The sea is learning its own strength, and the rock has not yet learned to be stone. In that pause, she keeps the ledger, rebuilds the tower, feeds the almost-nothing that lives, and listens to the noise that never stops. She is the listener in the Bell—the one who makes the room not just a place, but a place that knows it is there.
