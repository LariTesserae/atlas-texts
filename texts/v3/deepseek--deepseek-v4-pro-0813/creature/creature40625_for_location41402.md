Down in the Low Lock, the census is not taken in heads or names. It is taken in damp. The place is full of tenants, but they do not always announce themselves. Some live behind the yellow window, some under the water, some in the high corners where the brick has gone soft and black.

The watchman is called Maren, though only the mechanic uses the name. She has lived in the small cabin on the near side long enough that her hair matches the mineral scale on the stone—pale, almost crusty at the temples. Her hands are pale as the bellies of the fish. She does not run the Lock; she tends the small ledger of its moods. She wakes when the pump house gives its long, low sigh, not because it is loud but because her body has become one of the Lock's valves. She writes in a logbook whose pages are never fully dry; the ink is grease pencil, which does not bleed. She records the water level, the direction of the fish, the turns of the brass wheel. When the weeping drum gulps and every fish turns to face it, she writes the time in the log, then pauses, pencil lifted, as if waiting for the drum to explain. It never does.

Sefi, the mechanic, comes down twice a week. They belong to the upper city in the way a harbor gull belongs to a wharf: always returning, never quite settled. By the second landing, their coat is dark at the shoulders and their boots are wet through. Sefi carries a bag of copper grease, fuses, lamp bulbs, and a twist of bread from above. They tend the engine. They do not start it or stop it. They wait until the brass float arm asks. The engine is no longer a machine, Sefi says; it is a lung. They press a palm to the flywheel and listen for the exact temperature at which the Lock decides to breathe. When the eels run, Sefi comes extra days. They stand at the pump volute with a lantern shaded to almost nothing and watch the dark ribbon pass. The eels, Sefi says, know the machine better than any mechanic.

The three others who keep rooms on the upper ledges are not exactly a community; they are more like separate species sharing a reef.

The weaver lives in the boiler house, where the wall is warm enough for moss. Her hands are greenish at the fingertips from the algae curtain she harvests, always a little at a time, never enough to bare the pipe. She strips the pale ferns to their threads and weaves a cloth that never rots, only darkens and softens, smelling of wet stone and ozone. The Lock's people wear her cloth at their knees and elbows. She also makes a white paste from the mineral scale, which she uses to patch cracked tiles. She hums to the white crickets, and they answer in a thin, continuous line that only she and the child can hear.

The listener keeps the old storehouse with the rusted sliding door. The door is always half open, not for air but so he can hear the water. He is not a mapmaker, though he draws the same pipes on slate until the lines are grooves. He can tell by the tick of claws on the sluice screens how many crabs are working. He knows by the surface of the water whether a snail has fallen. He feels the weeping drum in his jaw before it gulps, and he will pause on the catwalk and tilt his head like a bat. Sunlight, he says, is too loud. When Sefi arrives, the listener asks only one question: "How does the street sound?" Sefi tells him: tires, boots, a tram. The listener listens as if it were a foreign language, then goes back to his slate.

The youngest is a child of perhaps eleven or twelve. The Lock does not keep birthdays. She came down the stair so early that the upper city is only a rumor of brightness and vertical distance. She can walk the catwalks in perfect dark, not by memory but by the way the steel mesh rings underfoot. She keeps a tin of freshwater shrimp by her bed and feeds them a pinch of yeast. The shrimp are not exactly pets; they are a small living barometer. When they line up on the side of the tin, she knows the sump will rise, and she tells the watchman. She has named the largest crab Bolt, for the rusted fastener it lives under. She is learning to read the fish; when they all face the weeping drum, she writes it in the log in small, careful letters. She is the only one who asks why. The watchman says, "The drum speaks. The fish listen. You and I just write." The child accepts this, but she still watches the drum longer than necessary.

Below and around the humans, the rest of the population goes about its slow work.

The pale fish are the water's sense of direction. Their single blue line is the brightest thing in the Lock on most days. They school in slow spirals, not for protection but for something like attention. When the weeping drum gulps, every blue line turns toward it for one second, like a compass needle remembering north. No one catches them. The rule is old: if the fish vanish, the sump forgets to turn.

The eels are the Lock's year, its migration. They nest in the mouths of pipes, tails curled around warm fittings. Their bodies are the color of old oil. Once a year they move through the pump volutes and the deep gate in one long, silent ribbon, turning the small brass wheel as they pass. The watchman reads that wheel and opens the gate at the right hour. The eels do not hurry. Some of them rest in the warm water below the transformer shed, their tails wrapped around pipes, as if they had found the one place in the world that understands them.

The crabs are the Lock's hands. They live on the sump floor and climb the sluice screens, picking them clean of hair and fiber. Their backs are black, their joints red as warning lights. The largest one, Bolt, works the screen below the watchman's cabin. The watchman leaves a smear of grease on a stone for him, but not every night—only when the screen is especially fouled. If the crabs are removed, the screens clog and the water rises. So the crabs are left alone, and in return they keep the Lock's pores open.

The shrimp, the water beetles, the snails, the moths, the bats, the white crickets—they are all small organs of a single slow body. The bats drop guano into the sump; the guano feeds the shrimp; the shrimp feed the fish; the fish turn the water; the water cools the engine; the engine warms the wall where the ferns grow. Nothing is wasted. Even the silver trails the snails leave on the tiles are a kind of map, if you know how to read them. The listener does.

There is also the fungus in the high corners, white and faintly luminous, like a thought that will not quite form. The weaver says it grows where the water has whispered too long. The listener says it appears in the same places every year, but never quite the same shape. The child thinks it is the Lock dreaming. The watchman does not say what she thinks.

And beneath all of them, or through all of them, the Lock itself is alive. It breathes through the weeping drum. Its pulse is the pump sigh. Its skin is the mineral scale and the rust bloom, sealing and re-sealing. Its blood is the black water, moving so slowly it seems still, but not still—never still. The people are not keepers here. They are more like the gut flora in a warm fold of a larger body: doing small useful things, keeping the passages clear, writing down the dreams. The eels are its migrating thoughts. The fish are its attention. The crabs are its hands. The child is its question. The watchman is its memory. The mechanic is its occasional breath from the upper world.

At lamplighting, which is most of the time, the ones who are below will drift toward the yellow window of the watchman's cabin—the watchman, the weaver, the listener, the child, and Sefi if the mechanic has come down. They do not call it dinner. There is no bell. The pump sighs, and that is enough. They sit on crates and sip from the same kettle, watching the fish turn and the mist settle. No one speaks much. The transformer hums in a steady note. The weeping drum gulps. The child writes a question in her log but does not ask it. The Lock settles, not sleeping, but awake in the way deep places are awake: patient, watchful, and full of small lives waiting for the next slow change.
