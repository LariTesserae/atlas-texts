The High Mat is not inhabited in the way a city is inhabited. It is inhabited the way a body is inhabited—by its own tissues, by symbiotes, by small warm things in the folds of a larger warmth.

The first inhabitant is the ground. The mat is alive, not as a backdrop but as the largest living thing on the high plain. It draws water up before dawn, lifts a finger-width, and settles at dusk. Its surface is warm from metabolism, not only from the sun. When it is cut, it heals slowly with a pale woody scar. It may be one individual or many fused; even the people do not know. They say the mat remembers. The cups that fill with fog water are made by its old broken crowns and folded leaves, and these cups are the only standing water for miles. The mat drinks first from that water, and everything else drinks after.

The towers are its vertical expression. They are not separate. They are flowering trunks sent up from the mat at intervals so wide that no living person has seen a new one begin. Each tower is hollow, fluted, and enormous; its interior is a warm, resonant tube. At the base of each healthy tower lives the drummer—an animal the people call the tower’s heart. It is the size of a crouching human, blind, soft-backed, slick with black resin. It strikes the inner wall with a knobbed tail of callus, sending the low boom through the flutes and out across the mat. The boom is not a call; it is a heartbeat slowed almost to the pace of stone. The drummer feeds on weeping sap and pale wingless insects in the inner dark. When it falls silent, the people know the tower is dying, and they will not camp there.

The tower’s upper hollows are packed with bats. At dusk they pour out in a black ribbon, so dense that the sky near the tower becomes liquid. They feed on glass-winged cicadas and night moths. The cicadas sit in the flutes by day, thousands on each tower, their transparent wings shuddering until the air becomes a single metallic drone. That drone is the sound of safety. When it drops, something is moving.

The ledgebirds nest on the outer buttresses. They are small and brown as old leaves, and they pipe at dawn and clatter their wings when the bats return. Their eggs are collected by the people, one per nest. The raptors hang above in the high indigo, watching for the hare-run. Most are long-winged and pale-bellied, almost invisible against the brightness. There are larger ones, too, that nest on the highest crowns and are seen only as broad shadows crossing the mat. The people do not speak their name aloud.

Between the towers, root-hares live in shallow crevices. They are no bigger than a human hand, with fur like dry leather and ears that lie flat. They graze on the thin film of spores and yeasts that grows on the mat’s surface, and they move in streams at dawn and dusk. Their running is a ripple, and the raptors fall on that ripple. The hares are the smallest visible pulse of the place.

The water cups are their own small worlds. Fog climbs over the rim each dawn and drags its belly across the mat. Water condenses and runs into the cups. Bladderworts bloom in the brown water. Tiny translucent swimmers hatch, feed, and die between dawn and noon. A cup-lizard with transparent toes drinks, then a root-hare, then a bird, then a person. By midday the cup is empty except for a damp ring. The next dawn it fills again.

The people are the only ones there who use fire and bronze. They have no name for themselves. Lowland traders call them cupwalkers; among themselves they say “we who listen.” They are thin and sun-dark, with pale scars on their palms from obsidian flakes and bone hooks. They wear long cloaks of tower-bark fiber, rubbed with resin until they shine like the mat itself. In the heat-shimmer, two walkers can vanish into the ground’s own color. Their feet are wrapped in soft leather, not to protect their feet from the mat but to protect the mat from their feet.

There are no villages. Only single-family shelters: a drystone arc against a root flare, a woven platform hung in a fork, a doorway cut into living wood and closed with a slab of bark. They stay near one tower for a season, then move when the cups run dry too early or when the drummer’s rhythm changes. They carry their tools in baskets: obsidian knives, bronze needles from the lowland trade, rope braided from tower-bark fiber. Fire is kept in a lidded clay pot and lit only on flat stones. Fuel is never cut from the living mat; they burn dried bat droppings, dead epiphyte stems, fallen leaf blades. They eat root-hares caught in noose snares, pale larvae from the tower sap, ledgebird eggs, and the gray moss that grows on the windward flanks.

Travel is always in pairs. Each walker carries a pole with a hooked bone tip, for probing the mat and turning over hares. They cross open ground only when the cicada drone is high. If the drone suddenly drops, they stop back-to-back and wait. They do not travel at dawn or dusk, when the hare-run and the raptors own the open. Near a tower, speech is useless—the sound is too thick—so they talk by tapping the hand and forearm: quick finger shapes for danger, water, home. At a distance they use little bone whistles that mimic ledgebird piping.

They cut the mat only when they must, and then only a wound no longer than a hand. They pour water into the cut and sit with it for a day. The wound heals over years, and the scar is remembered as a debt. Children learn to read those scars early. The dead are laid in the deep root galleries with a cup of water in their hands, and the mat closes over them slowly. The people say the dead become part of the ground’s rising.

There are other things on the High Mat that the people do not name. Sometimes the insect drone drops for no raptor. In the morning, a long groove is pressed into the mat, as if a heavy body dragged itself between two distant towers. The edges are not torn but folded, as if the ground yielded like skin and then tried to forget. The people place crossed stones at the ends of such grooves and walk around them, sometimes for days. They say the under-mat things are old and dream too widely. They are not enemies, but they are not awake in the same world.

So the High Mat is full. Its emptiness is only an absence of vertical trees; the life is pressed low and high. The mat is the fundamental note. The towers are the resonant bodies. The animals and people are the overtones. The sound is not background—it is the voice of every living thing in the place, remade each second by small mouths, wide wings, and the patient boom from the hollow wooden hearts.
