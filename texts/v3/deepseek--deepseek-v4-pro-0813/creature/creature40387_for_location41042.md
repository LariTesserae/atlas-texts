No one lives here now. But the place is not empty.

By the time you notice it, you have already been there longer than you meant to stay. The cold has climbed into your coat, and your breath hangs in the air like a slow thought. You have been looking at the moss, the water, the tree, the light that seems to come from inside the leaves. Then you see it.

At first it is only a darker green, a thickness in the moss at the base of the west wall, where the seep runs and the stone is always wet. You take it for a fallen branch, or a heap of stones the wall has shed, or a shadow that the bright sky cannot reach. But the more you look, the more the shape insists on itself. It has shoulders. It has a head. It has the long, folded line of a back. It is sitting with its knees drawn up, its arms wrapped around them, its head bowed. It is not hidden. It is only so still that your eyes had passed over it as part of the wall.

Then it breathes.

The movement is so slow you almost miss it. The narrow chest rises, holds, falls. It is not the breathing of a person; it is the breathing of something that has learned to live at the speed of frost. Each breath takes a long time, and between breaths there is no sound at all. You count your own heartbeats, and you lose count.

The skin is pale as the inside of a fern, grey-white, with a bloom of green along the left side where the water from the seep runs down the wall behind it. It is not rotten. It is not bruised. It is old, but not the way skin is old. It is the way stone is old, smoothed and settled, with the fine veins of root hairs showing through at the temples and the inner wrists. The arms are thin, but not starved. The fingers are long, the knuckles big, the nails thick and yellow-grey like horn, and moss grows in the spaces between them. A woodlouse moves across the back of one hand, and the hand does not twitch. The dweller does not mind.

The face is turned a little away, so you see the long line of the jaw, the hollow under the cheekbone, the ear full of shadow. A snail rests in the curve of that ear, its shell like a small lump of mortar. The hair is dark at the crown and matted, but where it touches the wet stone it has become green, strand by strand, as if it is turning into moss. The eyes are open. You thought they were two wet stones. They are not stones. They are looking at the water running down the wall in front of it, and they do not blink.

One bare foot is half-buried in the leaf litter at the edge of the drain. The toes are spread, pale, and the moss has grown over the ankle. The alder root passes under the dweller’s leg and disappears into the floor. A fern has rooted in the crook of its arm. It is not trapped. It is woven in. The whole place has grown through it and around it, or it has grown through the place, and it no longer matters which.

You take a step, and the sound is swallowed by the moss. The dweller’s eyes move, not to you, but to the place where the sound would have been, as if it heard the silence change shape. Then the eyes find you. They are the color of the wet stone, grey-green, with no white around them. They hold you without fear, without welcome, without question. It is the look the blackbird gave you, but older. Much older. It does not ask who you are. It already knows you are temporary.

Then it lowers its head again, very slowly, and a drop of water falls from a fern tip onto its forehead and runs down the bridge of its nose. It does not wipe it away. The water becomes part of it. A moth, pale and soft, lands on its shoulder and folds its wings. The dweller does not acknowledge it. The moth knows it is safe.

You begin to understand what it is. It is not a person who came here to live. It is not a ghost. It is not a creature that uses the place. It is the place’s own inhabitant, the point where the moss and the water and the cold and the silence knot together. It breathes in the cold air and out the moisture. The loop runs through it just as it runs through the tree and the stone and the springtails and the frogs. The frogs do not call because they learned silence from it. The snails move in slow circuits around its body. The blackbird comes down and watches, and does not sing, because the dweller has taken the sound out of the world here.

It does not tend the shaft. It has become the shaft. It is why nothing accumulates or empties. It is why the seedling succeeds and fails at the same rate. It is why the light stays bright and the cold stays cold. It is not keeping the balance. It is the balance.

The old women say that if you throw a stone into the Moss Well, the sound will not come back. It is not the moss that eats it. It is the dweller. It breathes the sound in and does not let it out. The children are told not to shout into the gap, not because it is forbidden, but because the dweller would hear, and it would not answer, and the silence after a child’s shout would be too heavy to carry back up the stairs.

On the wall above its head, half-hidden by the moss, there are cut marks. They are not the chisel marks of the builders. They are finer, older, a tally of years or names or returns. The dweller’s fingers could have made them. Or perhaps they were already there when it came, and it has spent a long time reading them with its eyes, the way you read the grain of a stone. The steps by which you entered are worn into soft scoops by feet that stopped coming long ago. Maybe those feet were its. Maybe the door was sealed from the inside, and it stayed because the silence had already begun to grow over it like moss.

You stay until the light begins to go, though you cannot tell when it began, because the light here is not from the sky alone. The dweller has not moved again. It will not move when you leave. The moss will grow over the place where you stood, and the silence will close behind you like water. It is not waiting for you to go. It is simply in its state, complete. You are the only thing out of balance. And as you turn to climb the narrow steps, you realize that you are no longer sure you have a voice. You do not test it. You leave the Moss Well to its tenant, and you take the cold out with you, folded into your coat like a secret.
