Do not look for a keeper. The keeper is gone. The stone hammer and the clay oil-cup on the ledge are only a shed skin, and no hand has lifted them in a long time. What lives in the Weeping Yard does not use such things. It lives below the water, below the causeway, in the long drowned hollow where the room is still becoming something else.

It is the black eel—the old one, the tenant of the west drain. Not the small eels that lie under the causeway. Those are its shadowlings, its echo, its lesser repetitions. The one that matters is older, slower, and much larger. If you stood on the causeway and looked straight down into the clear dark water, you would likely not see it. You would see your own face, warped by ripples. Perhaps a pale fish. Perhaps the faint gleam of a frog’s belly. But not the eel. The eel is under the room, in the part of the country that has no ceiling.

Its body is black, but not the black of polished basalt or of clean river stone. It is black as a bruise that has forgotten its source. Its skin is loose and smooth, made dull by years of silt and lime, and it gives off a slime that makes the water around it feel thicker, faintly sweet, as if the room itself were keeping a secret. When the grey light from the air-shafts touches the eel’s back, the light does not shine. It sinks in and is not returned. The eel absorbs the room.

It is long enough to be mistaken, in the shallows, for a sunken timber. Its body lies along the channel with a kind of patient weight, thicker than a man’s arm at the shoulder of its body, tapering to a tail no wider than a thumb. It rarely shows itself all at once. It moves by slow degrees, coiling through the drain’s throat, turning under the causeway like water that has briefly decided to become solid. The eel does not swim so much as extend. The water does not part around it; it consents to be displaced.

The head is the oldest-looking part. It is broad and blunt, not sharp like a snake’s, and the skin over its skull is smooth as a river-stone. It has no eyes. Where eyes should be there are two pale, sealed patches, like old scars grown over, and these patches shiver with every change in pressure. It feels sound before sound becomes sound. A footstep on the causeway arrives at the eel as a small collapse in the water. A frog landing is a body with two heartbeats. A stranger entering the room changes the whole acoustic weather, and that change reaches the eel as a cool line along its side before the frogs pause. It does not need to see. The chamber is its eye.

Its mouth is a long fold, almost gentle, set low on the head. At the corners hang four small barbels that taste the water. When a dead fish turns on its side and is pulled toward the drain, the barbels lift. The mouth opens only wide enough to receive the offering, and the teeth—small, backward-curved, the color of wet glass—draw it down into the long dark of the throat. It does not hunt. It does not rush. The room feeds it. Drowned frogs, fallen bats, white fish gone still, the occasional pale crab that lost its footing on the wet stone: all come to it eventually. It has eaten the leavings of keepers, too. Old bread, a twist of reed rope, once a shattered clay cup. The cup passed through it as a scattering of grit. It eats rarely and with a slowness so complete that the act seems like part of the tide.

It lives on the room’s clock. Twice a day, when the marsh tide presses down or the hidden siphon closes, the eel shifts in the drain. It does not think about it. It adjusts its body the way a sleeper adjusts to the shape of the bed. The water rises. The frogs fall silent. The bats stir and settle. Then, when the pressure changes, the eel uncoils, presses forward, and the drain clears with a sound like a wet cough. That sound is the eel’s voice. It is the only sound it makes, besides a low, almost inaudible grinding when its jaw wears against the stone. After it has spoken, the frogs begin again, but a half-step higher, not because they are afraid but because the room has changed. The eel has changed the room.

It is not a king. It does not patrol the ledges or enforce the territories of the crabs and bats. The vault belongs to the bats. The ledges belong to the crabs. The water belongs to the fish and the frogs. But beneath all of them runs the eel’s long road. It moves from the eastern carved mouth to the western drain and back, not often, but with a certain inevitable rhythm. When it moves, the silt bears a single long furrow, like a finger drawn through the dark. The smaller eels follow that furrow and eat whatever the great body stirs up. The crabs climb. The fish make way. No one commands this. It is simply the arrangement of the world.

Its memory is not a story. It remembers pressure and temperature. It remembers the taste of certain floods. It remembers the year a brick fell from the vault and struck the water, and how that sound traveled through every channel for days. It remembers the last keeper’s boot coming down into the shallows and not being followed by a leg. It tasted the boot, and then the boot was gone. It remembers the slow westward settling of the room, the way the floor has dropped by one frog-call, the way the bricks have all leaned slightly toward the drain. It does not hold these memories; it is made of them. They are layered in its flesh like silt.

In the dry season, when the water thins and the moss turns grey, the eel pulls back into the drain, where the mud is soft and the air is all humidity. It breathes through its skin and waits. In the wet season, when the channels join and the water rises to the first step, it may lie just under the surface, and the white fish gather near its belly as if near a slow warm current. The fungus glows on the walls, but the eel does not reflect the glow. It appears as a black absence in the water, a shape that is not a shadow, a long moving gap.

There are no keepers now. But the place is still kept. The eel keeps it by staying, by pressing its body into the drain, by letting the water rise and fall, by not leaving. It is the room’s low tide. Its oldest resident. Its slow, blind heart. The Weeping Yard weeps, but the eel does not weep. It only waits, and the water moves through it like a language, and the frogs sing a half-step higher because the world has shifted, and the world has shifted because the eel is still there.
