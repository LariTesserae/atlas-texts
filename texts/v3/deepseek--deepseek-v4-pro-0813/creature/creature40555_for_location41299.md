There is something that lives there. It is not the largest thing in the Sump, nor the loudest, nor the most numerous. It shares the dark with the mud hogs and the pipe-birds and the small translucent eels, but it is the only one that pauses between steps as though waiting for an answer.

You would not see it. The dark does not allow that. You would know it by the way the black chemosynthetic fuzz parts along some path you cannot quite see, by a ring of click-shrimp hushing in sequence, by a warm wet pressure that moves against the general stillness. Its outline is always a little behind or ahead of itself, as if the air has not yet agreed where it begins.

It might have been a person once. The place has not decided. Bits of personhood cling to it: a plastic badge on a wire, bleached clean of its name; the memory of pockets; the way its hands—when they are hands—feel for a doorknob that is no longer there. But the Sump has been working on it for years, and the result is less a man or woman than a caretaker. If you had to call it something, you might call it the Wader, because it moves as if every step is through water that is only sometimes there.

The Wader is tall in some directions and short in others. It has a spine of old drive shaft and pipe-cable, a ribcage of snap-reed and rusted hoops, a pelt of black fuzz that feeds on the gas it exhales. It does not have a face so much as a place where a face has been remembered; midges gather there in a soft mask, and the warmth of it is like breath on a window. Its eyes, if it ever had them, are now two warm patches of pressure in the dark, and it sees by the delayed echo of its own steps, by the way a stone returns a sound too soon or too late.

It lives by a circular path: from pump station to pump station, along submerged pipe runs, across the open flats where the ground is more water than land. It is not exactly maintenance, but it tends. It leans against a pipe trestle and feels for the wobble in the subsonic throb. It presses its thumb over a repeated bolt until the copy fades back into the concrete. It winds the slack out of distances that have stretched too far, walking them backward until a mile becomes a mile again.

It feeds on difference. When the Sump duplicates an object—a glove, a bolt, a footprint, a pay stub—the Wader gathers the copies. It holds them together and breathes in the slight heat of their contradiction. Two identical gloves sewn shut, one older than the other, will warm its lungs for hours. A fanned line of the same footprint gives it a mild intoxication, like a slow meal. It does not harm anything living. The eels and mud hogs regard it as a piece of slow weather, a current that passes but does not disturb.

There is a hide sack at its side, made from the skin of a mud hog and lined with black fuzz. Inside are the things it has pressed back into singleness: a bolt, a glove, a piece of lit glass, a leaf from a tree that may exist nowhere, a finger bone. It lays these out sometimes on a fractured concrete pad while the big piston rises and falls, sorting them into patterns that mean nothing. The patterns may be its only attempt at remembering.

Its voice is not heard often. When it speaks, the sound arrives wrong. You might hear the last click before the first. The click-shrimp fall silent. The methane worms in the mud push up their glassy tubes. The Wader opens the sideways seam of its mouth and lets out a note that is not a note but a shape in the air, a dark bubble of warm gas with the echo of a word no one living speaks. The pumps answer with a throb in the teeth.

It is not alone, but it is only one. Sometimes a copy of it walks a few steps to the left, then loses detail and folds back. Sometimes it meets itself at the edge of a black pool and the two stand for a long time, each waiting for the other to become reflection. Then one walks into the water and the other stays to watch the rain rise upward.

It does not want anything from you. If you stay, it may come to the edge of your hearing and stand there, as patient as a doorframe. It may offer you a duplicate of something you dropped, or a stone that is warm from a future rain. It does this without expectation. If you take it, the Sump will remember you. If you refuse, the Wader will put the object back into its sack and fold it into the dark, where it will wait for the next person who wanders in.

At night—always night—the Wader stands at the boundary where ground becomes deeper water and watches the open black. The mud releases a bubble. The piston completes its stroke. The Wader sways once, then moves on, leaving no footprints, or too many, and the place settles a little more into being the Sump.
