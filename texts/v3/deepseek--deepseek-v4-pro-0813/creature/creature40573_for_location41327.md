In the ring of stones, at the back of the hollow, something sits. It is the only thing in the cave not covered by the living fur. The pale creatures crowd up to the boundary of the ring, their bodies piling against the smooth foreign stones in a dense, quivering ridge, but they do not enter. The circle of bare wet stone is held open, and in its center is a figure.

At first it looks like a stone itself—a formation, perhaps, or a shadow cast by the light. But the longer you look, the more it becomes itself. It is human-shaped, or nearly: a body seated with its back against the wall where the tally marks are cut, legs folded, hands resting palms-up on its knees. Its skin is the color of wet limestone, a pale grey with a faint blue undertone from the cold glow. It is hairless, smooth, and so thin that the bones beneath read as shadows—the ridge of the skull, the cage of the ribs, the long bones of the arms. It is not starved. It is pared down, as if the cave has worn away everything unnecessary and left only the essential form.

Its head is bowed slightly, chin almost touching sternum. Its eyes are open. They are large, the irises pale as the moss, the pupils contracted to black points. They reflect the walls’ glow with a wet sheen, but they do not track movement. They do not blink. Its lips are slightly parted, and a fine film of condensation gathers in the cup of its upper lip, never falling. Its fingers are long, the knuckles prominent, the nails worn to blunt edges that match the scratches on the wall. The tally marks behind it stop abruptly, as if the hand that made them set down the tool and never picked it up again.

The bone splinters are arranged in a fan just beside the figure’s left knee. Up close, they are too smooth, too uniformly curved, to be from an animal. They might be from the figure itself—shed teeth, or the tips of fingers, or something else that the body no longer needs. They are not scattered. They are placed, deliberately, like a record.

The dweller does not seem to notice the light or the sound. The continuous metallic trill passes through it as if it were water. Its ears are small, folded close to the skull, but the skin over them vibrates faintly. Its body is the same temperature as the air. It does not appear to breathe. If you watch long enough—and time moves strangely here—you might see a single, shallow rise of the chest, a movement so slight it could be a trick of the light. A pulse, if there is one, is too slow to feel.

It is not dead. That is the strange thing. The stillness is not the stillness of a corpse. It is the stillness of a thing that has found its only shape, like the cave itself. The dweller is not waiting. It is not sleeping. It is simply there, as much a part of the hollow as the calcite ridges or the pale moss.

The creatures do not avoid the ring because the stones are foreign. They avoid it because the dweller’s presence has marked the space—a faint chemical or thermal signal that the colony reads as *not here*. The dweller is not a predator. It is not a competitor. It is a hole in the colony’s tissue, a place where the tissue does not grow. The ring of stones is the only boundary it maintains, and it maintains it without effort, simply by being.

If you were to enter the circle and touch its skin, you would find it cool and slightly tacky, like the film of water on stone. It would not react. Perhaps its eyes would shift, the pupils widening a fraction, but that might be only your shadow falling across them. The creatures would not move to defend it. They do not care. They are not its guards. They are simply another layer of the place, and the dweller is another layer, and the stone is another layer.

The dweller does not eat. The moss does not grow in the circle, and the water that condenses on its skin is enough. Its waste, if any, is minimal—a fine dust that settles into the calcite and is indistinguishable from the rock. It does not breathe as you do. Its metabolism is so slow that it might exchange gases through its skin, a passive diffusion that takes no effort. It has no need for the light, but the light is always there, and its skin has taken on a faint luminescence, not from the creatures but from years of exposure. In the darkness, it would glow, but there is no darkness here.

Its thoughts, if it has them, are not like yours. They are not sentences. They are not images. They are more like the slow growth of a calcite drapery—an accretion of moments, layer upon layer, each one almost identical to the last. It has been here for so long that the cave and the dweller have grown into each other. The tally marks were an attempt to hold onto time, to place a mark on each passage, but time here does not pass. It pools. The marks were abandoned because there was nothing to count. The figure’s posture is not one of despair or patience. It is simply the shape a body takes when it has stopped moving.

The dweller is the only thing in the cave that could change, but it does not. It has chosen—if choice still applies—to become part of the equilibrium. It is the one who stayed. The one who stopped. It is not the cave’s master, nor its prisoner. It is the cave’s answer to the question of what lives here: something that no longer needs to live, but is not dead.

If you leave and the seam closes behind you, the dweller will remain. The creatures will not fill the circle, because the dweller is still there. The water will condense on its skin. The light will shine in its open eyes. The sound will vibrate through its bones. And the cave will continue, perfectly, with its only inhabitant sitting in the bare circle, as motionless as the stone, as patient as the water, as bright and silent and still as the light itself.
