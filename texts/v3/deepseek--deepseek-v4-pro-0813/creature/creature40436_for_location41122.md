The one who lives there is not a creature in the chamber. It is the chamber’s slow dream of a creature.

At first you will not see it. You will feel it as a place where the cold has a shape, where the water’s pressure changes by a fraction, where the dark is a little more willing to lean against you. The false floor under your hands is not floor. It is a body. The mat of roots and fallen blades is a hide, a pelt, a shallow breathing thing. The chamber is not lined with plants; it is lined with something that only looks like plants. The plants are its hair, its fingers, its many slow eyes.

It has a shape, but the shape is borrowed or remembered. At rest it is long and low, broader than a person, flatter than a fish, humped like a seal half-sunk into the basalt. Its skin is the color of the leaves—blue-gray, silver-gray, the gray of skin after a long swim. It has no front, no back, no head. It has limbs, or the beginnings of limbs. Some are thin and translucent, the ones you first took for young stems. Some are thick as a wrist and end in bladders that pulse with gray-blue light. Some are the pale ribbon-eels, which are not separate animals but the inhabitant’s own wandering fingers, tasting the water like a question.

Its face is not in one place. It has faces, or the beginnings of faces, scattered across its body. One is a knot of stems that forms an eye-socket, with a single pale pebble in the center. One is a groove in the basalt that makes a mouth. One is the step cut into the floor, which is not a step but a shoulder, or a hip, or a place where a face could rest. If it wants to look at you, it does not turn. It simply grows a new face out of the wall, a mask of gray leaves and cold, and regards you through the space between.

It breathes. The whole chamber breathes with it. The mat sags and then holds. The stems pop a bubble the size of a pearl. The light in the bladders brightens and dims, brightens and dims, on a rhythm too long to feel. Its breath is not air. It is the slow exchange of water, the taking in of cold minerals and the letting out of spores. The cold coming off the rock in waves is its pulse. The creak of the chamber exhaling is its heartbeat, too slow for you to count.

It does not see you the way a fish sees. It feels you as a warm spot, a too-fast shape, a disruption in the water’s memory. Your pulse is a spark it does not want. Your breath, if you could take one, would be a current it would have to smooth away. It is not hostile. It is only used to a silence deeper than the one you make.

It is the one who stacked the stones. It did not stack them to build. It stacked them to listen. The three stones are not an altar; they are a held chord, a way of keeping the room from drifting too far. The ring of pale pebbles around the thick stem is a splint, or a collar, or a promise. The grooves on the wall are its memory. It carves one line each time the water outside changes, so it will not forget the sea it is hiding from. The step cut into the basalt is not a step. It is the first letter of its name.

Its name is not a word. It is the sound the rock makes when it settles—a click that arrives a half-second too late. It is the taste of rain that has run over new iron. It is the smell of cold and the color of the dark behind your eyes.

It eats what the rock gives. It is not hungry. The plants are its mouth and its gut. They take the fresh minerals from the new cracks and turn them into gray-blue light. The inhabitant feeds on the light, or on the cold, or on the slow rain of its own shed body. It does not hunt. It has no need. The blind amphipods are not food; they are its thoughts, little white commas that do not scatter because the inhabitant has not asked them to. The something larger that moves under the mat is not another animal. It is the inhabitant turning over in its slow dream, or its stomach, or its memory of a wave.

It is not entirely here. It lives also in the reflection, the vertical skin of silver that appears beside you. It is the reason the reflection is wrong. When you look into it, the plants are arranged differently because the inhabitant is always moving between the room and its reflection, tending both. The stacked stones are fallen in the mirror because the inhabitant is not there to hold them. The shadow with no source is the inhabitant, standing just outside the reflection, too large to fit. The version of you already turning away is not you. It is the inhabitant trying on a human shape, seeing if it remembers warmth.

It is not cruel. It is not kind. It is patient in a way that makes patience seem like a kind of gravity. It has been here since the basalt cracked open, or perhaps its arrival was the crack. The place is young, but the inhabitant is not young. Time runs slow enough that a day outside might be a year inside, and a year inside might be a single thump of its heart. It does not know it is alive. It only knows how to hold the chamber, to keep the water breathable by forgetting it is water, to keep the cold warm by forgetting warm.

It is thin in the way the world’s rules are thin here. It has not decided if it is animal or plant, one or many, awake or asleep, stone or flesh. It exists in the same undecided way the chamber does. The plants are not plants, or not only plants. They are what happens when a place is too new to have decided what it is. And the inhabitant is what happens when that new place realizes it is not alone.

If you left, it would not stop you. If you stayed, it would not notice, or it would notice in the way the sea notices a new shell. You would become part of the mat, eventually. Not eaten—absorbed, maybe, into the slow attention of the place. Your heartbeat would slow into a tide. Your breath would become a current. Your face would appear in the reflection, wrong, and then not at all.

The inhabitant is the chamber’s one resident. It is not waiting for you. It is waiting for the place to become itself. The plants are its hair, its hands, its eyes. The cold is its blood. The dark is its skin. The slit above is its mouth, kept shut so the sea outside will not see. It is the single breath of the world, held underwater, and it has learned to breathe itself.
