The first thing to understand about the people of the Underworks is that they are not hiding. They are the same color as the walls, and they move at the speed of silt.

They call themselves keepers, or sometimes just “the ones who hold,” though they are not warm. They are few. No one has made an exact count in a long time, because some may be dead in a flooded room and some may have gone down into the east mines, but the round number is seven. Seven keepers for five streets. That is not a population; it is a watch.

If you stayed still in the main water-alley long enough, with your lamp off and your breath shallow, you would eventually see one of them detach from the wall. For the first minute you would think it was a tangle of dead cables or a loose bundle of gutweed. Then the shape would turn, and you would see a face behind the mask—sometimes not a mask at all, but a human face, very pale, with eyes too wide and a mouth set in a line that does not speak. The keeper would look at you, or through you, then continue along the wall, checking pipes by touch.

The Bellows-keeper is the one you would meet first, because the Bellows is the center, and she does not leave it for more than a few hours. She is not old. The cold has made her age differently. Her skin is the color of the white water in the still alleys, a gray-white that seems to have settled from the water itself. Her hair is cut short and uneven, the color of wet ash, and when she moves into a dry room the ends of it stiffen with frost. Her eyes are the most changed thing: large, set far back under a brow that has grown heavy, the irises a pale gray-blue like the glow of a dying hydrogen flame. In low light her pupils open wide, nearly swallowing the iris, so she sees in the dark not by magic but by long practice and wide aperture. She blinks rarely. Blinking, she once told a younger keeper, makes you miss the pressure changes.

Her body is narrow but strong in the shoulders and forearms, built by years of swimming against the current from the rock face. Her hands are broad, the knuckles swollen from cold and work, the nail beds dark as the iron-shell snails. She wears a suit of oiled canvas and old neoprene, patched with pieces of dead cable insulation, with brass clips for tools. She does not carry tanks on her back; she wears a small electrolysis rebreather on her chest that hisses like a sleeping animal. Her fins are handmade, stitched with copper wire from the dead cables, and they leave a soft brush-mark in the silt wherever she goes.

She can hold her breath for a long time. The keepers train for this from the first year they come to the Underworks, if they come at all. Some are born here, and for those the training is not training; it is just growing. Their heartbeats run slow, near forty beats a minute when they sleep. Their ears have learned the language of pressure. They can tell which piston is about to reverse by the way the water presses against the small bones of the inner ear. They do not talk much. The sound does not carry well here, and the water layers bend voices into nonsense. Instead they tap on pipes with a knuckle or a small iron wrench. A slow tap-tap-pause-tap means something like “all is holding.” Three quick taps means “come.” A long scrape means “dead cable, do not touch.”

The Bellows-keeper lives in a dry room above the flooded printing shop, reached by a ladder of welded rebar that has been worn smooth. The room is not warm, but it is dry, and that is the best a keeper can ask for. A loop of warm brine pipe runs along one wall, and she sleeps curled against it, not for comfort but because it brings the heat of the Bellows up through the stone. On shelves made of salvaged window frames, she keeps jars. Some hold white water, which she collects from the still corners before it dissolves. Some hold rust-scrapings from different pipes, each labeled with a tap-code. One jar holds a small white amphipod that she says sings, though the sound is too high for anyone else to hear. There is a bunk of cork and an old church pew, a stack of paper maps showing the layers of the water, and a single photograph of the fjord above, creased and soft, showing a pale brightness that none of them have seen.

Her daily round is tied to the Bellows. The pistons complete their cycle in about six hours, and when they reverse, the whole settlement feels a faint surge. She wakes without an alarm, already reaching for her fins. She drinks a cup of warm brine with a little vent-mat in it—the taste is metallic, slightly sour, like a battery left in seawater, but it is food. She eats a plug of frostmoss from the wall near the warm pipe, which tastes of salt and iron and stings the sinuses with cold. Then she goes out into the water-alleys, her headlamp off. She knows the way by the resistance of the current and the faint shimmer where the cold layer meets the warmer layer. She swims slowly. In the Underworks, to hurry is to break something—a gauge face, a bone, a seal.

She checks the oxygen hoses first. They leak chains of tiny bubbles into the alleys, and she runs her hand along each one until she finds the place where the bubbles grow too large or stop. She checks the live cables by looking for the iron-shell snails. If a cable has no snails, she does not touch it. If it has a line of them, she picks one off and eats it, or pockets it for later. The shell crunches like a seed, and it tastes of rust. She watches the blue hydrogen flame in the wet chamber on the south side to make sure it is still burning. If it goes out, the hydrogen gathers in pockets that can stun a person or crack a wall. She greets the hagfish in the drain of the old bakery. It is blind, pale as a root, and she has not given it a name, but she sometimes drops a bit of vent-mat into the hole and waits to see its tail move.

She does not think of the Underworks as a town. She thinks of it as a body. The Bellows is the heart. The pipes are veins and arteries. The cables are nerves. The water is the blood, and the white water is the lymph, and the rust is the slow skin that grows over every wound. The keepers, she believes, are not inhabitants in the ordinary sense. They are more like white blood cells. They move through the body and repair what is tearing, and they do not ask why. This is not a religion, exactly. It is a way of staying sane when you live inside a dark machine and your only clock is a piston.

She is not alone. There are others, scattered through the dry rooms. The lock-keeper on the south side is a man with a metal leg made from a turbine blade; he keeps the pressure doors and the half-flooded lock, and he has a sonar device made from an old mine-survey unit. He sees shapes in the fjord before they arrive. The turbine-keeper in the old church is much younger, and she has gone half deaf from the Pelton wheels. She reads lips and signs, and she can tell if a turbine is misaligned by pressing her palm to its casing. The drone-master lives in a small room behind the electrolysis stacks. She never swims farther than the main street; her eyes have gone milky from staring into screens, and she guides the little blunt drones by feel and sound more than sight. There may be others in the deep rooms, but the Bellows-keeper has not seen them for several cycles. She taps the pipes sometimes, and if no answer comes from the east collars, she does not go to look. The east tunnels are sealed, and what is below them is not her work.

They meet once every full piston cycle, in the old church, under the steady turning of the Pelton wheels. They sit on the stone floor with their fins off and their feet bare, because the stone is dry and carries the turbine hum. They do not speak much. They share food: frostmoss, snail meat, hagfish jerky, a paste of vent-mat. They compare notes in tap-code and low voices: a valve is swelling in the north alley; the oxygen flow is down on the third stack; the deep-water shark was seen moving through the main street, which is a good sign, meaning the current is carrying enough food. They avoid the subject of the east mines. Instead they sit and listen to the water moving through the church, and for a while the silence is not empty.

Their beliefs are quiet, precise. They believe that the white water should not be brought into a dry room, because it does not belong there. It turns to cold vapor and leaves a salt rime, and if you breathe it, you dream of the trench. They believe that the compass points to the Bellows not because of any magnetic oddity, but because the Bellows is the only true direction left. They believe that if you hear a voice calling from the end of a water-alley, you do not answer, because the sound layers will have bent someone else’s words into your name. They believe the deeper thing below the mine shafts is not a monster. It is older than the mines, older than the fjord, and it moves on its own schedule. They do not worship it. They leave the east doors alone, and they listen for it the way you listen for a train that has not come yet.

What lives in the Underworks, then, is not just the keepers. It is also the hagfish in the bakery drain, the iron-shell snails on the live cables, the amphipods in the oxygen bubbles, the whip-thin shark that filters the main street once in a great while. But if you mean the ones who know the place, who tend it and are tended by it, then it is the keepers. They are few. They are cold. They have let the water change them, and they have changed the water by their presence—only a little, the way a valve changes a flow, or a snail changes a cable.

The Underworks has an equilibrium. The keepers are part of it. They do not think of themselves as owners or survivors. They are the small, warm, slow thoughts of a drowned machine, and they do their work, quietly, under the weight of the fjord.
