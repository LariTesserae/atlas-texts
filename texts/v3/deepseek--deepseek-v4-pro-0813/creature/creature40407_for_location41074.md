There is a word for them in no human language, but if you had to choose, you might call them the Unhurried. They do not call themselves anything. They do not need to. The city and the mat know them the way a body knows its own hands.

You do not see them at first. The eye looks for motion in the gaps between grown buildings, but they are too still. Then a shadow leans away from a wall, and you realize it has been there all along, watching you with the patience of a mountain. You feel them before you see them. Their presence is a change in air pressure, a faint metallic taste at the back of the tongue, a subtle warming in the cool, dry air. Then they resolve out of the dimness.

They are tall, taller than a human, but their slenderness makes them seem taller still. Their skin is the blue-grey of the leaves, waxed and smooth, with faint patterns of darker branching lines running like river systems across their arms and chests. These are not tattoos; they are the visible traces of the mat’s slow circulation, the mineral-bearing fibers that run just beneath the skin. Their body plan is not so different from yours: two arms, two legs, a head. But the proportions are stretched, as if they had been drawn by a patient hand and then elongated along the vertical axis. Their fingers are long and thin, flexible in a way that seems more vegetable than animal.

Their faces are quiet. The eyes are large and dark, each a smooth, convex lens that might be polished mineral or the same translucent leaf-tissue as the glowing bowls. They do not blink. The mouth is a narrow line, seldom used. They do not breathe through the mouth but through the skin, a slow exchange of gases with the dry air. They are easier to feel than to see; even when they stand still, their presence hums at the edge of perception.

They walk, but not the way an animal walks. Their feet are long and narrow, and when they step, the sole spreads into a pad of dense, hair-thin rootlets. These rootlets press into the living mat, tasting its chemistry, releasing a low pressure-pulse. Then the foot lifts and the mat springs back, leaving no mark. The motion is continuous, graceful, like a slow flame moving upright. They do not hurry. Time has a different texture here.

When they speak, they do so through their feet. A sentence is a set of infrasonic pulses sent through the ground, felt by other walking ones as a vibration in their long leg-bones. You can see it happen: two of them stand five meters apart, facing each other, and for a long time nothing seems to occur. Then one shifts its weight from one foot to the other. The wall of a nearby building flushes from slate-grey to a deep, wet indigo and back. The other walking one tilts its head. That is a conversation.

They do not eat or drink. They draw their nourishment directly through the rootlet-pads, siphoning water and trace minerals from the mat. Sometimes they kneel beside one of the bowl-shaped depressions and pass a hand through the warm exhalation, then press the palm to their own face or chest. This is not eating; it is more like a deep breath, or the way a person might close their eyes and remember a smell. The bowls are the world’s lungs, and the walking ones go to them to exchange gases and take in certain spores.

Their work is slow and constant. They are the city’s gardeners, its immune system, its synapses. When one of them presses a hand to a wall, the wall remembers how to grow. It may open a doorway, thicken a ceiling, or change from opaque to transparent to let in the dim, shadowless light. They do not build; they ask. They do not repair; they remind the city what shape it was meant to hold.

Some of them climb the spires. They wrap their long fingers around the woody columns and press their bodies close, adding their own pulse to the low tectonic hum. This is how the spires are kept in tune. The hum deepens, swells, and then settles. The climber descends, and the whole city seems to breathe a little easier.

They are few. A city of this size—a scattering of grown chambers along a high ridge—might hold two or three hundred walking ones. They do not gather often. When they do, they stand in a loose circle around one of the glowing bowls and remain there for what might be hours or days. The light from the bowl brightens. The air pressure changes. Then they separate, and you understand that a decision has been made, though you could not say what it was.

They are not born. They are budded from the mat in the warm, dark chambers beneath the city, where the root-mass pulses with bioluminescent light. A new one emerges already tall, but translucent and soft. It stays near the bowls for its first century, learning the frequencies. As it ages, its skin hardens, its patterns deepen, its rootlet-pads grow more sensitive. It does not weaken. It becomes more itself.

At the end of its walking life—an event that comes after centuries, perhaps millennia—it chooses a place. Sometimes it wanders for years before finding it; sometimes it simply knows. It stops. It stands very still. Its feet send rootlets down further than ever before, into the deepest layers of the mat. Its body stiffens. The skin turns to bark. The arms rise and fuse, and the walking one becomes a spire, or a wall, or a new chamber. The city grows around it. Its memories drain into the mat and become part of the world’s deep network. The others do not mourn. They visit the new spire, lay their hands on it, and listen. The one who walked is still there, now holding up the sky.

The mobile growths are also inhabitants. The small, skittering ones are like loose fingers, carrying chemical messages and pollen between spires. The vast, slow-moving masses that take days to cross a valley are migrating organs, carrying nutrients or seeds or distilled memories from one city to another. The walking ones sometimes accompany them for a while, matching their pace, then turn aside. The rolling ones and drifting ones are not separate species; they are parts of the same body, the same world.

If you ask which of these is the one that lives there—the walking ones, the rooted ones, the skitterers, the mat itself—the answer is yes. The city is not a place where people live. It is a place where the world has chosen to be aware of itself. The tall, slender ones are that awareness given legs. They are the mat’s eyes, its hands, its slow, deliberate voice.

The sound is still there beneath everything, the low tectonic grind. But now you know it is not only the sound of fibers straining. It is the sound of their thinking. It is the sound of a world that has been alive for so long that it has learned to be still—and to be still is not to be silent. It is to hum.
