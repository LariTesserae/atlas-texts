At first nothing lives there, and that is true for as long as you mistake life for meat. The Weeping Array is not empty; it is occupied the way a bell is occupied by its ring.

They are called the Wept, though the name may not stay where you put it. They are made by the same seeping that grows the travertine terraces and the aragonite spires. They do not eat, sleep, burn, or breathe air. They breathe water, and they are not stone creatures; they are stone’s slow exhalation of itself.

You will not see one until you stop looking for a face. First you see a pillar that is too symmetrical, too wet, with a faint crust at the base that looks like a footprint. Then it shifts, and you realize the pillar was standing on two legs or four, and the water running down its sides was not runoff but skin.

Their bodies are built of the same minerals as the plain: bone-white marble, pale dolomite, seams of black chert, copper oxide green, quartz white. They are always wet. Water seeps from their joints, drips from their many fingers. Where they stand, a pale travertine rim grows around their feet. If they stand long enough, they become a hoodoo. What looks like erosion is one of them forgetting to move.

Their hands have too many fingers. Not a fixed number—seven, nine, eleven, sometimes a hand is a branching fan of narrow stone digits. The petroglyph handprints with too many fingers are not stylized. They are pressed directly from the body, and the body changes. Ask a Wept how many fingers it has and it will look at its hands as if reading weather.

Their faces are not faces in the human sense. There are depressions where eyes should be, holding small pools that reflect the sky sideways. The mouth is a vertical fissure, usually sealed with a fine crust of calcite. When it opens, there is a sound like a stone splitting. Their voice comes from the whole body, resonant and percussive, a low chord of water pouring over a ledge and the distant applause of rockfall. They say little, but the wind carries their conversations across the basin. That is why the voices do not echo. They are not echoes; they are utterances still traveling through folded distance.

They move slowly, but you cannot judge their speed by distance. They know the local roads. A Wept will walk away from a terrace and be there. It will climb a path that descends and return without turning. It will step onto a carved stairway that ends in mid-air and continue upward, because the stair continues in a part of the day you have not learned to stand on. They walk along water that climbs steps and across pools that stand on vertical faces. They do not find it strange. To them, down is a habit, and the habit is no longer kept.

The circular cells are theirs. They are not shelters. A Wept does not need shelter; cold is another mineral condition. The cells are listening-rooms. Their doorways face away from the wind so the wind does not interrupt. The floors are worn smooth by centuries of kneeling and pacing, because the Wept press their stone bodies against the bedrock to hear the ground ring. They are listening to the mountain’s slow cracking, to water moving in fissures below, to the growth of aragonite in the pools. When you see one kneeling in a cell, it is not worship. It is conversation with the deepest part of the plateau.

The low walls around pools, around cracks, around nothing are not for animals. They are for teaching shapes to stay. A pool that is walled is being taught to hold its reflection level. A crack that is walled is being taught not to swallow the path. A wall around nothing may be a frame around a place where distance has grown thin, or where a new Wept may eventually condense. The walls are dry-stone because the stones must be able to shift; in a place where the ground rings and the rocks move, mortar would break. Fitted unshaped slabs hold by attention.

The wind-harps are their instruments. Slate tied with copper wire, tuned to the gale. The Wept pluck them with wet fingers, and the copper sings. Some harps play when there is no wind because a Wept is standing beside them, folded out of sight but still present, vibrating the strings with its body. If you watch long enough, you may see the air above the harp thicken, not with mist but with the outline of the player.

The cairns are not graves. They are anchors. The Wept stack them on ridges and saddles where the rules are worn thin: where a pillar casts two shadows, where a pool stands sideways, where a path loops without turning. The cairn repeats because the place repeats. A Wept does not build the same cairn on every ridge; it builds one cairn in many places at once, and the stone agrees. If you scratch a mark on one, the mark appears on all of them, or on none, because they are the same cairn seen through different folds. The Wept do not mind. They consider your mark another layer of weathering.

The petroglyphs are their records, but they are not fixed. The spirals, rings, and branching dendrites are maps of water and mineral growth, of folded paths and sideways reflections. The handprints are signatures, and they change as the hands change. If you look back at a wall, a spiral may have added a turn. It is not that the wall was rewritten; it is that the wall has agreed to be written on only temporarily.

Their tools are chert flakes, hammerstones, copper needles, tanged blades. They use them not to hunt or cut wood—there is no wood, no animal—but to shape the world by percussion and patience. A chert flake may carve a runnel to guide a seep. A hammerstone may tune a pillar’s resonance. A copper needle may sew a crack shut with copper wire, or incise a spiral into a cliff. The tanged blade is for shaving salt crusts and scoring the back of a hand before pressing a handprint. They do not burn. They regard fire as an insult to a place already loud and bright. Here, everything is taken slowly and returned as stone.

They reproduce, if that is the word, not by birth. New Wept precipitate. In a pool where the seep is strong and the water has been whispering to the mineral spires for a long time, the aragonite branches. They branch like frost, like coral, like nerves. One day one of the branching spires branches too many times, and the pattern becomes aware, and it climbs out of the pool. It is already old. It leaves a negative shape in the water, a crystal absence, which gradually fills with new growth. The other Wept may not notice; they have been expecting it for centuries.

They do not die; they settle. A Wept who has listened long enough will kneel in a roofless cell or in a pool and let the mineral growth take it. The water continues to seep, the travertine rim rises, and the figure becomes a pillar. The depressions worn into the floor by its knees fill with water. If you ask where that one has gone, the answer is: it is still here, but no longer moving.

They have no names in the way we do. They know each other by the sound of the pool they emerged from, or by the number of fingers they had at waking, or by a particular note from a wind-harp. To speak a name, you would tap a wet stone in a certain rhythm and wait. If you ask a Wept its name, it may open the vertical crack of its mouth and give a sound like a pebble falling into a deep pool. Later you will understand that the sound was not a name. It was the sound the plateau made when it first noticed you.

They are not malevolent. They are not kind either. They are the plateau’s way of being awake. They are what happens when water and stone have worn the world’s rules so thin that something has to rise from the pools and tend the fissures. The place is not empty. It is full of the Wept, who are only visible if you stop looking for creatures and start looking for stone that remembers itself.
