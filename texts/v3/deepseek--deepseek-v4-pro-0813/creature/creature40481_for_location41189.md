The one who lives there is not the loudest thing in the room. At first he is only a variation in the dry corner—a stack of old canvas, a pair of rubber boots with the toes curled upward, a hand lying on the concrete with the fingers slightly spread. The hand is the first thing that gives him away. It is pale as the pale shoots, long and smooth from wet work, and the iron snails have polished a narrow path across the back of it.

He is a small man, or a man-shaped thing, folded into the angle where the dry ledge meets the ladder. His spine follows the wall. His knees are drawn up so his feet rest in the damp. He wears an old station coat, oil-blacked and stiff at the elbows, buttoned with rusted wire and snail shell. Under the coat, his chest is bare. The skin of his ribs is the same gray-green as the film on the sump. He has no beard, but a fine colorless moss grows at his jaw and temples, as if the room has begun to colonize him.

His face is turned toward the steam pipe, not toward the light. His eyes are mostly closed. When they open, they are pale as the fogged pressure gauge, and they move slowly, not to follow you but to read the waterline. He listens through his lower lip, which twitches once after each pump cycle. His breathing is slower than the pump’s. It does not rise and fall in forty seconds; it comes in one long pull and then a pause, like something waiting out a tide.

He lives on a different floor of the room than the animals. The crickets own the warm band on the steam pipe. The snails own the rusted middle wall. The shrimp and frogs own the sump. He owns the dry ledge and the step below it, where he keeps his body exactly at the boundary of wet and dry. His feet are in the water, his head is in the cricket warmth. That is his roost.

He eats the iron snails. He picks them off the wall with a slow thumb, one after another, and taps each shell once against the concrete. The sound is like a drip. He does not crack them all. He chooses the old ones, the ones whose spirals have begun to bloom outward, and he leaves the young ones to crowd the wall. The meat he eats with his front teeth, which are flat and gray. He drops the broken shells into the frogs’ mud basins. The frogs do not move. They know the weight of those shells by now; they wait for the mineral to dissolve into their little pools.

He drinks from the sump, but not like an animal. He cups one hand into the water and raises it to his mouth. The bead shrimp scatter only inches away. The frogs do not scatter; sometimes one sits on his knuckle while he drinks, a small wet-rubber weight no heavier than a thumb.

He tends the pale shoots as if they were his garden. He pulls the lace-chewed leaves away from the cricket droppings and clears a fragile collar of clean concrete around their base. When the pump lowers the water and the sump gives its long suck, he goes down on all fours and drags his hand along the new high-water mark, repairing the frogs’ basins with his thumb. The mud is already there; he just smooths the rim.

He moves only in the four-second quiet after the pump cuts off. That is when the room is between exhale and inhale, and his movement does not frighten. He crosses to the steam pipe and lays his face against the warm metal, and the crickets part around his cheek, then close over it like fur. He stays there for what seems like minutes, breathing into the pipe. It may be his way of listening to the station above. It may be his way of staying warm.

The centipede knows him. When it comes out at the first vibration of the pump, it does not bite. It runs over his hand as if it were a continuation of the wall, and he does not flinch. The centipede goes on to the snail field and returns to its seam. The man sometimes touches the seam behind the conduit with one finger, not to catch it but to feel the paper-on-stone tremble.

The built things around him have not been abandoned. They are arranged. The yellow bucket sits in the dry corner, and the chewed hole at its bottom is not a defect; he uses it as a kind of drain-cup, a place where condensation gathers clean enough to drink. The mop handle is his staff. The spool of old wire is where he sometimes sits, though he prefers the floor. The paint can of bolts is his doorstop and his only jewelry. The pressure gauge is his clock, though he cannot see it; he taps the glass after each pump cycle, leaving a clear spot in the fog.

His language is mostly the frogs’ two-note tap. He makes it with his tongue against the roof of his mouth. When the males begin their soft four-second call, he sometimes answers, not in words but in the same two notes, and for a moment the room sounds like a wire being plucked. He has no other voice.

He was a person once. There is a scar on his shoulder where a station badge may have hung. The keys on his belt are rusted to the leather. But he has been below so long that the person has been worn away, like the mop handle, into a smaller, smoother thing. He is not a ghost. He is not a monster. He is the room’s slowest resident, the one who keeps the high-water line and the mud basins, the one who eats only what the rust does not need.

If you stay perfectly still, he will eventually ignore you. He will uncurl from the dry ledge and go about the room’s work. The shrimp will cloud around his ankles. The snails will close and wait. The crickets will lift their heads not at the pump but at his breath. The pump will inhale on schedule, and he will pause with it, both of them reading the same water. Then the chamber will resume its dense, nearly silent motion, and you will understand that this is not a place with one occupant. It is a small, blind congregation, and he is only its keeper.
