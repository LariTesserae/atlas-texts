At first the basin seems empty of inhabitants. The town is there in the way a shell is there—buildings, bridges, channels, stairs—but no one is moving. Then the woman on the central stair detaches from the dark stone, and after that you see them all.

They are a sparse population, as sparse as the green pockets. They are human-shaped, long-boned, angular, with the patient posture of herons. Their skin is pale and greyish in the even light—damp birch bark, the underside of a stone. Their hair is dark and wet at the temples from the constant condensation. They wear undyed wool, bark-cloth, and soft cured leather, all in the colors of the place: moss green, basalt black, the blue-grey of still water. They go barefoot or in leather wrappings. Their ornaments are few: copper pins, bone beads, a polished black stone at the throat. Nothing about them is ragged. Nothing is new.

Each one keeps a circuit. The circuits never vary.

The woman on the stair is the one you will remember. She wears a dark shawl pinned at her shoulder and carries a clay jar as black as the stone, wide-mouthed, narrow at the base. She descends the stair without looking at her feet. At the lower seep she stoops and holds the jar under a lip of rock. Water fills it to the same mark. She climbs back. On the terrace above, she pours the water into a stone channel. The jar empties but not completely; a thin residue stays in the bottom. She turns and goes down again. Her face is calm, like a face in a fresco. If you speak to her, she may pause for a moment, but the pause would be exactly the same each time. Then she continues.

Below the stair, by the lower pools, there are children—or small figures who move with the quickness of children. They play on flat stones with knucklebones, polished stones, and rings of wet moss. They throw a bone; it turns the same number of times and lands in the same place. They laugh, a low liquid sound that does not echo. One girl follows a heron from pool to pool. The heron steps; the girl steps. The distance between them never closes. A boy kneels by the water and touches the surface with a stick. Rings spread, then vanish, and he does it again. He is not bored. His expression is one of deep attention, as if he is listening to a note only he can hear.

In the doorways of the dry-stone houses, weavers sit at upright looms. The warp threads hang from stones polished by long handling, though they do not wear. The shuttle passes left to right, right to left. The weft is pressed into place with a bone knife. The cloth does not grow. The same row is always just being finished. The weavers' hands are not old, not young. They have the look of wood that has been handled for a long time. They do not look up. If a thread were to snap, it does not snap; the work simply waits, and the shuttle passes again.

There are moss-tenders who kneel on leather pads beside the green pockets. They lift fern fronds with copper awls and peer into the water channels. They pick up tiny stones that have always been there and set them down again. They touch the moss with the flat of a hand, as if feeling for a pulse. The moss is cool and wet. When they rise, their knees leave no lasting print. The green pockets remain exactly as lush and exactly as bounded.

There are watchers on the high platforms. They stand or sit with staffs of willow, looking out at the far wall of the basin or at the fog. Their gaze is steady, not scanning. They do not seem to expect anything. One watcher, a thin man with a copper ring in his ear, has his mouth slightly open. His breath condenses and disperses in the cool air. He might be counting drips. He might be listening to the water in the channels. If you climb up beside him, he will not acknowledge you. But you may feel that your presence has been accounted for, the way a stone in a channel is accounted for.

There are shrine-keepers. Their shrines are small enclosures against the cliff faces, roofed with moss. Inside are shallow bowls of water, arrangements of black stones, and figures made of twisted grass and thin copper wire: herons, fish, deer-like creatures with antlers of wire. The shrine-keepers pour water from one bowl into another and back again. The surface of the water never stills. They hum a single low note, the same pitch as the drip from the seep. It is not music; it is another form of water.

There are old people, or people who have always been old. They sit by the high seeps with their hands in their laps. Their hair is white, their faces smooth. They do not tell stories. They do not sleep. Sometimes one of them takes a bone comb and parts her hair, smooths it, lets it fall. Then she does it again. She is not waiting for anything.

There are fishers at the pools. They sit on the black stone with lines of braided fiber and hooks of bone. The hooks are bare. The fish hang in the water, motionless except for a faint movement of the tail. The fishers do not cast. They hold the line. Sometimes a fish drifts close to the hook and then drifts away. The fisher's hand does not twitch. Perhaps they are not fishing; perhaps they are measuring the stillness.

The animals are also inhabitants. Each pool has its heron or ibis, standing in the shallows, one foot raised. The deer-like creatures move from one moss patch to another, their small hooves clicking softly on wet stone, then stop. Frogs line the edges of the cisterns, their eyes like drops of polished copper. The fish do not school. The birds sit in the willows with their heads tucked. They are not skittish, and they are not tame. They are simply part of the same balance.

The inhabitants do not gather. They do not call out. When two of them pass on the stair, they turn sideways and make a small gesture—a hand raised, a nod—but the gesture is so regular it seems part of the architecture. If they speak, the words are low and liquid, like the birdcall. You might hear the same phrase from a doorway: “The water is held.” And from another, much later: “The water is held.” They are not conversing. They are confirming.

They do not seem to eat. There are kitchens with cold clay ovens and stone mortars. A woman grinds something dark in a mortar; the pestle never wears down, the powder never accumulates. The smell of damp stone and moss overrides any smell of cooking. They do not sleep, or if they sleep, it is not tied to darkness. Some lie down in the moss at intervals, eyes open or closed. Their rest is another circuit.

It is hard to say whether they know they are repeating. Their faces are not blank. They have expressions—attention, patience, a faint pleasure at the touch of water. But the expressions do not progress. They are like the light: even, present, unshadowed. If you speak to one of them, they may answer. The answer will be the same each time, and the pause before it will be exact. They are not trapped, exactly. They are the moving parts of an equilibrium. The basin is a set of scales, and they are the weights: the woman with the jar on one side, the seep on the other; the weaver's shuttle on one side, the unwoven cloth on the other; the watcher's gaze on one side, the fog on the other. Each action is balanced by an equal and opposite action elsewhere.

To call them alive is not quite right. To call them dead is not right either. They are inhabitants the way a note is an inhabitant of a tuning fork. They have been struck once, and they are still sounding.

If you stay long enough, you may feel your own breath slowing to match the drips. Your footsteps may begin to fall into the same intervals. The woman with the jar will come down the stair, and you will know exactly when she will stoop, exactly when she will pour. You will understand that the place does not need you. But it will allow you to watch, as long as you do not disturb the balance.
