The one who lives there does not have a name. Or, if it has a name, the name is not a word but a pattern of taps—two soft taps, a pause, a tap—sent through the root-mat and felt in the soles of the feet. You would not hear it. The basin is too loud; the wall of clicks and trills and steam-hiss would swallow a name like that before it left the ground. But the root-mat carries it anyway, farther than the air, and the one who lives there hears it as a tiny change in the floor’s tremor, like a muscle twitching under the moss.

You would not see it at first. You would feel it, maybe, as a difference in the dark: a place where the noise folds differently, where the warm breath of the ground seems to pause. Then a shape separates from a wall of vines. It is the color of a wet root: pale grey-brown, smooth-skinned, with a faint translucence where a blue fungus behind it shines through the webs between its fingers. It is not large—no higher than your ribcage when it stands—but long, drawn out, as if its spine and limbs had been stretched to fit the narrow corridors of the root-galleries. Its shoulders are narrow, its hips narrow, its belly soft. It has no fur, only a fine down along the backs of its arms and the ridge of its skull.

Its head is not a face. It has no eyes, or only two pale, blind spots set wide under a brow of bone, too small to see with. The head is mostly hearing. Two round tympanic membranes, stretched over shallow cups of cartilage, sit on the sides of the skull, one facing forward, one back. They are never still. They tilt and flatten like the ears of a bat, tuning the wall of sound into bands and layers. Along its jaw runs a row of small heat-pits, each one opening and closing as it tastes the steam. Its mouth is small and down-turned, almost expressionless, but the fingers are what you would remember.

Each hand has four long fingers and a shorter thumb. The fingers have extra joints, or seem to; they bend backward and forward, each tipped with a flat, soft pad that can feel the slightest movement in a root. The feet are similar, but broader, the toes spread wide to lie flat against the root-mat. When it stands still, it does not stand on the ground so much as join it: the pads of its feet read the thrum of gas moving below the basalt, the tap of a grub inside a rotting log, the footsteps of something heavy three terraces away.

It lives in the sound. That is not a metaphor. The wall of clicks and trills is its daylight, its map, its weather. It can hear the difference between a frog call and a seedpod splitting—not by pitch but by the tiny silence that follows, or the way the sound returns from the nearest wall. It moves through the noise by finding the gaps: the half-beat after a descending whistle, the moment when a steam chimney catches its breath, the pause in the midge-column’s whine. It places its feet in those gaps, so that even a step on loose basalt plates might fall in the exact instant the plates do not clatter. It is not stealth; it is timing.

When it needs to know where it is, it taps. A single finger, the middle one, against a root or a stone. The tap is not a sound to itself; it is a question. The root-mat answers with a map of hollows and water and the thin places where the stone is about to fall. The creature feels the answer through its feet and jaw and the skin of its belly if it lies down. It can tell a sleeping frog from a mushroom from a pocket of stale air by the way the tap returns. In the blackest parts, it taps at every step, a soft drumming that you would never hear above the rest but that runs through the mat like a pulse.

It eats what the basin offers, and it eats constantly, because the basin’s abundance is also its competition. Its main tool is a reed. It selects one with care—not any reed, but one still green enough to bend without splitting—strips the leaves with a single pull, bites the tip to a point or a blunt end, and inserts it into holes too narrow for its fingers. It listens down the reed, or rather it feels the vibrations traveling up the stem. When a grub or a blind cricket touches the other end, the resident withdraws the reed with a flick and sends its tongue after it: a long, pale tongue, sticky, forked at the tip, quick as a frog’s but thicker. It can clear a whole gallery of grubs in an hour. Then it drops the reed. By morning, the reed has been chewed by beetles or grown over.

For snails and the small, hard-shelled things that climb the steam-chimney stones, it uses a different tool: a flat stone. It carries the shell to a particular tilted slab of basalt that rings when struck, and drops the shell from a height. It holds the shell in its mouth and climbs a nearby vine, or stands on its hind legs and releases it with a twist of the head. It listens for the pitch of the crack. If the shell does not break, it tries again from higher. The stone is not a tool in the way we would keep a tool; it is a place. The creature does not own it. Other creatures use it too—a bird with a heavy beak, a crab with a different grip—and the stone is littered with broken shells that disappear into the mud by the next rain. The one who lives there does not mind; it never thought of the stone as its own.

It drinks from leaves. When the air thickens into a hot mist, or after a sudden hot rain, it finds a broad leaf—some are wide as a hand, some as a face—and folds it along the central vein. It holds the folded leaf under a dripping vine or a mossy overhang until water pools in the crease. It drinks with small, neat laps, then opens the leaf and lays it flat on the moss. The moss remembers the leaf for a while; by evening it is gone.

It sleeps in pressed grass, but not often. The basin does not have night and day—only thicker and thinner dark—so the creature sleeps in short, shallow bouts, never more than a few hours at a time. It makes no nest. It finds a hollow between roots, or a ledge above a steam chimney where the warmth keeps the larger predators away, and it presses the grass down with its body, turning once or twice. When it wakes, the grass begins, slowly, to straighten. It does not return.

Its voice is not a cry but a rhythm. To speak to another of its kind, it drums on a root or a hollow stem: two taps, a pause, a tap, then a longer vibration made by pressing its whole palm against the root and humming. The basin is so loud that air-borne calls are almost useless over distance—too many other whistles and clicks—but the root-mat is a shared nerve, and a good drumming can cross from one side of the basin to the other in seconds. The creature also has a whistle, a long descending note that sounds exactly like a frog call, or a seedpod, or the wind through a steam chimney. It does not use the whistle to say anything precise; it uses it to place itself in the wall of sound, to find the rhythm, to know if another of its kind is near by the way the return whistle comes a half-beat late.

There are not many of them. The basin is too full for many large things, but there are enough: maybe a dozen, maybe fewer. They are solitary for most of the year, but they overlap, not in territories but in timings. They leave no marks, no paths, no caches. They do not build or improve. They do not need to. The basin gives them tools and takes them back. The root-mat is their floor and their message-board; the steam-chimneys are their hearths, but they do not own the fire.

Once or twice a year, when the heat-rain comes every few hours and the midge-columns rise like smoke, the ones who live there find each other. Not by sight—there is no sight—but by matching their tapping rhythms until two patterns become one. They do not pair-bond. The female later gives birth to one or two young in a pressed hollow, and the young cling to her back or belly for a time, learning the gaps in the sound. They are not taught, exactly; they follow the taps. By the time the moss has straightened from the birth hollow, the young are gone, moving through the mat on their own.

They are not the top of anything. There are things in the basin that could eat them: a long, low, soft-footed hunter that moves by heat, and a bird that drops stones. The one who lives there knows this and does not fight it. Its only defense is timing, the gaps in the noise, the ability to be still as a root until the hunter’s attention is pulled away by a louder sound. When it is truly still, it can slow its breathing until its belly does not rise, and its heat-pits close, and its skin takes on the exact temperature of the moss. It becomes, for minutes at a time, a piece of the mat.

There is no loneliness in it, and no society. It carries no memory of a better place. The basin is all it knows: the black seeps, the hot mud cones, the fields of loose basalt plates, the root-galleries lined with blind frogs. It does not wonder about the rim. It does not look up. The sky is a rumor the light never confirms. When the earth shakes, it rides the shaking as a boat rides a wave, flexing its long toes and letting the ground move under it. When a gas vent opens under its sleeping hollow, it wakes already moving, the heat-pits along its jaw flaring, and finds another hollow before it is fully awake. It does not mourn the old one. The old one is already growing over.

You could meet it, if you sat still in the black-green murk long enough for the moss to stop remembering your weight. It might approach not because it is curious but because you have become, in your stillness, a new part of the root-mat—a warm stone, a source of moisture, a shape that does not fit the known map. It would circle you, not with its useless eyes but with its taps: a finger on a root near your left foot, a heel-drum in the soil, a heat-pit reading the air you exhale. It would know you are not a rock. It would know you are not one of the long, low hunters. It might, if you made no sound, reach out one long finger and touch the back of your hand, not to hurt you but to feel the vibration of your pulse. Then it would be gone, not suddenly but by degrees, its shape dissolving back into the dark and the noise, leaving only a faint tap-tap on a root, a name you almost understood.
