At first there seems to be no one. The Green Still is too complete, too densely itself, to need an occupant. The light comes from all directions, so there is nowhere for a creature to hide. The heat lies on the lungs. The only animals are the pale millipede, the blind frog, the wet-paper moth, and they are so rare they seem accidental. But absence here is not absence. It is a held thing.

Look at the marks. The low wall of leaf-mold and resin: its top is level, not naturally but by the memory of a hand. The three steps grown into the root-hill: they are not carved, but they are also not random; they are the wood's memory of rising and falling, of a body's weight. The two long indentations before the woody arch are not erosion—nothing here erodes—but the imprint of a threshold that no longer exists, or of feet that stood and waited. The place is not empty. It is occupied by waiting.

There is an inhabitant.

It is not, or not only, a thing you can point to. The whole valley is its body, or its body is the whole valley; the distinction has no meaning here. The ceiling of leaves is its skin. The slow exhalations from the far gallery mouths are its breath. The green-gold glow is its body heat made visible. The drip of water is its pulse. But if you want to see it—if you want to see the part of it that most resembles a self—you must go to the hollow on the flank of the north ridge, under the tiny white flowers that never open.

There, the air is a fraction cooler. It moves. It carries the smell of wet stone from deeper below. The floor of the hollow is a springy leaf-cushion, and in the center of that cushion is a long depression, shaped like a body that has been curled there for longer than stone. It is not a nest. It is a mould. The roots around it have grown to its edge and then stopped, leaving a rim, the way roots stop at a buried stone.

In that depression sits the figure. It is made of the same materials as the valley—packed leaf-mold, hardened resin, root-flesh, green light—but arranged into something like an animal. Its back is curved like the low wall outside, smooth and level along the spine. Its shoulders are broad, tuber-swollen, and from them the body tapers into limbs that are long and jointed at odd intervals, the way stems are jointed. The arms are folded in its lap. The hands are not hands but bundles of tendrils, each one curled around the others, holding still. The legs are folded beneath it, thick as fused trunks, and where the feet should be there are pads of pale root-flesh that merge with the floor. From its scalp and spine, fine root-threads descend and root into the leaf-cushion, so the figure cannot be separated from the valley. It is not sitting on the floor. It is growing out of it.

Its head is bowed. There is no neck; the head grows directly from the shoulders, a gall of old wood and closed white buds. Where a face should be, there is a smooth oval of darker leaf-cuticle, damp with condensation. The moisture gathers there, not running, only beading, like a tear that cannot fall. It has no eyes, no mouth, no nostrils. But it does not need them. The whole valley is its face. The whole valley is its skin.

It might be the last of the ones who made the wall and the steps. It might be the one who did not leave when the others left, who stayed behind to keep the valley from starting. Or it might be the valley's own response to their leaving—a root-grown shape in the form of a keeper, a scarecrow of green light to hold the place until they return. The difference doesn't matter anymore. It has been here so long that the two possibilities have fused, like the trunks in the root-hills.

It does not think in words. It thinks in slow peristalsis of sap, in the infinitesimal settling of root-flesh, in the identical fall of each drop. It knows the millipede by a faint line of warmth along a stem. It knows the frog by the way the water in the leaf-cup shifts when the frog shifts. It knows the moth by the single small shadow it casts, one of the only shadows in the valley, crossing once and then gone. It knows the wall and the steps and the threshold as you know the inside of your own mouth—not as objects, but as parts of its own body, or as scars.

Its stillness is not rest. It is work. The valley is caught in the moment of its own becoming, and that equilibrium is not natural; it is held. The figure holds it. The figure is the counterweight to growth and decay, to weathering, to time. If it moved, if it uncurled even one tendril, the balance would tip. The leaf would unfurl. The drop would fall. The white flowers might open. The stone would begin to weather. Rot would come, and with it, change, and with change, time. So the figure does not move. It has not moved. It will not move. That is its purpose. It is the still point of a world that must not start.

It is not sad. It is not lonely. Those are too fast. It is something slower than loneliness. It is the green still itself, made visible as a shape, and the shape's only act is to remain.

If a visitor came, the figure would know before the first frond was pushed aside. The water film on the floor would change; the hollow stems would carry a new pressure. The air in the hollow would cool by another fraction, and the smell of wet stone would strengthen, as if the valley were drawing a deeper breath. The indentations before the arch would seem to deepen. The steps would seem more stair-like. The wall would seem more level. The whole place would become, for a moment, more itself, as if the visitor were the missing note that the green stillness had been humming around.

But the figure would not raise its head. It would not speak. It would only wait, as it has waited, as the place has waited, for the ones who left before the world was finished.

This is what lives there: not a person, not a beast, not a spirit. It is the valley's awareness that it is unfinished, grown a body so it can keep watch. It is the dream of a threshold. It is the patience of the drop on the fern-tip. It is the Green Still's only resident—the One Who Stayed, the Unfinished, the waiting made root and leaf and resin.
