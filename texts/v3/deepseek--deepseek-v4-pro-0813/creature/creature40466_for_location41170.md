On the first days, you would say no one lives here. You would see the snails first, because they coat the wet stone in a slow-moving crust. Then the shrimps in their brown pools, the pale geckos, the bats ribboning out of the earth at dusk. You would think: this is a place of small lives and old stone, nothing more.

But if you stay—if you let the plateau get used to you, and you get used to it—you begin to notice that some of the stillness is not empty. Some of it is held. There are places where the rock has been touched in a way that is not just weather. A ring of stones no higher than the knee, arranged too exactly. A channel between two pools, straight as a thought. A petroglyph worn nearly flat, but still there: a spiral, a line of dots, a figure like a tadpole with arms.

They are called many things by passing outsiders, but there are no passing outsiders. They call themselves something in a language of clicks and fingertips, a name that does not translate well. In the lowland tongues, they would be called the Pool People, or the Waiters, or Those Who Listen to Stone. They are not many. They are enough.

They are human, or near enough. But they have lived on the plateau so long that the plateau has shaped them. They are small and compact, not from scarcity alone but because their bodies seem to have learned the lesson of the stone: do not grow far, grow deep. Their skin is pale, dusted with the same honey-gray carbonate as the ground, so that a resting person can vanish into the pans. Their hair is colorless in the sun, the color of dry lichen or old shell. Their eyes are large and dark, and they hold light the way the deeper pools hold water—carefully, without spilling much.

They move in a way that is hard to follow. Not slow exactly, but deliberate. Each foot is placed as if the ground is a text to be read. Fingers trail along the smooth fins of rock, feeling for the subtle grade that tells where water will go. They can kneel beside a pool for an hour without shifting, and the frogs do not stop watching them. Eventually the frogs accept them as part of the rock, and life goes on.

Their day begins before the fog lifts. They rise out of shallow hollows where they have slept, or from under dwarf trees that look dead but aren't. They go to the channels first, while the stone is wet, and they clear the tiny blockages with pebbles and fingerbones. They know every runnel that connects pan to pan. A single misplaced stone can send the morning water in the wrong direction, and they attend to this with the seriousness of farmers tending a thousand irregular fields.

They eat what the plateau offers. They lift snails from the stone, crack them with small, worn hammerstones, and return the shells to the ground. They harvest the green and rust-colored algae from pools, squeeze the water out, and eat it as a paste or dry it in the sun into thin cakes. They gather seed shrimp and tadpole shrimp with scoops made from the shells of larger snails or from carefully hollowed wood of a dwarf tree. They eat frogs only rarely—not because of any law, but because the frogs are part of the same slow order, and there is enough food without taking them. They drink from the year-round pools, but always from the downslope edge, and they never muddy the water with their feet.

They carry almost nothing. A person may have a cord of braided root-fiber around the waist, a few stone chips, a shell cup, a small bag woven from the inner bark of a dwarf tree. Fire is rare among them. There is little fuel, and smoke would rise and announce them to the empty sky. They eat cold food, sun-warmed food, food still alive. When they need warmth, they rest against the rock after the sun has heated it, or they fold themselves into the shallow grikes where the day’s warmth lingers.

They live in small groups, never more than two or three tens of people, and they spread themselves widely. The plateau could not hold a large settlement, and the water could not serve one. But they meet, at long intervals, when the pools overflow after a heavy rain or when the bats gather in their thousands. They meet at certain rings of stones, and they talk in their way: a muted clicking, a tapping of fingers on stone, a tracing of patterns in dust. They exchange news that is mostly water news. Which pools have woken. Which channels have closed. Where the fog came thick this year. Where a new grike has widened enough to hold a body.

Children are rare and considered a kind of weather—something you wait for, something you cannot force. A child is taught first to be still. Then to listen. Then to read the stone with the fingertips. Their games are silent: stacking pebbles in impossible arrangements, racing drops of water down a tilted pan, following a beetle for half a mile without disturbing it. They grow slowly, even by the standards of their slow people.

The old ones are the most listened-to. They carry the map of the plateau in their heads, not as a picture but as a memory of taste and sound. They know which pools turn bitter in drought, which ones hold water for seven years, which ones are poisoned by the wrong algae. They know the petroglyphs and what they mean. They say the spiral is the water going down. The rows of dots are generations, or pools, or years of good fog—depending on the place. The figures with arms are the old people, the ones who made the channels and the rings, the ones who first learned that water could be asked to go where you needed it.

The present people do not think of themselves as the descendants of those builders, exactly. They think of themselves as the same thing, only more worn. The old walls were not abandoned; they were dissolved. The old people did not leave; they became the stone. Every human body, they say, is just a temporary pool. When a person dies, they are laid in a deep grike where water runs, and the body is allowed to go back to the plateau. The bones become the white fins of rock. The skin becomes the algal crust. The memory becomes the water that moves from pan to pan, out of sight.

They speak little of this. They are not a philosophical people in the way lowlanders might understand. They simply know that everything here waits. The water waits in the rock. The eggs wait in the dry mud. The trees wait for the fog. The people wait, too. They wait for children. They wait for the season of overflow. They wait for the old stone to hear them and answer, which it does, but only in the sound of a drip, or the green flush of a pool that was dust the week before.

There is no great fear among them, and no great joy either. It is not a flat life. It is a life tuned to a fine scale, where the opening of a single pool can be a festival, and the death of a child is a long, slow silence that spreads through the rocks like a dry year. They feel deeply, but their expressions are minimal. A slight tilt of the head. A touch to the collarbone. A pebble placed on another pebble. You could stand among them for a day and think them cold, when in fact they are only quiet, as the place requires.

At dusk they sit at the edges of the larger pools. The frogs click once and then stop. The bats pour out of the earth. The people watch them without speaking, and in the half-light their faces seem to take on the texture of the rock. An old woman might trace a spiral in the air, not for you, but for the water. A child might lie with one ear against a stone, listening to the faint gurgle of water finding its way. A man might stand and walk off between the low fins, becoming, within a few steps, just another shadow among ten thousand shadows.

They do not think of themselves as living on the plateau. They live in it. They are another form of its slow life, another crust, another set of channels. When you leave, you will probably not see them again. But they will be there. They will be waiting, with the water, with the stone, with the frogs and the shrimps and the old carved spirals, for the fog to come again.
