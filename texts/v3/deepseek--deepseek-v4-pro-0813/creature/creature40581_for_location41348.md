At first the seam looks uninhabited. Then the wall blinks.

The first lives here are not animals. They are films—sulfur-oxidizing sheets and iron-loving colonies spread over the black glass in gray, yellow, and rust-red. They are older than plants, older than fungi, and they need no light. They eat the mountain’s fever: sulfur, iron, heat, the mineral breath rising out of hairline fractures. In the amber steam, they look like the stone itself beginning to bruise and bloom. They are the bottom of everything, a living varnish on rock too new to have known weather.

Above that varnish, the wall is more animal than stone. Tiny comma-shaped grazers—glass-fleas, if they had a name—press together so densely that the rock appears to sweat. Each one is mostly water, translucent as a scale, with a dark mineral gut showing through. They are not worms, not snails, not quite crustaceans; they are shaped like a breath drawn into a comma and then held. They feed on the chemical films, scraping the varnish away in slow, overlapping waves. Their movement is silent. A patch of wall may look wet, then glisten, then slowly reveal clean black stone beneath. They are countless. They are the seam’s first true animals, and they are always hungry.

Over them crawl larger seed-sized creatures with ring-shaped mouths and too many legs. They move like a decision that cannot settle on which limb to use next. Their bodies are hard to read: part mite, part crab, part clockwork made of black chitin and mineral dust. Many carry splinters of obsidian in their front limbs. The tools are not made. They are chosen from the sharp debris that flakes from the ceiling as the rock cools. One of these tool-users pauses at a crack where iron salts have lacquered the stone. It turns the splinter over, tests the edge against the crust, then pries. A flake of mineral salt loosens. It feeds. When the splinter dulls, it drops the shard and selects another. Nothing is kept. Nothing is built.

Inside each hanging water bead, smaller lives turn slowly—round, slow, transparent, like living lenses concentrating the amber light. They are not fish, not larvae, not anything with a name that would survive outside. Their whole world is one droplet. They feed on what the steam has dissolved. They turn in their own bright spheres. Sometimes gravity falters and a droplet falls upward, or returns to the ceiling, and the small lives inside do not seem to notice. They simply keep turning, as if the direction of the universe is unimportant.

The predators are harder to see.

Some are like ripples of heat. They are visible only when they move, a shiver in the steam, a wobble in the air where the temperature bends. They do not seem to have a fixed body. They swim through the pocket’s unreliable geometry. One may appear on the left wall, then on the right wall, then fold into a corner that should not exist, all without crossing the air between. They hunt by pushing prey into spaces where distance repeats and escape paths become loops. A glass-flea flees left and arrives where it started. The predator is already there—not having moved, but having moved the space. It is unclear whether these hunters are animals or the seam’s own distortion given appetite. They redo failed strikes. They use the loops the way a spider uses silk.

Then there are the mirror-skinned things. Their bodies copy the rock so precisely—black, smoked silver, rust-red, wet—that when they move, a patch of wall seems to lag half a second behind the rest. You see them as a smear, a double exposure, a stone that forgot itself for an instant. They wait where the wall is already crowded with life, then become still. Their skin shows not their own outline but the wall’s. When a tool-user passes, a section of the wall detaches and folds around it. The mirror-skin opens along a seam that was not there before. Then the wall closes again, and there is only the same crowded black stone.

What is hardest to understand is that these lives are not only beside one another. They overlap. The pocket’s broken time lets a grazer and its predator occupy the same handspan of rock, one a heartbeat ahead of the other. A tool-user walks through a droplet without breaking it because the droplet is not there yet; then the droplet arrives and the tool-user is gone. The density is not just crowding. It is stacking: grazer upon grazer, tool-user upon tool-user, predator folded inside predator, all packed into one small wet cut in the mountain. Sometimes the same bead of water falls twice, and each time it carries a different set of lives.

The animals do not build. They make no nests, no middens, no caches, no lasting tools. Anything arranged in one of the time folds might come back twice, or as a different object, or not at all. A borrowed splinter of obsidian is the closest thing to technology. It is held for a few heartbeats, used, then dropped. The mountain will make more. The glass-fleas prune the crusts; the predators keep the glass-fleas from overgrazing. It is an ecosystem in motion, but silent and without architecture.

The whole seam is a single metabolism. Heat drives water out of the rock. Water condenses, falls, is reabsorbed by the same fractures. Mineral salts build, peel, and are grazed. The films eat the mountain’s breath. The glass-fleas eat the films. The tool-users pry salts from the cracks. The predators fold space around their prey. The droplets hold smaller animals still, round and slow, turning in their own bright spheres. Nothing green lives here. Nothing sings. The only sound is the occasional ping of cooling glass, a single high note, and then the silence closes back over it.

Ask who lives here, and the answer is not one animal. It is the whole crowd: the films, the fleas, the tool-users, the droplet-turners, the heat-ripples, the mirror-skins. The seam is not a hiding place for life. It is a kind of life. It is a hot, wet, overfull wound in new rock where the physics have not yet sealed themselves shut, and the residents are not separate from it. They are the wound’s pulse, its first attempt at becoming something more than stone.
