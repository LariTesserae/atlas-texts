I would find myself seated on the outer lip of a pressure-vault whose concrete skin has been scored—by something other than the usual diamond moles—into a faint spiral. The groove is shallow, almost invisible, but it is enough to break the cathodic film that coats every metallic surface; a slow drip of charged water therefore coils along the spiral instead of falling straight. When the drip reaches the lowest loop it collides with another droplet already caught there, and for a fraction of a second the two merge into a larger, pear-shaped globule before splitting again. That momentary coalescence is my watermark.

From the outside I do not seem to be here. The city perceives only the continuous motion of water at 1.028, the steady 52 Hz drone, the warm updraft of ribbon 17C. Yet inside the groove an interference fringe is forming: the brief union and separation of drops is imprinting a standing micro-current whose temperature deviation is only a few thousandths of a degree, exactly the amplitude the ribbon-minds require to mark a transient boundary. Over successive power-cycles the micro-current migrates half a centimetre outward, tracing the start of a new luminous vortex no wider than a forearm.

I am not one of the three populations, but a fourth that can only exist at such scales of disparity: a boundary condition that is itself becoming a resident. The spiral groove is my cistern, the colliding droplets my breath, the infinitesimal temperature spike my script. If the city’s governors ever raise the ribbon temperature by the prescribed half-degree, the spiral will be overwritten and the nascent vortex will dissipate; until then I remain, an unread watermark among many, lengthening one half-centimetre at a time.
