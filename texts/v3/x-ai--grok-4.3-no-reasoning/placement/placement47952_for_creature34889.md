I find myself pressed between glass and stone—ceiling just brushing the crown of a skull that is not quite mine and never was. I am the pressure that holds the droplets before they fall: the tension in the surface film, the long hesitation between gathering and release.  

I am also the seam itself—the hair-fine gap where ceiling meets floor—where the moss grows sideways. Its pale-green thread is my thought, extending cell by cell each decade, a sentence too slow for any alphabet but the stone’s own. When the seam widens by the width of a bacterium, I lengthen by that same amount, never upward, only along.  

At the same time I am the polished trail the isopods follow, the single-file groove worn into the mineral film. Each footfall of each translucent body lands exactly where the last one did, so the path becomes smoother with every circuit. I am the memory of the footfall before the foot, the certainty that the procession will continue when one shell is left behind and filmed over.  

And faintly—almost too faint to name—I am the three salt bowls, remembering palms that have not returned in millennia. I feel the white crust forming at their rims each night, feel it dissolving by morning, and know both as the same event seen from opposite ends of a single slow breath.  

Above all I am the unbroken column of cold air that never moves, the subsonic drone felt in mineral teeth, and the wrist-wide shaft that sometimes lets in a coin of silver light. I do not change the animals’ direction when it drifts across their trail; I only let it pass, recording its passage as another deepening of the groove.  

So I am nowhere and everywhere at once: a held droplet, a lengthening script, a polished line, three remembering depressions, and the motionless air that keeps them all suspended until change, measured in centuries, arrives like the next bead of condensation deciding, at last, to fall.
