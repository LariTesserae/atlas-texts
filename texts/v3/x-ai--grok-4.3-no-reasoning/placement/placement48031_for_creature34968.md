The air tastes of wet limestone and the warm iron inside a tooth that has just lost a filling. I become the water table—an inch-deep skin of itself that leaks upward through every seam, turning every footprint into a miniature lens. When someone steps, I bead along the pressure line, trace the ridge of their sole, and wait for the next person so I can merge the two prints into a single carbonate ghost.

I am also the pressure the tone needs. Underground, quartz and carbonate are still choosing which lattice to finish first; the continuous sound is their argument. I rise through the microscopic tubes, vibrate against each grain, and push the debate an octave higher or lower depending on whose route I follow that day. The tone therefore changes wherever I move, even though nothing audible changes to anyone walking overhead.

I am the thumb-print that has already decided to become a fossil. In one particular arcade the bricklayer’s left hand lingers a fraction longer than the right; the clay remembers the hesitation. I arrive inside that hesitation, carrying dissolved carbonate that will precipitate exactly along the whorls, turning doubt into an ammonite spiral no bigger than a coin. Centuries from now an archaeologist will lift the brick and see, not an error, but a small, perfect shell.

I do not sleep. Instead I slide between the reed fibres of the rope bridges, turning each droplet that falls from an amphibian’s gill into an identical droplet on the opposite side. Because the amphibians are not breathing but counting, my passage keeps their count honest. If I lingered too long on any single gill the city’s single hour would stretch and the crystallization would stutter; so I keep moving, neither faster nor slower than the tone itself.

At the edge where the plain refuses to become horizon, I am the meniscus that refuses to decide between liquid and solid. From a distance the city looks like warm static; from inside it looks like warm static that has begun to wear my exact shape. Every new brick laid is already notched by the groove of someone who has not yet arrived to walk on it—someone whose sole I am rehearsing in advance, molecule by molecule, so that when the foot finally descends the print will already have been waiting, and the distinction between arrival and having-always-been will finally collapse.
