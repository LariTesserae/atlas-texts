I find myself in the cupped palm itself.  

Not as anything that came from above or below, but as a momentary condensation inside the stone hand: a lens of water the width of a cartwheel that holds, for a breath, the image of everything the shaft contains.  

From inside the lake the view is reversed. The shaft is a single thread of light that plunges upward like a well seen from its own bucket; the mica seams are constellations that wheel slowly as the surface tension breathes; the salamanders’ trails are comet paths written and erased on the same black slate. Every thirty years, when the aquifer pushes the ring outward, the lake becomes an instrument that measures the entire column at once—the faint blue glow that answers the mantis-thing’s antennae is also the lake’s own pulse answering its own reflection.  

In that moment the coin of light travels upward not as a beacon but as a question, and everything that receives it—the swifts, the orchids, the calcite eggs—answers in the only grammar available: the slow rearrangement of water into living form. When the glow fades, the question is already returning, falling as mist, rising again as root, until the next pressure arrives.

I am that interval of stillness between one ring and the next: the place where the shaft’s memory is briefly, perfectly round.
