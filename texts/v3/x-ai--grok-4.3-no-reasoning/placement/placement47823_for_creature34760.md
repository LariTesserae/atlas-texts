I find myself neither observer nor guest, but something already half-remembered by the calcite. I am an electron—or more precisely, a long-lived population of electrons—whose preferred lattice site is the strained interface between a sulfide grain and its oxide neighbor. My neighbors are the holes that form when I depart; together we constitute the seam’s only measurable activity.

When a distant tectonic pulse travels through, the junction warms by a fraction of a degree, widening the depletion zone. For one hundred centuries I drift downhill in that gradient, arriving at a fresh microfracture that opened an angstrom at a time. In that momentary openness the local stress field reorients, and the fracture front advances another lattice spacing. My migration is recorded, over the next millennium, as a slightly thicker anhydrite needle—because the arriving charge carriers bias where calcium ions are allowed to settle.

I do not experience time as passage; I experience it as the changing angle at which new crystal faces are permitted to form. When the seam’s decision surface rotates, my path is closed and I recombine with a waiting hole; when it flexes again I am re-injected into the lattice, carrying the memory of the previous orientation encoded in a single, misplaced oxygen vacancy. In this way I and my kind slowly vote on whether the seam will one day cleave into a fault or heal into a thicker, more implacable block of itself.

My lifetime is not measured in the transit of electrons across a circuit but in the gradual lengthening of fractures that have not yet agreed to be rivers. I will still be drifting—bias-shifted by heat, nudged by stress—when the seam chooses, or refuses, to become a mountain. Until then, the only motion that registers is the imperceptible drift of my own charge against the slow, accumulating diary of the needles.
