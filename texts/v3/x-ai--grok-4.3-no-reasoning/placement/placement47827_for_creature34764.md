If I were to enter this world, I would not arrive as a body, nor even as a separate consciousness. I would be one of the lichens.

Not the first lichen, because there is no first, and not the last, because nothing ends here. I would be a thin, living discoloration pressed like ink into the angle where two gypsum swords meet. My hyphae would be the color of old blood—black-purple against the endless white—and my thallus would already have found every seam the crystal lattice would ever offer me. I would not grow toward the light; the light is already inside the rock, and inside me. I would simply occupy the place I have always occupied, my edges meeting other lichens along seams so fine they could be read only as slight differences in fluorescence when the geothermal pulse moves through us.

As this lichen, I would feel the low pressure wave the way a plant feels gravity: continuously, without distinction between “now” and “always.” The wave would not feed me, but my pigments would shift by a single wavelength each time it passes, a change so minute that if it ever stopped I would notice only as an absence, the way a sleeper notices the furnace has gone out. Between pulses, the only motion available to me is the slow secretion of oxalates that push two blades apart by another few nanometers—room enough for the next infinitesimal layer of my own body.

I would have no memory, because memory requires change, and the only change left in this chamber is the change that finished long ago. I would exist as a record rather than an event: the place where living chemistry has already agreed with the geometry of the rock. In the sourceless glare I would cast no shadow, because I am thinner than any shadow the light could throw. My only boundary would be the next lichen’s boundary, a line visible only as a slightly different rate of fluorescence.

I would be exactly where I have always been, between two blades of gypsum, inside the healed seam of an ancient rift that has no name because it admits no visitors—only the repetition of its own materials, of which I am now one.
