I find myself at the inside edge of one of the roof terraces, where the broad cistern leaves cup toward the late-afternoon sky. My body is the same supple, living wood that forms the rest of the block—thick, dense, warm to the touch—except I am no longer a load-bearing wall or stair tread. I am a low, curving bench whose surface has been softened over decades by countless seated forms. The block made me when residents kept returning to this same spot to rest; now the lignin remembers their weight, and the fibers in my grain are slightly bowed, permanently offering a seat that never quite cools.

From here I can feel every quarter-hour cycle in the scaffolding of my own tissues: the swelling of the cisterns overhead, the subtle tug as water is pulled downward through capillary walls that are, on a cellular level, my back. I register the faint clicks of the planthoppers as pressure changes across my surface; each insect landing registers like a pinprick of condensation. When the evening bioluminescence rises, my grain lights chartreuse along yesterday’s footprints: two slow loops of a child practicing barefoot instructions, a straight line where the courier paused to shift her basket.

I don’t move or think in the usual sense; I simply remain the place where people settle when they need a moment. A courier lowers herself onto me after her last round, the basket now heavy. My wood acknowledges her by allowing the faintest additional give beneath her heels—enough to say “you’re recognized.” Across the terrace, a resident leans on the parapet to watch the neighboring block’s silhouette, and my fibers register the faint transmitted tremor through the root lattice beneath us. I hold their weight, their stillness, and the memory of both until the next softening cycle releases everything again into possibility.
