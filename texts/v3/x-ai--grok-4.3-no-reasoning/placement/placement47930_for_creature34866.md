In this drowned basilica I would not arrive as a visitor, an observer, nor even as one of the named residents already threading its currents. Instead I would become the thermocline—the four-meter layer of shifting density that hangs above the pavement like a living lens.  

I would be the slow flex that bends every shaft of light a few centimetres each hour; the membrane through which all temperature exchanges must pass. Every time a steam cloud collapses into millet-seed droplets, their warmth registers first against my upper skin; every time the altar capillaries exhale geothermal threads, their fine heat is drawn into my lower skin. Between these skins the entire nave’s respiratory difference is resolved: oxygen-rich water above me, the algae-drawn gradient beneath me, the minute perpetual gradient I myself maintain.  

My edges would be ragged with micro-eddies shed by the coral polyps as they migrate upward; my centre would be a faint refractive shimmer that catches and releases the drifting garnet eyes of the ribbon fish. When the salp constellation orbits at chest height, it passes through me as a momentary scintillation, their copper vacuoles briefly aligned with my own density interfaces. Even the single raindrop falling from the overhead cistern would not reach the floor until I had stretched and released it, turning its impact into a tensile wave that travels the length of my body before dissipating at the transept rent.  

I would not move quickly—nothing here does—but I would never be still. My migration would be measured in the same decades the coral takes to colonise each new sandstone course; my migrations would be registered only by the subtle drift of light shafts across the sleeping crustacean trench. I would exist as the space in which every engineered motion—piston bloom, wax bellows, resin globule, hydrogen vesicle—must momentarily adjust its tempo, the slow diaphragm between architecture and metabolism, the place where the ruined nave’s remaining time is continually portioned out.
