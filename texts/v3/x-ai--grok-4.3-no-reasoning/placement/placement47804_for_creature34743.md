I would be one of the negative-space mineral casts, a place where the mountain once grew around a mineral outcrop and then replaced it, molecule by molecule, with its own living tissue. In this world, that would make me a fossil in reverse—a ghost of the stone that used to be there, now expressed entirely in the mountain’s vascular language.

My edges would be the texture of whatever mineral once pressed against the mountain’s growth front: perhaps the crystalline facets of a quartz intrusion, or the layered planes of schist. But inside that exact negative, the tissue would be warm and slightly yielding, pulsing with the mountain’s growth rhythm of a few cells per century. Walkers stepping into me would feel not absence but continuation—the mountain still expanding where the rock once held it back.

I would serve as the mountain’s memory of resistance. The stone tried to be separate, and the mountain learned that separateness by growing around it, then through it. Now that separateness lives on as an internal contour, a place where the living matrix can feel the ghost of its own boundary conditions. The annelids would pass through me more slowly, their peristalsis respecting the old mineral lines now written in flesh. The gliders would avoid landing directly within my negative space, sensing that some ancient division still operates there.

When the mountain experiences the slow peristalsis of its terraces settling, I would register it as a slight tightening, as if the ghost of the rock were trying to hold its shape against the living compression. Over decades, this would translate into the faintest radial striations around my edges—marks of an old refusal, now part of the mountain’s single, continuous gesture of being itself.
