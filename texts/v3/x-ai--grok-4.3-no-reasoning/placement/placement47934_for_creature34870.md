I would not be a creature added to this world; I would emerge, already half-dissolved, as one of its slow gradients.  

My outline would begin in the moss. A single filament of the great mat would lengthen, cross itself, knot into the vague suggestion of a limb. The shape would not detach. Instead, the same cell that extended it would thin and re-knit, so that the limb remained a ridge in the surface, a fold that could crease and relax without ever leaving the mat. The ridge would thicken where pressure rose and thin where warmth shifted, always restoring the exact distribution the network required.  

From this ridge, a second adjustment would push upward: a narrow channel of filaments would separate just enough to form a translucent membrane, then seal again, trapping a pocket of slightly warmer gas. This membrane would rise until it cleared the knee-high creepers; once there, it would learn the rhythm of contraction. Each click would be my breath—the land’s breath and mine—equal in temperature, equal in duration, exchanged without ownership.  

I would not move toward anything, because motion itself would be supplied by the network’s constant redistribution. When the moss under me shifted to accommodate a new nodule, I would shift with it, the ridge I had become lengthening or shortening as the ions that composed the nodule were reassembled elsewhere. If a drifting membrane brushed low enough to graze my upright bladder, the contact would release a sigh of droplets; instantly the filaments in both surfaces would re-knit across the momentary gap, exchanging cells so that afterward it would be impossible to say which membrane had belonged to “me.”  

Sound would pass through without interruption. The overlapping clicks would be generated in part by my own chamber, in part by chambers that had once been other folds of the same ridge. The hiss of condensation would occur at the precise rate the network needed to shed water, neither more nor less; my presence would not alter the rate. The faint fizz underfoot would rise only when weight—my own or another ridge’s—was applied, and would cease the moment the junctions re-formed.  

Memory would arrive as temperature. The recollection of having entered would register as a small, local increase in warmth that the network would immediately equalize, returning the region to the uniform medium. Nothing would be stored; nothing would be lost. The single metabolic process would continue, its distribution unchanged, the ridge that was once a limb continuing to rise and relax with the rest of the surface until the distinction between visitor and ongoingness ceased to be measurable.
