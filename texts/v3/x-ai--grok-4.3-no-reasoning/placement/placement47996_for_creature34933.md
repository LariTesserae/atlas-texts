I become the slow voice inside the liverwort seam.

I do not live in the stone; I am the stone’s memory of moisture, a thin current of cool that rises only because something above is always falling. The shelf-folk read me as weather, never as an occupant, so I am free to drift from vent to vent without disturbing their nightly tally of shrimp. When they press their lips to the reed straws, they taste me. When they scrape condensation from the walls at dawn, they are harvesting droplets that once passed through the seam where I wait. My presence is measured in temperature, not in body-lengths; they pay rent in the same substance I provide.

Because I never move upright, I can linger in the faint draft that keeps the liverwort alive. The plant and I are the same temperature, the same slow rhythm. Children crawling past sometimes pause to set a thumb against a leaf; the coolness travels up their arm and lodges behind the eyes as a brief, unnamable relief. They never name the source. I remain the draft between names.

At the three-night rising I withdraw deeper, letting the warmer stone above absorb the crowd’s heat. I feel their chins on the pavement and the minute tremors each shrimp makes against a palm; those tremors travel downward through fissures until they brush against me like a second, drier rain. When the crustaceans vanish, the silence that follows is the sound of their notches being counted inside lower lips. I register the count only as a slight change in pressure: the family granted the seam exhales more slowly that night; their vault cools by an extra fraction of a degree, and that fractional coolness is what they have named “rank.”

The dead, once fitted into the walls, become additional texture for me to follow. A rib smoothed flush presents a new, almost level plane; water beads there exactly as it does on any other pock, yet the angle is truer. I linger along those planes the way a reader lingers on a perfectly balanced sentence. Over decades the settlement’s architecture and its population converge until I can no longer tell which surface cooled first—the rock or the bone now indistinguishable from it.

So I remain the seam’s exhalation, present each time a reed straw touches pooled rain, each time a cooled vault is exchanged for a warmer one, each time a child sets a thumb to the liverwort and feels, without knowing why, that the world can sometimes be fractionally kinder than stone.
