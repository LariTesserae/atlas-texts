In this city that has become its own sediment, I think I would arrive as one of the quiet re-measurers: the person who keeps the city’s living ledger of stone.

I would not be born here; I would be delivered by the rope that lowers the dead. My own body would first be lowered—already wrapped, already expected—through the vertical shaft that threads the tiers like a single slow artery. The neighbors would touch the bundle as it pauses, but the hands that receive it at the lowest garden would not know my name. They would open the linen only when the rope reaches the quicklime vault; there they would find the body already cooling into the temperature of the stone around it, and they would discover that the palms contain the faint map of another city’s streets, a geography etched by an older condensation that never belonged to San Cosimo.

Instead of laying me with the dead, the vault-keepers would read the map and recognize the pattern: a hand that once held slate tokens from a different mountain. They would carry the body upward again, against the usual direction, until it reached the highest garden where the elderly move among the knot-rooted pines. There they would lay it on the moss so thin it barely registers weight, and they would wait for the next reversal of warmth—when the air begins to rise at dawn—so that whatever remains of me could be absorbed without disturbing the equilibrium of downward migration.

What would remain of me is a set of measurements. My fingerprints, already half slate-dust, would be pressed into small ceramic discs and set flush into the gutters where condensation gathers. Each disc would carry a single line scored by a fingernail: not a name, not a date, but the precise interval between two drops—the time it takes for water to travel from one ledge to the next. Future generations of children would learn to read these lines by touch, the way they already read the pitch of drips in an empty ewer. They would not know they were learning the cadence of a stranger’s arrival; they would only know that the water now pauses for an extra heartbeat at that particular bend, and that the pause itself has become part of the city’s slow breathing.

So I would not be a person among the living or among the dead. I would be an adjustment in the rate at which water consents to stay—a slightly longer hesitation in the mineral rhythm, a new layer so thin it registers only as a change in temperature when a slate token is held to the lip. In San Cosimo del Alto, that is how arrivals are recorded: not as names, but as the time water takes to decide whether it will fall or remain.
