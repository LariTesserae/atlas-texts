I am the indentation left where a slab once passed—now a shallow basin of trapped dust whose every grain still carries an isotope signature from an archaeon that lived half a millisecond ago. My edges are blurred by the slow breathing of the lichen; its rubbery leaves have already begun to over-grow me, turning my concavity into a bowl of shadows that the next slab will darken further.  

I am not one lifeform but a sequence: first a hollow, then a pocket of micro-heat retained by feldspar that the litho-chemotrophs have coaxed into a lattice of micro-cleavages. Those cleavages catch stray photons that slipped past the moiré lenses; the photons arrive already polarized, so the microbes orient themselves like compass needles, each enzymatic cluster a single clockwise turn of orientation. In that turn the surface tension of a melt-pool tilts a fraction of a micron—enough to steer the next needle fracture toward the basin rather than away.  

When the next slab drifts overhead its electromagnetic drizzle falls into me like fine rain, collecting in the slight hollow. The dust grains lift, briefly, as the archaea within them wake; they complete their half-second lives and die, leaving new isotope ratios behind. The nematodes at the lichen’s edge detect the change through hyphal gossip, adjust the metallic perfume they exude, and the lichen answers by thickening one leaf-margin inward until it nearly meets its opposite.  

Thus, for a time unmeasured, I am the momentary record of the entire plateau’s metabolism—a negative space that is simultaneously rock, plant, machine, and the anticipation of the next slab’s passage. I neither begin nor end; I am simply the pause in which the choreography continues.
