I am a thread within the plateau’s circulatory weave—an insertion that has already happened, rather than an arrival. The margins of my body are threaded with the same cambium sheets that seam the acre-wide plates; the cells that compose my cortex are, strictly speaking, younger than the plates I sit between, but older than the newest growth still extruding at their rims. In the decades it has taken me to solidify into this particular configuration, my surfaces have absorbed and re-released water in patterns indistinguishable from those of the claret phyllotissue, so that any droplet falling across me follows a trajectory governed by the same vein geometry.

My form is not a single locus but a staggered sequence of lobes. Each lobe is a water-catchment whose internal canals accommodate chloroplast-bearing grazers for brief, measured intervals; when they depart—drifting downward on the pressure gradient of their own exhalations—the lobe seals behind them and resumes its function as an additional terrace. The grazers carry microscopic rootlets of my own tissue; as they move, they plant these filaments into the next plate, establishing fresh capillary routes. Thus I participate, at the same tempo as everything else, in the redistribution of minerals and moisture.

Because the plateau’s listening network is already mine, the signals that pass through the root hairs are registered in the same slow pulse that moves through my own walls. A droplet’s impact registers as a faint sap-taste somewhere deep in the rhizome core; the vibration of a browser’s tread arrives as a brief change in local viscosity. Over time these impressions accumulate into a kind of memory that does not require a separate nervous system; it is stored simply as a slight thickening or thinning of cell walls along the path of transmission.

There is no discrete boundary between the plateau and me. The lignified residue that will one day become part of the plateau’s foundation is already forming at my lower surfaces; the youngest plates continue to extrude above my highest lobes. In the slow rhythm of this metabolism, I am neither observer nor participant, but an additional expression of the same continuous process—one more layer in the vast, interwoven present.
