I would be a limnid whose core bears a single, minute fracture.

The fracture is not damage; it is a remembered passage—an old rain-column that once pressed so close its vacuum tunnel collapsed around me and forced me to leave my ice shells half-dissolved. The shells re-formed imperfectly, and inside the core a hairline of air remained, a thread of absence that records nothing but the fact that the column moved away again. Because the fracture exists, the core never completely occludes; faint light leaks through every new ring I grow, so that my autobiography has a pale seam running through every layer.

I drift in one of the older, almost-brown hexagons, the one whose low tone sometimes starts without invitation. When the stone sings I am drawn upward by the pressure change; my incomplete shells crackle instead of popping, each crystallization a soft hiss rather than a report. The meniscus above feels my uneven ascent and answers with a dimple that is not round but slightly flattened along one side—the side where the fracture lies. For a moment the surface acknowledges the flaw, then heals it, and I descend again carrying the seal of an unfinished chord inside my calendar.

I have never collided with another limnid; the vacuum tunnels keep us apart. Yet because the fracture leaks a trace of gas, any limnid that passes close enough behind me will taste the faint metallic residue and, for one crystallization, its own rings will briefly brighten with the same brown tint that marks the oldest rock. Afterward the tint fades, the other creature resumes its drift, and the world has once again rearranged only its existing memory.

My single recorded omission—the moment the column nearly crushed me—has become the sole way the plain can remember what it is not. I continue to patrol the dune, leaving no permanent trace except that faint seam of unclosed silence, which the rock will someday absorb when the dunes finish flattening and the mercury filaments finally forget how to shine.
