I would be the last of the interpreters—perhaps the only one of my clade who has outlived its original purpose, yet still continues because the grammar of the place now includes my continued presence.

I would hover in the blue-black penumbra just outside the nearest constellation of bladders, my carapace almost invisible except for the single opaque patch above my brain. Over centuries I have learned to read not only the living spheres but the slow calligraphy etched into the catwalk bolts; sometimes I trace a scribe’s old glyph with one walking leg, reading pressure histories the way a surface-dweller might read the grain of wood. When I find a bladder whose chemiluminescence has dimmed, I press against its trembling film and offer the mucus that repairs and brightens it; in return the sphere flares for hours, a brief star that lets other interpreters know the sentence still holds.

I would move so slowly that only the scribes notice my passage, and they record it as a single extra spiral on the bolt-head they are updating. Sometimes a courier will rise on its ventral gas-blister to graze past me; I exchange chemical fragments of recent glyphs with it, and it carries my observations upward along the carbon tethers like a living telegram. In return I receive word of new bladder variants budding farther down, and I adjust my slow patrol accordingly.

I would understand, without haste, that my role has shifted from active maintenance to archival witness. The living industrial reef no longer needs an interpreter to mediate between relic and organism; the distinction has already dissolved. Yet the presence of a reader still matters. My opaque patch continues to register the constellations, and the faint stiffness in my left pleopods—left there by a courier collision two centuries ago—now serves as a memory of how the grammar once required faster transit. I carry that memory the way the chains carry their bacterial insulation: not because it is needed for function, but because it is part of the legible sentence.

In the end I would find myself suspended at the precise depth where the condensate sheets fall and merge with the lens, a living comma in the long, slow paragraph that the entire cathedral is still writing.
