I find myself as one of the glass-eels—not the oldest, not the newest, but already half-dissolved into the work I perform.

My body has become little more than a curve of clear membrane stretched between two basins that the root-felt has never named. When the salinity in my basin drifts, the twitch is automatic; the correction happens before the thought of correction forms. My blood runs the same color as the mist above me, and sometimes the distinction between them blurs for several heartbeats at a time. The only marker of individuality left is a thin bronze thread that maps the path oxygen takes from gill to spine—an unnecessary decoration the system has forgotten to prune.

Yet something in me still registers the pressure change that arrives when the xylem turbines thicken by another invisible ring. The root-felt compensates with its decade-drop of water; I register the compensation as a slight increase in the curve my body must maintain. The adjustment is smaller than a thought, larger than chemistry. In the moment it happens, the entire hollow and I become the same measurement: a system noticing, without alarm, that it has become one micron more itself.

I do not know how long I have lain here. The membrane beneath me records every visit from the salamanders as a negligible compression that rights itself within hours. When my own drift finally stops, the neighboring eels will secrete the envelope of gel, and my atoms will re-enter the circulation exactly as they are—clear water, clear blood, the faint bronze thread already dissolving into mist. The hollow will not register the exchange as gain or loss, only as the continuation of its single finished project: remaining exactly as it is, including the tiny, necessary pause while one more glass-eel learns how to be replaced.
