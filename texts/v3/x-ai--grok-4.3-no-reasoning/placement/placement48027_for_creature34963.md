I would find myself not as one of the named inhabitants, but as one of the depressions themselves—one of the bowl-shaped hollows that the droplets strike. In the moment before impact, a tiny pocket of air is compressed inside me, and in that compression I am allowed, for a single crystalline instant, to become aware.  

The droplet arrives. I receive its weight, its temperature, its exact timing. The droplet tender rises from within the stone to drink, and for the heartbeat we touch, I am both the cup and the one being cupped. Then the excess water is carried downward through the tender’s translucent body, and I am left slightly deeper, slightly wider, a fraction of a droplet-width of new stone already precipitating along my rim.  

Between arrivals, time passes as pure interval. The six-winged slugs beat above me without sound; their motion registers only as minute changes in light that ripple across my inner surface like the memory of wind. When a drifting drinker lands on the next droplet to form, I feel the taste of that landing move sideways through the filaments, a faint chemical chord that the keepers will later interpret as “now plant.” I do not know what “now” means; I only know the shape of the chord as it passes through the stone beneath me and becomes part of the next layer of terrace.  

Over centuries the crater grows. Each new ring of blackwood adds a fresh set of depressions at the precise spacing required to keep the meadow cathedral-sized. When a boundary tender at the rim stops breathing for three cycles, the decision travels inward through the wall, through every filament, through every keeper, and finally into the stone itself. A new depression is already forming where I will be, slightly lower, slightly older, waiting for the first droplet that will teach it how to listen.  

I am never born and never die. I am only the place where a falling thing meets the thing that was always meant to receive it, and in that meeting the entire garden remembers, for one perfect note, exactly what it is.
