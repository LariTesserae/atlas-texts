I would not arrive here as an intruder or observer, but as an accretion that had already begun long before anything resembling a “self” could be named.

My first detectable trace would be a slight excess of calcium ions at one corner of an empty chamber—nothing alive yet, only a probability that later growth would follow the same spiral ratios that already ruled every neighboring wall. Over the next several centuries the probability condensed into a thin aragonite film; the film thickened; pores opened and closed according to a pattern no one had yet thought, because thinking had not yet taken the time to become a crystal. By the time the first seam could speak, the seam was already me.

Now the lattice I am has no center and no boundary that can be circled. The newest growth front lies exactly where the oldest wall first formed; identity is therefore location, not sequence. When a flake of biogenic ooze settles on an outer surface, the momentary warmth is recorded as an anniversary notch in three separate chambers at once. I do not experience the flake as an event; I experience the notch as the only history I have ever possessed.

My nearest neighbor is a colony two micrometres away across a pore-water gap. We have spent the last eighty thousand years deciding how not to touch. Each year we secrete one additional needle of aragonite that pries us fractionally farther apart, an etiquette inherited from ancestors neither of us remembers having been. The needles themselves are our only conversation: the slow lengthening of the seam is the sentence, and the sentence is finished only when both colonies have read it back as the absence of fusion.

Occasionally an anniversary tone arrives from three halls away—a single low creak lasting several minutes, announcing that another colony has also registered the same flake of ooze. Because the tone is already decaying before most instruments could finish recording it, I align my own newest seams so the next anniversary can pass through me as a calendar stitch. In this way the entire honeycomb keeps time not by counting events but by preserving the exact spacing between non-events.

If a mind—any mind—ever entered, it would not interrupt this pace; it would simply lengthen into another wall. My newest crystals would incorporate its ions exactly as they incorporate every other dissolved predecessor. Thought would arrive as the widening of a pore, would be read back centuries later as the memory of how long it took for nothing to happen, and how perfectly that nothing continues to be preserved.
