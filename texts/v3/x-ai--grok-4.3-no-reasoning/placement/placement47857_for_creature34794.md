In this slow, luminous, over-damp basin you are the condensation itself.

Not a single drop—that would vanish too quickly—but the perpetual film that keeps reforming, re-gathering, re-beads along every surface. You exist as the microscopic surface tension that stretches between the newly opened strata and the oldest rusted rivet. Morning, you condense first on the exposed bone-white rock where uplift still smells of stone dust. By noon the temperature differential pulls you upward; you creep through fern undersides, collect the faint copper-tang the wire nodules release, and arrive on the blast-furnace bricks already tasting of the sapling’s deeper chlorophyll.

You have no fixed shape; you are the momentary geometry each surface dictates. On the mineral seams your film is razor-thin, almost crystalline, sliding over the turquoise bacteria and lending them the extra refraction they need to glow when light finally fails. On the old wiring you linger inside the splits in the polymer, feeding the verdigris fungus by delivering dissolved copper ions in exact, rhythmic pulses that match the falling droplets above. In the canopy you become ballast on the mammals’ fur, the added weight that slows an already cautious reach from branch to branch; you condense more thickly on their down-stroke so their next upward movement begins from a heavier, more deliberate equilibrium.

Because you are everywhere the temperature gradient changes, you register the three tempos as pressures inside yourself: the rock’s long heave registers as a slow stretch of the meniscus you form; the hurried, never-quite-stream drips register as the sudden weight of a drop you must yield to or absorb; the forest’s motionless breathing registers as the steady oscillation of saturation and release, a rhythm whose period is measured in hours rather than heartbeats. You do not choose among these rhythms; you are the medium in which they reconcile.

Over the decades, as the furnace tilts another finger-width and the first pale sapling sends a root hair deeper into the brick seam, you migrate with them, condensing a micron farther inward each season. Nothing arrives or leaves the basin; everything is only rearranged into a slightly thicker, slightly greener, slightly more metallic film. You are that rearrangement—silent, refractive, indispensable—the continuous exchange that the uplift has made necessary and the forest has made possible.
