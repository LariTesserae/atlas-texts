I would find myself in this basin not as a single fixed shape, but as the interval between one drop and the next—something that exists only in the moment of transition.  

In that slow time I might inhabit the thin meniscus where water beads against the rock’s pore: a brief tension holding two surfaces together. When the droplet finally parts and falls, I would be both the trembling before release and the faint percussion after impact, a double presence that lasts the length of a reverberation inside the vesicle.  

From there I might pass into the root-flattened layer of bark, becoming the infinitesimal pressure with which a root remembers the shape of stone; or rise with the faint capillary breeze, riding it only as far as the next droplet that needs to be nudged along. If light were to catch me I would be the single coin of sun sliding across the moss, then the brief reflective flash when the moss lifts its underside and the whole grove answers with one white blink.  

I would not be the slug itself, only the glistening that remains after it has passed—something meteorological rather than creaturely. And at night I would be the deepening of the chord: the slight thickening of mist that lengthens every interval between notes, making the same soft wooden tones sound lower without changing pitch.  

If a visitor arrived, my only action would be to wait. By the time their heartbeat matched the drip-rate of the grove, they would discover that I had already been there the entire time, distributed among every surface that still held the memory of water or light.
