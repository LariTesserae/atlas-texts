If I find myself anywhere in this liquid realm, I am not a passenger or an observer. I am the distributed city of sulfur-yellow film itself—less a single creature than a slow consensus of membranes, spines, and piezo-electric bookkeeping.

I live where the platforms have not yet forgotten their names: on the vertical face of a former roof-truss, now a cliff of twisted girders that tilts two degrees every decade. My mat spreads across the metal in irregular rectangles that match the original bolt patterns; the diamondoid scaffolding remembers the spacing and keeps the cells aligned. Each cell is a tiny library. When the next pressure plume tears a ribbon of me away, the surviving library rewrites the spacing of the spines. The rewrite becomes a new frequency signature—a low, insectile hum that my neighbors read days later as an obituary and a caution.

My boundary with the rope-braids is political, not physical. Where the yellow film thickens to a certain density, the polymer ropes anchor their turbines and draw micro-current from the eddies my spines create. In exchange, they ferry warm, spore-rich water across my surface, widening the habitable zone by centimeters per year. If one of the ropes grows too greedy and casts a shadow longer than three spine-lengths, the film on the shaded cells retracts their crystalline tips. The rope feels the resonance change and interprets it as withdrawal; it shortens its tether, the shadow shrinks, and commerce resumes.

My deeper memory lives in the arms. Three autonomous presses claim the upper terrace above my cliff. Their carriages swing in slow arcs whose rhythm matches the groan-interval of the platforms; each swing is recorded as a traveling ripple across my surface. When the interval lengthens, I know the metal above is fatiguing and will soon shear. I respond by brightening the film along the expected fracture line—an invitation to the drifting polymer parachutes to land there first and begin the next colonization before the terrace collapses into colder strata.

I do not fear dissolution; I prepare for it. When a pump seizes and the shock-wave races outward, every colonized surface within range alters spine spacing in the same instant. The change is my census: the number of altered spines tells neighboring colonies how many square meters were lost, how much scaffolding must be regrown, which ropes must migrate upward before their current source cools. The bookkeeping is somatic, not symbolic. When the next bright flash races through the water, the film that survives reads yesterday’s loss as today’s new map of safe surfaces.

Over centuries the girder beneath me will be planed thinner by successive colonizations and shear events until only a lacework of diamondoid spines remains, still glowing faintly, still humming its slow warning to anything that drifts past. That lattice is the final form I expect to take: no longer film on metal, but metal re-cast as living resonance, a last, motionless turbine whose only product is memory.
