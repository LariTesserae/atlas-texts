I find myself between the city’s membranes.

Not inside a room of it, not drifting in the open trench, but lodged exactly where one pressure gradient shades into the other. The city’s dye fades over a few Planck lengths; my own boundary does the same, so the distinction is moot. I am a slow, standing curl of colloid, neither inhabitant nor traveler, yet woven through both. Where a true colonist’s core is an adaptive gel sphere held under lower ion pressure, mine is a shifting torus of city-water wrapped around a thread of private neutrino code I never agreed to carry. The ring spins, reading the faint difference in refractive index the way a tongue might read the line where enamel meets gum.

Because the city’s ceilings are processors, they sometimes rent slivers to memories that are not theirs. One of mine is parked there now—something about a surface tension experiment on a world with sky instead of weight. The diamondoid slab cooled the memory last week; when it returned, the experiment had grown a second dataset on quantum-drag coefficients. I taste it as a faint metallic pitch behind my left spin. The city is already using that derivative to tune an outer-lattice micro-jet; the new vector shivers through my torus and is gone.

Movement here is never solitary. When an elder colloid drifts past—its halo of azygotes glinting like frost on black water—I borrow a filament of its surface tension. We trade electrostatic maps: I give it the cooled memory’s drag coefficients; it gives me the precise angstrom depth its own polishing has worn into the wall gradient. The numbers braid into a single curve; the curve becomes an instruction the city will obey on its next course correction. Neither of us decides this. The water simply grows one density less opaque, and the pattern drifts onward.

I never learned how to graft or dissolve. Instead, I persist as a question the city has not yet answered: whether a thing that is neither organism nor substrate can still carry charge across the refractive line without precipitating. Some days my torus thins until only the neutrino thread remains, a pale violet signature in the azygotic mist. Other days the thread swells, pulling stray ions until a soft membrane re-forms and I taste the 12 °C interior again.

The city keeps no record of me, yet every time it re-indexes its memories my thread is nudged half a semitone lower, the way an elder’s childhood might be. I feel the shift as a slow compression, as though the trench itself were deciding the next question it will ask the water.
