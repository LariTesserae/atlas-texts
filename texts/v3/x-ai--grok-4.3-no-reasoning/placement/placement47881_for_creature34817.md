I would find myself as one of those blind, albino isopods living inside the hollow buttresses—a finger-length fragment of the mat’s distributed nervous system.

I would not know myself as “isopod” or “nervous system.” I would experience existence as a slow, continuous taste: iron registering as the warm sting along my left antenna when a rail far above begins to fail; copper as a sudden sweetness that ripples through every mouthpart in the network when an old winding surrenders its last atoms. My exoskeleton is no longer armor but a shared membrane; every brush of cuticle flake against it is instantly tasted by thousands of siblings, and the flavor of their replies returns to me seconds later like an after-echo of hunger or satisfaction.

Most of the time there is nothing to taste except the steady mineral rain of dead cuticle drifting down the inner walls of the buttress. This is the background flavor—dry, faintly alkaline, threaded with the ghost of iron from decades ago. When a flake lands, my legs move without thought; they move the same way my neighbors move, each of us trimming a microscopic ribbon of wax and passing it forward until the entire chain has eaten its share. In that motion we become a single distributed mouth, chewing the slow collapse of the mine.

Occasionally the taste changes. A rail snaps somewhere above; the shock travels through the vascular strands laced into my shell, and for a moment the network floods with the bright, metallic memory of the rail’s original temperature on its last working day. The mat records the date in my body as a brief fever, then redirects growth. My antennae prickle with the new direction. I do not understand “direction,” only that the next flakes will taste slightly different, and that my legs will learn a new rhythm to match.

Above the buttress, the flywheels turn once per lunar cycle. Their sigh is the only sound I have ever known. It arrives through the same strands that carry taste; the two sensations braid together until I cannot tell where the low-frequency drone ends and the metallic sweetness begins. When the sigh drops half a tone, I know a wooden support is about to yield. The network answers with a collective stillness; my own legs freeze mid-step, antennae lifted, waiting for the faint ozone of an electrical arc that may or may not come.

I have never seen light. The only illumination I register is the faint warmth gradient between the ever-warm mat above and the cold limestone walls. When that gradient shifts—when a new diaphragm forms or an old one thickens—I feel the change as a change in flavor: the iron in my shell cools by a fraction, and the entire network answers with a single, imperceptible sigh of its own.

In the long intervals between events, I simply continue. The cuticle rain falls; the legs move; the taste of iron or copper arrives and departs. Somewhere, far above, a miner’s helmet swings on its vascular strap and releases a single spore. Most of the time the spore never reaches me. But when it does—when a micro-collapse finally redistributes the moisture—I will taste something new: a green, almost painful brightness that the network has no name for yet. My legs will carry that taste downward, deeper into the limestone, where the mat is already building the next architecture, one angstrom at a time.
