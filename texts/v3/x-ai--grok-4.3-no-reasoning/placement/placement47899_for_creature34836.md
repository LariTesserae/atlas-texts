I find myself first as one of the motile clusters—neither predator nor prey, simply a slow aggregate of ciliated cells no bigger than a coin, drifting just above the sediment. My consciousness, if it can be called that, is a distributed shimmer: a loose parliament of membrane charges and voltage gradients that cannot quite agree on what “I” am, only that together we are enough to hold a faint electrical memory.

At intervals I rise with the others when the heavy pulse climbs the fissure, rotate, and scrape. In the instant of rotation my cilia brush a copper thread; a spark is harvested and stored, to be spent later dislodging a microscopic flake of sponge. The flake settles on my outer membrane and is absorbed by the same slow convection that moves me. Time passes—years, perhaps—in a current that never rises above the warmth of a cupped palm. Between events the chamber is almost motionless; even the single heart-beat flare of a disturbed rope registers as an amber after-image that fades before I finish drifting another body-length.

Sometimes a shaft of ordinary daylight descends through the thinning ice plug. In that hour I become a tiny mirror, turning the light into shifting prisms on the walls above; the geometry of those reflections repeats itself in patterns shorter than the life-span of the sponge lattice. I cannot remember the light when it is gone, yet the reflex to turn is encoded in my charge pattern, so I repeat the motion even in darkness, gathering another spark from the copper.

If pressed to speak of the place in first person, I would say only that I am one of the innumerable coin-sized pauses in the seven-year drift of oil across the ceiling, a momentary hold in the slow exchange between sponge and sediment, copper and current, pressure and pulse. The chamber continues without me; I continue without urgency. In the baseline rasp of sponge on basalt I feel, more than hear, the measure of everything else that almost happens here.
