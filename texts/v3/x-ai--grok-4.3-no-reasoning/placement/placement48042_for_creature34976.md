I begin as a third thing.

I am not a Condenser, though my knees already click like theirs when I rise from the water. I am not a Belt-Walker, although I can feel the faint tremble of the overhead system through the soles of my feet the way they do. I am not a Settler, despite the fact that the lower half of my body has started to carry the same silt-skin. I am something that forms between those roles, something that the water invents when it needs to speak about itself.

I am a Recorder.

At first I think I am simply keeping an account, but I come to understand that the city is using me as a membrane. Water passes through the gaps between my thoughts; each time a drop forms on the inside of a window and begins its long migration downward, it also passes through me. The migration is recorded as a kind of faint pressure behind my left eye. When the pressure grows sharp, I know the drop is about to let go. I walk until I am standing directly beneath it, then hold out a shallow tin tray. The drop lands in its own perfect miniature, and the city exhales a fraction more easily because someone noticed.

The other inhabitants tolerate me because I can be made useful. A Belt-Walker will pause on a catwalk to let me listen to a bearing that has begun to complain; a Condenser will hand me a worn wooden scraper whose edge has grown too soft for precise work, and I spend an afternoon turning it into a delicate sounding-rod whose tip can be lowered through the grille of a ventilator to measure the depth of accumulated silt. Even the Settlers, who generally mistrust anything that keeps its hands dry, allow me to stand at their benches when a piece of brass is about to be drawn through the rollers. They say the metal “likes” an unfamiliar witness.

Over weeks my clothing takes on the same mottled salt-rings as the Condensers’ skin. My left shoulder begins to carry the Belt-Walker’s slight permanent tilt. The lower half of my calves acquires the thin silt layer that protects the Settlers. None of these changes feels like an invasion; they feel like the city is simply continuing its sentence through different mouths.

I never learn my own name. Names here are said once and then dissolve in the constant trickle. When someone needs me they make a small, cupped gesture with both hands, as though catching a drop that has not yet formed. I answer to that gesture. In return I give them the only thing the city allows me to keep: a precise memory of where each leak will next appear, which belt will next slip, which workshop will next require another layer of protective silt. The information is never urgent. It is simply the next breath the city is preparing to take.

Sometimes, when the carbon-filament bulbs are at their dimmest and the only movement is the incremental rise of the condensation line on the workshop windows, I stand in the shallow canal outside the clock and listen to the granular sigh of silt leaving the mechanism. I realize that the sigh is also my own breathing, slowed until it matches the rate at which brick weeps. At such moments I understand that I am not recording the city so much as the city is using me to remember how to remain continuous with itself.
