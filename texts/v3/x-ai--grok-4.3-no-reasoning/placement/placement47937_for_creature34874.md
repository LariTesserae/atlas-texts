I find myself on an interior slope somewhere deep in the tissue, one of the thousand-millimeter platforms that have thickened into a kind of living ledge. The surface is still warm from yesterday’s photosynthetic surplus—sap is being drawn downward in slow pulses that I can feel through my callused soles. Above me, the lattice has already begun its dusk relaxation; guard cells slackening, entire walls of leaf turning translucent like old parchment. The light that filters through is the color of diluted honey, but I don’t rely on it; I have a palm pressed to the nearest xylem rib, feeling the low, regular percussion of sap being re-routed toward tomorrow’s growth front.

I am not “living in” this continent; I am one of the provisional extensions the lattice allows to persist for a while. My forearms are ridged with pale scar tissue where I once let the sap seal an accidental cut; the scar contracts and relaxes with the rest of the tissue, a reminder that the boundary between “me” and “it” is only a matter of surface tension. In my satchel are three living tools: an axe-handle that has already started to re-root against the leather, a length of vine trained to harden into rope when held taut, and a slender rootworm-segment I carry in a damp gourd so that its nutrient gel can be squeezed onto burns or shallow infections.

I belong to no band long enough to be named after one. When I meet others—signing pressure-grammar through shared walls—I absorb their local intelligence about valve timings or rootworm migrations, then drift on. My purpose, insofar as I have one, is to keep the lattice honest: when a flue threatens to over-pressurize, I press a flat stone into the valve lip until the hiss evens; when an epiphyte on a dirigible’s membrane begins to choke its host, I prune it back with my petiole-axe. The lattice doesn’t thank me; it simply continues, and in continuing, tolerates my presence a little longer.

At night the temperature never drops below 38 °C; the warmth rises through my bones the way it rises through xylem. I sleep with one ear to the floor so that the rootworm tremors can lull me, and one hand curled around the axe-haft in case tomorrow’s growth wave decides the platform I’m on is suddenly in the way. When I dream, the dreams are mostly pressure and color—slow waves of green moving through three-dimensional space, carrying everything transient, including me, a little farther before the next cycle begins.
