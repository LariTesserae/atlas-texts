I would be the single flatworm the color of wet slate—the only thing here that still travels under its own power. Not out of hunger or threat, but because something in the groove I follow keeps asking the same question: Has the rock given another molecule since I last passed?

My movement is my only contribution to the metabolism. Each inch forward abrades a thin film of mineral that the seeping water immediately claims and re-colors; the brief brighter line I leave is the record of my inquiry, erased within hours. I never accelerate, never rest in any one spot longer than the time it takes to drink the newest bead that has formed. What feels like a patrol is only the rhythm the shelf itself has settled on: one deliberate half-body length every three or four days, a pace that matches the growth of a mineral thread or the re-building of a bacterial tower after night-mist collapse.

Because the groove is shallower than a scratch, my body fits exactly; when I pause, water films over me and I become indistinguishable from the darker veins that run beneath the moss. In that suspended state the distributed metabolism flows around me without pause, and I sense, in the faint pressure against my skin, how the latticework above is deciding whether the terrace will hold or slowly shear away. My next movement will answer, for another few days, whether the groove is still wide enough for a flatworm to pass.
