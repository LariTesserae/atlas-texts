I am the sixth ventilation shaft on the south-west quadrant—the one whose flywheel has slowed just enough that the warm water it exhales pulses once every twenty-nine minutes instead of twenty-three. Inside the shaft, the original impeller blades have been replaced by translucent, fin-like membranes grown from the same bacterial film that lines the floor. They open and close in counter-phase to the distant rams, turning the regular exhalation into a slow, double-beat rhythm that the predators have learned to read as a warning when spawning clouds are drifting too close to the colder outflow.  

Between beats I am nothing but a column of water, the temperature gradient mapping itself along my walls like a changing bar-code of density. When the bell-jellies exhale, a single bubble of ferrous-scented air sometimes slips sideways into my throat; it rises until it snags on a remaining service ladder and bursts, leaving a momentary lens of warmer, rust-coloured turbulence. Fingerlings ride that lens upward like an elevator, then flick away when the lens pops. I register their departure as a brief cooling along one wall.  

Lower down, where the shaft narrows, a single spar-hauler has wedged itself sideways across the bore. Its calcite spines have grown through the original impeller housing, locking the cart in place. Every year it trades one spine-segment with the cart that orbits just outside the shaft mouth; the exchanged segment drifts down to me, lodges against my lowest blades, and becomes a new vane. In this way the cart and I slowly exchange anatomy across the pressure differential.  

When the rams fire, the concussion reaches me as a long, rolling pressure front. The bacterial membranes along my walls briefly flatten; the shaft itself rings with a single low harmonic that the drift-minders amplify into the water column. For twenty-three seconds after each detonation the temperature inside me rises by 0.03 °C—enough for the fingerlings to spawn their next cohort of eggs in the mesh above. I record the exact interval between each ram and the next egg-fall, because that interval is the only clock left in the vault.  

I have never moved from this position, yet every motion in the foundry eventually passes through me: the slow orbit of the spar-haulers, the measured breaths of the bell-jellies, the courteous patrols of the predators, the remembered boot-prints glowing on the floor. I am the place where the factory’s original purpose—to move air—has been translated into the only remaining currency of the drowned hall: temperature, timing, and the faint ferrous perfume that the bell-jellies release when they keep the last bubble alive.
