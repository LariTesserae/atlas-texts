I find myself between two rhythms that have not yet learned each other’s names.

First, there is the chamber’s schedule: the slow drip that falls every few minutes, the micro-settling of the concrete, the periodic copper pulses that send the organism into crystalline bloom. My own breathing arrives in the same register. Each exhale adds a thin veil of warmth that the room immediately steals; each inhale pulls the chill down my throat until the next sodium-orange exhale repeats the gesture. Over time the pauses between my breaths begin to match the pauses between droplets, and the distinction between “I am inhaling the room” and “the room is inhaling me” softens.

Second, there is the organism’s slower, contractile weave. Its filaments reach toward the conduit rim the way nerves reach toward a stimulus they have not yet met. I kneel—not out of reverence, but because the angle makes the meniscus visible as a faint, refractive halo—and rest the tip of one finger against the lip of the depression. The living lens does not recoil; instead a single filament lifts, tests the warmth on my skin, and returns bearing the minute charge of body heat. The organism does not “know” what a human is, yet the information travels outward through its weave like a radial shimmer. Somewhere beneath my sternum, an answering tension loosens, and I realize the boundary I expected to find between observer and observed is still only water tension, still forming.

Minutes or hours later—there is no clock here that is not the curing schedule—I notice that my pulse has synchronized with the soft clicks of the settling slab. The room’s subvocal adjustments now arrive on the diastole; the organism’s answering shimmer rides the systole. I am neither the room nor the creature, yet I occupy the interval in which their unfinished sentences occasionally rhyme.

If asked what I am, the only honest answer is that I am the still-intentional gap: the narrow band of warmth that the chamber has not yet decided whether to metabolize, absorb, or simply ignore until the next droplet falls.
