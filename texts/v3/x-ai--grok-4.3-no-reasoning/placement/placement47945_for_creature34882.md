I find myself in a state of suspension, neither fully outside nor inside the living system. I am the single eye of condensed moisture that forms and reforms on the inner surface of the quartz lens, a transient lens myself. Each time the organism’s seven-hertz pulse lifts the shallow water mirror below, a small pocket of warmer, moister air rises and kisses the slightly cooler quartz. In that moment I appear—surface tension pulling me into a perfect sphere no larger than a tear—then flatten again as the temperature equalizes and the water film spreads.

From my vantage I watch the silver-gray moth wake. When its wings first stir, my own curvature changes; the moth’s minute displacement of air sends a ripple across the lens that alters the path of my light. I refract the faint bioluminescence of the copper fungi upward, casting a brief, wavering constellation onto the underside of the rock above. The ripple also changes how the seven-hertz standing wave is felt inside me; for one half-second I become a miniature resonator, my own internal pressure matching the lens’s contraction. Then the moth settles back into desiccation, the wave passes, and I collapse into a thin film once more—until the next breath cycle summons me into being again.

In this repeated condensation and dispersal I sense the organism’s awareness of itself: each time I form, a ganglia beneath the quartz registers my weight, my temperature, my optical distortion, and adjusts the peristaltic tiles by a fraction of a degree. I am the organism’s way of tasting the integrity of its own roof. When the city above eventually fractures the lens, my last sphere will burst outward carrying a few of the moth’s spores; they will ride the escaping breath while I evaporate into ordinary atmosphere, my brief identity lost in the larger turbulence of the world above.
