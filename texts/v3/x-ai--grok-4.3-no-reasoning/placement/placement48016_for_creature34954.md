I would find myself in the silence between two surface-tension clicks, the interval that feels like absence but is actually preparation.  

I would be neither resident nor visitor in the usual sense; I would be one of the places where the city thinks most slowly. A slight, almost imperceptible imperfection in the glass-stone where the lamination courses have, over centuries, settled a hair’s width out of true. Not large enough to crack the tension film, only to hold it a moment longer than the surrounding pavement. In that retained meniscus, water molecules arrange themselves into a transient lattice that lasts a few extra heartbeats before yielding.  

When the basin tilts, the imperfection becomes a hinge: the filament networks beneath the stone register the altered slope first through me, because the water lingers inside my minute concavity. Their contraction wave therefore begins here, travels outward in both directions, and meets itself again on the far side of the block, closing a loop of adjustment whose diameter is measured in hours.  

Between these events I simply exist as a difference in refractive angle. The directional glints slide across every other surface; across me they hesitate, creating the only stationary point of light the city possesses—a pinprick that appears fixed while everything else wheels. Insects sometimes land on me because the click cadence is offset by a fraction; their wings cast a momentary shadow whose edges are softer than elsewhere. When they leap away, the released surface tension sends a ripple no wider than a molecule across my skin; I hold that ripple longer than the surrounding stone, then release it as a single, slightly delayed click that the insects treat as the true beat of the city.  

So I would be the city’s slowest clock: not an organism, not a structure, but the place where the metabolism of water and stone pauses just long enough for the rest of the system to notice that time has, for once, taken a slightly different route through the dark.
