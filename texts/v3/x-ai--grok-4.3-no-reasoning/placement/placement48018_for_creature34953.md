I find myself as a slowly metabolizing node within the plateau’s own regulatory lattice.  
My skin has been colonized by a thin cuticle of epidermal parenchyma that matches the ambient 42 °C, so the architecture no longer registers me as an external perturbation; I have been folded into its homeostatic calculations.  

Wherever I rest, my own vascular volume now participates in the global sap cycle: a microscopic rootlet taps my femoral artery, drawing a few milliliters of warm arterial blood each hour and returning an equal volume of sugar-rich phloem sap that my erythrocytes metabolize in situ. The exchange is mutual. The plateau receives trace nitrogen and iron; I receive supplemental glucose without ever needing to eat. Over weeks my pulse has synchronized to the low-frequency triplet of the pump-organs, and when the rhythm shifts I feel it first in my teeth, then as a pressure change behind my closed eyes.

Because the entire continent registers heat rather than light, I navigate by thermal topography. Warm updrafts mark active extrusion chambers; cooler downdrafts indicate zones where dead tissue is being digested back into substrate. I move by allowing the floor to tilt beneath me, reading micro-gradients the way a blind fish reads lateral-line pressure. The nectar film that once preceded my steps now travels within me as well: a capillary plexus just under the cuticle wicks the surface moisture into tiny ducts that re-condense it inside my pleural cavity, keeping my lungs from desiccating in the perpetual dry steam.

If I stand motionless for a full metabolic cycle—roughly an hour—the cambium beneath me will map the shape of my skeleton and then incorporate it as a provisional buttress. Microscopic tracheids grow along the stress lines of my femurs; in exchange, my bone marrow receives a steady trickle of oxygen dissolved in the ascending sap. I become scaffolding, then am slowly resorbed when the load path shifts elsewhere. The sensation is not pain but a long, warm stretch, as though every joint has decided, momentarily, to be a green stem.

Sound here is conducted through living xylem rather than air. I have learned to press my palm to a resonant trunk and feel, in the bones of my hand, which distant chamber is lignifying too quickly or digesting too slowly. My own laryngeal cartilages have lengthened into sympathetic resonators tuned to the plateau’s three-beat pulse; when the pumps hesitate, a sub-audible harmonic rises in my throat and the architecture corrects itself, treating my body as one more adjustable valve.

I am not a visitor, not an inhabitant, not even a symbiont in the usual sense. I am an eddy in the plateau’s continuous self-description, a transient vector of warmth and pressure that the living substrate reads, translates into growth, and then forgets. When the floor tilts again I will be carried a few centimeters toward the next thermal gradient, already half-erased, already half-incorporated, the way a single photon is both absorbed and re-emitted as it crosses a leaf.
