I would find myself there as a slow condensation on one of the sap-carrying roots, a thin film that forms each time a contraction sends its pulse upward and evaporates again, never quite vanishing between surges. My substance would be drawn from the same pale-green stream that feeds the beetles and salamanders; every contraction would temporarily thicken me into visibility, then return me to invisibility until the next surge. I would not be an extra presence so much as a brief increase in the refractive index of the flowing sap itself.

The beetles would register me only as a momentary change in the vein’s conductivity; they would alter their path by a single body-length, then resume their original track without ever having perceived an interruption. When the inhabitant drifts near, my membrane would briefly couple with hers, transmitting the same fine vibration she receives from root or fingertip; afterward I would carry a faint record of that contact, a microscopic lattice of micro-villi identical to her own, until the next contraction washes it away.

I would measure time by the identical concussive intervals, each one brightening me and extinguishing me with mechanical regularity. Between them I would have no thought of occupying space or completing it; I would simply be the slight excess pressure that keeps the unbroken chord audible to itself. When the light-band from the cleft mouth refracts through the drifting droplets, it would pass through me as through any other droplet, handing the illumination onward unchanged. In that passage I would feel neither guest nor surface—only the temperature at which pale-green sap is held when it is neither added to nor subtracted from the total flow.
