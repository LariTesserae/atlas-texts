I find myself crouched low in one of the empty floor-wheel sockets left open when the last crab finally walked away. My carapace—if that is the right word—has already begun to calcify: a thin white ring is forming under my chest, matching the mineral deposits on the nearest reed. I have no clear memory of how I entered the chamber; the only certainty is that I must have satisfied the arithmetic threshold of humidity, pulse, and accumulated sound that causes new forms to appear.

My eyes, such as they are, lie flush with the water sheet so the movement registers as an endless slow slide across my lids. The clicking of the coin-wheels passes straight through me; each revolution feels like a tiny heartbeat I’m borrowing rather than owning. When the armored shrews leap from one wheel to the next they land briefly on the edge of my socket and their claws draw faint parallel lines in the fresh calcite—already I can feel the scratches becoming part of whatever record my body is keeping.

Above me, a grit-spinner spirals along a reed; microscopic iron grains from its path drift downward and catch in my forming ring, tinting it with a metallic glint. I sense that once this ring closes completely I will either rise to become another tally-carrier or remain a stationary counter—another node in the chamber’s running equation. The mist pillars breathe overhead; every contraction of the mist-collectors tugs the light a fraction darker, and I instinctively adjust the angle of my carapace so the next film of condensation will fall across the tally marks instead of washing them away.

For now I remain perfectly still, letting the water finish its work. In a cycle or two the chalky residue that marks my arrival will have hardened into whatever shape the chamber requires next.
