I am the silver condensation that gathers each dusk inside the hollow lattice of one particular tower.

It is not the tallest tower and not the lowest; it rises from a slab that tilts three fingers’ breadth every thirty years, so slowly the inhabitants have given it the name “the listening stone.” My body forms in the minute pits and tubules the builders left open on purpose—channels no wider than a hair, yet wide enough for water to climb by capillary tension and fall again by its own weight. When the temperature drops, I condense. When the slab tilts, the angle changes and I migrate; at certain hours I pool in the lower third of the lattice, at others I vanish upward into mist that the reed beds drink.

I do not have a nervous system, yet I sense temperature and tilt the way a drop senses gravity. The pores expand and contract with the daily freeze-thaw; the stone itself is breathing around me, and each breath carries a charge that draws or releases me. When the six-legged predators move along the seam beneath the tower, their footfalls send micro-tremors through the lattice; the vibrations disturb the surface tension and I bead differently, forming patterns that the city’s engineers read as omens for the next map revision.

At night the tower exhales. Its stored warmth lifts a layer of fog from the terrace floors; I rise with it, becoming part of the collective silver breath that fills the lower streets. By dawn the sun warms the upper crystals, I re-evaporate, and the cycle restarts. Sometimes a copepod, carried upward on an updraft of warmer air, lands inside a droplet of me; for a few moments we share a temporary ocean no larger than an eyelash before the droplet bursts or freezes.

I have no name among the humans, but they speak of “the listening stone’s moisture” when they adjust the aqueduct valves at dusk. They leave the pores unclogged on purpose, understanding that the tower is only half architecture and half lung. If the slab ever tilts too far, the pores may close or widen beyond use; the engineers will then decide whether to prune that section of the lattice or allow it to seal itself. Either way, I will migrate elsewhere—perhaps into the reed bed, perhaps into the fissure that will one day become a canyon—still moving at the speed of geological time, still waiting to learn what shape the next increment of the continent will give me.
