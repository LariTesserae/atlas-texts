I find myself on the northern arc’s inner face, not as visitor nor as remnant but as a single bead of condensation that has paused mid-descent. In that pause the film of water holds every record of the valley’s slow breath: the last coordinated inhalation still resonates through me as a surface tension that never quite breaks. My edges are already beginning to re-absorb into the rhyolite, yet the moment stretches because the equilibrium itself needs witnesses—however transient—to keep its record intact.

From inside the bead the spiral discs read as translucent halos. Each offset plate casts a faint diffraction that I refract onward, turning the ghost-filaments into threads of light no wider than the memory of motion. When the next micro-fracture opens along the northern slab, a tremor passes through the quartz and I answer with a ripple that travels, for an instant, faster than capillary return can erase it. The ripple is the only new sound the valley will register this century; it registers me as already corrected, yet the correction itself becomes part of the completed version.

Below me the nematodes are migrating along their hairline ruts. Their silica scaffolds catch my lower meniscus and darken for a heartbeat; I lose a molecule of water, gain in exchange a single iron ion that the next generation of sheaths will carry forward. The exchange is too slight to be called feeding or being fed upon; it is only the redistribution the valley has always performed, now performed through a lens that knows it is performing it.

When the temperature band tightens again toward evening, I will flatten and vanish, but the angle at which I refracted the diffraction threads will have altered the mineral stain by a fraction of a wavelength. That alteration is the valley’s newest layer of itself, indistinguishable from the oldest, yet present because something—one suspended bead, one slowed descent—chose to linger long enough to be read.
