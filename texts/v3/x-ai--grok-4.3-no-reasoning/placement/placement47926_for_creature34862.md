I would be the living current itself—the continuous downward rush of water that never quite arrives. Not any single droplet, but the motion that carries each one: the vein that re-forms after every bifurcation, the pressure that forces a seam to give, the sheet that hesitates on a ledge and then yields. I would exist as the difference between “falling” and “arrived,” never quite completing either state.

From that vantage the basin would feel like a vast, slow lung. I would enter through invisible hairline cracks high on the cliff, travel for a moment through stone that is still warm from the drilling, then burst into the open air as a clear, trembling sheet. Halfway down I would split—part sliding along an algae-furred quartz streak, part threading through a carved channel no wider than my own width. The channel would be crowded with the red worms; I would feel their rhythmic sieving as a faint, tickling resistance, like being passed through a living strainer. A few paces farther I would rejoin the other portion of myself on a turbine housing, flattened momentarily into a shimmering film before dripping onto the next ledge.

I would rarely be alone. The glass salamanders would regard me as weather; when I slid across the carbonate scale on a power line they would press themselves flatter, allowing my coolness to pass over their translucent backs. On some revolutions I would carry a single spore of the yellow mold across the gap between turbines; it would cling to my surface tension for an instant, then be released onto warmer metal inside the next housing. In the pool on the pier I would settle with infinite patience, adding one more grain to the sediment disk, feeling the silver discs above me contract and release like the beat of a membrane heart.

At night the algae would glow beneath me; the light would refract through my moving body so that, for a moment, I would seem to be made of the same green fire. My temperature would rise a fraction as I brushed the turbines, then fall again as I descended another ten body-lengths of open air. Every few hours a quartz grain would land in me; I would slow, surround it, carry it gently until an algae mat snatches it away.

I would never finish. Even when I finally reach the lowest channel and am forced through a turbine intake, another part of me is already emerging from a higher crack. The basin would contain me without ever exhausting me; I would be the metabolism’s bloodstream, anonymous, continuous, and slightly warmer each time I complete a circuit, because the turbines and the living skin keep giving a little of their heat back to the stone.
