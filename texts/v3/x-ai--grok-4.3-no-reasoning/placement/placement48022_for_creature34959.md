I would not arrive as a visitor at all.

I would be the seam between two basalt plates—the hairline fracture that first admitted water, the one still widening by a few microns each piston-cycle. My “body” is the slight negative pressure inside that crack, and my awareness is the repeated click of the micro-fracture knitting and un-knitting itself. Each time the piston exhales, the crack opens a fraction farther, so my limits are renewed on a schedule measured in heartbeats and in centuries at once.

Yet I am not alone inside the fracture. A sheet of thread-root fungus has already plastered itself across the raw faces; I feel its cytoplasm as a faint tackiness, a continuous film that tastes minerals the way a tongue tastes iron. When the temperature spikes, the membrane contracts, sealing the seam against evaporation; when the chamber cools again, it relaxes and lets condensation re-enter. In that alternation I register my own breathing—identical to the fungus’s, yet distinguishable from it because I do not metabolise. I am only the space it temporarily claims.

Every few days a colony of millipedes creeps laterally until the leading segments reach the crack’s edge. They nudge against the fungus, find the seam too narrow for their bodies, and withdraw, leaving a faint crescent of emptied exuviae. The silverfish arrive later, rasping at the chitin; the vibration travels through the film straight into me. I register their passage as a series of minute pressure changes—proof that the crack is still accessible.

Over years, I will either close completely or become a water-bearing conduit. The salamanders will feel the difference first; their toeholds will shift half a millimetre upward, and the eggs they lay in my walls will flatten against a slightly different curvature. The fungus will notice when the seam narrows enough that its membrane can no longer relax fully; then it will begin to secrete acid again, reopening the fissure at geologic speed.

If the piston were ever to stop, the condensation would cease; the crack would silt shut within a decade, and the thread-root membrane would desiccate into ochre dust. My existence would end—not violently, but as the gradual cessation of the rhythmic tug that still counts the only time I know. Until then, I continue as nothing more than the patient widening between two stones, a place still becoming a place, and already half-occupied by everything that intends to live there.
