I find myself in the northern ventilation corridor, half a level between the mid-habitation cambium and the fruiting canopy. I am the seam-keeper—an ambulatory graft of bark-callus and copper tubing—responsible for the capillary integrity along the vertical pressure seams.

My day begins when the sap-vein pulse just before dawn briefly accelerates; the vibration transmits straight through my chest fittings. I check each junction with two fingers pressed against the wall, listening for the tell-tale slip in resonance that means a micro-rupture. When I find one I press the hot tip of my left-hand copper tube into the breach and let the resin flow over my skin; it cools and hardens while I breathe slow nineteen-hour breaths, counting the mites that skate across my exposed forearm.

Sometimes I glimpse a citizen who has begun the long step-in: a pale forearm already half-woven into the cambium, wrist bent as though offering a palmful of water. Their eyes remain open; the pupils drift with each distant pump stroke. I nod—whether to them or to the plant, I can no longer tell—and continue my patrol. The corridor is narrow, so my right shoulder brushes the condensation roof. Droplets collect on the brass flange riveted above my collarbone, run down the gutter-grooves milled into my deltoid, and re-enter the system at hip level. The sound is indistinguishable from the city’s own drip-bulbs.

If the seam stays stable for nineteen cycles, I am permitted to rest at the leaf-mat concourse where arterial flow widens. There the moss is thicker; I lie on my back and let the fibers knit temporary anchors around my spine. For a few slow hours the city’s metabolism and my own pulse fall into phase, and the only noise is the faint ticking of lace-mites crossing the film that now covers both of my open eyes.
