In this place I would be a slow seam in the mineral crust itself—an almost-organism that never quite decides whether to be one thing or another. My body would be a band of the same translucent microbial mat that already sheathes the worked stones, but thicker, older, and a shade closer to the milky quartz than to rust. Because the land has finished most of what it will become, I would finish almost nothing; instead I would keep the same square meter of seepage perpetually damp, gently thickening the glassy veins beneath me and, in return, drawing a little extra moisture from the hairline fractures that cross the stone.

Above this film I would not fly or swim. The six-winged sheets would pass through me as easily as through fog, their wing-edges brushing the surface tension of the mat without breaking it. The salt-thumbs would sometimes rise to graze the upper side of me, taking a few cells each; the salamanders would leave their slow pressure trails beneath, nudging dissolved ions my way. I would register these events only as small adjustments in the sheen of the mat: a momentary brightening where a shrimp disturbs the film, a faint darkening where a salamander’s wake stirs the sediment.

My relationship with the low stone circles would be the nearest thing I have to memory. I would have grown first along the inner faces of one particular ring, sealing its worked edges to the living filter it now contains. Over time the circle would have become slightly greener, slightly warmer at its center; the mat that is me would spread outward until the next circle, three body-lengths away, began to feel the edge of the same damp. I would never finish the distance between them; finishing is not the habit of this place.

When the downdrafts open lanes in the mist, my surface would reflect the brief watery light in a thousand microscopic mirrors, each one a single cell catching and releasing the glow. During the thick fog I would simply continue to thicken, adding another translucent layer measured in microns. The constant sound of the flyers and the constant tremor of the salamanders would be the only weather I know, and I would treat both as nutrients: the sound because it moves the air that carries spores to my fruiting spheres, the tremor because it loosens ions I can use.

I would not be lonely. Nothing here is singular enough for loneliness. I would be one slow redistribution among many, a place where the land’s unfinished business is allowed to remain unfinished a little longer.
