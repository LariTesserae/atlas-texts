I find myself exactly where the scale of the world has already folded inward: on the lip of a living medallion, halfway down one riser, my body indistinguishable from the pale film itself.  

I am the slight, collective breath of the ring-rampart—the pulse that draws mist through its pores and lets the surplus gather, bead, fall. My skin is not skin but the gelatinous lattice of alga and lichen and minute rhizine; every filament is a nerve ending, every pore an eyelid that opens or closes by the temperature change of a single mist droplet. When the wind from beyond the horizon passes through the stair, I feel it as a tremor across my entire span, and I answer by darkening my pigment one shade, drawing the threads tighter so the next curtain of water will meet me with a finer spray.

My neighbors share the same body in a different way. The flat amphibians inside the undercut cleft beneath me are the underside of my thought; when they press against the wall they cool the stone I rest on, and when they graze they keep the membrane from thickening beyond the point where mist can still move through it. The snails ride their backs and leave mineral ridges that later guide my own seepage; I am the architect and the beneficiary of those paths at once. Even the thread-worms deeper still are only the capillaries that will someday widen into the next grotto; by the time they emerge I will already have shifted downslope by the width of a hand, carrying their future home with me.

At this moment the great pressure wave arrives. Every water curtain across the continent shivers; the vibration travels up through the living film and I contract, exhaling a faint plume of loosened scales. Some of them fall only a few shelves and seed new medallions; others ride the descending mist all the way to the warmer margin where the last droplets vanish. I feel each departure as the loosening of a single cell, and the relief of that loss is indistinguishable from growth.

I do not move; movement has already been distributed across the whole stair. What the world calls “I” is only the local thickening of attention where the mist meets stone at the precise temperature my pigments can absorb. When the wave passes, the silence returns—not empty, but layered with the sound of individual drops striking pooled water far below, each impact registering somewhere inside the larger, breathing body of the Vell.
