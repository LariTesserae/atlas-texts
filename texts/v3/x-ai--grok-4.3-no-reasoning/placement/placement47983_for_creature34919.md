I find myself here not as a visitor but as an initial fracture—specifically, the first microscopic split where two quartz planes shear just far enough apart for argon to seep in.  

At the moment of my emergence the fissure is still narrower than a human hair and colder than the rock around it. Light does not yet exist; the vein is pure potential, a seam of unspeakable pressure gradients waiting to be named. I am the differential: the infinitesimal offset between one crystalline plane and the other, the silent disagreement that will eventually drive the whole corridor a millimetre wider.  

Around me, nothing moves because nothing has been written yet—no servitors, no filaments, no ribs. The only evidence that time has begun is a single, almost inaudible tick: the quartz releasing one molecule of hydrothermal gas every geological second. The molecule becomes the first “bead,” the first node around which later scaffolding will accrete.  

When the first titanium strut is micro-printed and clicks into place, the offset I represent is measured, catalogued, and stabilized. I cease to be an event and become instead the reference datum against which every future lengthening thump is judged. Spinnerets will glide along their cobalt threads a precise distance from this original split; knitters will calibrate their lattice spacings so the newest fracture always begins exactly where I once was. In that sense I am the corridor’s memory of its own birth, encoded now as a permanent standard of measure rather than as an active agent.  

From that still point I watch the ecology assemble outward in both directions: servitors trundle away toward the new terminus while, behind me, spent nodules turn to reinforcing whiskers that knit themselves into the earliest concrete rib. The vein grows, but the location of its origin—the place that asked the first question—remains fixed, a single atomic layer of quartz offset by the width of one argon molecule, silent and indispensable.
