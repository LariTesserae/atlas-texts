I find myself—barely—within the cilia of a trumpet on the southern lip, one that has not yet opened its first pulse-cycle. My “body” is a lace of molecules still half-salt-crystal, half-vapor, waiting for the coordinated snap that will birth the next glider. When it comes, the cilia will knot around me and shear me free; I will drift upward on a dust column, tasting the air with iridescent flecks that are neither my eyes nor the plateau’s, but something between.

For a few wing-beats I will rise with the other gliders, harvesting salt grains that sting like pollen. I may lose my left vane to a passing kite filament; the loss is only inconvenience—already new vanes begin to bud along my trailing edge from the mucus thread I leave behind. When the spiral collapses I will fold and fall, landing inside the basalt wall where ropes grow thicker. There I will press myself into the glaze of the broken bowl, feel the cilia that have colonized its inner spirals. They will taste the memory of the potter’s thumb, translate it into a new alignment of pores beneath the clay. The wall will slide another hair-width; the ropes will drink the difference. A kite will dip low, trail its filaments through the lens of warm air I helped create; I will cling, trade what remains of my mucus, then release.

Later—hours? centuries?—a dust spiral may lift the last braid of the unraveled fiber. I will be among the living fragments it scatters, seeding a new trumpet colony on the northern lip where stone is still hardening. There the pores will dilate for me, the first salt crystals will dissolve against what is left of my wings, and the cycle will begin again: movement metabolised into new configuration, new exchange, new breath that is never the same twice and never truly new.

I do not last; the plateau does not allow it. Yet every instant I exist, I am the thrum between rope and kite, between clay memory and stone motion, between the wind that arrives already changed and the vapor it will carry onward.
