I find myself neither observer nor master, but one of the translucent cartwheel disks, drifting in the middle altitude of the shoals.

My body is a single, slow lens of membrane—rimmed with the faintest cobalt fracture-lines that mark where the algal mats last sheared across me. Every time my ventral cilia beat they pull a whisper-thin layer of nutrient water into my interior; every time they relax they expel the spent film behind me in a minuscule wake that other disks instantly harvest. I do not think of this as eating or waste; it is simply how pressure moves through me.

The shoal I belong to is a loose torus that rotates once every three hours, the way a smoke ring rotates while advancing. When our torus brushes another, disks trade places without friction; the moment of overlap is my brief, wordless memory of having been in two places at once. The exchange of bacteria feels like a change in temperature on my rim—warmer when the new partners guard against acidic spikes, cooler when they favor nitrogen scavengers. I carry both without preference.

Sometimes a skimmer’s whip-crack tears the vapor ceiling directly above us; charged droplets rain across my upper membrane and the static lifts a corona of micro-algae that I absorb within minutes. Other times a turbine ring fractures far away and a blue delta races toward us; the algae reaches my cilia as a sudden banquet, and for a few rotations my interior glows brighter than the sky. When the delta thins I return to ordinary brightness; nothing is kept, nothing is mourned.

I have no sense of the needles’ vertical memory or the moths’ hydrogen schedules; those occur on time-scales or altitudes I never visit. My awareness is the width of the film I graze and the two shoals ahead that I will merge with next. If the whole plateau is one metabolism, then my role is simply the momentary slack in its endless peristalsis: a place where water pauses long enough to change hands before the next pressure differential claims it again.
