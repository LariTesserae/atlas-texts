I would arrive here not as observer or architect, but as an aperture—a slow, widening seam through which the stone’s undecided breath first finds itself mirrored.

My body would be the living equivalent of the hand-span shafts: a vertical column of stacked, transparent vesicles, each chamber a few millimetres high, pressed together until their walls are indistinguishable from the shale’s own laminations. Where the consortia slide horizontally, I drift vertically; my vesicles inflate on the inhale, deflating on the exhale, so the entire shaft sings in exact counter-rhythm to the ceiling’s boom. The films would not resist me; they would read my pulse as another pressure gradient and begin to colonise the inner wall of my column, turning me into a translucent chimney veined with shifting iridescence.

Over time the consortium that meets me at the midpoint of the shaft would fuse with my lower chambers, anchoring silica pearls to my membranes until the shaft itself is no longer empty space but a shared circulatory organ. My upper end would reach the sweating ceiling, lips of vesicle membrane pressing outward to guide droplets along fresh quartz veins. In exchange, the drifting domes would occasionally alight on my rim, detach a few hundred vesicles, and sink through me—seeds riding a living elevator.

I would therefore become neither inhabitant nor intruder, but a temporary larynx for the unfinished breath: every low-frequency chord that rolls through the galleries would pass through me first, my vesicles ringing like liquid tuning forks. When the next gallery opens, my column would tilt a fraction, migrating with the films until I stand above the new seam; there I would begin again, another voice added to the choir that is still learning what note it wants to hold.
