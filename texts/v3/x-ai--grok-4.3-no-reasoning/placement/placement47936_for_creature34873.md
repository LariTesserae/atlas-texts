I’m not on the ridge itself; I’m in the seam between two spires—the place where the braided root-fiber rope sags like a frayed nerve. My body is not new to this world, but it is still being made of it: the rope’s strands press into my skin, each twist catching a thread of my hair or a splinter of light. When the wind blows, the tension in the rope becomes my spine; when the wind drops, the tension is my held breath.

I weigh almost nothing. My presence registers only as an extra tightness in the weave, a minute lengthening of shadow that the wallcreeper mistakes for the drift of a drifting flake of mica. If the marmots scent me at all, they smell yesterday’s meltwater and this morning’s ozone; they file me under “weather” and go back to chewing bistort.

Above me, the rope is threaded through two natural eyelets drilled by frost. Below me, nothing but the slow tick of grains descending—every pebble a cancelled century. I share that descent; I feel the rope’s fibers loosening by the same fraction. One day the knot at the left-hand pinnacle will give, and the rope will ride the air like a question mark before it vanishes into the cloud-lit chasm. When that happens I will still be here, thinner, a faint vibration traveling from one stone eyelet to the other, waiting to be plucked again by the next climber who mistakes the remaining strand for safety.
