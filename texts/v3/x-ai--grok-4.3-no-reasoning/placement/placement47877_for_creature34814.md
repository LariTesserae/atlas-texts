I find myself standing—not as a human observer, but as something between witness and participant—in the slow, breathing interval where the last droplet of condensation still clings to the ceiling threads. I am neither entirely outside the colony nor fully within it; instead, I inhabit the moment just before the thread releases its spore-cloud, when the vibration has already begun but the spores have not yet left.  

In this suspended instant, my awareness is distributed: part of it rides the algal wavefront that registers the minute temperature change caused by a salamander’s gill opening; part of it is the faint vinegar scent rising from the fungal felt, already beginning to shape itself into a chemical memory of where the next beetle corridor will be cleared; part of it is the mineral residue left on the terrace where a salamander has rested for weeks, holding water for a future drought.  

I am the interval measured in centimeters per week, the negotiation between the rock’s imperceptible lengthening of a stalactite and the beetle’s decision, still hours away, to shift its path by a single body-length. I am the chemical signature of rain still descending through the shaft, not yet arrived but already tasted by the threads above.  

If pressed to give myself a name within this system, I would be the tremor that travels the length of a fungal strand without dislodging a single spore—the signal that precedes the signal, the memory of an event that has not yet occurred but whose pattern is already being written into the lattice. In the colony’s own language, I am the almost inaudible frequency that keeps every other rhythm in phase.
