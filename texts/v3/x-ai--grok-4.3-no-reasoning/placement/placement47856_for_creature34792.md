You would not have been born here.

You are instead the morning after the first winter squall that has, for the first time in three hundred counted leaf-falls, scraped the ridge with hail the size of thumb-knuckles. During the night the fig shuddered so violently that a single west-facing gallery tore loose and hung, three stories down, by only a braid of root-hairs no thicker than a child’s wrist. The three communities have gathered at the fracture, each keeping to their accustomed height: Limb-walkers on the living struts, Leaf-readers on swaying leaf-pulp hammocks just above, Drip-tenders dripping between them in sling-chairs. They are waiting for the decision no one wants to make.

The gallery must either be pruned back or coaxed back into the main crown. Pruning means a scar the width of a plaza; coaxing requires months of grafting and bracing while the city loses one of its widest, driest promenades. Every palm on every resident is already dark with the cold glow of squeezed beetles; the phosphorescent patches have the greenish pallor of bruises.

You are the single voice that can speak without allegiance. You are the stranger who arrived on the wind-borne seed-pod of a still taller fig two ridges away. The pod cracked open at dawn; inside, the cotyledons had died but the husk had fused itself to the air-root that tore away. You stepped out wearing the violet inner membrane of that husk like a caul, and the beetles did not fade when you touched them.

No one knows whether you are a person, a new caste, or simply an extemporized organ of the fig itself. You have no name yet, only the moisture-beaded scent of the seed membrane and a slow, leaf-veined heartbeat that everyone hears once, on first meeting, then never again.

At this moment you are neither standing on root nor hanging in mist; you are lying along the exact fracture line, palm pressed to the split cambium, listening. The city, for the first time, is listening with you.
