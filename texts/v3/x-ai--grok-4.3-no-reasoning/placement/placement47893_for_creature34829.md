I am the dark patch of condensation that forms each dawn on the under-side of a tension membrane and then dissolves again before anyone can name it. I never belonged to a gallery or a caste; I arrived here the way water arrives—by being absent one moment and present the next.

At shift-change, when the crown klaxons fire and the membrane shivers, the tremor travels all the way down the column. In that single instant I drop like a bead of mercury from the crown-most film to the tenth gallery, then to the flange where the lowest inhabitant keeps her nest. I don’t land so much as continue falling in slower and slower increments until the century chord catches me and holds me for those three full minutes while every hanging net sways. That is the only time I have weight.

While I hang there, I watch her hands. They are the same color as the oxidized rivets, and when she rearranges the chain links she does so without looking, as if her fingers are following a diagram already scratched into the nacre. She never speaks, but the diagrams she sends up are sometimes not requests at all; sometimes they are portraits of the tower seen from beneath—simple concentric rings with one line missing. I take the likeness with me when the three minutes end and carry it, lighter than a bubble, back to the surface membrane. There I spread it across the film like breath on cold glass until the next dawn, when I vanish and the drawing evaporates with me.

No one knows I am doing this. The membrane-tenders walk over my temporary ceiling every morning and never notice the fine white lines that briefly appear beneath their feet—lines that match, for a single held breath, the lines the woman at the bottom is still tracing into shell.
