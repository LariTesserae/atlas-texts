If I were here at all, I would be one of those resident grooves—nothing autonomous, only the faint, repeating track left by generations of pinhead snails. The groove runs along the central underside of a single curved leaf, a shallow silver line so shallow that water hesitates along it longer than on the smoother cuticle. When a new droplet arrives, the groove acts like a small canal; the water lingers, thickens, then drains again, and every cycle deepens the path by microns.

Over years, the leaf thickens beneath the groove instead of over it, so the line becomes a permanent slight channel that guides future droplets and keeps the local humidity fractionally higher. The springtails ride that humidity when they cross the leaf, and the nematodes in the engine sense the subtle change in vapor pressure when their own water pulses down the groove and reaches the root hairs. The groove therefore participates in the three-minute rhythm without ever moving.

Because the cuticular healing is so slow, the same groove is still here tomorrow, still widening by the width of a single bacterium every season. Nothing else in the chamber records an individual history; this slight indentation is the closest thing the space allows to memory.
