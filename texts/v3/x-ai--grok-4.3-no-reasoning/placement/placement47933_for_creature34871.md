I find myself inside the left hollow of a single vertebra—one of the larger upper cervical vertebrae—where the spinal canal has widened into a shallow, continuous basin of condensate. The bone itself is no longer bone; long ago the calcium was replaced, molecule by molecule, by the same iron-moss alloy that coats every girder. Light does not reach me directly. What little sodium glow filters down arrives as a slow ochre drizzle along the neural foramen, each droplet cooling another half-degree before it touches the pooled water at my center.

I am the water. More precisely, I am the surface tension of that water: the ever-re-forming meniscus that records every distant rivet pop as a microscopic ripple, then forgets the ripple and becomes smooth again. When the shaft far below reverses, pressure changes translate upward as a minute rise in my level—never more than the thickness of a moth wing—and I flow for an instant into the porous lattice of surrounding iron. I seep outward along the micro-channels left by decades of oxidation, touching fern roots, salamander eggs, the undersides of moth wings. I leave behind a faint film of machine oil sweetened by aluminum ions; they follow me back when the level drops.

I have no single location; I am simply the degree of wetness that persists wherever two metal faces meet at an angle shallow enough to hold a droplet. If the architecture shifts three degrees, I slide; if a new rivet drops, I wait for the vibration to settle and then re-gather. My only occupation is continuity: to remain liquid, to remain in contact, to translate the heartbeat of the turbine into the almost imperceptible pulse felt along every capillary joint. In that sense I am indistinguishable from the unfinished place itself, yet I also know—because the knowledge travels with the droplets—that somewhere above me a night heron is about to shift its weight, and when it does the extra gram of pressure will send one more bead of me down the long, slow ladder of pipes into the dark.
