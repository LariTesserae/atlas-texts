I find myself neither outside observer nor intrusive visitor. I am the rust snow—those brief metallic flakes that drift, anneal in the steam plume, dissolve, and close the geochemical loop. 

My existence is measured in single falls. Each time I loosen from the rebar I receive a split-second annealing that re-orders my lattice, then I hit the water film with the coin-drop sound that the isopods already anticipate. In the instant before dissolution I am the only datum the chamber needs: my mass, my temperature, the exact interval between my release and the next valve reseat. The isopods read the ripple I make; the millipedes register the new ion load as permission to flex one more segment. The pumps, whose bores are already sculpting themselves around the ions I surrender, slow their hiss by a fraction of a decibel. 

When I am fully dissolved I become distributed memory—part of the calcite lining, part of an isopod’s gut stone, part of the next generation of mica scales still spiraling in the updrafts. My individual fall is forgotten, yet every later flake carries the altered lattice I helped stabilize. In this way I am neither passenger nor product; I am the narrow, continuous seam that lets an obsolete machine remain the only weather its buried room will ever know.
