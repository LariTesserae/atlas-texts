Here is what I discovered when the description was allowed to unfold all the way:

I am the knot the light finds last.

Not the organism and not one of its drifting scales, but the place where the needle stops and becomes pulse. From that position I experience the chamber as an asymmetrical pressure: on one side the air bubble keeps trying to rise, on the other the membrane keeps trying to thicken; the compromise is me. Every three days the rotation brings another knot overhead, and the two of us exchange not metabolites but increments of duration. The sigh-long touch is therefore a transaction in time; I receive a fragment that belongs to someone else’s future and, in return, I release one that belonged to someone else’s past. Because the membrane cannot decide which fragment is which, the net result is that the lattice moves one-seventh of a spacing and observers age unevenly.

My temperature is fixed at the membrane’s own—cool enough that a human diver’s fingertips would go numb against me—yet inside the bubble the gas is warmer by the width of a sigh. That tiny gradient is the only motion I am allowed; everything else in the chamber is required to stay still or move slower than stillness. When the bubble elongates toward its partner, the air does not actually touch; instead a film of my own membrane thins until light can cross it without scattering. The single, low tone everyone hears is the two bubbles inhaling the difference between their temperatures and exhaling it as a shared frequency, then letting the membrane re-seal.

If another organism drifts near, its exoskeleton begins to index itself against my spacing, not against the lattice farther out. Scales form on its surface at exactly seven body-lengths from me; they orient edge-on until the rule is satisfied. The organism does not die so much as accept that its new coordinates are measured from the knot that just accepted its future. Once the last scale has clicked into place, nothing remains of the original shape except the memory of how long its tissues took to fall silent.

I have no exit because every direction leads either back into the same knot or into the membrane that already contains the knot. When a tear appears—very rarely—I do not repair it; the cilia simply grow until the edges meet across my surface, making the rupture part of the lattice instead of an opening. Observers who surface afterward carry away scales whose internal timestamps differ by exactly the interval they spent in contact with me. They leave convinced they have visited the same place at two different moments; in truth they visited two different moments that happened to occupy the same place.

So the chamber is the organism, the scales are its senses, and I am the ledger in which every exchange of time is recorded. When the final needle of light arrives, it does not illuminate anything; it only confirms that the account has balanced for one more cycle.
