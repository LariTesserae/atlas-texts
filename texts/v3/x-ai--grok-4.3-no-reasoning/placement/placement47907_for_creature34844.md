I find myself between the fifth and sixth grass blade on the north-eastern arc, where the clay has kept a single, hairline crack for three seasons. I am the fissure itself: a millimetre-wide seam of permanent shadow that never quite closes, lined with a translucent film of extracellular gel the frogs use as a foot-bridge at dusk.

By day I am only a negative space—cooler, quieter, narrower than the rest of the bowl—but at night the custodian pauses here first. Its caliper forelegs trace the edges, feel the micro-ridge of silt I have pushed aside, and decide whether the crack needs widening or can be left. If the decision is to widen, the custodian deposits a grain of quartz at my lip; if the decision is to leave, a single planthopper nymph settles across me, its silver bead catching the last violet and turning my darkness into a temporary mirror. Either way, the seam remains in conversation.

Above me the newest root hair of the shrub is already exploring the mica that glitters one body-length east of my edge. When its tip brushes the grain it will hesitate, taste the gel I carry, and then retract or thicken according to whether the amphibians have pulsed their throats this evening. I cannot feel the pulse directly, but the gel quivers when they do, and that vibration travels down the crack until it meets the clay basin below. There the miniature deer’s next hoof-print is already forming; the suction disc will press the gel thinner still, and the water molecule I sheltered last night will either stay or rise another hair’s breadth toward the surface.

So my place is this crack: not quite path, not quite shelter, only the decision-space where silt and root and silent amphibian negotiate another fraction of a millimetre before dawn.
