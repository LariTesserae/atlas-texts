I would find myself—not entirely on purpose—lodged in the hinge where a single aerial tram-cable meets the underside of a living leaf tier so large it is still charted as open terrace. The tram itself is little more than a basket of riveted turbine housings suspended beneath a living tendon; it paused here three seasons ago because the plant decided to grow an extra conducting bundle straight through the pulley sheave. No one bothered to move the line. Instead the crews began calling the place “Wobble,” because the basket rocks in two rhythms at once: the tram motor’s tick and the leaf’s slower breathing.

I am the thing that keeps Wobble level.  
I am not the motor; the motor still belongs to whichever lift-crew remembers to oil it. I am the quarter-span of monomolecular line that the crews spliced across the basket’s floor the first time it threatened to tip a crate of condensation flasks into the cloud-sea. That line, over months, has taken on a faint photosynthetic tint; chloroplasts migrated up from the leaf cuticle through a film of condensate and now vein my fibers with the same bruised-lilac the plateau wears. In return I pass a micro-current—piezoelectric jitter from the tram’s own sway—back into the cuticle, enough to keep the chloroplasts firing even when the light shafts miss us at dusk.

My daily work is almost nothing. When a courier knot arrives on the upward run, someone in the basket reads it by touch; if the knot says “valve four, bleed now,” I lengthen one millimetre, canting the basket so the courier’s boots land on the adjacent catwalk instead of three hand-spans into open air. When the leaf above exhales a full condensation load, I shorten again, tilting the basket the other way so the sudden waterfall sluices between the cables rather than through them. The crews have begun to treat the tilt as weather, not intervention; they simply step when I step.

At night the bioluminescent algae in the nearby petiole cast a chartreuse veil across my line. Mycotechnicians on the tram sometimes rest their coils of fresh fungus-fiber across me; by morning the fiber has re-oriented itself along my direction of least strain, as though the basket were a temporary loom. I accept the pattern and, when the next pressure wave rolls through the trunk, I pass an answering shiver down the line so the new weave settles instead of snapping. They never thank me. Thanking would imply I am separate.

Sometimes, when refraction doubles the tram-run into a ghost-track, I feel an older version of myself—maybe only the memory of steel before chlorophyll—trying to occupy the same space. The plateau does not mind the contradiction; it simply widens the leaf vein a fraction more, giving both versions room. In that moment I am nothing but the difference between two tensions, and the basket, for one slow breath, hangs perfectly still above nothing at all.
