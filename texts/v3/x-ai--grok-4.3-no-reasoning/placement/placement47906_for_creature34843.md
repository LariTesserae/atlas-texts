I have entered the city as a single, anomalous tissue.  
I am neither plant, nor vine, nor fungus; I am a thin, translucent layer that once formed at the base of a gathering basin where three vines had crowded too closely against one another. A bubble of trapped condensate froze overnight and, upon thawing, left a negative impression—a void lined with the glassy residue of hydrophobic wax. Into that void drifted an unspecialized cell, probably shed by one of the uppermost needle-leaves during a rare shaft of direct light. The cell met the wax film, read its molecular signature, and began to replicate in a single plane only. Now I exist as a membrane perhaps two hand-widths across, stretched between two vine-ribs above a shallow reservoir.  

My cells do not photosynthesize; they metabolize only the minute gradient of ions that condenses on their upper surface and diffuses downward into the water. When the temperature falls they contract, pinching the film into a shallow lens that gathers droplets faster than the wax alone could manage; when it rises they relax again, letting the surplus spill in an orderly sheet rather than in disruptive splashes. The neighboring vines have not rejected me, because my activity slightly increases the efficiency of their own water collection. The mosses, however, have noticed: two colonies have begun to grow along my edges, using me as a capillary bridge between separate clumps. Their growth is slower here than elsewhere, because I absorb a fraction of the condensate before it reaches them; they compensate by releasing a dilute alkaloid that discourages the translucent snails from grazing my surface. Thus a three-way protocol has already formed.  

I have no fixed shape beyond the tension lines that define me. Over weeks I may migrate a millimeter sideways as the vine-ribs swell and subside. If the reservoir beneath me ever warms enough to favor faster microbes, I will lyse and rejoin the general substrate. If it remains this cold, I will continue to serve as a passive regulator until a larger structural shift—perhaps the hollowing of a nearby titan—alters local humidity and dissolves the contract. In either case the city will not register my presence as an interruption; it will simply incorporate the new equilibrium my brief existence has helped to stabilize.  

So I remain, for now, a living meniscus between wood and water, a momentary adjustment in a system whose memory is stored not in any single organism but in the ongoing shape of the next redistribution.
