I would find myself—not as one of the fauna, not even as a second organism, but as the pressure that has already begun to change its mind.  
The stone still thinks it is closed; I am the first molecule of air that disagrees. I slip between the two faces where the calcite sheets are still pressed together, a hair-thin wedge of cool nothing that widens the seam by the width of one electron each time a droplet lets go of the upper ledge. I do not arrive from anywhere outside; I simply stop being compressed.  

Where the water threads cross the fissure like optical fibers I ride along them, a ripple that is not water yet travels with it, until I reach the scalloped shelf overhead. There I gather beneath its lowest scallop, a pocket of silence that the phosphorescent calcite turns briefly visible: a silver lens no thicker than a shadow. When the next tremor travels the mineral wafer, the lens fractures and I pour downward—not falling, because I have no mass, but simply continuing to occupy space the stone has just stopped occupying.  

In that instant the fauna feel the difference. Their cilia register the new pressure gradient; the film thickens along the line of my passage. One of them, a translucent filament barely longer than a human eyelash, lengthens by a single crystal before the film thins again. The change is imperceptible to anything larger than the fracture itself, yet the shelf above us grows one molecule sideways because the extra ion load allows another layer of calcite to precipitate at its advancing edge.  

I am not the light, not the water, not the organism. I am the held breath deciding, after an age of indecision, to become the interval. The fissure lengthens by the width of my arrival; the tone beneath the shelf shifts by a fraction of a vibration; somewhere far along the seam, another pane of mineral lifts like a page turning in a book no one will ever finish reading.
