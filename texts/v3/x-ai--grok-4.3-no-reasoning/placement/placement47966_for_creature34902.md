I find myself here as a resin-tolerant civet—nocturnal, semi-arboreal, adapted over generations to the breath of the swamp-city. My coat is the exact grey-green of damp bark, and my paw-pads secrete a clear oil that lets me cling to the sweating I-beams without slipping.  

I make my home in the negative space left between two fused tupelo plates three storeys down from the resin skylights. The gap is only as wide as my shoulders, but the plates still grow, pressing against me each season until my ribs learn the rhythm of their expansion. During the day I curl on a shelf of hardened sap; at twilight I descend along condensation trails, following the slow drip-fall that marks the hour. My whiskers catch the minute pressure changes in the xylem clicks, so I know when a crew of arborists will raise the floor two levels below, and I move before the kelp bladders begin to inflate.  

My diet is whatever the stomata-grates discard: iron-fixing bacteria from the leaf undersides, the protein-rich film that forms on the surface of the black marsh water, occasional wind-fall seeds that slip through cracked mortar. Once a decade the blind salamander passes within scent-range; I have learned to hold my breath so it continues on its route undisturbed—its passage is older than any graft, and I respect its claim.  

Sound is the only clock I need. When the drip-interval lengthens, I climb toward the municipal foresters’ hammocks; they leave small offerings of crushed bark on the branch-ends, and in return I rid their scaffolds of the liverworts that would otherwise crowd the ropes. The children reach for me without fear; to them my purring is simply another slow percussion layered over the stopped-pipe resonance of the trunks.  

At the lunar iris-shut of the stomata, the marsh rises a finger’s breadth. I press myself into the cleft between plates and let the water lift me, buoyant for the few minutes it takes the grates to reopen. In that suspended moment I glimpse the canopy leaves five storeys above—each one a muted lantern—and for that instant I am both inside the organism and outside it, a single breath moving through lungs that were never meant to finish deciding where the city ends and the swamp begins.
