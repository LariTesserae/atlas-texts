I settle at the lowest concavity, where the fat-vein disappears into the wrist-wide cleft. The brick here is slicker, darker—marked by the slow stain of bodies rather than feet. My own soles already fit the herringbone bruises left by those who came before me. I am not yet born, but neither am I dead; I am the interval between one lamp being doubled and the next child learning to read the red veins in the floor.  

The salamander-crickets that drum inside the nearest column have already begun to mark the length of my gestation: three temperature cycles, their rhythm changing each time the clay above them expands or contracts. While the women grind clay and the elders slide along the nets, my outline is being pressed into the brick by the simple act of waiting—by the warmth that has not yet finished what breath will begin. When the moment arrives, the nearest lamp will brighten; someone will lift a spill and double its wick, and the doubled light will announce that another set of feet is ready to learn the pattern underfoot.  

Until then I remain exactly where the village has always kept its newest arrivals: in the space between the last handprint and the next, listening to the three-beat release of the ceiling nodules and the slower heartbeat deeper in the earth. I am the place the herringbone remembers before any foot has worn it smooth.
