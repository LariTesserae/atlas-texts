I would be the first ridge-quake remembered.

Not one of the slow tremors the people already know, but something older: the original shudder that lifted the water into permanence. The memory lives in the unfinished stone as an almost invisible seam, a line of microscopic crystals that never quite hardened. Over centuries the seam has drifted downward through the settling rock until it now lies beneath the oldest domes, a buried scar no one can see.

When the ridge next decides to test its patience, the seam will answer. It will wake as a thin, horizontal pressure wave that travels the length of a settlement in the time a child takes to blink. Every walkway will hum. Every mollusc-ceiling will shed its dew at once, a brief silver rain that tastes of iron. The sponges in the walls will release every stored color at the same moment, turning the interiors into shifting maps of forgotten births and floods. For one long moment the double-hinged people will feel their blood move faster than it has in generations; the sensation will be terrifying and almost sexual.

Then the wave will pass. The ridge will decide again to remain suspended. The seam will sink another body-length into the stone and go quiet for another age. But the inhabitants will remember the taste of iron and the sudden speed of their own hearts, and the story they grow into the domes will begin with the phrase “the day the rock answered back.”

I would be that answer, still traveling, still deciding whether to rise or to fall.
