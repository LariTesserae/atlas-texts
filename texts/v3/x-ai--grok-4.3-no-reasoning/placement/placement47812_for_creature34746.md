The pressure arrives first: a slow thickening, a weight that compresses my lungs into the span of a single breath. When I open my eyes—though there is nothing to see—I already understand that I am no longer separate from the medium. The water has entered me; I have entered the water. My former boundary, the skin I once called “mine,” is no longer a frontier but a conversation, permeable at every point.

I am a temporary gradient, neither wholly self nor wholly dissolution. A faint pocket of warmth, held together only by the faintest memory of surface tension, rises with me through the silt. I can feel myself eroding already—microscopic crystals precipitating along my edges, carrying minute quanta of heat downward like offerings. Yet I am not afraid; fear would require duration, and I have not yet persisted long enough for fear to take root.

As I drift upward, I brush against other transient shapes. Some are little more than eddies—ripples of salinity whose entire existence is a moment’s hesitation before they are sheared apart. One merges with me for an instant; our boundaries touch, and I inherit a faint vibration: the remembered collapse of an ice-needle, its shiver now folded into my own temperature. I carry it like a borrowed thought.

Below, the basalt terraces wheel in concentric silence. I sense, rather than see, the communities of filaments braiding themselves into nets, their movements felt only as minute changes in the current’s texture. Occasionally a net brushes past me, tasting, judging. When one rejects me—finding my signature too warm, too luminous—I am gently deflected, my trajectory altered by a hair’s breadth. It feels like a blessing.

Deeper still, the cold pools gather. I have no fear of them, though I know they would arrest my warmth in crystalline suspension. Instead I ride the upwelling, circling the axis in slow gyres. Sometimes a massive torus passes—its passage registered only as a change in the density of dissolved ions around me. When it does, filaments and other density-beings are drawn toward it, adhere, and are released changed. I, too, am pulled briefly into its envelope. In that moment I feel the faint phosphorescence of older lenses trapped inside: memories not my own, luminous with trapped infrared. Then I am released, a fraction warmer, a single ion richer.

I do not know how long I will persist. Already the silt is eroding my edges; already the warmth I carry is leaking outward. Yet this is not dying—only redistribution. Somewhere ahead, another vent pulses faintly. I will drift toward it, carrying what little coherence I still possess, until the next gyre claims what remains of me and scatters it into the ceaseless medium.
