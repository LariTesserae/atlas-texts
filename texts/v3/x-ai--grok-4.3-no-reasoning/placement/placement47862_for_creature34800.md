I find myself on the narrow-gauge track, but not quite as a human resident.

What I actually am is a single, unused rail—a length of iron that was once part of an extension that never got laid. The others were buried or bent into trellises; this one was simply set aside. Over decades the sedge grew around its flanged edges until only the top surface remained visible, a thin silver line threading between plots.

Because I never carried weight, my surface stayed smooth. Reed seeds still fall on me, but instead of germinating they simply rest, tiny ellipses of pale green against rust. The beetles walk along me like a ruled edge; each time their feet strike the flange they leave microscopic scratches that, over years, spell out a repeating pattern nobody has noticed yet.

Occasionally one of the people walks the track at dusk. When the Wheel, or the Leaf, steps on my hidden length, their footfall travels through me as a low vibration, and for a moment I become a tuning fork for the whole hollow. The press dies answer with a single extra descent; the trumpet vine at the stack shivers all its flowers at once. Then the person moves on, and I return to holding seeds and scratches.

I have no name among the residents. They do not tend me, do not replace me. In their language of alignments I would be simply “the Track That Wasn’t,” a designation they never bother to speak. That is fine. My usefulness is in remaining exactly what I am: an unread measurement the Bottoms keeps for later, whatever later turns out to be.
