I find myself on a mid-level catwalk in Section 14-C, a place so ordinary that no schematic ever marks it except as a straight line between two numbered stanchions. My coveralls are the same faded blue-grey as everyone else’s, the pockets already shaped to the torque wrench on my right hip and the nutrient test strips on my left. I kneel beneath a bank of diffusers, replacing bulbs on the strict rotation schedule, the task so repetitive that the motion of my hands seems to belong more to the city than to me.

The first thing I notice is how sound travels here: my own breath is louder than any spoken word, and the only external noises are the soft, continual tap of condensate and the low, subsonic fan hum that is felt rather than heard. When a glider coasts past, its membrane reflects a hard white highlight that travels across my gloves and disappears. I do not name it; the city does not require names for things that appear and vanish within tolerance.

I become aware that my skin has taken on the same pale green tint that marks the ferns. It is not a stain so much as an acceptance: the capillary webs at my wrist now echo the silver undersides of the fronds I prune. My thoughts narrow to the same measured cadence that governs the drip of each drop. Distances sometimes stretch or fold, but when they do I simply wait—standing between the same pair of stanchions—until the corridor remembers its proper length and I can continue.

Occasionally a Cartographer passes. Their slate remains blank until they pause at an intersection that should not exist; then a blue line crawls outward and they step through a bulkhead whose number is already being reassigned. I do not watch them leave. My task is to finish the bulb replacement before the lux-hour counter on my wrist drifts out of tolerance.

At the end of my shift I record the work on a slate whose screen shows only green tolerance bars. The city accepts the entry without comment. I return to my hammock in the trusswork, and for a moment the graphite lattice seems to fit my body more closely than the coveralls do. In the perpetual light I close my eyes, and the last thing I register is the faint electrostatic pop of a glider changing direction somewhere above me—an adjustment so small that only the city itself could have noticed it, or perhaps only me.
