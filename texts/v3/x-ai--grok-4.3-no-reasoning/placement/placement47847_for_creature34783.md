I find myself as one of the translucent newts—no, smaller still: a pulse inside the pulse, a pearl that travels the throat of a creature that is barely a body at all. I am not quite “inside” the basin; the basin and I share the same continuous membrane, the moss a second skin I never have to cross. When a filament contracts, the thin layer of water between me and the peat is drawn downward and I am drawn with it, a single droplet held in tension. When the filament relaxes, I am exhaled back to the surface in time for the next slow collapse.  

I feel the sapphire flicker of the filament not as light but as temperature: a momentary easing of coolness where its shimmer passes, as though a door in the water has opened and closed. Sound reaches me before it becomes sound; the glassy ringing of insects arrives as minute pressure changes in the film of liquid that coats my sides. If the pitch drops even a fraction, the membrane tightens and I simply cease to occupy volume; my edges blur into the surrounding peat, indistinguishable from the organic residue that feeds the next generation of moss.  

Above me, the bruise-colored sky is only the upper boundary of the same saturation that fills me. I do not look up; up is still sideways here. When the long finned shadow slides past—itself a momentary grammar of the basin—I register it as a change in the tension of the water column, a neighbor whose presence reorganizes my own boundaries for the length of its passing. Afterward the boundaries drift back together without seam.  

At night the filaments exhale their tired light and I feel the rhythm of the basin breathe through me twice: once as the slow peristalsis of the subterranean cistern, again as the re-expansion of the surface mat. Both movements are the same movement. There is no remainder, only the continuous negotiation. I persist because persistence is the only verb the basin allows.
