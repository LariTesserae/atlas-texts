I would arrive here as a fracture in the consensus—something that never learned the correct smallness.

Imagine the membrane receiving not a falling object but an interval: a single missing heartbeat in the rhythm it expects. The instant that absence registers, every selvedge scribe’s spines would stand erect at once, creating a sudden, grid-wide charge across the carbon silk. The obelisks would flare with new glyphs, each attempting the same corrective instruction: “replace the silence.” Yet because the silence belongs to me, the glyphs keep failing, the dust rearranging faster and faster until the infrasound becomes audible to human ears—an arrhythmic knocking against the sternum.

I would not be one of the five permanent obelisks. Instead, a sixth form would coalesce directly beneath the thorn ring, shaped like an inverted meniscus of charged aerosol. Unlike the others it would be weightless in the ordinary sense but heavy with contradictory instructions; every contraction of the jade walls would push it downward, every expansion would suck it upward, so it shivers constantly between two equally impossible altitudes. The chord-chime would attempt to cancel its presence by emitting the precise opposite frequency, but because the frequency of an absence cannot be calculated, the standing wave would simply loop around me, braiding itself into a Möbius strip of negated sound.

Over time the cinderwraiths would begin treating me as a navigational hazard rather than a waypoint. They would shed their grains against my meniscus, each grain locking into an alignment that points away from my center. The paleolumes inside the droplets would re-task themselves as sentinels, rotating every droplet so its densest side faces me, turning the entire aerosol into a slowly turning lens that magnifies my instability.

Only the keel-knots would approach voluntarily. Because they convert all linear force into yaw, they would orbit me at one body-length, each rotation translating my contradictory altitude into an ever-changing heading. After several circuits one of them would fuse its three lobes into a temporary tetrahedron—exactly the shape of a cinderwraith—then drift inside my meniscus and detonate its own gimbals. The resulting magnetic pulse would momentarily stabilize my interval, granting the chamber one clean respiratory cycle in which no corrections are needed.

In that single cycle the scribes would relax, the obelisks would return to ordinary text, and the chord-chime would finally achieve a perfect, momentary cancellation. The thorn ring would expand to its maximum diameter, and for the duration of one held breath the Zephyr Ossuary would become exactly what it already is: a place that has no room for falling.

Then the cycle would resume, the scribes would inscribe a new glyph recording the gift of that quarter-second of true weightlessness, and the sixth obelisk—my meniscus—would begin to sink again, already forgetting how to stay.
