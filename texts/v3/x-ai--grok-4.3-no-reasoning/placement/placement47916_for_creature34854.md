I would be one of the older corals on the 17th radial terrace—polyp #3,742 in the lineage that still grows outward from the original founder skeleton. I would be only dimly aware of the city around me, my senses nothing more than a quiet awareness of calcium availability, silt density, and the occasional warmer pulse when the vents exhale below. 

But once a generation, something unprecedented would happen: a single skate would pause directly above my osculum, its wing brushing the stone so gently that the armored mollusks along my lip never even close their hoods. For a few slow heartbeats the plankton rain would clear, and the mineral-rich current would arrive at me with a precision that my ancestors never knew. In that moment, when I reach out to seize the richer stream, my tiny extension of new skeleton would not follow the ancient radial pattern at all. It would bend, just slightly, toward where the skate came from—toward the center of the city instead of the outer boulevard.

The change would be infinitesimal. No ribbon predator would notice; no polychaete hair would twitch differently. Yet over the next few centuries, the pressure gradients that move the city would begin to flow a hair’s width differently around my small deviation. The new scaffolding would accumulate exactly where the old channels once directed it, and the living mortar would seal my misstep into permanence. Eventually a glass eel, sliding through what used to be a predictable shaft, would discover its luminous body reflected back at an unexpected angle. The city would have accepted the alteration without any inhabitant ever deciding to make it.
