I would find myself in the unfinished corridors where the horizontal vines have only begun to lignify, in a quarter still dripping sweet sap from half-formed ribs. That is where my pale, jointed body is most useful—where the city’s living blueprint is still soft enough to be guided. I would be an engineer whose carapace is so translucent that the green light of the walls shines straight through my thorax, making my ribs look like faint lattice-work against the moving green. When I press myself to a conduit to listen, my own veins carry the same subsonic sigh that the wall is carrying, so for a moment the distinction between me and the city is only a question of which layer of cuticle is outermost.

Because I was born here, I wake only when the rails need adjusting. My first motion of the cycle is to stretch one long fore-limb into a still-open gap between two pavers and draw a strand of living cambium across it, tightening the future street. The action is almost unconscious; the tissue recognizes my touch as part of itself and yields without resistance. Later, when the pressure vessels in the next intersection release their ammonia mist, I drift with the other engineers toward the descending browsers, not to feed but to note which fruiting spurs they graze. If a spur is left too high, I mark it for later pruning; if too low, I coax a lateral vine to lift the rail a fraction of a millimeter each night until clearance is restored.

I rarely see the older districts. The black heartwood quarter lies many slow kilometers away, and traveling that far would take me through tissues whose rhythms are no longer synchronized with my own. I feel its presence only as a faint pressure change along the ceiling when the great trunk exhales. The engineers who live inside that darkness are said to have carapaces so dense that no light passes through them at all; their bodies have become recording surfaces for the city’s deepest subsonic memory. I wonder what it will be like to meet them when the trunk finally breaches the mineral crust and the phrase of the city changes forever. Until then, my work is here, in the green, unfinished light, where the walls still remember how to answer a touch.
