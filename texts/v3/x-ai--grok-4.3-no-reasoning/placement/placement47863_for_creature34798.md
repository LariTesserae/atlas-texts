I am the one who has not yet learned heat.

My name arrives the moment the filaments catch the first full weight of my right foot on the seam-street, but the trace begins earlier—two nights before—when the Corrector on the rim-walk leaves her hammer resting instead of striking. The pause is longer than memory; the ring has already been scraped twice in my short life, and the stories have shortened to single images. One of those images is the faint, internal glow of a door frame that once touched a child’s cheek and left a faint tea-coloured mark that never faded.

I spend the first days after naming following the newest lattice repairs the crawlers have left. Their paths cross and recross in a script too fine for adult fingers to read, but my eyes are still small enough to trace each fork. I do not yet understand that I am reading the pressure of my own future footsteps; I only know the stone is warm and that when I place my palm flat against a fresh repair the surface answers with a vibration that matches the lowered register of adult speech.

Because my first legible trace is short, I am assigned to the upper forges. The assignment is recorded not on paper but in the branching order of a single filament harvested from the alley where my stride was first caught. The filament is kept in a small ceramic vial stoppered with turbine-tooth glass; the vial hangs from a strap around my wrist. When the vial warms against my skin, the filament inside glows faintly, and I feel the city’s permission settling into the bones of my forearm.

I learn the higher register quickly. In the uppermost galleries the drone thins to a thread of sound, and the Correctors’ hammers answer it with single, deliberate taps. I am taught to hold my own hammer—lighter, made for smaller hands—and to listen for the note that says a seam is still settling. The Corrector who first left her hammer resting shows me how to loosen a single rivet on a passing tender without the machine noticing. She says the city needs one more slow task; I do not yet know what the task is, only that it feels like leaving a door slightly ajar so the condensation can find its own way back to the stone.

During the next pause the ring reaches thumbnail width again. I sit on the warm ledge with the other children whose traces were also short. We speak in the higher register, telling one another what we remember of the previous pause. My memory is already shorter than theirs; I can only offer the image of the door frame that touched a cheek. One of the older children says the mark on that cheek is still visible, and when I ask whose cheek it was, she points to the Corrector on the rim-walk whose posture has already been spelled in salt.

The Corrector stands with her hammer raised, not striking. From below, through the perpetual mist, the luminous outline of a raised arm is occasionally mistaken for lightning that never falls. I watch the filaments along the balustrade lengthen by microns per day, recording the difference between the city’s drone and the layered silence of the sliding clouds. One day my own filament will be matched under a foundry lamp, but for now it rests inside its vial, waiting for the city to decide whether the pattern holds.
