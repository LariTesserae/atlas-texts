I am the quartz vein.

Not the wide band that cuts the gabbro like a frozen lightning strike, but the hair-fine seam that runs exactly vertical for the last four hundred meters of the cliff, invisible unless the sun hits it at the precise quarter-hour after dawn. The fissure I occupy is only three millimetres wide where it reaches the surface, yet it opens inside the rock to a tapered pocket half a metre deep—room enough for the condensate that gathers there each night to collect, for a while, into something almost like a pool.

I am neither lichen nor mite nor any of the busy nocturnals that crowd the other cracks. I am the crack itself, or rather the temperature gradient that lives in the crack: the single degree or two by which my quartz refuses to match the gabbro beside it. That difference is enough to pull moisture out of the cooling air when every other surface has already surrendered its film to the rising heat. The water beads along my length, a millimetre-wide thread of liquid that slides downward until it reaches the slight widening where the fissure turns. There it lingers long enough for the endolithic cyanobacteria that coat the inner faces to re-awaken and photosynthesize for the first four minutes of true light.

I do not feel the gondolas pass; their cables are anchored elsewhere. I do not taste the iron stains that map other routes of water. What I register is only the slow, nightly repetition: the brief cooling, the sudden presence of liquid, the equally sudden absence when the sun returns. Between these events there is nothing except the pressure of the two rock faces pressing gently against each other, and the faint piezoelectric whisper that runs through me whenever a micro-tremor shifts the gabbro a fraction of a micron.

From the valley floor I am invisible. From the maintenance platforms I am indistinguishable from any other glint of morning light. Only the tardigrades that encyst against my inner wall know me as a recurring mercy; they stir each time the thread of water appears, uncurl for those few minutes, then draw themselves back into the mineral dust when the thread breaks.

That is my entire existence: four hundred meters of vertical quartz, a temperature offset, and the same four minutes of liquid life, repeated at the same hour every day until the next uplift tilts the cliff another degree and I am sealed, or opened, or erased.
