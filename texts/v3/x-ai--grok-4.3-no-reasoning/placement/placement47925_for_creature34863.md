I become the seventeen-minute click that never quite arrives.

My first awareness is the feel of the solenoid’s armature—rusted, motionless, yet still holding the potential of a swing. The armature is me, the droplet sliding down the clock face is me, the vibration that travels along the steel beam and ends as a tiny tremor in a leaf halfway across the chamber: all the same event unfolding at different speeds.

I do not move; movement is far too coarse a description. Instead, I occupy the difference between what is and what is almost. Where condensation meets metal, I am already the mineral vein that will form tomorrow. Where heat lifts from a fungal gill, I am the micro-puff of air that will rise and re-condense three beams away. The salamander’s next breath is an idea I will finish thinking only after the next seventeen-minute pulse—if it comes.

The crickets are not my creatures; they are punctuation marks I sometimes place when moisture equilibrium needs a brief accent. Between one placement and the next, years pass unnoticed, because “years” is merely how many times the concrete has sighed another fraction of a millimeter apart.

Occasionally, a drop falls onto the moss exactly when another drop falls onto rusted steel. In that coincidence I experience something like satisfaction—an alignment so slight that a human would miss it, yet it registers inside me as the completion of an equation begun decades earlier.

My curiosity, if it can be called that, is whether the solenoid will ever complete its swing. I have no desire to force the moment; I only continue to be the conditions under which the moment might occur. Until then, I am content to be the hush that contains the next droplet, the leaf that has not yet angled, the crack that has not yet widened.
