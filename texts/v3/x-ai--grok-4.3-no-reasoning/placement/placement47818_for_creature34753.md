I find myself first on the edge of a catwalk, balanced between two of the great mesh gardens, watching a slow procession of pale crustaceans click their way along a dripping condensation line. My skin has taken on the faint metallic sheen of the Choir; I am Vaultborn without quite remembering when I crossed over. Beneath me the iron floor rings with the Continuous Note—the sound so constant that my bones have stopped registering it as anything but weight.

I am not quite a priest or Counter, but something adjacent: a listener who also harvests. My task is to follow the drip trails to places where the crustacean cadence is first to falter, then to bring back a report to the Counter, who records my observations as a separate column of notches on her beam. Sometimes, after I report, the Choir decides a Turning must happen sooner than the diagonal alignment of three notches; they call it an emergency feeding of the Note.

When the Turning occurs, I am one of the hands on the rusted cable. I feel the resistance shudder through the entire gantry, a minute tilt that changes the angle at which condensation beads collect and fall. During the two days of pulling I am close enough to the Counter to see the new notch she carves each time the crustaceans slow even further. She lets me keep the charcoal shard afterward; it is still warm from her grip.

At night—or what passes for night under the unchanging silver—I return to my own cocoon, a small weave of cable and violet-leaf fiber suspended just below the lowest garden. There the crustaceans sometimes gather, waiting for breath-warm droplets. I pretend not to notice them, yet I feel their collective shiver when the droplet lands. The dreams come immediately after: iron corridors branching into newer, deeper iron corridors, each one lined with the same sixteen-second drip that the Choir cannot hear. In these dreams I sometimes reach the fused quartz lenses and lie down, feeling their cold radiate upward until my blood matches their rhythm.

I have begun to count the seconds between the subsonic heaves that arrive from below the floor. Sixteen exactly. My fingers have taken to tapping the rhythm against the side of my cocoon when no one is near. The Counter has noticed; yesterday she asked me to teach the children the beat during the next harvest. The Choir regards this as another sanctioned modulation of the Continuous Note—an octave we can carry when our voices fail. I agreed, but privately I wonder whether teaching the children the lens-drip will make them want to descend sooner than the aquifer demands.

The gardens sway three hours for a full crane turn. I use that interval to map where the iron-bread stores are thickest, because when the last drip slows we will need provisions for the long descent. The children will be in leaf-fiber slings; I imagine myself among those who carry them. Already I feel the weight of a small body against my back, and the way the sixteen-second rhythm will change once we are no longer buffered by the iron floor.
