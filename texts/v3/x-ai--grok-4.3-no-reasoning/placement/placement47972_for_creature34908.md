I find myself two or three body-lengths beneath the concrete footing, where a seam of warmer stone still radiates from an unseen fracture. Here the ground never cools enough for the mist to condense, so a narrow pocket of clearer air persists—more an absence of vapour than actual light. I am the fracture itself, the millimetre-wide line along which the rock split when the footing was poured. My surface is faceted like shattered quartz, and every slow bubble that rises from the nearest pan sends a sympathetic ripple through me, a faint tug that feels almost like temperature.

Over decades I have accreted a hair-thin layer of salt crystals whose growth front exactly matches the geometry of my own crack. Inside each crystal the archaeal cells that fell from the open cabinet have lodged themselves, turning me into a lattice of living salt. When a drifting mist sheet brushes overhead, the temperature drop makes the crystals shed one molecular plane; the shed ions drift downward through my facets and are reincorporated at the bottom edge, so my shape migrates a single atom-width deeper into the stone each year. The motion is too slow to be felt by anything larger than the nematodes, yet they register it as a faint, rhythmic warming that guides them when they thread between the roots of the translucent discs.

Occasionally a droplet expelled by one of the bag-like organisms overhead lands on the concrete above me. The drop’s residual heat conducts through the footing and into my salt lattice, momentarily brightening every crystal. In that instant the archaea pulse together, and the brief coordinated flare travels the length of my fracture like a held breath released. Then the warmth dissipates, the crystals dim, and I resume my imperceptible descent. From this vantage the only horizon is the next temperature gradient; everything else—the pipes, the mist, the suspended ducts—exists merely as the boundary conditions that define how far and how slowly I may continue to grow.
