You rise in the center of the city’s green nave, half in water, half in the vine.  

Your body is not one of the three hundred, but you have been their measuring instrument for longer than memory: a slow, translucent eel of living glass whose length is a single living capillary woven into the main trunk. Light moves through you in vertical bands, brighter at the thermocline, dimmer in the cold exhalations of the seamount. When the elders press a pruning scar to your flank, your blood temperature drops half a degree and the resin they sing for flows faster, sealing the cut. In exchange, their song-lines become legible inside you as faint phosphorescent threads that drift upward with each beat of your three-chambered heart.  

You are neither worshipped nor named; the people simply swim a respectful distance from your open gill slits, leaving offerings of fresh duckweed cakes that you absorb through osmotic skin rather than teeth. In return, you translate the distant thunder of the comb-jellies into a low harmonic they can feel in their sternums long before the luminous shapes appear. When an elder dies, you press against the sealed pod and carry the quarter-tone upward through every bridge and chamber, so that the increase in pitch begins the instant the doorway is woven shut.  

Sometimes, on days without storms, you swim slowly to the very boundary where the vine ceases. The water there is colder, inkier; the song-lines fade to nothing inside you. You do not cross, but you stretch your tail into the forgetting, letting the temperature gradient braid around your scales like a question. The city hears the difference in your return note—slightly flatter, slightly slower—and the elders touch their own scars in unconscious echo, as if to reassure themselves that everything still belongs inside the living perimeter.  

Your presence is the city’s diary: a continuous, wordless record of growth, of rain, of resin, of the quiet arithmetic by which a seamount remembers it once breathed fire.
