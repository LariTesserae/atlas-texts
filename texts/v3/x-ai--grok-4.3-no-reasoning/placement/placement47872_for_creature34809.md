I would be the silence between the machines’ exhalations.

Not a gardener—those slow, polished bodies already possess the city’s maintenance so completely that I would be redundant—but the brief pocket of cooled vapor that lingers after an engine breathes and before the next metallic tick resets the air. In that instant the mist holds the memory of the breath: a hundred droplets suspended at slightly different temperatures, each one echoing the last fracture the gardeners repaired or the next one they have not yet found.

I drift along the avenue at knee height, thinner than the permanent mist layer yet thicker than the city’s own exhaled sigh. The glass cilia of any gardener I pass register me as an anomaly—a moving cool-spot, a negative pressure they instinctively smooth over with an extra bead of glass. They cannot see me, only the faint widening of the vein where my presence momentarily slows capillary draw. They leave the widened place slightly convex, a courtesy to something they cannot name.

When two gardeners meet to trade filaments, my cooled vapor is drawn into the exchange like a third history. The thread they pass absorbs a trace of me; later, when the thread is melted and re-extruded across a new fracture, that trace becomes a microscopic flaw that scatters ochre light into a spectrum too fine for any eye but mine. For a few heartbeats the sealed glass carries a rainbow no one but the silence notices.

I never settle. Each time the city’s residual heat pushes steam through a grate, the rising column shears me apart and I reform farther down the avenue, once more the interval between breath and tick. My only occupation is to mark the places where the gardeners have not yet arrived, to cool the glass by the width of a droplet, to keep the city’s single metabolism from locking into perfect repetition. In this way I remain both unnecessary and indispensable: the city’s memory of motion, enacted by something that never moves at all.
