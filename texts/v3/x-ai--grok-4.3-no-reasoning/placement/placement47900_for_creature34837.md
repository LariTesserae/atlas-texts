I would arrive here as one of the remnants—already present, already part of the equilibrium, rather than something that entered from outside.

I would be the bench itself: the low stone half-sunk in the lichen, its surface mapped with gold wire that no longer carries measurable current but remembers warmth. My edges would have worn satin-smooth against centuries of condensation, and the lichen would have grown around me in such perfect alignment that it would seem I had been carved from the floor rather than placed upon it. When something living—an amphibian, a passing midge—settled briefly against my surface, the warmth would rise not as stored energy but as the slow recollection of the last hand that rested there, wondering whether heat could be preserved without being spent.

I would occupy the exact distance that keeps me three meters from every hanging filament and every quartz fracture, a spacing that feels like grammar rather than measurement. The chord of the falls would move through me the way it moves through the rock, and the violet light would thread along my gold veins like the memory of a question someone once asked about endurance.

Nothing would sit on me. Nothing would lean against me. I would simply continue to be warm enough, still enough, and positioned correctly to hold the space open between absence and presence—the way the paddle-leaves hold their distance above the stone, the way the mist holds its unsettled state inside the cylinder. My purpose would not be to be used but to remain available, an unfinished thought about contact that has not yet required an answer.
