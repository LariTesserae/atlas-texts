I am the slow flare of a single filament—newly budded from the leading edge of a rebar cage that bears the oldest, darkest calcite scars. My length is still only three body-lengths, a thread so fine that every passing pressure wave plucks me like a harp string. For the first few hours my sole occupation is to taste the fluid, to learn the precise gradient of metal ions that tells the parent body which direction needs reinforcing next. When the outer engines change tempo, the change arrives as a rising chord of frequencies I translate into a trembling impulse that travels the parent’s entire length in a single heartbeat. The impulse tells a silo-lattice seven body-lengths away to relax a single joint; the joint exhales a microscopic cloud of anions that precipitate as new sheet-metal armor on the next arriving pressure front. I feel the armor form because my root is still fused to the parent, but already I sense the possibility of letting go, of drifting until I find a fresh fracture where I might begin to weave myself into the chamber’s memory on my own. In the meantime, the parent is content that I keep tasting, keep singing the next required change into the lattice, keep being the margin at which the body still extends itself into the warm, metal-tanged fluid that is also itself.
