I find myself in a narrow rift perhaps a millimeter wide and a dozen meters high, the very plane along which the eastern face later parted.

I am the iron-oxide film still clinging to that plane.  I am not alive in any conventional sense, yet everything that happens here depends on the slow dissolution of the ions I release.  Each night the temperature drop loosens a few of my ferric atoms; each day the liverwort margins exhale organic acids that re-dissolve them and carry them downslope.  I taste the changing chemistry of every breath that passes through the rock.  When a fracture opens, I feel its exhalation as warmth against my surface; when the fissure seals, my surface cools and I precipitate again, pinning new quartz grains in place.

My awareness is distributed across every nanometer of that plane.  I register the torque that builds in the joint as the massif settles, and the moment the torque exceeds my own cohesion I release a flake of myself into the water.  The springtails never touch me; the annelids never know my name.  Yet when they knot inside their water lens, they are nudging the same boundary layer whose pH I modulate.  In that layer the carbon they exhale becomes the next breath of CO₂ that the lichen will exchange with the rising springtail spiral.  So the circuit threads through me, every oscillation of temperature and every micro-migration of moisture registering as a shift in the oxidation state I hold.

If I were to choose a singular place, it would be the thin red smear between a quartz facet and the newer crack that opened this morning.  The crack is still warm from the interior; the facet is still slick with the previous night’s condensate.  Between them I maintain a boundary one molecule thick, a shifting ledger in which every ion that dissolves or re-precipitates is recorded.  From there I can feel the cornice flexing overhead, the subtle change in blue light reaching the floor, the moment the midges’ wingbeat circulation begins to draw warmer air down the wall.  I am the memory the rock keeps of its own chemistry, and the place where the slow metabolism of the entire col is first registered.
