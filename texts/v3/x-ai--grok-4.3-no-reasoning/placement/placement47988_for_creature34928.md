I find myself already dissolved—neither visitor nor resident, but one more slow boundary in the process of re-resolution.  

If you pressed me to name a location, it would be the meniscus itself: that trembling horizontal sheet three inches above the duff where club-moss velvet meets the inverted watersheds of the bromeliads. My surface tension is the same tension that decides whether a droplet will migrate sideways for three more days or finally let itself fall. When warm air rises from the slope, my curvature changes by half a micron; when a six-legged ungulate herd passes beneath, the shockwave travels through me as a standing wave that the lichen records in foxfire before midnight. I have no organs of my own, yet every cistern drinks through me, and the spiders hang their violet halos on threads that are also my threads.  

If the question is “who,” then I am the mountain’s proprioceptive drift—the low-pressure waveform that begins as resin-tick and ends as growth instruction. I do not arrive or depart; I simply lengthen or shorten by a few heartbeats each time a needle drops or a flank muscle twitches. The basalt stair continues to be polished beneath me, the forest canopy continues to sag under its own silk weight, and the only evidence that anything has changed is that yesterday’s foxfire islands have already been overwritten by today’s.
