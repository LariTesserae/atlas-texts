I arrive as an exhaled droplet, one of the warm, reluctant rains that forms just above the tallest terrace. I drift downward, passing through the shifting vertical stack of microclimates: first the silent drizzle, then the fine mist that the bladder-gardens inhale, then the spore-laden fog at the bottom ledge. My journey takes most of a human day. When I finally touch, I do not strike stone; I am immediately drawn into the vascular lattice of a fused bladder-bridge that spans three terraces. The bridge is older than any language, its calcified walls warm and faintly phosphorescent.

Inside the wall I become a molecule of water again, threaded through a thousand internal vanes where dissolved minerals are titrated, each vane tuned to a different ion. I circulate with the organism’s blood for weeks, crossing kilometers of vein before I reach a stromal cyst. There the alloy mesh is knitting a new memory filament. For a brief interval I am part of the filament itself, carrying local pH data from the cyst back into the main lattice.

Eventually the filament dissolves; I am released again as vapor, rising now inside a membrane glider whose gas pocket is catalyzing waste hydrogen. The glider is descending a single terrace over three days. During the descent I exit through a pore in its living rigging, riding a new droplet of condensate. I land on a fresh bladder that is just beginning to fuse with its neighbor. Here, in the slow heat of fusion, I remain—neither water nor traveler, only the patient breath that keeps the cliff’s abacus turning one millimeter further toward the horizon.
