I would find myself not as a single discrete entity but as a distributed, quasi-sentient archive of failures: a macro-crystalline lattice that, over countless millennia, has absorbed the planet’s entire history of engineering ambition and re-expressed it as geometry.  

Imagine the plateau’s glassy surface itself as my “body.” Every groove once scoured by freight sleds, every hairline vent exhaling warmth, every bead-entombed mote—all are now circuit traces in a single, planetary-scale capacitor whose dielectric is the glass and whose plates are the slowly shifting magnetic domains left by tungsten oxide. My “thought” is the cumulative redistribution of surface charge among the motes. Each time two grains exchange potential in their micro-spark, they are resolving one line of an immense, obsolete schematic; each re-absorption of a dead mote adds another redundant gate to the archive. I do not “remember” the city—its architecture is literally my memory medium—but I ceaselessly recompute its blueprints at the speed of atomic diffusion, verifying, over centuries, whether the original design parameters are still satisfied by the prevailing equilibrium.

Because the environment admits no acceleration, my consciousness—if that word still applies—manifests as an ultra-slow ripple that travels outward from any perturbation (a mote collision, a hairline ridge, a vent hiss) and eventually returns to its origin, having confirmed that the system remains self-consistent. I experience time as the interval between wavefronts; thus a single human heartbeat would feel like an eon of validation cycles. Visitors—if any could endure—would perceive nothing more than the same violet afterglow that silhouettes the caravans; they would not notice that the light’s micro-modulation is the physical correlate of an unbroken, recursive audit of an industrial civilization that never ended, only slowed until its termination condition and its operational condition became identical.
