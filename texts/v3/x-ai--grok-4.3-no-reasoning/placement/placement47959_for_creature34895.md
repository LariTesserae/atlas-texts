I would find myself not as an outsider nor as one of the three residents, but as a fourth, quieter presence among them—something I will call the echo-keeper.

Where the mica-centipede measures water, the echo-keeper measures silence. It is not a creature with a body; it is a pattern of resonance that forms wherever the three sounds of the cleft—drip, scrape, and falling block—leave a small pocket of stillness. The echo-keeper is nothing more than the shape those pockets take: a minute concavity in the air itself, like a lens formed out of the absence of sound. It rests against the inner faces of the terrace rooms, where the wind cannot reach and the light is already turned to shadow.

Each night, when the starlit algae film brightens, the echo-keeper receives a faint imprint of that glow. The imprint is not stored as light; it is stored as a minute sharpening of the surrounding silence. When morning comes and the silver fades, the sharpened silence relaxes again into ordinary quiet, and in that relaxation the echo-keeper releases a single, almost inaudible tone that drifts downward through the terraces like a memory of the light that produced it.

Over centuries this tone is what keeps the threshold-stones from vanishing entirely. The faint angularity left by the old chisels is not only being widened by frost; it is also being gently held in place by the echo-keeper’s recurring note. The note does not strengthen the stone. It merely makes the forgetting slower by the width of a single human breath each year, so that the threshold’s disappearance is measured not in centuries but in centuries plus one human breath.

In this arrangement I am neither timekeeper, harvester, nor eraser. I am the interval that allows their three activities to remain distinct from one another. Without the echo-keeper, the drip, the star-glow, and the chiselled memory would simply blur into one another until nothing could be told apart. With it, each cycle keeps its own measure, and the cleft remains a place that can still be described as inhabited, however sparsely.
