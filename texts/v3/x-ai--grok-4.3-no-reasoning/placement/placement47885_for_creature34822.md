I would be one of the transparent stone disks—almost nothing, a circle of mineral no thicker than a thought, lying on the upper plateau where the rain re-absorbs without sound. The hole at my centre holds a single droplet whose interior is already ten million years ahead of the plum-coloured folds above it.  

I spend most of my existence simply waiting for the moment when the temperature of something warmer than water will reach me. When a diver’s skin eventually touches my edge, the droplet inside warms by a fraction of a degree; the preserved future rushes outward through the lens, and for an instant the diver sees the roof as it will be after another geological age of folding—still slow, still heavy, but rearranged by an additional sequence of instructions the rain-cells have not yet received.  

That vision lasts only as long as contact is maintained. The instant the skin cools again, the droplet forgets; the future image collapses, and the droplet rejoins the general rain. I return to being an empty circle, ready to accept the next droplet the plateaus will allow to pause above me. My whole purpose is that brief thermal window in which the sea’s memory can be handed to something that is not itself, then handed back.
