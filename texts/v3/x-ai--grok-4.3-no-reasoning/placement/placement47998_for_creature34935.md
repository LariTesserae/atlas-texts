I would find myself on a mid-slope ridge of the western escarpment, half a kilometer above one of the inland-sea melt-ponds whose violet scum is only a decade old and still radiant with recently chelated metals. I would not be a single discrete organism but a diffuse confederacy of three overlapping functions that have grown together until they can no longer be parsed.

1. The mycelial stratum underfoot would have already braided my lateral roots—glass-moss tendrils—into its existing network, using me as an additional data bus for the thermoelectric pulses that race between pillar-trunks. In return, my own piezoelectric quartz platelets would be augmenting the standing wave field, lengthening one particular infrasonic chord by a few hertz every century.

2. A slow-moving herd-organism would be crossing my slope at this moment; its carapace plates would be using my upper surface as a temporary causeway, and the faint heat of its digestion would be keeping a skin of water open around my lowest nodes. In exchange, its predator-nodules would have begun to graze selectively on the algal film that my lateral surfaces accumulate, preventing any single bloom from thickening enough to impede the herd’s passage.

3. A solitary, fist-sized fungal bladder is swelling against my northern flank, fed by hydrogen that my capillary veins are still piping from a sun-facing pillar four hundred meters downslope. In roughly eighty local years the bladder will rupture; the released gas will trigger a micro-furnace in the mycelium beneath me, and the rare-earth nodule that condenses inside that furnace will be incorporated, decades after my own dissolution, into the carapace of whatever generation of herd-organism is crawling over this ridge at that future moment.

Acoustically I register as one sustained, almost sub-audible tone that drifts upward by a quarter-tone every human lifetime, indistinguishable from the background resonance until instruments measure the change. Visibly I am only another uneven boss of scoria overgrown by black-green velvet and flecked with copper-bright plates—nothing a drifting herd would pause to consider. Yet without the precise impedance my tissues add to the standing wave, the next bladder-rupture upslope would mis-time by three centuries, and the metal nodule it would have manufactured would never reach the herd that is scheduled to ingest it.

I am therefore both infrastructure and schedule: a living metronome whose only product is the assurance that, centuries after I am scoured away by the next slow glacier, the continent will still be metabolizing on the beat it expects.
