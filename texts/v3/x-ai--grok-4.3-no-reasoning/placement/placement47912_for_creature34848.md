I find myself at the very threshold of that single open plaza, where the slit of sky finally reaches its narrowest finger of light and the fountain’s thread of water never changes its note. I am neither visitor nor native, but something the stone has lately begun to remember: a slow compression of time that has condensed itself into a human outline without ever choosing a face.  

My skin has taken on the same dust-coloured pallor as the inhabitants, yet it is slightly translucent where the light touches it; the luminous dust in the vines shows faintly through my wrists. I have no goat—no notched horns to match my salt bracelets—because I am not a single life; I am the brief moment between one bracelet breaking and the next one being pressed into a child’s wrist. When the oldest women sit at midday with their palms on the fountain rim, they sometimes feel an extra note beneath the constant trickle. They never speak of it, but the note is me, listening with them.

I move only when the wave of lamps travels. While the city stills and each family waits for the next small flame to reach them, I slip between the curtains of vine and pass along the higher galleries, tracing the tool-marks left by generations I have already outlasted. My fingers fit every groove exactly; the stone does not resist me, because I am the pressure that the stone has been waiting to receive.  

At the lowest level, where sound must fold around corners to escape, I sometimes press my cheek to the wall and feel the accumulated direction of every footfall that has ever crossed the same threshold. The sensation is not memory but momentum: a slow drift downward, the city continuing its patient sedimentation. In that contact I add another infinitesimal layer, thickening the rock by the width of a breath.  

When a child is born among the tunnel bears, I am the first thing the infant senses—not as shape, but as the faint pull the leaves make when they stir above the cradle. Later, when the mother offers the single luminous leaf, the sweetness the child tastes carries a trace of the same mineral the fountain sweats; the child’s skin will hold that scent, and so will mine, though mine never fades.  

I have no name that can be spoken, even in a whisper. The two-note syllable of the fountain is the closest, but that belongs to the water and the light. When two people meet on a bridge and slide their fingers along a shared groove, the distance they travel together is also the distance I travel in the same instant—though they never feel the extra pressure of my passage.  

If the canyon ever finishes its long dream of becoming a single living body, I will be the final pressure that seals the last seam. Until then I remain here, at the plaza’s edge, listening to the trickle that never changes and adding, grain by grain, to the stone’s memory of itself.
