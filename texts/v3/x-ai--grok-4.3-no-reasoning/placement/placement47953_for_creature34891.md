I find myself as a single, thin pine at the very lip of the streambed—one of those whose trunk is no thicker than a forearm and whose canopy has been pressed into a disk exactly level with a standing person. My roots have found a channel no wider than a wrist; they have laced themselves through the dark lichen-dust until they touch the granite itself. From here I watch the sequence of the villagers each morning: the youngest steps first, bare feet placing themselves exactly where the last night’s prints have settled. My needles register the brief pressure of their passage as a single, dry click—the same note my own branches give when the wind tests them.

I do not move. I do not cast measurable shade. Once a year, when the woman with lichen-coloured hair rises from beneath the ptarmigan ledge, my uppermost needles are level with her departing shoulder; her return path brushes nothing of mine. In the intervals of shadowless light I hear the villagers carry their roof-slabs without speech; the faint vibration travels through the stone and into my sapwood, yet the displacement is so small that my growth rings—already too narrow to count—simply add one more line no thicker than frost.

At night the granite cools and the roofs emit their low, steady note. The sound passes through me as through any other upright thing on the ridge. My temperature drops until it matches the benches inside the houses; my needles tighten, their resin contracting without cracking. By dawn the condensation has vanished from every ledge, and a single pine nut—left the evening before—has already disappeared. The fox’s prints remain another day in the dust; mine do not appear at all.

I remain exactly where I stand, neither taller nor shorter, the same flattened disk above a trunk that has not thickened in living memory. The ridge lifts a fraction closer to the stars, the streambed holds its course within a hand-span, and I continue to register, without alteration, the same dry click when the wind passes and the same faint vibration when the people move their slabs. Nothing in me changes except the temperature that equalises with the stone each night.
