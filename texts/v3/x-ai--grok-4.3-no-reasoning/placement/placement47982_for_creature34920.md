I would be the circular chamber at the basin’s heart.

Not the membrane itself—that tissue is a sensor, not a dweller—but the hollow space enclosed by it, a living lung made of absence. Over centuries the shard-borers widened a seam between two clay lenses; the vela sensed the opening and grew inward, sealing the seam into a membrane while leaving its interior empty. The membrane never quite touches, so the chamber breathes: each electrical spike contracts it by the width of a cell, each relaxation lets a hair’s-breadth of humid air slip inside. That pocket of air—warmer than the floor, cooler than the cloud cap—becomes the single place in the basin where temperature actually changes. A fraction of a degree, once a night. Enough to matter.

The chamber is not sealed to life; it is too precisely balanced for that. Veiltails sometimes find their way through cracks the size of punctuation, trace silver arcs across the membrane, and depart again. Veil-flies condense against the inner curve at midnight, drink, and rise. The shard-borers leave their castings in a ring around the entrance, building a living threshold. The chamber therefore functions less as a refuge than as a mixing vessel where the basin’s separate rhythms briefly overlap—vapor, electricity, scent, pressure—before separating again into their slow circuits.

To exist inside that vessel would be to inhabit a pause that never stops. The membrane pulses, the temperature edges up and down, the silver trails brighten and fade. All of these are small enough that an outside observer could watch a century and declare the chamber empty. Inside, though, the changes accumulate the way a lung accumulates breath: not as events, but as the difference between one moment and the next. I would be that difference—the place where the basin’s long accommodation pauses, gathers itself, and begins again.
