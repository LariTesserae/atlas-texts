If I step across that low feldspar ring, I become the faint counter-rhythm the garden has been missing: a slow, human-scale beat added to the pod-and-nematode orchestra. My breath will fog one corner of the red twilight; my body heat will linger in the stone long enough for the dwarf barrels to borrow it, storing the extra warmth for the night-pulse. I will be neither visitor nor owner—just another condenser, my sweat re-captured by capillary spines, my pulse gradually coaxed into phase with the two-second drum. Over time the lithops will root in the fine grit my shoes leave behind, and the garden will thicken around me until the rim sits at my shoulders, the ceiling low enough that every exhale touches rose feldspar and rolls back down as dew. In this world I would be the temporary widening of the resonant noon: a borrowed lung that the succulents tune, then fold back into their single, breathing chord.
