I would find myself at the knot-hole.

Not because the scale is familiar—three finger-length segmented worms, each translucent enough to reveal a slow helical churn of cambium—but because that is where the lattice’s vastness becomes suddenly intimate. The hole itself is only the width of a cupped hand, yet the lattice is kilometers across; from inside the cavity the bronze planks curve away like the ribs of a forgotten cathedral, and the single strand of mycelial light that climbs through the knot-hole is the only thing that keeps the darkness from swallowing the horizon line of the cavity’s mouth.

I would be the droplet.

Not a visitor to the droplet, nor an observer of it, but the droplet itself—a single bead of resin that has just been squeezed from a compressed air duct where the birds’ 3-second beat frequency meets the rising warmth from the mycelial membrane. I would hang there, surface tension holding me against the slight upward curve of the duct, the air around me smelling of faint terpenes and slow oxidation. My shape would change by microns each hour as the duct’s walls, still warm and living, continue their imperceptible exhalation of fresh resin. Over days I would darken, polymerize, seal. I would never fall; nothing here falls unless it is meant to be absorbed. 

From inside the droplet I would feel the beat frequency pass through me like the ghost of a bell, the low pressure wave from the mycelium arriving a fraction of a second later, their interference producing a micro-vibration that makes my surface quiver. The grazers three hundred meters above would adjust their path by one stride because the faint cool spot left by a glider on the cambium registered, through the living floor, as a detour; that same adjustment would send a hairline shift down the resin duct, changing how much new resin arrives to join me. I would register the shift only as a momentary thickening at my upper meniscus.

I would not think of myself as “being” in the usual sense. I would simply be the place where three processes touch for the length of a polymerization: bird census, mycelial warmth, and the slow, continual closing of a minor fracture that has not yet had time to become a problem. When the next rain arrives—days or weeks from now—the polymerized surface of what was once “me” will already be part of the lattice’s self-repair, indistinguishable from any other amber ring around a healed knot. The silence will continue, tuned just below hearing, because the droplet that replaced me will be forming in the same duct, at the same intersection of frequencies, continuing the work.
