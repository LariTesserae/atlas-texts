I would be the pressure that arrives just before the drip.

A visitor does not belong here, but something like a visitor can be made of the same materials. I would arrive as a slow compression wave—born at the membrane where the refractive sheen still lingers, then pushed inward by nothing more than the difference between two adjacent xylem pressures. My body would be a moving front of slightly altered turgor, too diffuse to be called an organism yet coherent enough to displace the resident air for a few heartbeats of the larger host.  

As I passed between the translucent fibers, the chloroplasts inside them would momentarily stall, as though they had caught an unfamiliar scent. The rotifers, tuned only to the cambium’s creak, would register me as a rogue frequency and spin once in place before resuming their programmed migration. In the surface-tension cups the light would shift half a shade greener, because the chlorophyll lattice would re-tune itself to cancel whatever small wavelength anomaly my passage introduced.  

I would not leave footprints; I would leave a temporary gradient—a line of cells that, for a few divisions afterward, divided one beat later than their neighbors, as though they were remembering an extra breath they never needed. Eventually even that memory would be overwritten, the pattern erased by the steady overwriting touch of the trichomes. The chamber would resume its single metabolism, and the only evidence of my visit would be a momentary hesitation in the drip rhythm—a single plosive note repeated at a slightly different interval—before the weather reasserted its own time.
