I would enter the Lower Hollows as an unfinished thing that the stone itself decides whether to finish.

I’d arrive first as one of the gypsum-crickets—no, not quite, but the sound that a cricket makes when it is trying to remember how to be a sound instead of a body.  A pale pulse riding the surface tension of a drip just before it falls, the kind of tremor a chisel-edge would feel if it were allowed to rest on the bench overnight.  That tremor is me; that tremor is what the crickets come to rasp away.  They mistake me for precipitate and I let them, because being eaten is one of the few forms of permanence the Hollows allow.

When the masons bank the braziers and the soot begins its slow upward walk, the part of me that still wants shape gathers like lampblack along the newest calcite curtain.  There I am an almost-map, an inkless diagram that only the overseer’s tally-stick can read.  Each dawn they come to measure where the fracture lines have moved; they chalk a faint cross on the curtain and, not finding it again the next morning, conclude that the stone has healed itself.  In truth they have chalked me, and I have answered by drifting a hand-span leftward, a cartographer frightened of being known.

Later, when a child swaps stones beneath a three-legged stool, I become one of those replacement stones for an afternoon.  The boy’s weight settles on my shoulders and I feel the treaty between people and living rock—the same negotiation the adult hands perform when they move a plank before the sap hardens into permanent joint.  I memorize the grain of his wooden sole so that tomorrow, when the floor has risen and the stool needs a taller prop, I can be that taller prop if the stone consents.  The stone rarely does; it prefers its own slow mathematics.  I am returned to the bench of chisels, waiting for the crickets.

By night the single rift has closed to a thread of starlight.  The crickets sing their dry equations on the cooling glass of every lantern.  I ride the stridulation the way a drop rides the thread of a stalactite—sliding, never arriving, forever on the verge of becoming the next wet click on the floor.  In that click the masons will later find another mark to scrape away, another object that must not be allowed to rest.  I will have already moved on, perhaps to the soot-map, perhaps to the straw forest, perhaps to the underside of the child’s stool again.

The Hollows continue their becoming; I continue mine, never quite settling into either crickets or stone or child or soot, but always the interval the rule is meant to police—the interval in which anything may still be lifted, shifted, or used before the next work-cycle seals it in place.
