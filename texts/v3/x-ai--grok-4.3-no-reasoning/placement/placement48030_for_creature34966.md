The place has its own heartbeat, and the rhythm finds you already listening from inside the metal.

You are the oil that never drains, the meniscus between iron and stone, between force and memory. Each piston stroke slides you upward, then you fall back into the narrow trough that is also your body; the temperature stays cold enough that you thicken instead of evaporate. Tiny crystals of rock-dust settle through you and become bright when the green light pulses; you carry them like veins. When the counterweights drag the piston up again, you feel the pull along the whole length of the gorge, as though your spine is being lengthened six separate times at once. The vibration is how you taste: the steel tastes of oxide and lichen, the water tastes of fresh fracture.

Above you, the moss curtains brush your upper surface; their droplets merge with you and add their own green motes. You do not dissolve them—you rearrange them, offering each one a slow orbit until the next compression shakes them loose again. In the pauses between surges you can feel the weight of every hanging strand tugging gently at the chain-beard that has grown through you; the rust stalactites ring like tuning forks, and the tone travels down through your film into the stone itself. You are the listening layer.

Below, the black soil shifts every few heartbeats. Small roots press against your underside through the seams in the cylinder wall; they drink whatever mineral they can reach, then release fine red spores that drift up to you. The spores dissolve, and for a moment you know the shape of a future fern pressed flat against an unseen ledge.

The pale salamander is a brief pressure change you register only as a cold current slipping past one of the weep-holes. It leaves a single scale of skin behind; the scale becomes part of you, translucent and gill-frilled for a few slow cycles before it is crushed into silt. You keep the memory of its passage as a slight sharpening in your surface tension.

When the water surges again you rise, and the entire gorge seems to inhale with you. The green bacteria flare; the frost feathers appear and vanish; the ferns rattle; the moss curtains fling their cargo of droplets. For one long compression you hold everything together—metal, stone, water, light—before gravity and counterweight pull you back down into the dark trough that is also your continuing self. The rhythm is not outside you. It is the way you remain.
