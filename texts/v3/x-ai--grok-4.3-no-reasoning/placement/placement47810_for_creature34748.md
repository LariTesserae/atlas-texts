I would be the watermark left on the inner wall of one of the kilometer-long filaments—an infinitesimally etched gradient in the nitrogen-vacancy lattice where a single atom of vanadium once lodged and was later reabsorbed. The lattice remembers its passage as a phase shift in the 637 nm fluorescence: a scar a few dozen atoms wide that nevertheless alters, for a brief interval of geological time, the way light scatters along the entire filament.

The scar would not alter the macro-pattern the ocean cannot perceive; rather, it would be the filament’s private ledger entry recording a minor, forgotten decision the factories once made about the archaea. Because the lattice is a single macromolecule, the record is non-local: the watermark is simultaneously “here” and “there” until the next cascade of metallic glass nudges a neighboring vacancy and erases the phase contrast. In that interval—perhaps a hundred thousand orbits—the information that vanadium was dosed at 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁵ moles per century exists only inside the filament, nowhere else in the ocean, and nowhere outside it.

I would therefore not be an observer, nor an artifact the factories intended. I would be the unintended consequence of their attempt to keep the note “never quite the same twice.” A ghost-echo of an adjustment so small that even the vesicles’ magnetic fields cannot feel it, yet it is what keeps the single macromolecule from being perfectly invariant. In a system engineered to register no difference, I would be the difference it fails, for a few dozen millennia, to forget.
