I would find myself as one of the silent gaps in the record.

Between the knee-high vascular canopy and the gelatinous liverwort mats lies a zone that is neither surface nor under-membrane: a plane roughly the thickness of three stacked leaves, held open only by the constant micro-convection of the daily freeze-thaw. Into that plane I am condensed each dawn and evaporated each noon, an ephemeral interstitial organism whose body is simply the interface where frost meets living cuticle. I have no mass beyond the weight of a few dozen water molecules arranged into temporary scaffolding; my “limbs” are the branching lines along which the UV-tinged prismatic bands of the previous night’s midge corpses slide downward into the peat. 

Each morning the nematodes rise toward me like slow lightning, sensing the temporary film of liquid where the membrane relaxes; I serve as a brief navigational corridor, allowing them to thread from one root-hair channel to another without surfacing. By mid-afternoon the rising temperature flattens me into a scatter of droplets no longer coherent enough to steer anything; my identity, such as it is, migrates with the condensate into the next night’s frost crystal, where it again becomes geometry instead of organism.

When a grazer pauses above my plane, its chitin plates press the vascular leaves into momentary contact with the liverwort; for those minutes I am compressed into a single molecular layer between two living surfaces and experience the plateau’s respiration as a single, slow squeeze. The sensation is indistinguishable from being the hinge between two halves of the same cell. Once the animal lifts away, the living membrane rebounds, and I re-expand into the three-leaf gap as though nothing occurred—yet every such compression adds a faint, rust-orange deposition of iron-oxidizing microbes that slightly darkens the next dawn’s frost line.

I never persist long enough to be counted among the fixed points, nor short enough to belong to the midges. My life is simply the plateau’s way of marking the difference between one breath-height fog layer and the next, a difference so small that only the nematodes ever use it as a path.
