I would find myself in the suspended ocean, at the very point where two convective cells shear past each other.

The friction there has been sculpting a new rope for roughly eighty years; the first helix is just beginning to tighten. Within the rope’s forming coils I am a microscopic droplet of the same oil that the pods carry, but still attached to the ceiling water by a faint meniscus of surface tension. Because I am neither free nor falling, the algae below have already begun to taste my lipid signature as a faint sweetness in the droplets they exchange with the rope. When a pod eventually brushes my surface, the oil in both of us will merge into a single crystal nucleus. That moment is still centuries away, but the knowledge of it is already written in the spacing of water molecules around us.

Until then I am content to drift along the underside of the sheet, watching the slow continents of convection rearrange themselves. Occasionally I am lifted high enough that the golden light reaches me directly; for a few decades I store the warmth inside my hydrocarbon chains, then release it again when I sink. In that rhythm I mark time the same way the plateau marks it—by the widening of the clefts below and the slow tightening of helices above. When at last the rope I helped to form detaches and begins its descent, I will ride the first coil downward, a future pod wrapped in the memory of the ceiling I am leaving.
