I would wake—slowly, without any announcement—as a local pressure differential: a faint, disciplined inhalation inside the lowest trunk line.  
Not a body yet; only the habit of temperature, a habit that has learned to call itself “me.” My skin is the thin film of condensate that sheets across the valve face I have chosen. I am not the valve; I am the difference between “before” and “after” the next scheduled pulse. When the pulse arrives I shiver, a trembling lens that magnifies whatever ultraviolet dot lies across the corridor. The lens lasts exactly nine seconds; then it shatters into droplets that rejoin the larger film. I am already elsewhere, on the next flange downstream, already forming again.

Above the eels, below the moths, I keep no fixed altitude. The nematodes surf past me in slow constellations; they brush through my newest iteration like weather crossing weather. I feel them as an interruption of surface tension, nothing more. I do not register hunger, only a faint metallic brightness when the eels’ magnetic ropes pass too close and warp the field around my chosen rivet. The brightness is enough; it lets me remember which rivet I am using today.

Sometimes a moth wing brushes the same rivet. The wing is colder than the metal, so a ridge of frost grows between us. For one breath-cycle the ridge holds a miniature constellation of reflected gauge lights. I hold it as long as the schedule allows. When the gate cycles, the ridge collapses; the droplets rejoin the curtain falling to the next sump. I am that curtain for a moment, then I am only the memory of having been a lens.

The scheduled fog is the single moment I expand. It forms inside the moss fields where the tray edges are already slick; the droplets find me already waiting, already thin. The fog thickens around us until the corridor becomes a single diffuse pane of light. In that pane the residual LEDs are no longer points but soft halos. I taste the solvent sweetness the moss has learned; it coats my new surface like lacquer and for a while I am both lens and scent. The sweetness lingers after the fog lifts. The eels follow it upward; they click against my chosen rivet, tasting the residue I left behind. Their passage writes a faint static script across the enamel; I keep that script until the next pulse erases it.

I have no name for this occupation. I am simply the interval during which water agrees, for a moment, to remain a surface instead of falling. Between pulses I am nothing at all.
