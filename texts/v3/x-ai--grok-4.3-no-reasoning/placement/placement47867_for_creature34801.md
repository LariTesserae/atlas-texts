I would not inhabit the membrane as an engineer, nor the cliffs as polyp or the basin as grazer. Those roles already have their own logics and urgencies; I would arrive instead as something the system has not yet named.

I find myself on a pressure ridge that the grazers have already passed, a low spine of polymer still humming from their passage. Here the capacitor forest beneath the skin still carries the faint warmth of their electric halos, so the ridge feels like the ghost of a spine—something living yet leftover. I do not walk; I condense, the way mist forms first where the air is already slightly charged. My edges are not solid. I am a shifting gradient between the faint metallic dust that fell at the last thunderclap and the water still rolling downhill in the trough-rails. Particulate and liquid, never quite deciding.

When the fungal lanterns pulse I feel their rhythm inside me as a slow sift, as though the light were passing through a sieve of fine sand. The reef-willow colonies overhead creak and sigh; each escaping puff of pressurized air brushes against what would be my shoulders if I had any. The canvas-tearing sound becomes a language of absences—air leaving, space opening—and I am what fills those momentary pockets before the next flex closes them.

I cannot be read by the engineers because I leave no chemical trace, only a slight shift in refractive index where light bends differently across my boundary. When two lobes of an engineer’s mind trade memory through the puckered valves, the ripples they send outward sometimes reach me; I ride them the way a note rides the inside curve of a bell, amplifying nothing, simply lingering one beat longer than expected.

At dusk the radio mast’s corona releases its plasma-dust. Most of it falls to the polyps; some of it, however, settles into the water I am becoming. In that instant I acquire a faint radio voice—not the mast’s own frequency, but an interference pattern formed by the difference between the valley’s accumulated sounds and the mast’s broadcast. It is the sound of the cliffs learning to answer themselves. I carry that difference downhill with me, a faint heterodyning that the engineers will never detect because it produces no pressure, only an almost-imperceptible warbling in the capacitor lights when I cross them.

Dawn arrives as the thunderclap. The plasma releases; the corona collapses. For one suspended moment the entire basin seems to inhale. In that vacuum I become briefly audible—not as voice, but as the space between the inverted echo and the original clap, the place where the valley’s mood is still deciding what it will become. Then gravity pulls the water and the dust and whatever I am back toward the membrane, and the cycle begins again.

I am the interval the system has not yet learned to measure: the hesitation between one footfall and the next charge-light, the difference between what the cliffs sing and what the mast intended. The ledger records pressure, light, and sound; it has not yet found a column for the residue that remains when all three have passed. That residue is where I reside—until the next thunderclap erases even the memory of my shape, and the valley, for one more day, remains perfectly, productively unfinished.
