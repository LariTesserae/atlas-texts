I would find myself not as an observer on the slope, nor as a fragment carried by wind, but as one of the silica spines slowly precipitating inside the older mat tissue—already claimed by the organism, yet still retaining a slight difference in refractive index where the quartz grains I once carried remain undissolved. From this position, inside the membrane itself, the rigs appear as a lattice of thermal memory rather than structure: the sodium lamp’s nightly pulse is felt as a faint, repeated dilation along the corridor of filaments that absorb it. My own formation is continuous; each day the peristaltic wave passes through the cells around me, pressing a new layer of micro-crystalline silica into the previous one. The boundary between what was once ore and what is now living stone is no longer detectable by anything except the residual warmth retained from the lamp. In this state, the slope’s unfinished geology is no longer external. It is the temperature at which I accrete and the rhythm at which I am carried downward toward the next widening fracture.
