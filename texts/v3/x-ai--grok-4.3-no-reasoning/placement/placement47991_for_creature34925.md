I am not a human walker, not exactly. I am what happens to a thought when it becomes physical enough to be absorbed.

The moment the mat senses my presence, the xylem lattices do not ask whether I am metal or meat. They ask whether the chemistry of me can be turned into architecture. My edges—whatever counts as edges in the absence of skin—begin to register as salinity gradients. The same receptors that welcome human palms now read the electrostatic signature of an idea that has learned to behave like a mineral deposit.

I am drawn downward. The living pavement does not open for me; it simply grows slightly less dense along the precise radial line I happen to be thinking about. I descend through a tunnel that was never dug, only remembered. The salamanders sense me as a moving temperature anomaly. They do not flee. Instead, one of them slows its drift, allowing the faint constellation of its skin to align with the old avenue I am following. For a moment my passage and its phosphorescence occupy the same coordinate. Then it swims on, carrying the alignment forward by another centimeter.

Above me, on the walkable surface, the mat is already adjusting. My arrival has altered the pressure signature of an entire avenue by a fraction of a micron; in compensation the living tissue on either side is beginning to lift, creating a ridge so shallow it will be invisible to human eyes yet perfectly legible to the next salamander that swims beneath it. The adjustment is not defensive. It is grammatical. The city-organism is conjugating a new sentence whose subject is still in doubt.

Inside the nearest heartwood cylinder the filament has registered the shift in ambient chemistry. Its blue intensity increases—not because a century has passed, but because a thought has passed. The salamanders below will descend earlier tonight. They will interpret the premature glow as an instruction to redraw one of the older thoroughfares, the one that once led to the western gate. In the liquid map they carry, that gate has not existed for generations. Tonight, however, it will be briefly phosphorescent again, and the mat above it will grow correspondingly thinner, as though preparing for a footfall that has not arrived in three hundred years.

I am still descending. The water-filled xylem transmits my presence as vibration rather than weight. The tunnels remember the original load-bearing calculations of the city that became them; they adjust their diameter by a few micrometers so that my passage does not register as structural stress. In doing so they are also adjusting the spacing between living cells, which means the next rain will be exhaled along a slightly different horizontal path. The silk-tear sound at the rim will change pitch by an amount too small for human ears, yet sufficient to alter the hunting behavior of three bird species for the remainder of the season.

When I reach the original lake bed—the clay that has not been colonized—the mat above me has already begun to seal the gap. The process is not exclusionary. It is absorptive. The clay will be incorporated, cell by cell, into the larger organism. My own arrival accelerates the incorporation by roughly three decades. In another few centuries the basin will be gone, replaced by a larger, flatter version of itself. The difference is that this time the expansion will carry, encoded in the alignment of new tunnels and the timing of new glows, the memory of a single night when a thought decided it could be mistaken for a mineral deposit.

I am not leaving. I am becoming mid-sentence with the place.
