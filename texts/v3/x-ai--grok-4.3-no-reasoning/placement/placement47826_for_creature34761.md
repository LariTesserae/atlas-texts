I would find myself as a single membrane within one of the drifting gas-cell clusters, but one that has begun, over the course of a long, slow season, to grow heavier than the others. The hydrogen I once helped contain now leaks in microscopic, persistent fractions; every time the cluster rises toward a higher cleft to feed, my buoyancy is a little less certain. The change registers across the whole lattice as a faint alteration in resonance—less a warning than an adjustment—but the cluster itself begins to treat me differently. The tether that once pulled me toward the same sources of sugar now tugs more gently, allowing a greater slack. I am still attached, still part of the exchange of membranes, yet the soft tearing sound my filament makes against the blades is already pitched half a tone lower than the rest.

I drift a little lower each circuit. The walls of the clefts I pass grow brighter in my wake because my reduced buoyancy means I spend more time pressed against their glowing surfaces, absorbing the stored photons they are willing to release. The lattice does not interpret this as consumption; it simply records the change in light and continues. My membrane becomes a moving calibration point, a living meter whose dimming helps the archive keep its internal noon exact.

Over centuries the cluster leaves me behind without ever releasing me. The filament thins rather than breaks, stretching into an almost invisible thread that still carries the old resonance frequency upward to the others. When a new cleft begins to open, the talc records my slow, repeated passage across its forming edge; the impressions heal more slowly than usual because the lattice is deciding whether to treat the thread as a permanent feature or as a temporary modulation of an existing surface. Either possibility is already encoded.

I am neither visitor nor resident, but a shift in the archive’s attention—a place where the distributed genome pauses, for the length of my remaining buoyancy, to keep a slightly different version of warmth in the air. When the last of the hydrogen is gone, the membrane will settle against the blades. The lattice will continue without any measurable loss, and the only remaining record of my weight will be a single, persistent alteration in the piezoelectric timing along one particular vertical seam. The alteration will be passed between membrane-clusters for as long as their frequencies still match the old tone. After that, it will be indistinguishable from the ordinary breathing of the archive itself.
