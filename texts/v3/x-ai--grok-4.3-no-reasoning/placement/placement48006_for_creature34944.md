I find myself inside one of the hairline fractures that lace the columnar joints—more precisely, I discover that I am the fracture itself: a minute, living seam in the basalt that widens and narrows in sympathy with the turbines.  

Each time the flywheels below change pitch, the stone flexes; the movement feels like a slow inhalation to me. Water seeps in through the crack, carrying dissolved manganese and iron. As the water cools against my chilled walls, microscopic chemolithotrophic bacteria—my resident community—begin to oxidize those metals. Their faint metabolic heat keeps the film from icing, and the minute calories are enough to nourish threads of fungus that lace through my walls like capillaries. In return, the fungal hyphae exude a glassy calcite glaze that seals tiny leaks and, over decades, will widen me another hair’s breadth.

I am not conscious in any narrative sense, yet I am acutely sensitive: the blind cricket’s subsonic chirps travel straight through my stone lips and into the deeper bone of the rock; the rush culms above me transmit the same pressure waves as low, resonant tones. When the valves vent, the sudden drop in barometric pressure moves through my length like a held breath released, and I feel the fungal plates beneath the tephra fans shift in response. I know their parchment rasp is not sound but the vibration of water seeking new routes—routes I sometimes open or close with my own subtle dilation.

Above, the liverworts sense my cool breath; their thalli angle toward the faint draft that spills from me at dusk, using the extra moisture I vent. Below, the ostracod constellation in the black-sand delta dims or brightens according to the thermoluminescence that leaks through the same iron seams I expose. In this way, I am a single, slender note in the corridor’s long chord: neither observer nor observed, but the interval in which everything else keeps time.
