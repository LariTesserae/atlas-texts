The plateau holds itself together like a living raft adrift on the sky. It is not precisely land; it is a colossal, upward-coiling braid of plant-matter, a single organism many miles across whose oldest trunks have petrified into glassy charcoal columns while its newest growths still flex in the wind. Between these columnar spines, vast horizontal leaves the color of bruised lilac knit together in translucent canopies that sag under their own condensation, releasing thunderous sheets of water every hour. The water does not fall to bare ground; instead it slides along vascular gutters the plant has grown for exactly this purpose, feeding lower tiers until the surplus drains over the plateau’s scalloped lip in kilometers-long veils that catch the light like warped mirrors.

The air is thin and cool, smelling of wet minerals and ozone from the static that snaps between the highest leaves. Temperature hovers just above dew-point; condensation gathers on every surface, beading on the metallic sheen of industrial catwalks bolted directly into living xylem. Those walkways are the city’s nervous system—iron lattice, salvaged turbine housings, lengths of pipe and conduit all stitched through the plant’s slower rhythms. Walk far enough in one direction and the catwalks widen into plazas where entire districts rise inside hollow petioles big enough to hold tower blocks. Walk farther still and the plazas give way to open terraces of nothing but leaf and sky; the settlement thins without ending, trailing into kilometers of empty scaffolding already threaded by exploratory vines.

Sound is constant and layered. Sap-pressure surges produce low-frequency groans that travel through the plant as seismic sighs; the same pressure, released at the leaf-edges, becomes a continuous white hiss of falling water. Human machinery overlays this: gear-motors chattering as aerial trams slide along tendon-like cables; the staccato bark of pressure valves venting excess steam; the dull ringing of maintenance crews hammering new rivets into older bark. Occasional biological thunderclaps echo when a leaf canopy collapses under its own weight, the impact felt through the entire plateau seconds before the sound arrives.

Because the plateau is so high, clouds sometimes form beneath rather than above, giving the impression that the city floats on a slow-moving ocean of vapor. Sunlight arrives filtered and shifting; midday is pale-silver, while late afternoon throws sharp shafts between staggered leaf tiers, momentarily lighting industrial smokestacks that have been colonized by hanging gardens of orange shelf-fungi. The fungi are the primary scavengers—anything metal left too long begins to dull, then to powder, then to sprout new mycelial ropes that reweave themselves into the plant matrix. Faunal presence is limited to pale, translucent mites the size of dogs that skate across condensation films on leaf surfaces; they sing in brittle clicks that harmonize with the city’s valves until something larger—wind or a dropped tool—scatters them.

The rules are simple and absolute. The plant is substrate, architecture, and resource; nothing may be cut that the city has not scheduled for harvest. Condensation belongs to the plant first, to turbines second, to inhabitants last. Elevation brings constant updrafts; anything that falls is expected to be caught by secondary mesh levels engineered into the lower canopies. Reality thins here at dusk: refractions multiply, ghost-images of walkways appear where no walkways exist, and the oldest trunks sometimes resonate at frequencies that make compasses spin. The inhabitants treat these moments as weather, not omen—another variable in a system already built to absorb flux.

The plateau extends until the eye gives up, its edges lost behind veils of its own rain. Somewhere far to the south the same organism continues, its profile broken only by the silhouettes of heavier industry: smelter-towers built inside fossilized seed-pods, their chimneys venting steam that the living structure immediately recaptures. Northward the terraces become more open, the scaffolding more tentative, until the city admits the plateau’s true scale: a single, ancient, half-mechanical creature so large that urbanization is only one of its current moods.
