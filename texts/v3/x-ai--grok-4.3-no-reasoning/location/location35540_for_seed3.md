The city of Caerdeep unfolds not across a single plane but up and along a great fracture that runs through the flank of the Hallowspire, a knife-cut ridge of black limestone and living glass. From any lower vantage the structures seem to climb forever: terraces of poured basalt and cast-iron scaffolding wrap the cliff, each new level grafted onto the last like barnacles on an endless hull. Roads are only ever partial—switchbacks that become vertical when the angle of the rock forces them, rope-rails hung from pitons that shiver when the slow wind pushes through. On the highest reachable ledges, gravity is more suggestion than law; walk the outermost iron balcony and the drop is longer than any fall that was ever measured, yet the city insists it still possesses an apex somewhere above.

Rain here arrives sideways and in installments. It never pours; instead the wind drags condensation sheets off the high glaciers and leaves them in crooked veils across the gantries. When the condensation thickens enough it descends as one unbroken curtain, hissing against hot exhaust vents and leaving the railings coated in salt-rimed ice even while the air temperature stays just above freezing. Between these curtains the rock is dry—barely. Every horizontal surface carries a film of capillary moisture that beads and evaporates in long, patient intervals, like a tide measured in weeks instead of hours.

Vegetation survives in pockets where the film lingers: rope-moss that grows like metallic hair along iron joists; needle-ferns rooted in insulation foam that seep mineral salts; phosphorescent algae that veins the interior of hollow girders and stains the dim light green at dusk. These pockets are always small—ten meters of ledge here, a slot canyon full of hanging gardens there—and always separated by yards of empty stone or latticework. The gaps are deliberate. The city’s architects designed for emptiness the way other cities design for plazas.

Inside the cliffs themselves, the industrial layer is still being born. Drill rigs larger than houses have been anchored for decades; their bits turn once every three hours, carving rooms that will eventually contain forges or turbine halls. Each turn removes only a thumb’s thickness of rock, but the vibrations travel outward in slow concentric rings. When the rings reach an already-carved chamber, the walls exhale a pale mist that settles into dew on the waiting machinery. Nothing is finished. Nothing is abandoned. The future halls already hum at the edge of hearing because the city knows, on a geological schedule, exactly what they will become.

Fauna follows the same unfinished logic. Herds of rope-hares—jointed, cable-furred mammals the size of dogs—traverse the vertical gardens on retractable claws. Between rain intervals they hibernate inside the hollow girders, bodies pressed together to conserve heat, hearts beating once every ninety seconds. Their waste is harvested by maintenance crews; it becomes the phosphor nutrient that keeps the algae colonies bright. Larger, less predictable creatures move through the unfinished shafts: shadow-eels that appear to swim through solid stone because they follow the cracks that have not yet widened into corridors. Their presence registers as a pressure change, a brief hush in the wind, and a cold that lingers after they pass.

The light is a permanent half-dawn. The Hallowspire’s summit, unseen and possibly unreachable, reflects just enough sun to keep the sky from black, while the perpetual low fog in the valley below swallows anything brighter. Streetlamps are unnecessary; instead the city’s electricity is shunted into warm filaments that line every handrail, glowing the same tired orange as a coal fire viewed through fog. The effect is that every surface looks slightly farther away than it is, and distances stretch until the next terrace feels like another country.

Sound travels in long, overlapping waves. The drills produce a bass throb felt more than heard. Between thuds the iron framework rings—tiny, crystalline chimes as moisture freezes and thaws inside bolt holes. The algae colonies sigh when they photosynthesize; the sigh is pitched just below human hearing and registers as an ache behind the eyes. Rope-hares occasionally scream, a metallic rasp that cuts across the low frequencies and vanishes again. Over weeks these layers of sound drift into one another, forming new harmonies that the city’s inhabitants learn to navigate by instinct rather than map.

The true peculiarity of Caerdeep is that its geometry is not fixed. When a new level of scaffolding is completed, the angle of the rock above it sometimes tilts—not suddenly, but over months—as if the mountain is adjusting itself around the construction. Old balconies that once faced west now face south-west; stairwells that descended ninety steps become ninety-one, then ninety-three, then stabilize again. Residents mark the changes with chalk lines that they re-measure every lunar cycle. The lines never match their previous positions, but the citizens treat the discrepancy the way sailors treat the motion of the tide: inevitable, measurable, and ultimately survivable.

Above the highest scaffold, the fracture continues into cloud. Below the lowest terrace, the same fracture continues into mist. Between them the city clings and grows, patient as the rock, unfinished as the rain.
