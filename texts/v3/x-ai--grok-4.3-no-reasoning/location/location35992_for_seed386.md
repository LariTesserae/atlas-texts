A high basin pressed against the spine of a shield-plateau, hot enough that the horizon shimmers like pale metal. The whole land sits a little above tree line yet has somehow remained a peat-saturated bowl three valleys wide; the plateau itself is the result of a long-lived vertical uplift of basalt that has not fully cooled at depth. The basalt columns rise as ribs, their faces scabbed by slow trickles that never dry. Fog that forms on the slopes each night slides downward in coherent sheets; by midday it has turned into a fine, salty mist that keeps the rock faces weeping while the rest of the sky stays cloudless.

No one has ever paved a path here. The trails you see are animal lines pressed into the black soil, narrow and slick where they cross the wettest seams. Those seams, in turn, are the mouths of aquifers forced upward by the pressure of the plateau’s weight; each seam opens into a short, fern-choked gorge that ends at a pool no deeper than a man’s chest yet never empty. Because the water is slightly warmer than the air, condensation beads on every upturned leaf and falls again in minute, audible pings. The sound is faint, but it is the loudest regular noise in the basin; distant thunder over the plateau rim arrives only as a subsonic roll felt through the soles.

Vegetation has worked itself into every crack. Iron-hard cushions of moss cap the basalt; between them grow lacework fern-trees whose fronds extend straight out rather than up, forming low, overlapping canopies just high enough to shade the saturated ground. In the shaded gaps, liverworts and liver-colored fungi rise shoulder-high, their surfaces dusted with spores that turn silver when the light catches them. The air smells of warm iron and bruised leaves, thick enough that breathing it feels like swallowing silk.

Fauna moves in constant, small increments. Tiny crabs the color of wet coal scuttle along the water films, dragging soft tubes of peat behind them; each tube becomes a new micro-dam that slows the next trickle for a few hours. Overhead, translucent, veined gliders—something between insect and bat—ride the mist sheets down-slope, their membranes catching the light as pale crescents before they vanish into the ferns again. Larger shapes sometimes breach the surface of the deepest pools: sinuous, blind eels whose lateral gills exhale a constant stream of tiny bubbles that pop in the heat. Nothing hunts by sound; the mist muffles everything except the dripping.

On the far side of the basin a single lava spine still exhales faint, rust-colored vapor, the only visible reminder that the rock beneath is still industrial in temperature. The vapor drifts in low coils across the pools and is captured by the fern canopies, condensing on the underside of the leaves into water that drips back into the same pools it came from. The cycle has no off-switch; each exhalation from the vent warms another pulse of groundwater that rises again, keeping the entire bowl at a steady, oppressive heat.

No wind moves the upper air. The only motion is the slow, downward creep of mist and the lateral slide of crabs and eels, an almost geological patience made audible by the small, repeated sound of water returning to water.
