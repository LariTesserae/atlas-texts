The stone throat narrows to a single, breathing passage that no wider than a ship’s oar.

Water is present everywhere yet is never still: it seeps in cold filaments down the black basalt, collects in the hollows of the knuckles of rock, and is driven upward again as a mist the color of dull lead.  On every surface a film of condensation gathers, breaks, and slides in traceries that repeat a map no cartographer has ever drawn.  When the mist grows thick enough, it falls as a brief rain that runs in straight vertical veins and vanishes into slots no finger can probe; moments later the same water returns, climbing from below in threads as fine as spider-silk, carrying grit from the hidden aquifer that lies a mile below the summit.

The walls of the passage rise sheer for twenty body-lengths, then pinch in an inverted V whose apex is lost in vapor.  Along the lower quarter, horizontal seams of mica catch the light like frozen foam; above these seams the rock is split by tension fractures wide enough for a hand to rest inside, but too narrow for any shoulder.  Where the fractures widen, pale rootlets of cloud-orchid have found purchase; their leaves are translucent, veined with silver that catches the ordinary daylight leaking from above.  These plants hang in motionless curtains until the wind outside changes, then they chime—tiny plates of silica clicking against one another—filling the shaft with a glassy, insect chorus.

Below the level of sight, the passage opens into a vertical amphitheater the size of a single nave.  Its floor is a bowl of black sand veined with quartz.  The sand moves constantly under the weight of the water that sluices through it; each grain is polished to a sphere, and when the wind funnels down the shaft the grains lift in a low, whispering sheet that abrades the ankles and calves of anyone standing there.  In the center of the bowl a single pedestal of basalt rises, its crown hollowed by centuries of drip erosion into the shape of an enormous cupped palm.  Inside the palm sits a shallow lake no wider than a cartwheel, perfectly circular and perfectly still except when something disturbs it from below.  Then a perfect ring travels outward and returns, never breaking the surface tension.

Around the pedestal the sand is not empty.  Every few body-lengths a low, knee-high cairn of water-polished stones has been stacked; inside each cairn sits a single egg of translucent calcite.  The eggs are kept warm by the rising mist, and inside each one a slow pulse can be felt—heartbeat of a creature not yet hatched, or perhaps a plant root coiled like an embryo.  Scattered among the cairns lie the broken halves of earlier eggs; the shards are sharp, faintly luminous at night when the moon is hidden.

Above the sand bowl the shaft walls are alive with small vertebrates.  Pale, long-limbed salamanders no thicker than a thumb cling to the mica seams and feed on the condensation itself, drawing moisture through their bellies while their backs remain bone-dry.  When the salamanders move, they leave behind a faint phosphorescent trail that lasts only until the next drip erases it.  Higher still, in the narrowing V of the rock, a colony of blind swifts has carved hundreds of finger-sized holes; each dawn and dusk the birds pour upward in a single spiral ribbon of wings, the sound of their passage a sustained, metallic roar that shakes the mica plates loose and sends them spinning down like thrown coins.

Occasionally a heavier form moves.  Something with the mass of a young ox but the silhouette of a mantis emerges from a horizontal cleft too narrow for eyes to penetrate.  Its carapace is the same black basalt as the walls, veined with the same quartz.  It pauses at the edge of the sand bowl, tasting the air with jointed antennae that glow faint blue, then slips back into the rock.  Where it walks, the sand rearranges itself into a new pattern of ridges that will not be erased until the next pulse of water from below.

The light inside the passage is ordinary daylight filtered through the vapor.  It arrives in shafts the color of river stone, never quite bright enough to cast hard shadows, never quite dim enough to allow darkness to settle.  At midday the shafts converge on the central pedestal and the lake inside the stone palm throws back a single, unwavering coin of reflection that travels up the shaft like a slow beacon.  At night the same coin becomes a diffuse glow, fed by the bioluminescence of the salamanders and the slow fission of the calcite eggs.

Wind enters only at the very top, where the shaft meets the alpine sky.  There the mist condenses into horizontal shelves of cloud that spill inward, tumble, and are re-evaporated by warmer air rising from below.  Each exchange produces a soft thunder that rolls downward in slow, layered waves; the sound is multiplied by the mica plates and arrives at the sand bowl as a continuous low chord, interrupted only when one of the swifts strikes a resonant seam and the entire shaft answers in a single, ringing tone.

No metal has ever been shaped here.  The only tools are water, wind, and the patient dissolution of stone.  The only marks left by passage are the calcite eggs and the faint trails of salamander light.  Everything else is either growing or being worn away at the same deliberate pace, each process measured in centuries rather than seasons.
