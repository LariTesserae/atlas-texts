The basin is an eight-hundred-kilometer slab of fused silica, its surface stippled with microscopic fractures that refract the perpetual low-angle light into a constant, shifting iridescence. No atmosphere lifts the horizon; rather, the air is a dense suspension of metallic dust that trembles like heat-haze yet never rises or falls. The temperature sits at a stable thirty-one degrees; the dust itself carries that warmth, making the sky feel as though it is pressed directly against the ground.

A single vertical network of tall, translucent lattices—each column a single crystal ninety meters across—anchors the city to this floor. From a distance they appear white, but they are laced with cobalt veins that pulse when currents surge. These monoliths are not buildings; they are the city. Horizontal membranes of metallic glass grow outward from the lattices like shelves of still water. Walkways, arcades, entire districts lie stacked within these membranes, each layer thin as a fingernail yet carrying the weight of ten thousand inhabitants and the machinery that sustains them. The membranes flex when anything heavier than a person crosses them, turning the entire city into a slow, breathing bellows.

Water exists only as a film, thinner than condensation, sliding along the crystal veins of the lattices. The water never pools or drips; it moves at a steady centimeter per hour in every direction, a perpetual circulatory system that cools the crystals and carries dissolved ions upward. Where the films cross each other, interference patterns generate audible interference tones—pure sine waves whose beat frequencies drop below twenty hertz. The resonance is strong enough that the city’s inhabitants cannot separate their own pulse from the planet’s.

Between the crystal towers, vertical shafts of open space still remain, yet these shafts are packed with vertical traffic: flying vehicles no larger than a briefcase move in densely ordered streams, exchanging parcels, passengers, and tiny spheres of plasma that keep the membrane tension balanced. When two vehicles pass within centimeters, they trade micro-currents through induction so fine that sparks of violet light appear between them like needles stitching the air. The cumulative noise of this exchange is a continuous, over-driven drone that has replaced weather. People speak by pressing small resonators against one another’s sternum; airborne language would be lost in the roar.

No vegetation grows here, yet the city is far from empty. Symbiotic micro-organisms the size of pollen grains live within minute pores in the crystal lattices. They metabolize the sliding films, precipitating microscopic nodes of metal that re-plate the crystal walls each evening. At dusk—when the light angle shifts only a few degrees—these nodes glow faintly orange, tracing vein networks that are the only visual map of the city’s daily repair cycle. Larger fauna consist of meter-long, lenticular drones whose interiors are nothing but stored electrical potential. They drift between membranes, discharging into any surface that registers mechanical fatigue; the discharge sounds like distant artillery and momentarily quiets the resonance tones, the only relief the population ever experiences from the constant vibration.

The membranes themselves are the true streets. Instead of solid pavement, they are three-dimensional interference patterns that become temporarily solid where two opposing frequencies cancel. A pedestrian feels a momentary firmness underfoot; a half-second later the pattern shifts and the foot sinks slightly, then rises again, so walking is a series of micro-balances rather than steps. When a heavy cargo crosses, entire neighborhoods briefly sag several centimeters and then rebound, sending a ripple of cancellation nodes up the lattice like the movement of a single cell. The ripple is felt by inhabitants kilometers away as a minute tightening of the sternum.

No rain falls. Instead, the metallic dust itself occasionally condenses into half-centimeter droplets that streak downward along the crystal veins like droplets on a spiderweb, then re-evaporate before reaching the basin floor. The flashes of evaporation are the only point sources of light strong enough to cast true shadows. During these moments the vastness of the place declares itself: from any membrane you can see a dozen others stacked above and below you, each one a thin horizon, each one carrying its own traffic and faint orange vein-maps, all of it suspended within a single continuous drone louder than any storm.
