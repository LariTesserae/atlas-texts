They call it the Rootweave.

Seen from high orbit it is an emerald bruise across a dead world, a planet-scale raft of living architecture that has been growing for so long its edge reaches across three time zones and its depth is measured in miles. From the ridgelines that still remember bedrock you cannot see its margin; you only know the horizon itself is a slow-turning green wall that rises into cloud, curls, and descends again as an endless curtain of hanging gardens.

Below the canopy, water does not fall. Water is grown. Every leaf ends in a half-transparent bead of condensed night-fog that never quite releases, hanging until some faint thermal drift makes it roll into the next bead, a thousand-mile capillary motion that a traveler can watch for weeks if the wind stays gentle. When the beads finally fall they fall inside the walls themselves, running through vein-cavities lined with sponge-root, emerging again as curtains of cool mist at the joints of the megastructures. The rainfall is a side-effect rather than an event.

Everything is built. Nothing remembers a time before the building. The plant-architecture remembers its own blueprints: the way branches cross themselves into perfect 120-degree triangulations at every scale, the way the lattices leave exactly the right hollow chambers for the temperature to be held even when the sun is only a faint red smear two hundred miles above. Stone and metal are still present but invisible, wrapped in concentric cylinders of cambium, phloem, xylem, bark like armor-plate thirty feet thick. Walk any distance and the cambium shows colors that encode purpose: blue-green for load-bearing, orange for gas exchange, black for memory. The builders worked only in wood and in the living membranes that conduct the planet’s own piezoelectric pulses; all other material has been grown over and away.

Light is never direct. It arrives as an inheritance. The upper canopy filters the red sun into a shifting rose-gold lattice that moves like the shadows of gigantic slow fish. Where the lattice intersects vertical shafts, the shafts turn it into a river of motionless ruby mist. Deeper still the shafts pinch to capillaries so narrow that a human shape would have to crawl for hours; in those capillaries the light is already the color of arterial blood, pulsing with the photosynthetic rhythm of the world itself.

The silence is not empty. The silence is the sound of water molecules deciding, very slowly, where to go next. Every few hours a single wooden joint, somewhere, expands or contracts by the width of a cell, and the resonance travels through the megastructure at the speed of sound in living wood. The note is so low that most creatures feel it in the long bones rather than hear it; the smallest flying animals orient their migrations to it the way terrestrial birds once used magnetic fields.

Fauna has adapted to the void-between. The density parameter creates a cathedral emptiness: enormous hollow chambers the size of cities whose walls are never closer than a mile, yet the chambers are crossed by filament bridges no thicker than a finger, alive with traffic. Hanging gardens drift on counterweights of gas bladders so vast they would be mistaken for thunderheads if you could see their entire scale. Between the bridges, air is clean enough that you can taste the metallic sweetness of the resin the plants exhale to seal micro-fractures. Creatures that look like six-winged dragonflies the size of dogs live in that sweetness; they never land, only fold their wings and drift until the resin-coat on their bodies has been renewed.

The weirdness parameter shows itself at the scale interfaces. Walk a single corridor long enough and its cross-section will begin to repeat at larger and larger intervals: first the pattern of veins on a leaf, then the pattern of corridors viewed from an aerial survey, then the pattern of entire megastructures viewed from orbit, and back again. The repetition is not metaphor; it is functional. The world uses fractal reinforcement so that any wound large enough to be seen from space will be healed by the same cellular logic that heals a torn leaf.

Erosion has finished its work; there are no sharp edges left. Even the outermost structural ribs have grown rounded shoulders where condensation runs in sheets for centuries, polishing them to the color of old jade. The balance is not static; it is a slow exhale. Every thousand years the outermost layer of bark thickens by a hand-span and the interior chambers adjust their humidity by one percent, a breath slow enough that no single generation of anything that breathes can notice it, yet the cumulative effect is that the entire planet has already re-designed itself twice since the last time its star flared.

Nothing is natural. Nothing is finished. Everything is still happening, but at the speed at which a continent decides to become something else.
