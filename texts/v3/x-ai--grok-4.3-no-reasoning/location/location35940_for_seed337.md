In the subterranean district that the map-makers still call the Lower Hollows, the rock itself has not finished arriving.  Where ceilings ought to be, curtains of pale calcite hang in wet folds that gleam when lantern-light slides over them; where floors ought to be, the same mineral is rising in knobby stalagmites so new that the drip-cups are still soft and the water that feeds them lands with a single, distinct tick.  Nothing here is yet settled into permanence; every surface is in the middle of becoming.

The passage that threads through the district is wide enough for two mule-carts to pass abreast, yet the walls are crowded.  On the left, a row of barrel-vaulted chambers has been cut and roofed with dressed limestone; on the right, the natural dripstone has been coaxed into pillars, each one braced with raw timber so fresh the sap still scents the air.  Between pillar and wall, between wall and wall, there is scarcely a hand-span left; stone-masons, gypsum-scrapers, and young apprentices move in single file, their leather aprons brushing, their copper chisels clicking softly as they trim the last calcite from a new lintel.

Light arrives through a single rift sixty paces overhead, a crack that admits a steady column of daylight.  The beam is broken by threads of falling water; each drop catches a prism-flash before it shatters on the unfinished floor.  The air is cool and slightly metallic.  No wind travels here, so the vapour rises in faint, stationary veils above the workmen’s braziers.  When the daylight fails at dusk, the braziers are fed with slow-burning moss-coal; the smoke stains the stone above them in broad, upward smears that will someday become the district’s first cartographic soot-maps.

Vegetation is little more than a green-black stubble of liverworts clinging to damp mortar joints.  The plants sprout, yellow, and collapse within a week, feeding a thin film of humus that the masons scrape away each morning.  No trees, no shrubs, only these momentary colonies that mark where the drip is regular.

The only permanent residents are the gypsum-crickets.  They are finger-long, chalk-colored, and almost translucent; when one lands on a lantern-glass it vanishes, its body matching the flame.  They feed on the minute calcite precipitate that forms overnight on every tool left lying.  During the quiet midday lull their faint, dry stridulation is the only audible motion.

The district’s single rule is that nothing may rest in one place longer than a single work-cycle.  The builders learned early that any timber left on the floor will begin to petrify at the base within three days; if a block of dressed stone is not mortared into place, the continual seepage will lock it to the living rock and make repositioning impossible.  Therefore every afternoon the overseers walk the length of the passage with chalked tally-sticks, marking each object that must be lifted, shifted, or used before nightfall.

Farther along the passage the unfinished chambers give way to the first occupied dwellings: low, barrel-roofed rooms whose walls are still striped with the chisel-marks of their excavation.  Inside each room, families keep their belongings on wall-niches so the floor can continue to rise.  A child’s wooden stool stands on two stones that were not there yesterday; tomorrow the stones will be taller and the stool will need another prop.  The people of the Lower Hollows have accepted that their ceilings inch downward while their floors inch upward, and they adjust their postures and their furniture rather than resist the motion of the living stone.

At the far end of the district a single, larger cavern remains open to the drip.  Its ceiling is a fretwork of new straw stalactites, so closely spaced that a person standing beneath them must turn sideways to pass.  Here the masons have stopped work.  They have left the straws in place as a reminder that the district, however packed and industrious it becomes, will always be younger than the water that continues, drop by patient drop, to rearrange it.
