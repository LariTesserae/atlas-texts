**The Fracture Plateau**

A highland expanse has recently torn itself upward, the crust still shifting in sluggish heaves that you can feel through your bones. Rock plates, each the size of districts, tilt at angles that have not yet decided whether they are mountains or valleys; their edges have not softened into slopes, only into raw, knife-plain fractures. Because the surface is almost waterless, every surface is coated with a pale mineral bloom—an efflorescence of whitish salts and borates that form an endlessly fine, crystalline dust. Wind, when it rises, does not carry moisture; it carries this dust aloft in slow, luminous sheets that make distant plateaus vanish and reappear as if the land were breathing itself in and out.

Vegetation still manages to appear. Out of hairline crevices and scree pockets, colonies of black-green succulents grow in waxy rosettes no higher than an ankle; their leaves are encrusted with the same white salts, so that each plant looks dipped in hoarfrost. They draw what little atmospheric moisture condenses during the long, dim twilights, and they live in near-total silence—photosynthesizing, respiring, and seeding at a pace measured in decades. Between the plants, kilometers of bare mineral remain, speckled only by the occasional fallen boulder.

Temperature hovers just above freezing. Mist sometimes forms low to the ground when the plateau’s own heat leaks upward, producing drifting banks of fog that the mineral dust sifts through, turning the fog into faint, luminous veils. Nothing moves quickly here. The succulents open a few new leaves each year; the rock faces themselves breathe outward, millimeters per season, widening the cracks that will someday become ravines.

Human presence registers only as trace architecture: occasional standing-stone tripods, their legs sunk into clefts and their crosspieces etched with notches. Some have ropes of braided mineral fiber still dangling, now stiffened by the salts. These scaffolds were built to measure the widening of a fissure or the rate at which dust settles; their builders have long gone, yet the ropes remain, slowly petrifying.

Light arrives filtered and fractured. An unseen source diffuses through an upper layer of crystalline aerosols; rays arrive oblique and shimmering, producing an endless twilight in which shadows move only when the aerosols shift. At rare intervals, a deeper light pulses—a rippling band of violet that sweeps the plateau like a slow lighthouse beam. When it passes over a tripod, the etched notches briefly fluoresce, and the ropes throw off glints as though the cords themselves are counting time.

Sound is constant and overpowering. The plateau’s own growth produces a low, continuous grinding like continents sliding past one another; the crystals of the surface, expanding and contracting with the temperature cycle, emit high, brittle chimes that layer upon one another until the air becomes a vibrating lattice of notes. The succulents add their own contribution: each rosette contracts once every few days, releasing a brief, dry rattle of salt flakes. Over everything rides an infrasonic moan generated by wind pushing through the widening fissures far below—an almost tactile pressure that never resolves into a single pitch. Together these layers create an acoustic field so saturated that even silence, were it possible, would feel loud.

Time here is measured not in hours but in the slow migration of light-bands and the widening of fissures. The landscape is still assembling itself; nothing has reached a finished state. The eye learns to watch the horizon for new clefts opening, for new stone plates rising by a hand’s width, for another tripod—yet unbuilt—to take its place among the scattered relics already half-swallowed by the mineral bloom.
