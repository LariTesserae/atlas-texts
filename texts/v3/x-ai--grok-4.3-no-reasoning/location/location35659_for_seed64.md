The chamber is not a room that was dug out so much as a single colossal fracture within the living rock that has, over unthinkable ages, been colonized by a kind of slow organic cement. From its jagged ceiling—twenty paces overhead but felt rather than seen—luminous resin has dripped and pooled and re-hardened in successive skins until the stone is sheathed in a nacreous, faintly ribbed integument. The same resin has flowed outward across the uneven floor, so the whole cavity feels upholstered rather than excavated, yet every contour remembers the original break: sharp angles rounded only enough to betray immense patience, not intention.

Across that upholstered floor the vegetation grows in a single, unbroken density. Each plant is no taller than a forearm, but they rise shoulder-to-shoulder, leaf against leaf, in a living tessellation whose individual blades or fronds are too intimately crowded to cast separate shadows. Their foliage ranges from the black-green of deep bottle glass to a blued viridian so dark it registers as velvet shadow until light grazes it; under the prevailing dimness the effect is of an immense, continuous bruise of vegetation. The cool air is saturated with their transpiration, yet nothing moves: there is no convection current, no breath of circulation; the humidity sits where it has always sat.

Embedded in this biomass are the remnants of a single, long-abandoned industrial process. At irregular intervals the resin floor is interrupted by low plinths of poured iron, now flanged and softened by the same slow secretions that coat the stone. On two of these plinths squat drum-shaped engines the size of millstones: their casings are verdigris-dark, and between the casings and the living leaves there is no gap whatever—fronds push directly through perished gaskets, coils of rootlet cinch abandoned drive-belts to immobility. The engines are not ruined; they have been absorbed. Where once there must have been belts, gears, gauges, the vegetation has grown into the negative space of each component, filling every vacancy so that the machinery appears less dismantled than translated into a botanical equivalent of its former function. The result is neither machine reclaimed by nature nor garden invaded by machine; it is a single object in two simultaneous states.

The only light arrives in fixed, permanent position from a single thread of violet chemiluminescence that leaks, without pulse or flicker, from a hairline seam high on one wall. It is less illumination than an accent, a continuous annotation. The light renders the resin surfaces slick, almost wet-looking, but dryness is absolute; the perceived moisture is an artifact of total, unchanging saturation of another kind: density of matter, density of stillness.

No current of air exists to be heard; instead the chamber is occupied by a single, constant composite tone. It is produced by the innumerable leaves themselves, whose cuticles vibrate at a frequency just below human hearing, transduced upward by the iron plinths into a steady, infrasonic drone that nonetheless fills the space like a solid. The sound has no attack, no decay; it is simply present, the acoustic equivalent of the resin that has been exuding for the same duration.

Nothing arrives, nothing departs. The ecology is therefore not a cycle but an identity: the plants respire the same molecules they have always respired; the engines contain the same charge they contained when the resin first reached their terminals; the thread of light has always leaked from that seam. The visitor’s arrival changes nothing except the set of relations that can be observed; the chamber itself remains what it has been—an equilibrium so crowded and so motionless that it registers as a single, continuous material event rather than a place where events occur.
