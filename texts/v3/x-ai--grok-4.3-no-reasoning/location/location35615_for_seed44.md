**The Moss-Tier**

This is a low, warm, wet city woven almost entirely from living plant tissue. Its streets are not laid stone but elongated, fibrous mats of trunk-moss that knit themselves together into level walking surfaces, supple enough to give underfoot and then slowly re-level after each passage. Above them rise multi-storied structures whose walls are continuous sheets of living cambium, their veins pumping nutrient sap in slow, rhythmic pulses that register as faint vibrations through bootsoles. These living walls seal at the joints with glassy resins exuded by specialized cells; the resins harden into translucent panels that let in the filtered daylight while blocking the heavier rain.

Water never pools here. Instead it is threaded through the entire system in capillary gutters that wind between the moss tiers. Every surface is scored with hair-fine channels that slope only a fraction of a degree, so the flow is almost imperceptible—a slow, continuous film that keeps the living architecture perpetually moist. From the eaves of buildings hang feathery drip-moss, each strand a translucent green filament that conducts condensation downward until the strand ends in a bulb that releases the collected droplet directly into a feeder gutter. The sound is the aggregate of countless such quiet releases: a steady liquid whisper, like rain on glass heard from inside, layered with the deeper bass note of sap circulating through the thicker trunks. Only when a vehicle or walker passes does the volume rise: the living road emits a muted crackle as its topmost layer briefly compresses, then relaxes, like a carpet of tiny, delicate joints.

Steam rises in thin ribbons wherever the sap-carrying veins near the surface are exposed to the warm air; the ribbons drift upward, condense against the undersides of the overhead moss bridges, and re-join the capillary system. In the densest quarters the air feels almost broth-like, saturated and faintly sweet, the temperature holding at a constant, skin-warm level. Engine-driven pumps—hulking cylinders of polished ironwood bark wrapped in brass fittings—sit within sunken courtyards, their pistons moving on yearly cycles. When they fire (once every few weeks), the muffled thud travels through the plant substrate like a deep heartbeat felt in bone; most of the time they are silent, their flywheels turning so gradually that only a faint creak betrays the stored torque.

Fauna is scarce. The city’s architecture is too continuously occupied by the plant substrate itself; there are no niches left for birds, mammals, or even large insects. The only non-plant presences are tiny, transparent water mites that skate the capillary films; their motion produces a barely audible ticking when thousands of them move at once across a single panel. Otherwise the soundscape belongs entirely to the city’s own fluids: the soft percussion of drip-bulbs, the syrupy rush inside larger veins, the resonant creak of cambium stretching a micrometer at a time.

The entire settlement occupies an area no larger than a modest town square, yet it contains four distinct vertical layers—ground-level transit moss, mid-level habitation cambium, upper-level fruiting canopies, and a thin condensation roof. Boundaries are marked not by walls but by abrupt changes in plant species: one step may take you from the tight-woven trunk-moss of a residential lane onto the broader, springier leaf-mats of an arterial concourse. Because the living substrate heals and re-grows, the city never looks finished; it is always in the process of knitting over its own minor wounds, smoothing old footprints, and slowly closing gaps left by the infrequent removal of a dead branch or the replacement of a corroded brass fitting.

Light filters through the translucent panels in a dappled, chlorophyll-green spectrum, enough for human eyes yet never harsh. The overall impression is of a single enormous organism that has consented to contain human activity within its body, its slow metabolism dictating both the pace of the city and the scale at which any human intervention can succeed.
