The basin of Three-Touch is a city that never finished dying. It lies beneath a shell of ancient flood basalt, cracked open centuries ago by an undulating black river that still passes through in pulses. The river itself is the only reliable water source; its course is measured in slow, heavy surges that arrive every fourteen days, rise for one night, then drain through sinkholes at the city’s western rim. Between pulses the basalt channels stand slick with condensation that beads in long, salt-whiskered rows down walls and lintels, giving the streets a perpetual damp sheen without ever pooling into actual pools.

The city is stone layered upon stone, every tier cut from the same dark basalt that frames the basin. Roofs are low, capped by flat plates of iron shale whose edges have worn to the color of wet slate. Nothing grows on these plates, yet their surfaces are never truly bare; they host a network of dark brown fungal mats that spread like arterial lace, feeding on condensation rather than light. The fungus is harmless to people so long as it is never allowed to reach full fruiting; when it does, the spore cloud arrives as a half-heard susurrus that coats the tongue with iron-taste.

Three-Touch’s streets are wide but empty of traffic. Most intersections are occupied by single stone pillars—kiln-fired ceramic cores inside dressed basalt—that act as load-bearing anchors for the low vaulting above. These pillars bear hand-chipped pictographs: simple ladders, water drops, and radiating lines that indicate depth rather than direction. The pictographs have been polished smooth by generations of hands seeking grip in the dark; where the city lies directly beneath the roof of the flood basalt, small perforations allow shafts of light that shift across floors and walls as the day lengthens. The shafts are never still; they slide slowly across old water stains, catching on the raised edges of the pictographs and projecting them onto opposite walls like moving shadows.

Fauna here is matter-of-fact rather than exotic. Large, flightless cricket analogues the size of small dogs clack along the upper edges of the vaulting; they drink condensation from the shale plates and leave coin-sized pits where their mandibles rasp. Below them, flat-bodied skink-lizards the color of kiln soot scuttle through the narrow gaps between pillar and wall, emerging only when the crickets molt. Occasionally one lizard will drag a discarded carapace down into a wall niche to feed; the sound is a dry rasp that dies quickly. No larger predators are visible, but the tunnels that slant beneath the main plazas are said to harbor nest-clusters of blind eels that surface during the river pulse, attracted by the vibration of returning water.

The only built mechanisms that still function are gravity-driven: stepped cisterns carved into the central plaza catch the night river and funnel a single channel through the city’s lower tiers, where clay pipes with simple wooden valves distribute measured trickles into household jars. Every valve is operated by hand-polished bone toggles; each toggle bears the tooth-mark signature of the family that owns the rights to that outlet. When the river is absent the valves are left open; the residual drip-echo is the only regular sound apart from the cricket rasp.

The turbulence of the place is seasonal rather than instantaneous. Every two weeks, when the river returns, the entire acoustic signature changes for six hours: the drip-echo becomes a low, continuous roar beneath the basalt; the cricket calls accelerate; the lizards press themselves flat against the warmest walls. The turbulence is felt less as storm and more as an enormous held breath that finally releases. Between pulses the city slips back into a bright, slow silence; light shafts creep, condensation gathers, fungal maps lengthen by millimeters.

Three-Touch’s strangest feature is not visible at first glance. The iron-shale roof plates occasionally produce faint electromagnetic crackles when struck by the moving light shafts—soundless static that travels through bone rather than air. Residents learn to sense the change as a pressure shift behind the eyes; they mark time by it when the river is late. The static is strongest where the shale lies directly above an old kiln pit, suggesting some interaction between residual heat and the basin’s latent metallic veins, though no one has the metallurgy to investigate.

In the long intervals between surges, the only movement is the slow migration of condensation down every vertical surface and the incremental lengthening of the fungal lace across the shale. The city remains intact, unchanged except for these quiet accretions, until the next pulse arrives and rewrites the soundscape for a single night.
