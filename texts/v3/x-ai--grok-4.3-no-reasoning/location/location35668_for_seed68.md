The Borrovale Bottoms are a single flat hollow, scarcely six hundred paces across, yet every inch of it is worked ground. The depression was once a river meander; the river itself is gone, but a memory of it lingers in the slow, rusty seep that threads the center of the hollow like a vein. Moisture collects where the ground dips, darkens the brick-colored clay, and never dries completely, even under the ordinary daylight that falls without shadows or glare.

The hollow is hemmed by low concrete berms—old flood walls—now softened by mats of pale moss and low, silvery grasses. Between these berms, the land is parcelled into narrow plots no wider than a loading dock. In each plot a different machine sits half-buried: a rivet press, a belt grinder, a squat dynamo whose copper windings are furred with verdigris. They are not abandoned so much as paused; their gearboxes tick every few minutes as cooling metal contracts. Between the machines grow stands of trembling sedge and waist-high reeds whose seed-heads rattle like distant maracas when the breeze moves them. The reeds never quite touch one another; each clump is an island in a sea of packed clay that still carries the faint grid of long-ago cart tracks.

Small concrete footings rise between the plots, their edges sharp, their mortar still pale. They support roofless frames of angle iron from which hang loops of drive belt, now colonized by climbing peas whose tendrils follow the spiral grooves once meant for V-belts. The peas drop pale blossoms onto the clay; the blossoms are gathered by a constant low tide of small brown beetles whose wing-cases click like typewriter keys. Farther in, a single smokestack—brick, two stories—leans five degrees west. Its flue is plugged with a living stopper of trumpet vines whose orange flowers pulse, open and shut, to the rhythm of warm updrafts from the idle furnaces below.

Temperature holds steady in the cool middle register, never quite warm enough to steam the moisture from the clay, never cold enough to stiffen the oil that still beads on exposed gears. The air smells of turned earth, hot copper, and the faint, sweet rot of reed roots. Sounds arrive in slow surges: the clack of the beetles, the creak of settling iron, the low, bronchial sigh of a ventilator fan that turns once every quarter hour when thermostatic vanes finally trip. Nothing hurries. Even the smoke that leaks from the vine-clogged stack rises in a single, patient coil that spreads only after it clears the berm and meets the ordinary light above.

A narrow-gauge track, its rails burnished by hand-pushed trolleys, runs from the smokestack to a corrugated shed at the hollow’s lip. The shed doors stand open; inside, a waterwheel—fed by the seep—turns an overhead line shaft at roughly one revolution per minute. The shaft still carries leather belts to a row of stamping presses whose dies are shaped like leaves. Each slow descent of a die presses another vein into a sheet of tin already patterned by earlier, identical descents. The tin sheets are stacked in the open air, one upon the next, and on their surfaces the reed seeds land, germinate, and lift the upper sheets by a millimeter or two before someone—something—comes to harvest the new crop of metal leaves.

Across the hollow, the concrete berms are beginning, very slowly, to crack. In each crack a single sapling of ironwood has rooted; its trunk is no thicker than a finger, its leaves already stamped with the same vein-pattern the presses create. At this scale, the forest and the foundry are exchanging blueprints, and the exchange itself is the only industry the Bottoms still runs.
