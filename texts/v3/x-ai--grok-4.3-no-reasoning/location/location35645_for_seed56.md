The land here has not yet finished arriving.  
Everything still possesses the texture of work-in-progress, a half-built floor that never quite settles.

At this depth—some thirty body-lengths beneath the ordinary surface—the rock is not yet rock. It is still a paste of almost-geology, chilled to the temperature of deep winter wells, slick with continuous film. Dew beads on every exposed curve and refuses to fall; instead it slides along gravity’s faintest inclination, collecting, pooling, disappearing into microscopic channels that will not become fractures for another hundred thousand years. Between the wet sheets live swaths of translucent plant tissue, membrane-thin, rose-pale, veined with living silver that carries water both upward and laterally. They press against one another like pages pressed between a bookbinder’s boards, yet each page drinks from its neighbours.

Because there are no spaces empty of life, the “floor” is a living lattice of these layered sheets, stacked until the living mass itself begins to bear the overlying weight. Between the sheets, seams of darker, cartilaginous rhizomes have already started to mineralise; in another age they will be pillars. For now they are only soft cables, bent double under compression and quietly re-knitting. Every few dozen spans, a node swells outward into a fist-sized bladder filled with slightly warmer fluid; the bladders rise and sink on cycles measured in seasons, a slow communal breathing that never quite syncs from one cluster to the next. When a bladder deflates it releases a fine mist that drifts upward for only a hand’s breadth before being reabsorbed by the ceiling.

Fauna here is everywhere and nowhere. The sheets themselves are cohabited by microscopic motile bodies, smaller than dust motes, that travel inside living xylem like silverfish in library stacks. Larger residents move more deliberately: limb-less, cartilage-soft “rovers” the size of open palms glide along the sheets by secreting temporary adhesive pads, then dissolve them again. Their passage leaves a temporary glistening trail that other sheets quickly drink back, erasing evidence. Occasionally a larger drifter descends from the dimness above—an inflated sac filled with buoyant gas and festooned with feeding tendrils that comb the sheets for the microscopic passengers. The encounter is soundless to human ears, yet the sheets quiver for long minutes afterward, sending a low-frequency thrum through the living floor.

Light arrives from nowhere particular. It is the same faint cyan that leaks from stressed minerals when they are compressed, generated inside the coldest veins and diffusing outward until absorbed by the silver veins of the plant tissue. The air—if the word applies—is supersaturated with water vapour and trace aromatic esters exuded by the living sheets. The vapour never condenses into drops that fall; it simply thickens until it diffuses back into the sheets again. The resultant mist is dense enough to carry scent, thin enough that distant movement still registers as a change in light.

The dominant sound is a layered, almost musical low-frequency friction produced when adjacent sheets exchange water at different rates. Over hours the tones shift as temperature gradients migrate, creating slow glissandi and overlapping drones that the ear learns to treat as silence. Louder, but still sub-vocal, are the bladder pulses: each deflation produces a soft percussive thud felt more through the sheets than heard. Far rarer, and stranger, is the occasional release of stored elastic tension when a rhizome straightens by a millimetre; the snap is audible as a single bright click, after which the surrounding tissue holds perfectly still for the length of a slow breath.

No outside agency has yet arrived to name these processes or to claim them. The space remains cathedral-small, bounded on all sides by the unfinished edge where the paste-rock still oozes inward, millimetre by patient millimetre. Until the boundaries close, until the bladders synchronise, until the rhizomes remember how to become stone, the whole arrangement continues its unhurried, water-heavy work of becoming a place.
