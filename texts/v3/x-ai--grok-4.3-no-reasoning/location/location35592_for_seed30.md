A rise of unfinished rock, not yet weathered into patience, lifts from mist that refuses to clear. Water has not chosen its final routes here; it seeps and stops, pools and disappears again, leaving pale calcite skins that glisten like newly minted skin. What little soil exists sits in cupped depressions no wider than a shield, already threaded with hair-fine roots that drink before the water can decide to move.

Above, fog condenses against the rock face and runs downward in braids, each droplet carrying its own faint charge. Where two braids meet, a thin aurora of static light flickers—brief, harmless, almost conversational. The same charge drifts in the open air, turning droplets into slow, tumbling lanterns no brighter than dusk.

Between these drizzles the ground is pale and pocked: surfaces still sharp from their separation, edges yet to round. Sparse tussocks of a silver-green sedge have anchored where fractures allow a hair’s width of dust; their blades ring, softly metallic, whenever a droplet strikes. Scattered through the sedge are low domes of what look like kiln-fired clay, each no larger than a crouched person. They are not ruins; their seams are still warm, the clay still breathing faint heat. Inside each dome a lattice of obsidian threads pulses with subsonic rhythm—pressure regulators, living kilns maintaining an atmosphere fractionally richer in oxygen than the mist outside. Small ports open and shut at intervals, exhaling warm vapor that rises, cools, and rains back onto the same dome, closing the loop.

Above the domes the air is never empty. Iridescent midges, each the size of a lentil, trace helical paths in tight squadrons. When two squadrons intersect they exchange brief lattices of blue light—mating codes or weather reports—before scattering again. Larger flyers, membranous and tri-winged, coast between updrafts created by the warm clay. Their undersides are translucent; one can watch half-digested condensate glowing inside them as they metabolize atmospheric charge into lift.

Now and then a dome’s port flares wider. A warm pulse travels outward through the sedge; the metallic blades ring louder, and the midges drop for an instant, re-aligning themselves to the new rhythm. In the same moment a thread of deeper mist descends, thicker than the rest, smelling faintly of struck flint. When it touches rock it does not soak in; it beads, crawls, and begins to carve—embryonic rivulets feeling their way toward an unseen drainage that does not yet exist. The rivulets shine with the same static that lights the braids; as they move they etch hairline grooves, the first drafting lines of a future watershed.

Sound carries strangely. The ringing sedge forms a continuous overtone; the clay domes add a slower, felt thrum; the insects generate overlapping clicks like a thousand tiny abacuses. Human ears hear none of these as separate noises. Instead there is a single, dense quiet—the loudness of many small processes insisting on their own continuation.

At the scale of a large room the terrain already contains everything required for a world still deciding what shape it will hold: water writing its first maps, heat remembering how to rise, life negotiating which frequencies it will sing in next. Nothing here has finished arriving, yet nothing is waiting.
