Above the world, the sky is normal; below, a continent is being born.

The land rises in long, unfinished slabs that have not yet learned the dignity of erosion. Pale rock, cracked but not smoothed, surfaces like whale-skin through a skin of water that is never quite a lake and never quite a river—more a low tide that forgot to fall. Every few hundred meters the water finds a seam and slips downward, whispering as it does; the seams themselves are still deciding whether they will become canyons or veins.

The temperature hovers just above freezing, enough that the water condenses onto every exposed crystal and then freezes into a transient glaze that cracks again each midday. Breath becomes visible at once, then vanishes; the difference between fog and your own exhalation is mostly a matter of scale.

Vegetation arrives late to these altitudes. What grows does so in the joints of the stone: low, blue-black lichens that seem to bruise when touched, and thread-thin reeds whose roots drink from the film of water sliding over the rock. Between these patches the ground is empty for long, wind-scoured distances; one can stand in the middle of a flat expanse and see no plant larger than a thumbnail in any direction. Yet the emptiness is not sterile. Beneath the water film, microfungal nets lace the cracks; above it, spores drift in nearly invisible clouds that settle only when the wind sleeps.

Fauna is abundant in the way desert fauna is abundant: most of it too small or too slow or too translucent to register at first glance. Pale copepods the size of eyelashes orbit in temporary puddles no wider than a footprint; they complete entire generations between one sunrise and the next. Larger, jointed creatures—something between a tardigrade and a shrimp—emerge only at dusk, rasping the lichen with mouthparts that leave silver trails. Higher-order predators follow the water seams on six delicate legs that bend like grass; they are the color of wet stone until they move. Birds, if they can be called that, are rare; the few that pass do so at extreme altitude, their calls arriving as faint arithmetic problems on the edge of hearing.

Sound travels poorly here. The air is thin, cold, and strangely still between gusts. When wind does arrive it carries a fine, freezing mist that hisses against the rock and then falls silent again. The only steady voice is the low, recursive murmur of water negotiating its way downward through the unfinished fissures; it sounds like someone repeating a word they have not yet learned to pronounce.

Human settlement arrived after the water, before the rock finished deciding its shape. The city is therefore a negotiation rather than an imposition. Where the slabs tilted too steeply for footing, the builders—using methods that leave no visible seams—grew curved terraces that follow the future drainage rather than fighting it. Towers stand not on foundations but on columns of artificially stabilized crystal that continue downward into the living rock like reversed roots. Streets are narrow, covered aqueducts; they slope gently so that condensation collected on their roofs feeds the reed beds planted along their verges. The buildings themselves are pale and porous, exchanging moisture with the air in a rhythm that matches the daily freeze–thaw cycle. Nothing here looks finished; everything looks as though it is waiting for the next increment of geological time.

Light behaves normally. The sun crosses the same arc it would on any other world. Shadows are sharp at noon and diffuse at dusk. The only anomaly is that, because the land is still forming, some shadows fall across surfaces that did not exist the day before, and a person standing still for an hour may watch their own silhouette migrate onto newly risen stone.

Weather systems form above the continent’s own heat differential. In late afternoon, small thunderheads condense over the warmest slabs, drop a brief, needle-sharp sleet, and dissipate before sunset. The resulting film of water is just thick enough to refract the lowering light into pale, shifting spectra that slide across the city’s terraces like slow breath on glass.

Because the geology is still writing itself, the city’s engineers must read the rock the way a sailor reads a forming wave. Every decade they redraw the maps; every decade the maps become obsolete when a new seam opens or a slab tilts another fraction of a degree. The inhabitants have learned to treat permanence as a temporary phase between two movements of water. They build lightly, listen constantly, and leave wide margins of empty stone between districts so that the next fissure can be accommodated without demolition.

At night the temperature drops further. The water in the seams slows; the copepods enter torpor; the city’s terraces exhale a faint, silver fog that pools in the lower streets until dawn. Somewhere far below, the unfinished continent continues its argument with gravity, and the sound, though mostly inaudible to human ears, is nevertheless present—a low, patient friction that will one day become the voice of rivers that do not yet exist.
