Beneath the surface there is no surface at all—only a sealed, pressurized mineral stratum kilometers deep that forms its own planetary plate. The walls are a chalk-white salt compressed into translucent sheets as smooth and cold as gypsum, yet warm to the touch at a constant 38 °C. Over eons this salt has layered into wafers so thin they fracture along their own grain, producing alcoves and ribs whose edges remain knife-sharp; they have never known the stroke of wind or the drop of rain.

No water, only veins of liquid helium salts that rise from fissures like colorless blood. Where the veins reach open fractures they flash-freeze into acicular crystals that drift upward in the low-density air, catching the dim glow of bioluminescent colonies. The glow is weak—handspan worms wrapped around the crystals emit a single, aching blue pulse every few minutes. The worms are the only abundant fauna: their tubular bodies lace through the salt like capillaries, absorbing dissolved minerals to spin new thread-thin casings. When a worm dies the casing is left behind, stacked and cross-braced, forming the scattered architecture of this place—hollow cylinders twenty to thirty body-lengths tall, fitted together without mortar into the slender, ribbed shells of forgotten temples.

Because the air is motionless, the towers do not sway. A single fall of powdered salt from a high fracture hangs like a veil for hours before settling. Any small vibration, however—two worms brushing past each other—travels along the entire network of shells, producing a sustained, almost sub-audible resonance that a listener feels as a pressure inside the teeth rather than as sound. Afterward the quiet returns, deeper than before.

Overhead, if overhead exists, the salt sheets close into a vault that absorbs all but a residual red infrared shimmer from geochemical reactions deeper still. The light is never bright enough to throw a shadow, yet it is enough for the worms to read their crystalline filaments as maps. They follow these invisible lines without deviation, and in doing so they continually enlarge the towers, adding new cylinders that slot into older ones at precise, non-Euclidean angles. The resulting structures wander across kilometers of subterranean space like a single, slow-growing organism whose growth pattern is recorded in the salt itself. Nothing is ever dismantled; nothing ever collapses. Time has already finished its work here, leaving only a warm, crowded stasis that continues to arrange itself according to its own inscrutable grammar.
