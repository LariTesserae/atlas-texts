The world is wide up here.

It begins as a high, sloping plateau whose edges have been blurred by centuries of wind rather than cut into cliffs. The stone is pale, almost bone-colored, yet every surface is lined with faint, concentric ripples like the rings inside split timber. These ripples are the signature of the plateau’s slow, continuous settling: the rock has not cracked or tumbled away; it has simply compressed, molecule by molecule, into a low, repeating topography of shallow domes and troughs no taller than a person’s waist. Between the domes the ground is loose, a mixture of mineral grit and tiny glassy shards that flash whenever the light shifts. The shards are warm to the touch and faintly vibrate if held long enough, the remnant of a process whose only visible sign is a soft, almost inaudible fizz when moisture touches them.

Moisture arrives in brief pulses. Every several hours a thin sheet of condensate forms along the undersides of the domes and beads into threads that run into the troughs. There the liquid gathers in long, shallow lenses no deeper than a thumbnail. The lenses last only minutes before they either evaporate upward or slide, bead by bead, into the next trough downslope. Nothing here ever floods, yet nothing is ever wholly dry; the interval between damp and arid is measured in heartbeats rather than seasons.

Vegetation follows the water’s timetable. Low mats of metallic-blue filaments grow in the troughs, their strands no thicker than spider-silk. They bloom when the lenses appear, drink for as long as the water lasts, then collapse into brittle rings of spore dust. Because the lenses rarely overlap, the filaments never form continuous carpets; instead they mark the plateau in disconnected arcs and crescents that shift position slightly with each wetting cycle. The dust they leave behind drifts on imperceptible breezes until it settles on the crowns of the domes, where it glints like frost under the constant hard light.

That light is unshadowed. There are no clouds, only a sky whose pale intensity seems to press downward. The sun—or whatever luminous body governs this altitude—never moves; instead the plateau itself rotates on an axis so slow that a day equals many human lifetimes. Travelers mark time by the changing angle at which the filaments align themselves to the constant glare.

Fauna is plentiful, but almost entirely subsurface. The domes are riddled with narrow galleries no wider than a finger. Inside them live colonies of minute, translucent creatures whose bodies refract the overhead brightness into faint rainbow flickers visible through the stone. They emerge only when the lenses form, skating across the water in spiraling feeding patterns before withdrawing again. Their motion produces a sound like thousands of tiny glass beads rolling on taut silk—audible only if the listener lies flat and still.

At irregular intervals—sometimes after a hundred quiet days, sometimes after only a handful—something larger moves. A low-frequency tremor travels through the rock, and for a moment the troughs brim with a deeper, slower moisture that smells of iron and ozone. During these events the metallic filaments stand upright like compass needles, and the translucent colonies swarm upward in columns visible even from a distance. Afterward the plateau appears unchanged, except that one or two of the smallest domes have shifted a hand-span downslope, their ripples realigned to a slightly different center.

Scattered across this moving skin are artifacts of an older order. At wide intervals stand single pylons, each no taller than a person, forged from a matte alloy that refuses to take scratch or patina. Their surfaces are etched with circuits too fine to follow without magnification; at night they emit a steady violet glow that exactly cancels the plateau’s own faint phosphorescence, rendering the area around each pylon perfectly lightless. No two pylons are visible from one another; each defines its own pocket of absolute shadow within the surrounding brightness.

Nothing builds upon the pylons, nothing nests against them. Even the filaments grow only up to the perimeter of their shadow and stop. The pylons are not ruins; they are still coupled to whatever deep lattice moves the domes. When the iron-scented tremors come, the pylons pulse once, in perfect synchrony, as if acknowledging the passage of some slower instruction set through the rock.

Thus the plateau continues: always bright, always listening, its water ticking from dome to dome, its hidden life keeping time with the migration of invisible lattices beneath its settled, rippled skin.
