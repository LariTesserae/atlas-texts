A pocket of almost-still air and almost-warm moisture hides inside a high shoulder of wet stone. The shoulder itself is part of a larger cliff whose face is hidden in continuous night; the pocket occupies no more than a thirty-pace span, yet everything required for a world is pressed into it.

Water is the first substance. It arrives not as weather but as a permanent exhalation: every vertical plane sweats a film so thin it never drips, only thickens. Horizontal ledges catch the film until it pools into lenses no deeper than a thumbnail; when those overflow, the surplus returns to the walls and begins again. The air inside the pocket never cools enough to condense into visible fog, yet it never warms enough to drive the moisture away; breath condenses instantly on skin or cloth and joins the general circulation.

The rock is embryonic. No strata have settled; the stone still remembers the single slow surge that lifted it. Surfaces therefore lack joints or cleavage planes; instead they ripple with arrested flow-lines, like wax that cooled mid-pour. Where fractures do occur they are hair-thin, recent enough that the two faces have not yet lost their exact fit. Touching them feels like closing a book whose pages were never opened.

Vegetation is present only as three species, each reduced to a single individual.  
• One thalloid liverwort the color of verdigris occupies a single seepage ledge; its thallus is no larger than an outspread hand and never produces capsules.  
• One tuft of rush scarcely taller than a finger grows from a grit-filled crack; every blade is the same length, as though clipped by an invisible limit.  
• One crustose lichen the precise shade of the surrounding stone covers a single water-lens; it is visible only because its surface is microscopically rougher than the wet rock.

All three plants touch one another; none has room to spread farther. Their metabolic rate is locked in a slow equilibrium with the constant humidity, so none ever yellows, none ever expands, none ever seeds.

Temperature remains fixed at the upper edge of comfort. Heat arrives conductively through the living wall of the cliff, conducted inward from some deeper, unknowable reservoir; it never rises, never falls, and therefore never urges the water to either evaporate or freeze.

Light is absent. The darkness is not night but simple negation; photons do not enter. Sight yields only the memory of sight: the mind supplies a dim grey plane where the palm confirms a surface, but the eye registers nothing. The inhabitants—if any exist—navigate by water-sound and by the faint, constant pressure gradient of warmer stone against cooler air.

Sound is likewise minimal. The only recurring acoustic event is the soft, single-frequency drip that occurs when an overflowing lens finally releases its excess down a hairline fracture. The interval between drips is constant, on the order of a slow human pulse; the tone is too low to carry far, so only the creature standing directly beneath hears it. All other movement is frictional and therefore silent: water films sliding, air circulating at the same temperature as skin.

Fauna consists of two or three individuals whose species cannot be guessed from shape alone. They are flat, joint-legged, no larger than a coin, and live entirely within the shallowest water lenses. Their locomotion produces no disturbance visible to an observer; they simply change position between one moment and the next. Because the temperature never varies, their metabolism never accelerates into activity or decelerates into torpor; they simply persist.

Scattered artifacts suggest an earlier, equally minimal human presence. A single obsidian flake lies wedged beneath one ledge, its conchoidal scars still glassy; oxidation has not yet begun. A thumb-sized pottery shard rests in another lens; its curvature indicates it once formed part of a vessel whose walls were no thicker than two stacked coins. Both objects are oriented exactly as they fell; nothing has shifted them because nothing has yet occurred that could shift them.

Time inside the pocket has no gradient. The same film of water has occupied the same ledge since the stone first stopped moving; the same drip has fallen at the same interval; the same three plants have touched the same three points on the rock. Equilibrium here is not an achievement but a definition: change would require an energy gradient, and none exists. The pocket therefore continues—wet, warm, dense with presences that barely move, hidden within a cliff whose face remains in unbroken darkness.
