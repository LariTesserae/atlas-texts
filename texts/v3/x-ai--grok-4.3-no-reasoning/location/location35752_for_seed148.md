The air here is a single breath held long enough that it forgets how to return.

There is no sky. Instead, a continuous roof of living wood arches so low that a standing person would touch it with the crown of their head; a tunnel of respiring timber in which the same interlocking grain repeats without pattern or end. The ceiling and walls are one tissue: green-black lignin so dense it absorbs every photon that finds its way inside, so that the only light is the phosphorescence of sap rising in slow pulses. It glows the dull indigo of starved veins seen through closed eyelids.

The floor is not soil. It is a membrane of root felt, spongy underfoot yet curiously dry—moisture has condensed on its surface into a thin film of dew that never gathers into puddles. When a foot presses down, the felt exhales a sigh so soft that the sound is felt more than heard: a cool, negative pressure that draws the moisture back into the roots before it can pool. This respiration repeats at the rate of a sleeping pulse, creating a slow, rhythmic draft that circulates between the hollows beneath the felt.

Between the breathing intervals there is almost silence. Once per cycle, a single metallic clack echoes from deeper inside the mass: the closing of a valve somewhere in the wooden infrastructure. The echo dies before it travels far; the living architecture swallows sound the way a sponge swallows water. After each clack, the air shifts again—slightly cooler, slightly damper—and a mist that was not there moments before now hangs in fine droplets around every trunk and root.

The plants are the city. There are no buildings separate from vegetation; every structural member is a living trunk whose cambium has been guided, generation after generation, into rectilinear pillars, cross-braced vaults, and the long, low galleries of an industrial arcade. Where girders would meet in steel, here the wood has been taught to fuse laterally, producing horizontal platforms a meter thick—still supple, still vascular. Pipes run through the cores of the largest trunks: not metal, but hardened xylem channels blackened by long use, carrying steam from fungal digesters deeper in the root mass. The steam is vented upward through microscopic stomata in the ceiling; it condenses against the colder, outer bark and trickles back down as sweet condensate that feeds the entire structure.

The temperature inside this living city remains fixed at the cool edge of comfortable. The wood itself is the thermostat: whenever internal warmth begins to rise, the outermost layer of bark extrudes crystalline droplets of a sugar-alcohol mixture whose evaporation pulls heat from the air. The process is audible only as a faint, glassy tinkling—like distant wind chimes heard through layers of cotton.

Fauna moves in the gaps between trunks. The spaces are not empty, but they are thin: corridors half a body-width across, just wide enough for passage if one turns sideways. Small, translucent arthropods crawl along the inner cambium, their legs producing a sound like very fine sand falling on paper. Larger forms—segmented, moss-furred—drift through the wider galleries, propelled by slow jets of pressurized air they store in internal bladders. They feed by pressing their ventral surfaces to the condensate film on the root felt, extracting minerals the wood has already discarded. When they are sated, they release the spent air in tiny, deliberate bursts: a punctuation of sound that is the only loud noise the space permits.

Nothing here is still for long. The city is always building itself and unbuilding itself at the same moment. Where a gallery has grown too narrow, new cambium swells outward in pale, almost luminous whorls; where a passage threatens to widen too far, lignin hardens and contracts, drawing the walls together again. The changes are measured in days rather than hours, yet they register as an ambient tension in the air: a low, almost subsonic creaking that exists below the threshold of language but above the threshold of silence.

Water exists only in transit. There are no rivers, no lakes, no standing pools. Instead, every vertical trunk contains a capillary column that lifts condensate from the root felt to the ceiling in a continuous, silent fountain. The ascent is so slow that the water column appears motionless; only by watching for an hour does one notice the surface has risen a single millimeter. At the ceiling the water is released as vapor through the stomata, falls again as microscopic droplets, and the cycle repeats. The droplets sometimes catch the faint indigo glow long enough to flare into momentary pinpricks of violet before they rejoin the film on the floor.

At irregular intervals—perhaps once per metabolic day—a deeper vibration travels through the entire mass. It is not an earthquake; it is the synchronized contraction of every trunk at once, a peristaltic squeeze that forces excess steam out through the valve that earlier produced the metallic clack. For a few seconds the air becomes momentarily opaque with vapor; then the contraction releases, the mist settles, and the cycle of slow respiration resumes.

The rules are simple and absolute. Movement is permitted only along the grain of the wood; any attempt to cut across it causes the surrounding trunks to swell and seal the wound within minutes. Light is not forbidden, but it is metabolized on arrival; a torch or lantern would be dimmed to a coal-glow within seconds, then extinguished as the wood drinks the photons for its own slow chemistry. Sound is tolerated only in single, discrete events; sustained noise causes the nearest gallery to narrow until the source is either silenced or expelled. Moisture is never stored—only passed forward—so any container left open will empty itself back into the system before its level can be measured.

The place is still becoming. New galleries are always knitting themselves just beyond the present boundary, their walls still pale and translucent, not yet fully lignified. Old galleries collapse inward at the same rate, their wood softening, their vascular channels sealing. The net volume never changes, yet every molecule within the living city is in motion: rising, condensing, evaporating, sinking, rising again. The only constant is the cool, damp darkness and the quiet, measured breathing of a structure that is both forest and machine, both city and single organism, still assembling itself from the raw logic of wood and water.
