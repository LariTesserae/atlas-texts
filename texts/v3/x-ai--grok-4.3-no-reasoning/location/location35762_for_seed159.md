Here the world is still deciding what it wants to be, and the deciding itself makes the noise.  

We stand inside a single, gently inclined plane of dark carbonate stone—shale-like in its laminations, yet newly formed—whose sheets have not yet separated into distinct strata.  They are warm to the touch, radiating the faint metabolic heat of crystallisation rather than geothermal venting.  Moisture arrives as a fine, ever-present mist that condenses on every upward-facing surface; rivulets no wider than a finger trace their way along seams before dripping into fractures that have not finished fracturing.  The water is not rain from above; the ceiling, some forty metres overhead, sweats.  It is the rock itself that is still wet from its own slow birth.  

Between these sheets, hundreds of narrow vertical shafts—some only a hand-span across—drop away into blackness and rise again into other galleries.  No surface wind moves here, yet a constant low booming rolls through the shafts whenever any pocket of warmer, damper air surges upward and cools, exactly the way a kettle sings before it boils.  The sound is multiplied by every vertical conduit, so that the boom becomes an overlapping choir rather than a single voice.  Intermittently, a sharper crack interrupts the choir: a newly opened joint venting its first exhalation of steam.  

Nothing grows, yet the walls are not bare.  Colonies of gelatinous mineral films—each only a few molecules thick—spread outward in iridescent polygons.  Their colours shift from moment to moment, responding to the same pressure changes that produce the booming.  When two films meet they do not merge; they repel each other by a hair’s-breadth of electrostatic tension, leaving hairline channels of darker stone between them.  These channels carry droplets the way a ploughed field carries irrigation, except the droplets here are exactly half-solid: silica sols that flow until they strike a cooler wall and freeze into minute, bristling stalagmites no taller than eyelashes.  

The fauna is the films themselves.  The polygons are not passive chemistry but coordinated sheets of vesicles, each vesicle a millimetre-scale pressure chamber that can inflate or deflate in a few dozen heartbeats.  A sudden pressure drop in one gallery causes thousands of adjacent polygons to deflate together, pulling mist inward like an inhalation; moments later the same network inflates, pushing that mist into another gallery.  The air therefore circulates without wind, in coordinated surges that keep pace with the cracks and booms.  Observers sometimes feel the floor tilt fractionally underfoot; the films are redistributing their own mass, gently reshaping the incline they rest upon.  

No tool-using species has appeared yet, but the geometry already implies one.  At irregular intervals, vertical grooves have been rasped into the stone—exactly the width of a vesicle network when fully inflated.  Inside each groove lies a line of fused silica pearls, spaced at the average stride of the shifting polygons.  The pearls are not decorative; they are abraded from the rock by the vesicles themselves, and already their spacing matches the new fractures that opened yesterday.  The films are, in short, excavating a future city before the bedrock has finished deciding where its seams will lie.  

Light arrives from above as an even, nacreous wash.  The source is a lattice of quartz veins that have crystallised in the ceiling like a fractured skylight; every droplet that falls from them refracts the faint bioluminescence of the films below, turning the whole ceiling into a single diffuse mirror.  The light is therefore neither day nor night; it is the average brightness of the biological weather system that is still negotiating its own climate.  When a pressure-surge forces mist upward, the mirror dims; when the films exhale, brightness flares.  Observers describe the sensation as standing beneath a living cloud ceiling whose every exhale is also an optical pulse.  

Because the rock is still laminating, the floor occasionally sheds a translucent flake the size of a fallen leaf.  Each flake lands with a sound like muted ceramic and is immediately overgrown by new film before it can be carried away.  The flakes themselves begin to reorganise: vesicles migrate onto their surfaces, inflating them into tiny domes that then detach and drift upward on local thermals, adding fresh mineral dust to the ceiling.  In this manner the landscape is simultaneously wearing itself down and building itself upward.  Nothing is stable, yet nothing is being destroyed; the turbulence is its own form of architecture.  

There are no horizons here, only the soft termination of laminae at the limit of lamplight.  The next gallery may already be opening; the next polygon may already be coordinating a migration that will close the one we stand in.  The only constant is the ceaseless low-frequency chord of the stone still deciding its own seams, a sound that is also the place’s unfinished breath.
