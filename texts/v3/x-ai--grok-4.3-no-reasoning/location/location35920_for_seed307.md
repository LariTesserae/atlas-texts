The place is a single collapsed lava bubble — a chamber the size of a large walk-in closet, hollowed years ago when a roof of cooling basalt sagged and punctured. It sits inside a cliff that is itself inside a larger mountain, a layered secret that no human path has ever reached.  

At the floor of the chamber, the lava that once occupied it has frozen into a ring of black glass ridges, sharp-edged, still faintly iridescent where vesicles catch the light. Between those ridges the floor has been shattered into coarse pumice gravel; the gravel glitters with sulfur crystals that sweat a continuous film of moisture. The crystals are warm—almost uncomfortably so—and when a droplet grows large enough it rolls into the next hollow, where it meets another crystal and begins to steam. The steam rises only a few centimeters before it condenses again on the nearest outcrop, so the air itself never clears; every surface carries its own micro-climate of droplets, trickles, and re-condensing vapor.  

Above the steaming floor the walls fold inward like petals of a heat-shattered rose, each fold no wider than a forearm, each fold bristling with needle-thin spines of freshly extruded obsidian. The spines are so close together that they almost touch; windless, the chamber nevertheless exhales a soft, constant sigh whenever a drop detaches from a spine tip and falls. The spines themselves are still plastic at their cores. Every so often one will re-heat from within, swell, and burst, releasing a single bubble of sulfurous gas that pops without echo.  

Nothing grows here, nothing macroscopic, yet the chamber is crowded. In the fractures between the spines live filaments of mineral wool—thread-fine, metallic, barely thicker than light. Each filament spirals slowly, continuously, always toward the hottest nearby surface. When two filaments meet they braid for a few millimeters, exchange a pulse of orange light, then part again, their direction changed. Where the braiding occurs, tiny beads of liquid sulfur collect and immediately begin to flow back toward the warmer filament, feeding it. The whole colony moves a few centimeters an hour, a quiet mineral tide.  

Temperature and pressure are locked in an uneasy balance. The ceiling, closer to the original magma tube, remains hot enough that the rock itself is faintly luminous, a velvet orange. Heat rises along the folds, meets the saturated air, and triggers brief, localized drizzles that last only until the water re-evaporates three hand-spans lower. The cycle repeats. No gust ever escapes the chamber; the only movement of air is the refraction shimmer above each new rain spot.  

Time inside the bubble is told by the incremental re-orientation of the mineral filaments and by the slow widening of the fractures where the spines continue to extrude. Every few dozen cycles a new spine tip will breach the surface of an older one, splitting it lengthwise and exposing a fresh mirror of glass. When this happens the light that already exists inside the chamber is briefly multiplied, a white flash no brighter than a struck match, gone before the eye can finish seeing it.  

Nothing here is larger than a fingertip. Nothing here is older than the slow collapse that created the chamber. Everything here is either still forming or already breaking so it can form again.
