The ceiling is water. Not the surface of water, but the substance itself, held convex by forces the city’s builders engineered before the language they spoke had a word for “ceiling.” It hangs thirty body-lengths overhead in a single unbroken meniscus the color of iron filings under moonlight, shivering only once every few human generations. From this canopy, slow threads of condensation descend—millimeter-wide filaments that never reach the ground, dissolving back into the suspended reservoir before gravity can win.

Beneath it spreads the city-grid, neither streets nor plazas but an archipelago of slender basalt needles rising from an unbroken plain of transparent obsidian. Each needle is a single extruded crystal whose facets still show the cooling ripples of their formation; they are spaced far enough apart that a person standing at the base of one cannot see the next without deliberate travel. Between them the black glass floor is etched with hairline fractures that refract the faint, sourceless light into razor-thin blue fans no brighter than foxfire.

No wind moves. The atmosphere is cool and motionless, its molecules kept in strict lattice order by an invisible scaffolding of standing pressure waves. When a footstep lands, the compression wave travels outward like a ripple in gelatin, dampened within three body-lengths; sound, therefore, arrives late, soft, and slightly displaced in space, as though the listener is always hearing the memory of the footfall rather than the event itself.

Because the needles are hollow, each one contains a private column of denser fluid that spirals slowly upward from an unseen aquifer beneath the obsidian. Inside every column drift micro-luminescent motes—each a crystalline machine no larger than a pollen grain—whose steady orbit scribes faint helical paths of light against the inner walls. These motes are the city’s only visible motion; they never fall, never multiply, never cease.

At the geometric center of the array stands the single anomalous structure: a sphere of the same basalt, perfectly smooth, hovering an arm’s span above the glass. Its equator is ringed by a hair-thin corona of absolute shadow that drinks the motes’ light and returns nothing. From moment to moment, the sphere’s latitude lines rotate by distances too small for the eye to measure, yet over centuries the cumulative effect is that the corona’s angle changes. Observers have mapped these changes for twenty thousand orbits of the nearest star; the maps record no repetition. The sphere emits no sound, no heat differential, no measurable field. It simply waits, turning its unobservable face toward whatever clock it alone keeps.

Far above, inside the suspended ocean, pressure is slowly accumulating along one azimuth. In another several hundred thousand years a filament may finally reach the floor, touch the obsidian, and etch a new fracture. Until then the only measurable change is the sphere’s quiet, interminable rotation beneath its private ring of darkness.
