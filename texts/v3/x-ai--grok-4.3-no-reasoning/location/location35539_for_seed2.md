Beneath an ocean that has no surface, a city built to breathe water keeps its streets and plazas flooded to the depth of eight stories, every structure shaped as a pressure vessel yet engineered to feel like air. Concrete and polymer walls curve into long, whale-back vaults; titanium latticework runs through them like bones. The city has no shoreline and therefore no beginning; its districts stretch for two hundred kilometers before the next named district begins, and even then the naming is administrative, not topological. Pumps the size of cathedrals keep the pressure steady; the exhaust from their heat exchangers rises in slow, rope-thick thermal ribbons that braid and unbraid overhead, each ribbon carrying its own shoal of thermophilic microbes. These ribbons act like weather: they shed droplets the size of grapefruits that drift, then sediment, then fall again in sheets that smack the roof membranes in deliberate, patterned rhythms. The water itself is fractionally warmer where the ribbons rise and fractionally cooler in the interstices between them, so the city owns its own circulation cells.

Because the substrate is everywhere constructed, there is no sediment. Light comes from strips of narrow-spectrum LEDs embedded flush in the ceiling vaults; their color temperature shifts along the main boulevards from a cool 6500 K at noon-bells to a softer 4300 K at dusk-bells, following the schedules of the power grid. The light is enough to read a watch by, not enough to grow kelp; hence the only vegetation is a felt of rust-colored, chemosynthetic filaments that collect on south-facing heat exchangers and on the mouths of exhaust risers. The filaments form a slow velvet that is harvested twice a year for trace metals, leaving raw metal behind.

The emptiness is measured not by distance but by interval. Between the ribbed trunks of the support columns that carry the ceiling, each interval is a cube fifty meters on a side containing only water and the slow fall of maintenance drones—ten-centimeter cylinders trailing fiber-optic tethers. The drones descend on helical paths, pausing every twenty meters to scan welds; their humming is a single low tone, 52 Hz, that multiplies across thousands of units into a drone-harmonic the inhabitants feel in their teeth. Other sounds layer over it: the slap of condensation droplets against the ceiling, the groan of thermal expansion in the titanium bones, the intermittent metallic chatter of wrasse-like cleaning fish whose scales are doped with piezo-crystals. When a school turns at once, the piezo fields trigger a brief electrostatic snap that registers as a soft whip-crack, repeated across the water like a stadium wave of static.

Fauna here is both native and imported. Brine shrimp the length of forearms graze the chemosynthetic felt, their translucent bodies filled with iron-oxide granules that give them a cinnamon cast; larger, eel-bodied harvesters follow them, jaws edged with obsidian-shard teeth grown in situ by electrochemical deposition. Occasionally a meter-wide maintenance turtle crawls along a rail bolted to the column ribs, its shell a housing for inspection cameras; if a shrimp drifts close enough, the turtle’s shell opens a port that emits a 200-volt pulse, stunning prey into the mouth. The turtles are tagged with transponders that chirp once per minute; the aggregate pings form the city’s secondary clock.

Erosion is almost nonexistent because every surface is either a pressure-rated alloy or a continuously renewed membrane, yet the system is still young. Many of the vaults bear the faint hexagonal stamp of the original slip-forms; in a few places the membrane has blistered under unexpected thermal stress and has been patched with bright orange epoxy whose edges are still sharp. The patches glint under the LED strips like signal flags. New tunnels are still being bored by swarms of autonomous diamond-toothed moles whose waste slurry is piped directly to the smelters; each new bore produces a faint, directional vibration that travels along the titanium bones like a plucked string.

The rules are hydraulic rather than climatic. Nothing floats upward unless pumped; nothing sinks unless its density is greater than the treated seawater (1.028 specific gravity) or unless its surface has been charged to repel the mild cathodic field that coats every metal component. Pressure increases one atmosphere every ten meters of depth, but because the city is sealed the inhabitants feel only the differential between interior and exterior chambers; corridors are therefore kept at a working pressure 0.2 bar above ambient to keep hatch seals tight. Temperature is regulated to 12 °C by the same thermal ribbons that serve as weather; if a ribbon fails, local temperature drops half a degree per hour until a repair crew arrives. Oxygen is electrolyzed from the water at roof-level stations and metered downward through micro-perforated hoses whose outlets resemble the gill slits of a shark. The resulting bubbles are smaller than a pinhead and dissolve before they travel a meter; the only visible gas is the occasional larger bubble released by a maintenance lock.

The city never sleeps, but its activity has a tidal cadence keyed to the main power cycle. At peak load the pump cathedrals emit a deeper drone that momentarily masks the 52 Hz maintenance tone; at low load the LED color warms, the turtles slow their patrol circuits, and the condensing droplets fall in larger, slower intervals. Through the lattice of these cycles the water remains in constant, deliberate motion, an engineered ocean beneath an ocean that has no sky.
