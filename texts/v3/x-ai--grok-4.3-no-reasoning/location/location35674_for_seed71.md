Above the continent-sized plateau called the Carved Verge, the atmosphere is high enough that the air feels half-remembered.  A permanent haze of ice crystals drifts like milk in water, catching whatever light leaks through the perpetual overcast.  Within the haze, colder pockets condense into long, low veils of fog that roll slowly between the ridges.  Where fog meets any exposed edge—timber upright, stone slab, even a resin-sealed timber joint—tiny beads of water collect, linger, then re-freeze into a granular white fur that crusts and drops away when the next thermal pulse arrives.

The plateau itself is not so much a single flat as a lacework of flatnesses: immense, ancient platforms each the size of a modest inland sea, separated by canyons so wide that daylight lingers across them even at dusk.  These platforms were once the summits of folded mountains; now they have been polished by so many ages that their tops are faintly convex, edges crumbling into ramparts of rubble no taller than a person.  The rubble is not loose; each block is locked against its neighbors by centuries of freeze-thaw, forming stair-like aprons that descend into shadow without ever quite reaching the canyon floors.

Across these terraces, vegetation grows in patient arcs.  Knee-high tussocks of salt-tolerant sedge mark the faint runoff lines from the last condensation event; between them spreads a silvery lichen whose thallus is thicker than felt and darker where the ice crust still clings.  Occasional wind-stunted firs stand singly, each trunk canted the same direction as the prevailing high-altitude current.  Their needles are small, cupped, almost scale-like, and they click softly in the thin wind.  No insects crawl the bark; no birds settle on the branches.  The quiet is so complete that, when a fir does creak, the sound travels for kilometers before it is absorbed again by the ice haze.

Human presence is written on the platforms in low, concentric walls of unmortared stone, each ring no higher than the waist.  Inside the outermost ring, open spaces are paved with the same stones set on edge like teeth; narrow channels between them carry the brief films of meltwater that arrive after fog settles at night.  Inside the next ring sit circular pits—half cellar, half kiln—lined with the same stone and floored with charcoal-blackened clay.  No smoke rises now, but the soot pattern on the upper walls records centuries of careful, low-temperature fires.  Between rings, small platforms hold basalt anvils and grinding slabs, their surfaces scooped into shallow basins by generations of hands that are no longer present.  The entire settlement pattern is repeated at irregular intervals across adjacent platforms, each copy aligned with the next by subtle notches cut into the stone lips that face one another across the canyons.  Together they form a single, continent-long chain whose purpose is no longer remembered, only maintained by an instinct for alignment.

Because the plateau is high enough to generate its own weather, the fog occasionally thickens into low, fast-moving cloud that scrapes along the stone like a living membrane.  When it passes, the air momentarily clears; the ice crystals overhead glitter for an instant before the membrane reforms.  In those clear instants one can see the next platform several horizon-widths away, its concentric rings a pale ghost repeating the same pattern, its firs a line of darker punctuation against the haze.

Light never arrives as a single source.  It filters downward through the crystal veils in a soft, directionless wash, bright enough to read lichen color or stone grain, yet casting no hard shadow.  Temperatures remain a few degrees below freezing; the cold is steady, never sharp, as if the entire landscape has already surrendered its heat to some deeper sink and is merely conserving what remains.

Sound is almost entirely the sound of absence: the faint rustle of the crystal veils turning over on themselves, the muted click of fir needles, the occasional soft report of a frost-loosened pebble falling a few handspans from its socket in the rubble apron.  Voices, if any were raised, would be absorbed within a few body-lengths; a footstep on the paved rings produces only a brief, dull pressure against the soles before the stone returns to its former quiet.

Nothing moves except water, air, and the slow, inevitable collapse of the ancient stone lips.  The ecosystem—if it can still be called one—consists of moisture cycling between vapor, film, and frost; of lichen metabolizing at the rate of centuries; of firs releasing seeds that lie on bare stone until the next slow crumble of rubble covers them with enough grit to sprout.  The rings of the vanished builders remain exactly where they were left, neither added to nor taken from, because the only active processes left are the ones that do not require hands.

Yet in the intervals when the fog lifts, a strange displacement occurs.  For the span of a held breath, the next platform in the chain is no longer where alignment says it should be; it hangs a few degrees off true, as though the world’s geometry hesitates between one configuration and another.  Then the membrane of cloud settles again, the platforms realign, and the quiet resumes—vast, settled, and older than any living witness.
