High on a limestone plateau that still remembers the first rains that carved it, the land stands in open silence. The stone itself is young—cream-colored, pocked with fresh solution pits no larger than a cupped hand, their rims as crisp as the moment the water left them. Between the pits the rock stretches in broad, unbroken sheets that slope almost imperceptibly eastward, so that gravity is felt more as invitation than compulsion.

From deep clefts, water emerges in threads so fine they do not fall; they condense in mid-air and rejoin the stone a few paces lower, leaving vertical stains the color of old bone. The threads never vary in volume; they are part of the plateau’s equilibrium, a continuous condensation–reabsorption cycle that never allows the surface to dry, yet never floods. The air is warm and still, carrying only the faint mineral scent of saturated limestone.

Vegetation arrives as single, widely spaced figures. Tall, jointed reeds grow straight from the stone where a solution pit has become a permanent shallow basin; their blades are the pale green of new celadon, and each joint bears a single drop that never falls. Farther apart stand solitary fig-cactus hybrids, their pads so thick with stored water they have flattened into pale, translucent plates that catch and softly diffuse the ordinary daylight into a gentle, shadowless wash. Between these plants the plateau is empty—kilometers of gently undulating stone interrupted only by the reeds and the cacti, each specimen hundreds of paces from the next.

Where the plateau edges into a broad valley, the town begins. Its buildings are not stacked upon one another but set at measured intervals across the same cream stone, as though the settlement were an extension of the spacing already present in the reeds. Walls are dry-laid from the same limestone, fitted so precisely that no mortar shows; roofs are shallow vaults of thinner slabs, their undersides still showing the fossil scallops of ancient seabeds. Courtyards lie open to the sky, each containing a single reed in its central solution pit. No smoke rises; no voices carry. The only motion is the slow, unchanging seepage of water down the inner faces of the vaults, tracing paths that have not altered since the stone was first set.

Farther out, the valley floor repeats the pattern on a larger scale: scattered clusters of low, single-story structures linked by foot-worn paths of crushed limestone that never deepen or widen. The paths converge on a circular plaza whose center is a wide, perfectly circular depression where water gathers to a depth of one knuckle and then simply stays—surface tension and continuous condensation keeping the level fixed. From the plaza the eye can still trace the gentle upward curve of the plateau on all sides, the horizon lost only in the soft haze of suspended moisture.

Light here is even and without source; it seems to rise from the wet stone itself. Sound is the absence of sound: the drip never quickens, the reeds never rustle, the footpaths register no footfall. The only presence is the plateau’s own equilibrium, holding water, warmth, vegetation, and settlement in a single, changeless distribution across an expanse large enough to walk for a day without reaching its edge or finding another living thing.
