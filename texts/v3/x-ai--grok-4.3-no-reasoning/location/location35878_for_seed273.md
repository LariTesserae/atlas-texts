Under an unsparing noon the crags themselves are forged: tall, seamless blocks of porcelain-fused brick that rise sheer from mist-ribboned gorges. They lean together like a hinge that has never opened, their joints so fine they glow faint lavender whenever moisture beads there. No soil meets them. Instead, the architecture rolls downward in shallow steps—each tread wide enough for an avenue—until those steps vanish into vapor-white fog below. The upper terraces are tiered gardens, but their beds are mosaic pipes: fired clay veins three finger-widths wide, permanently damp, pumping spring-water from reservoirs that hang beneath the whole city like chandelier weights.

Steam curls from the pipe-grids in thin, slow scarves. Where the vapor touches cooler brick, beads collect and chase one another along the glaze, merging until a drop heavy enough to fall does so. The falls make a faint, continuous ticking, like thousands of porcelain clocks losing one second per minute. In the intervals between ticks, another noise asserts itself: the low, resonant thrum of enormous flywheels turning under the lowest tiers. Their axles pass through the brick like roots; rings of condensation mark their exit points, proof that the bearings never heat enough to dry the film. The wheels drive water-screws that lift river water from the cloud-sea below, feeding it upward at a rate just sufficient to offset evaporation. The city does not move; it breathes in and out once every several hours.

Vegetation carpets every horizontal surface. Ferns thrust between brick lips, their fronds brushing anyone who walks the narrow cantilevered catwalks that serve as streets. Orchids root in pipe cracks; the warm, steady trickling keeps their pseudobulbs glossy. Vines with rubbery leaves spill from upper cornices, forming green curtains that part only when a body presses through. Between leaves one glimpses the real inhabitants: long-bodied, emerald-black lizards whose ribs expand visibly with each slow inhalation. They spend whole afternoons pressed to a sun-warmed flue, then descend at dusk to sip condensate from the same drip-line they occupied the day before. The air is dense with their exhaled moisture and with the spice of bruised fern sap. Insects—glossy beetles and midges the size of thumbnails—navigate the droplets like buoys, their wings producing a continuous, glassy rustle.

Farther up, whole plazas are trellised with living bamboo. Its internodes have been drilled; tiny turbines spin inside each hollow, turning the rising water into a delicate susurrus that layers over the flywheel drone. The bamboo also serves as scaffold for aerial walkways, their boards a pale, salt-impregnated timber that never rots because salt spray blown upward from the invisible sea below constantly re-cures it. Under the walkways, maintenance crews move on hand-cranked trolleys whose steel wheels click along iron rails mortared into the brick. Every few minutes the trolleys halt, workers consult brass gauges embedded in the wall, and then resume their patient meter-by-meter inspection. Nothing here is rushed. The gauges themselves change their needles only once per day, when hydrostatic pressure within the pipe-veins shifts by a single, repeatable increment.

High above, terraces give way to vertical orchards suspended on cable nets. Each net is strung between two parallel brick spines; fruit-bearing vines grow along the cables, their leaves forming a second, translucent roof. Condensation rains through these leaves in a fine, unfelt mist that lands on upturned faces as the softest coolness. In the intervals between mist-fall, sunlight—magnified by cylindrical lenses of bottle glass set flush into the uppermost parapets—pierces straight down the channels, warming the fruit without drying the soil beneath.

Nowhere is the masonry interrupted by raw stone or unpaved earth; even the wind has learned to travel only along the designed corridors. Doors are oval hatches that pivot on moss-cushioned gimbals, each one scored with the same lavender condensation rings. In the deeper interior halls, electric lamps buzz behind milk-glass bowls; they are dimmed at night by rheostats that require half an hour to traverse their full range. The light therefore fades as slowly as a sunset seen through closed eyelids. When the lamps finally rest at their lowest, the city exhales again, a measured sigh that moves fog upward through the vertical orchards and out over the unseen plains far below.

Life adheres to these rhythms. The lizards time their foraging runs to the rheostat cycle. Bamboo turbines slow when the lamps dim, reducing vibration to a barely perceptible tremble that does not disturb nocturnal pollinators. Workers finish their rounds before the lamps reach quarter-bright, then retreat into dormitory chambers whose beds are canvas slings suspended over pipe grids, kept warm by waste steam. Above them, in the hanging orchards, ripe fruit loosens from stems once every several days and drops into padded collection nets, each impact a single muffled thud that echoes upward between the porcelain towers and is then forgotten.

Morning returns as imperceptibly as it left. Condensation again lengthens into beads, the flywheels maintain their patient revolution, and the city of brick and vine, water and warm light, continues its deliberate, unchanged exhalation, generation upon generation, without haste, without pause, without any visible end to its height or its depth.
