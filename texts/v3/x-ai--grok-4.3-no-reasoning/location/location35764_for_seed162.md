A realm where the land itself has only recently decided to exist.

The high ground here is not stone in the usual sense; it is still half-molten in memory, its skin only minutes cooler than the moment it poured out of a new seam in the crust. Every ridge is a razor of glassy obsidian, edges so newly hardened that the fractures have not yet found time to round. Between these knife-crests, pockets of steam pool like milk in the hollows of broken teeth. The steam never lifts far; the ambient pressure is wrong. It hangs at knee height, condensing against anything warmer than the rock, then dripping back in slow, glassy beads that leave faint trails of salt.

Water exists as condensation more than rain. The sky is no true sky. It is a low, bruise-colored lid of vapor that has not yet decided whether it is cloud or ceiling. When droplets gather on its underside, they fall in sudden, heavy spatters rather than drizzle, as though the atmosphere must occasionally shrug them loose. A walker feels them hit warm skin and linger, reluctant to evaporate.

Life is already negotiating the new terrain. Sheets of crimson lithophyte—something between lichen and furnace scale—spread across every cooling surface within hours of exposure. The lithophyte moves when the light changes; its thallus crawls a hand-span per hour toward the least dim patches, leaving a wake of desiccated older tissue behind it. The sound of its progress is a faint, papery rasp, the only consistent noise in a world that otherwise hushes itself. The atmosphere damps vibration; even the cracking of cooling rock arrives muffled, like distant knuckles.

Above and below the lithophyte, the air is thick with motile spores that have yet to agree on physical law. Some phases of their life cycle obey gravity; others treat it as optional. At times a single cluster will drift upward for no reason, then, as if remembering, collapse back into a tight spiral and settle against a warm vent. The result is a constant slow rain of micro-things that touch skin and then hesitate—some burrow a few micrometers and die, others simply ride body heat until they cool enough to drop again.

Fauna has arrived even faster than the vegetation. Wingless, membrane creatures the size of open hands scuttle along the newest fracture lines, harvesting the lithophyte’s sugary exudates before the sheets can fully form. Their movement is a whisper of scales against glass. Periodically the membranes inflate with internally generated steam; for a few seconds the animals become lighter than the local air and drift upward like paper boats on a pond that refuses to stay level. When the steam condenses they fall again, sometimes several body-lengths away. Each drop is a muted tap that is immediately swallowed by the general quiet.

Industrial residue without industry drifts through the same space. Filigrees of metallic filament—copper-red, impossibly fine—hang between obsidian spires like abandoned power lines. They were not strung there; they condensed out of the cooling exhalations of the rock itself, aligning to some geomagnetic whim that has not yet stabilized. Touch one and it vibrates at the frequency of a failing transformer, a subsonic thrum felt more in the teeth than heard. The filaments re-grow wherever they are broken, re-knitting across minutes, suggesting a metabolism rather than a mechanism.

The land is already attempting weather. Along the larger valleys, pressure differentials large enough to be called fronts chase one another in tight, drunken loops. A gust may rise, carrying flakes of lithophyte and the membrane animals, only to collapse three heartbeats later when a downdraft of colder vapor punches through it. The result is a continual, localized churning—miniature haboobs no wider than a city block—that form, shred, and reform without ever exporting their energy outward. The turbulence keeps the spore layer stirred; the quiet is not peace but a lid held down by constant, small violence.

Elevation continues to increase in real time. From certain vantage ridges one can watch new material well upward from below, bright as fresh weld metal, then darken through cherry to obsidian within an hour. Each new upwelling tilts the previous surface a few degrees, creating fresh fractures. The sound of those fractures is felt more than heard—a sub-audible click that travels through boot soles and seems to reset the local gravity for a fraction of a second. Those standing still sometimes sway as though the horizon itself has hiccuped.

Nothing here is old enough to have a settled ecology. Every organism is still negotiating whether it requires lungs, roots, or neither. The landscape therefore feels less like a finished place and more like a sequence of geological and biological experiments running in parallel, their results still arriving. The only constant is change so rapid that yesterday’s map would already be useless; yet the hush remains, as though the world is holding its breath between each iteration, listening for whether the next version will finally agree on a single set of rules.
