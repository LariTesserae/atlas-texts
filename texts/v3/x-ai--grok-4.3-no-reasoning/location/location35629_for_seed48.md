A low basin ringed by shallow mineral ridges, once carved by water long before anything alive arrived. The ridges are slivers of iron oxide glass and wind-polished basalt, barely taller than a single-story house, their slopes slick with dark condensation that drips once every few seconds, never more often. The depressions between them hold standing films of water no deeper than a hand, stained ochre by iron salts and trembling in the heat. No steam rises; the air is too warm to cool the liquid, so the water simply lies there, dark and still. Under the surface, sub-centimeter ripples stir whenever the ground settles, as though something below flexes.

The basin floor itself is neither rock nor soil. It is a fused matrix of olivine glass and old foundry slag, laid down in wide panels that tilt five or ten degrees toward each depression. Where panels meet, narrow lead ridges rise, each ridge no wider than a boot and knotted at intervals by cooling blisters. Between the panels run shallow gutters; condensation collects, slides, and sometimes pools. The gutters are lined with the same glass, but thousands of faint grooves run along their bottoms—micro-channels left by centuries of gentle flow, never deep enough for a current, just enough to guide droplets toward the next depression.

No plant or fungal trace exists. Every surface is coated with a uniform micron-thick layer of crystallized mineral salts that glint dark bronze under the dim sky. The salts are so thin they cannot be scraped away; they re-form within minutes on anything laid bare. The result is a world the color of old cast iron and dried blood, faintly iridescent where the film thins over curvature.

Above, the light is the color of weak, smoke-filtered daylight—no clouds visible, only a uniform brown haze that absorbs most wavelengths before they reach the basin. Shadows are present but soft, never black, so every panel edge and droplet carries a hair-thin highlight. The temperature makes the salts slightly tacky; touching them leaves a momentary print that erases itself by surface tension within half a minute.

Structures rise at measured intervals, each a single-story rectangular block cast in the same olivine glass as the ground. Their walls are unadorned except for the continuous horizontal groove at waist height that doubles as gutter and conduit. Pipes of dull iron run from each roof edge straight into the nearest depression, carrying roof-runoff. The blocks are spaced far apart—hundreds of meters—so their presence defines the space rather than filling it. Each block emits a faint subsonic hum, the sound of pumps and heat exchangers working below floor level. The sound does not carry far; the thick glass deadens it within a body length, leaving only the ghost of vibration through the soles of boots.

Along the outer edges of the basin, where the ridges begin to rise, stand taller assemblies: skeletal derricks of riveted iron, twenty or thirty meters high, motionless. Their only motion is the slow dripping of condensation from cross-members into waiting gutters below. Between derricks, wide catwalks of perforated iron plate bridge the depressions, each catwalk bolted to lead ridges. The plates are worn to a satin polish along the exact center line where countless unseen feet have passed. Yet no footsteps sound; the material damps impact so perfectly that only the lowest frequencies travel, felt rather than heard.

The fauna of this place is overwhelmingly present yet nearly invisible. What lives here moves inside the gutters and beneath the panels: micro-faunal aggregates that register as faint pulses of pressure change rather than discrete bodies. Whenever a water film thickens past a threshold, the aggregate contracts, displacing liquid into an adjacent micro-channel exactly the way a valve would. The motion is too slow to see in real time; only time-lapse or touch reveals it. Larger forms—hand-sized translucent slugs—cling to the undersides of catwalks, their bodies exactly matching the bronze salt film. They shift position perhaps once every few hours, leaving no trail. Their presence is sensed as a faint increase in local surface tension wherever they pause.

The rules of the place are simple and consistent. Water never evaporates; it only migrates along the engineered grade toward the next depression. Heat never spikes or drops; it remains high enough to keep the salt film tacky and low enough to keep water liquid. No new construction occurs; the existing blocks, pipes, and derricks are maintained by processes so quiet and automatic they register only as temperature gradients across panel joints. The entire basin is therefore in an odd balance: industrially produced, yet functioning with the settled patience of something that has operated unchanged for centuries.

At the lowest point, where several depressions converge, a single circular cistern of the same glass holds water a meter deep. From its center rises a low iron pillar, its top flush with the water surface. Condensation trails run down the pillar in perfect radial symmetry, meeting the cistern water without splash or ripple. The pillar is warm to the touch. The water around it remains perfectly still. Nothing grows on it; nothing lands on it; nothing disturbs the symmetry except the occasional, almost imperceptible shift of pressure from the micro-fauna in the surrounding gutters.

The basin continues outward in every direction until the ridges slope up and out of sight beneath the brown light. Somewhere beyond the last visible derrick, the same pattern of panels, blocks, and depressions repeats without variation or interruption, the same slow droplets, the same subsonic hum felt through glass, the same bronze salt film re-forming over any surface touched and then left alone.
