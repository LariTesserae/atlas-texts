At these heights the air feels thick even though the altimeter insists the pressure is thin. Every exhaled breath lifts only a few hand-heights before it condenses into a drifting droplet veil that beads across every surface. The condensation falls again, soft and slow, like warm rain reluctant to leave. Below the shoulder, the cliff dives three vertical kilometers to a valley floor that is still a plateau by ordinary standards. What look from orbit like parallel ridges are in truth terraced ledges, each one wider than most terrestrial river plains, stair-stepped by some unimaginable hand or by unthinkable time.

Stone here is stratified in colors that should not occur together: layers of anthracite graphite alternating with pale coral limestone, shot through with seams of transparent crystal that ring like bells when the wind fingers them. Because temperature is constant and heat high, the rock is never quite solid; microfractures heal themselves slowly with mineralized dew, and the faces of the cliffs are always glassy, like obsidian still cooling. Veins of an opalescent metal with no known terrestrial lattice run through the strata. They pulse—once every three days—like a slow capillary. Where they reach the surface they bead into perfect spheres of liquid alloy that crawl, without observable force, toward lower elevations; these spheres occasionally merge, then fission, rearranging the cliff’s topography by millimeters across centuries.

Vegetation has colonized every millimeter that is not actively crawling. The plants are vascular but have abandoned leaves; instead, each stem terminates in a translucent bladder that pumps the near-boiling condensate through a labyrinth of internal vanes. When enough bladders touch they fuse, forming suspended aerial gardens that can span entire cliff faces. Some of these gardens are older than recorded civilization, their fused bladder walls calcified into lattice bridges wide enough for a caravan. The bladders glow—not bioluminescent exactly, more like heat-haze given shape—shifting through peach, rose, and sulfur depending on the dissolved minerals they process.

Because the scale is regional and the elevation changes abruptly, weather is a vertical stack of microclimates. At the lowest ledge you are inside a constant warm fog whose droplets carry live spores; fifty meters higher the fog clears, and the bladders open pores to let the same droplets fall through them as fine mist; another fifty meters and the droplets have already re-condensed into a slow, vertical drizzle that glazes the next terrace. No wind accelerates any of this; air simply swaps places over hours, so the precipitation is gravitational and gentle. Yet because every surface is either warm stone or living tissue, the droplets never reach terminal velocity; they coast, trailing threads of vapor.

Fauna is abundant, slow, and strange. The most numerous are membrane gliders the size of open umbrellas, each a single cell wrapped around a pocket of lighter-than-air gas harvested from the seeping metal veins. They descend from ledge to ledge by controlled deflation, then re-inflate by lapping alloy beads; the refill can take days. When one lands on a garden it is quickly colonized by symbiotic bladder-vines that thread across its membrane like living rigging. The glider will carry the vine kilometers before both of them grow too heavy and sink to the next terrace, where the cycle restarts.

Smaller creatures live among the glider crews: translucent crabs no larger than fingernails that farm patches of crystal fungus on the undersides of the membranes. The crabs move only when temperature gradients shift, which may be once per season; their harvested fungus produces minute piezoelectric charges that the gliders use as a weak compass. From ledge to ledge, the collective motion of tens of thousands of gliders sounds like distant silk tearing—loud when you stand among them, nearly inaudible a terrace away. That low, continuous rip is the dominant acoustic signature of the region, punctuated rarely by the single clear chime of a crystal seam resetting after a micro-fracture heals.

Scattered across the terraces are the only unambiguously artificial remnants: squat stone cylinders two meters across and one tall, open at the top. Inside each is a shallow basin lined with the same crawling alloy. When a sphere reaches a basin it pauses, flattens, and stretches a hairline filament across the rim. Over months the filament braids into a delicate mesh. Observers have recorded the meshes collapsing back into spheres that roll away, apparently unchanged. No known tool marks exist on the cylinders, and vegetation grows across their lips without interference, yet nothing living enters the basins. Their placement follows no obvious grid; they are simply “where spheres pause longest,” an answer that itself moves a meter or two each decade.

The prevailing rule of the place is patient metabolism: nothing here is in a hurry except heat, and heat only maintains the slow chemical economy that makes the metal crawl, the bladders pulse, the gliders descend. Every process measured in human lifetimes registers as a faint drift—the expansion of a garden bridge by a hand-span, the widening of a crystal seam by the thickness of a page. Yet because the cliff system itself extends beyond the visible horizon, those drifts add up. To a visitor standing on any single terrace, the landscape feels eternal, but the horizon is actually migrating, one bladder-width per generation, as the vertical terraces rearrange themselves like a vast, quiet abacus.
