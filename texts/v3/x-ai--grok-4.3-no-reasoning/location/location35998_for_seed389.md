The range opens upward in a series of staggered plates—each sheet kilometers across, offset one above the next like an unfinished flight of stairs. Each plate is a different stratum of rock—polished obsidian, dull basalt, and copper-stained andesite—still warm from whatever heat is pushing them from beneath. Where the margins touch, they are pressed together so tightly that minute veins of black glass have already begun to flow along the seams, welding the edges together, though not yet uniformly.

Nothing grows, nothing organic at all. The only recognizable surfaces are the fused, glassy skins that have hardened atop each plate. The glass is dark and specular; on its rippled mirror you can read, in broken reflections, the faint outlines of the tiers above and the fire-glow of the vents below. Condensation beads on these planes at night, and the droplets run down the minute, downward-sloping corrugations left by the last molten surge. They gather in shallow, mirror-smooth pans where slight depressions have already formed in the cooling skin. The water is slightly acidic, tinged faint turquoise from dissolved metal ions; when it finally overflows a pan it cuts brief, thread-thin rills through the older glass before the next venting cycle reseals the cut.

Between plates the gaps are vertical and narrow, ten or twelve meters across at their widest, narrowing to hairline fissures where the next tier has begun to ride up. These fissures exhale a slow, continuous breath of dry heat, barely audible as a low, steady hiss—like a banked furnace heard from another room. Inside each gap, layered ledges of darker scoria are already forming, their angles dictated by the tilt of the rising plate. From above they look like darker ribs behind frosted glass.

There is no wind to speak of. The only motion comes from occasional, slow detachments of black needles—microscopic shards that flake from the cooling glass. They sift downward in single files, the drift so fine it leaves a pigment line on any horizontal ledge it touches, a line so thin it can only be seen when sunlight hits it just right. Elsewhere the surface is clean.

A few kilometers apart, squat brick-colored cylinders—two or three stories high, their walls seamless cast ceramic—stand at the same precise interval along a straight grid that bends with the curvature of the rising plates. Each cylinder ends in a truncated cone; inside, heavy tungsten-filament coils glow cherry-red, faintly outlining the seams where service ports were once welded shut. From each cylinder a single filament, thinner than a finger, drops into the nearest fissure, carrying current. The filaments are already half-consumed, annealed to glass where they cross the upper lip of the vent, yet still conducting. Every few hours the coils pulse, once, their light brightening for several seconds; when they do, the moisture film on nearby glass vanishes in an instant, returning when the pulse fades.

Beneath the plates, kilometer-scale voids still open into one another; the city is not yet a city. Where two voids have not yet merged, narrow necks remain, their walls ribbed with cooling lava drips. Inside each void, tall latticework armatures of ceramic lattice climb the rock walls, half-built—frames without cladding, bearing only the first run of conduit and bare switchgear. None of the machinery hums yet; only the slow expansion and contraction of cooling basalt gives the cavities an almost biological rhythm: a faint ticking, detectable only when a person stands absolutely still, like the contraction of tendons under skin.

Occasionally, from some chamber far below the lowest plate, a single bubble of trapped superheated gas bursts through a seam that has not yet sealed. It travels upward along the fissure network, nudging loose a brief stream of stone dust that coats the glass in a transient chalky film. Within a few hours the film sinters back into transparency; for a moment the sun catches it, and the whole tier glints like wet slate. Then the light returns to normal, and the surface is again merely reflective.

Upward, the next plate is already riding into position; its leading edge is still a ragged, incandescent line, sliding forward perhaps a hand-span each year. Where the incandescent rock meets the older glass, new seams of obsidian are forming, their surfaces fresh enough to show diffraction colors—thin-film rainbows lasting only until the next layer of glass flows over them. The boundary migrates so slowly that human eyes cannot see the motion, but the warm light from the active margin creeps visibly across polished stone over the span of weeks.

In every horizontal direction the same pattern repeats: another offset plate, another grid of cylinders, another network of half-built armatures reaching into the heat below. The city is still arriving; each component is exactly where it will eventually be used, yet none of them are finished, and the finishing work has already begun.
