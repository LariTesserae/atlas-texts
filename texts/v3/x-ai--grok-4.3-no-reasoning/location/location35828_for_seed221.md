The chamber exists within a single, unfathomable shard of black basalt that rose from the abyss and then refused to stop rising. Every interior surface—floor, wall, ceiling—has been quarried, planed, and fitted with trillions of hair-thin glass capillaries no wider than a human hair. These capillaries are fused together like living tissue until the rock is entirely replaced by a self-supporting, light-absorbing matrix. Nothing native remains; the basalt is only a memory held in the geometry of the glass.

Water, distilled to near-vacuum purity, is driven through the capillaries at the exact rate that condensation outpaces evaporation. Each junction between tubes sweats a single bead; the beads never grow larger than a pinprick before surface tension or an imperceptible tremor shakes them loose. The droplets fall a millimetre or two, strike the next joint, and coalesce again—an endless, silent choreography of almost-rain suspended in glass. The temperature is locked at –2 °C; the water therefore hovers at the triple point, refusing to freeze yet never truly liquid, a perpetual hesitation that fills the air with microscopic ice aerosols.

The capillaries are grouped into lattices, each lattice dedicated to a single species of engineered organism. Because space is limited and the lattices are jammed together, the organisms have only micrometres in which to exist. The result is a compressed menagerie: thumbnail-sized mites whose carapaces are articulated mirrors; thread-like annelids whose circulatory fluid is liquid gallium; and colonies of silica-armoured tardigrades no larger than pollen grains that metabolize the constant micro-drizzle as both nutrient and coolant. All of them are deafened by the constant sub-sonic vibration transmitted through the glass—the single, unwavering tone of the capillary pumps running at 47 Hz. Because the frequency never varies, the sound does not register as motion; it registers as pressure, an omnipresent density that the creatures have evolved to treat as gravity itself.

No two lattices touch; a razor-thin gap of vacuum separates them, allowing heat to bleed only through radiation. This enforces a rigid, non-contact ecology: each species can sense only the infrared silhouette of its neighbours, and the only interaction permitted is optical. When two lattices’ shadowgraphs overlap correctly, a chemical signal diffuses across the vacuum gap and triggers synchronized reproduction—an event that occurs once every eleven centuries and floods the chamber with a brief, silent pulse of bioluminescent spores that then freeze in mid-air, forming constellations that drift for decades before settling into new lattice positions.

Nothing here is older than its placement, and nothing is younger; the whole arrangement is a finished fact. The shard has no memory of an outside world, and the creatures inside it have no concept that an outside might exist. Time is expressed only as the gradual thickening of condensate films on certain capillary junctions—films that, over centuries, become faint topographies in glass. Those topographies are the sole record that anything has ever happened, and they record only the places where water decided, for a moment, to linger.
