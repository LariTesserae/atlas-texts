The place is a city that has grown in layers until it has forgotten any ground beneath itself. Walk long enough and you will eventually reach one of the edges where a balcony or walkway simply ends above a grey haze; no one speaks of what is down there, and no map records it. Everything above is solid and engineered: riveted plates, I-beams, panelled walkways that rise and step in the same measured rhythm as the ventilation grilles that exhale faintly warmer air. The metal is always cool to the touch because the entire assemblage is hung inside an envelope of constant high-altitude wind; even at noon the temperature refuses to climb. Condensation skins the undersides of every horizontal surface, forming droplets that gather, fall, and strike the level below with a soft tap repeated across millions of surfaces until the whole structure seems to tick like a vast clock.

Vegetation exists only where intention put it. Narrow trays hang beneath the brightest sodium lamps; fern-cousins with silvery undersides grow there in ordered rows, their roots drinking collected mist. Between the trays run service catwalks wide enough for two people. Sometimes a technician kneels to prune, gloved hands moving with the same measured patience that governs everything else. If the fronds touch the railings they leave a faint green smear that dries almost immediately; nothing is allowed to spread.  

Fauna is abundant but almost silent. The most common residents are small six-legged gliders that nest inside the trusswork. Their wings are translucent membranes stretched over graphite struts; in the perpetual bright they reflect hard little highlights as they coast from strut to strut. They do not call; they communicate in the shifting angle of their glide, in the faint electrostatic pop that marks a change of direction. Occasionally a larger silhouette—more bird than insect—glides across the upper vault, its wings cutting the glare into momentary blades of shadow that travel the decks below at the same stately pace as everything else.  

Every surface is either load-bearing or a conduit. Pipes, ducts, fiber-optic runs, and pressurized hoses are clamped or welded into the same lattice that supports the walkways. The network has no visible beginning or end; technicians replace sections only when a gauge drifts out of tolerance, and even then the replacement is prefabricated and swapped in minutes. No excavation disturbs the order because there is nothing underneath to excavate.  

The light never softens. Banks of full-spectrum lamps are mounted behind diffusing panels that span every ceiling plane. Maintenance crews replace bulbs on strict rotation schedules; failure is measured in cumulative lux-hours rather than sudden blackouts. Because the light never changes, there are no noon shadows or twilight colors; time is told by the pressure in the steam lines or by the slow color shift in the indicator salts that line the nutrient trays.  

What breaks is not machinery but the rules that ought to govern distance. Sometimes a corridor simply continues for longer than its measured length. A person may walk an hour and find they are still between the same pair of numbered stanchions. Other times an intersection appears that does not exist on any schematic; two technicians can stand at its center, watch their wrist-chronometers agree that an entire shift has passed, and then step back into the corridor they left with nothing altered except the memory of having been elsewhere. The city does not protest these contradictions. Gauges still read within tolerance, trays still yield their harvest, and the gliders still coast between the same struts. Only the sense of scale quietly rearranges itself, like a slow hydraulic adjustment too gradual for any single observer to notice until they try to retrace a route and discover the old distances have lengthened by the width of a thought.

Sound is limited to the soft, omnipresent percussion of dripping condensate, the low, almost subsonic hum of the ventilation fans, and the occasional metallic click of a magnetic relay engaging somewhere inside the walls. Voices are used sparingly; conversations are conducted in the shorthand of work orders. When a person does speak at length, the words travel only a few meters before they are absorbed by the baffles and insulation that line every vertical plane.  

Nothing here is natural, and nothing pretends to be. Every material was brought in, shaped, fastened, tested. Yet the longer one remains, the more the distinction between construction and growth grows uncertain. The nutrient trays are swapped on the same schedule the structural joints are inspected; the inspection drones follow the same flight paths the gliders use when they are not gliding. The city does not expand so much as accumulate itself, each new conduit or catwalk added in the same patient rhythm that governs the fall of each drop of condensate. In the perpetual bright, among the slow arithmetic of maintenance cycles, the structure perfects itself without ever seeming to hurry.
