The place is a high shelf no wider than an orchard terrace, suspended between two slow-weathered cheeks of stone that rise like shoulders on either side. Long ago, the rock split and the interior of both cheeks began to sweat: from every crack and every faint crease, clear water seeps and beads, follows lichen paths, and gathers in dark, skin-deep pools that tremble with each drop. Nothing splashes; the sound has already been absorbed by the surrounding moss.  

The moss itself is the only true floor. It is dense and nearly black where the water lingers longest, but thins to pale emerald toward the open edge where light catches it, leaving a hand’s width of stone visible beneath. The rock shows ribs of darker mineral—old veins that have resisted the seep—so the floor has the slow, banded look of a riverbed seen in plan. The moss piles up in low cushions no higher than a shin; each cushion is its own tiny watershed, its surface filmed with condensation that never quite becomes a drop before the next night’s mist re-wets it.

Above the moss, three or four slender trees grow. They are not forest trees; they root into hairline fissures no wider than a blade, and their trunks stay small, their bark white and faintly translucent where the water inside shows through. Their leaves are single large plates, each veined like a river delta. When the wind (if it ever comes) bends one plate, its neighbor remains still; movement travels leaf by leaf, never branch by branch. The leaves are spaced far enough that noon sun can lance between them and paint long vertical blades of light across the moss.  

At intervals the seep-water pauses at a ledge no deeper than a cupped palm. There the water turns faintly milky; minute threads of mineral grow upward through it, forming translucent spires no taller than a finger. They lean toward one another until they touch and fuse, creating small natural lattices that sit half-submerged. Around the lattices the water temperature drops just enough that a filament of colder air sometimes rests like a veil over the surface, faintly distorting whatever lies below.  

Beyond the shelf the cliff face continues, but so far out of reach that the space between feels closed, a chamber of sky. The air here remains still; only the slow seep and the slower growth of the mineral threads mark time. The light that enters from the open side never brightens or dims abruptly; it grades, over hours, from the pale straw of morning through the copper of late day and back, always arriving at the moss at the same low angle.  

Nothing else moves. No footfalls, no wings, no voices. The only variation is the almost imperceptible lengthening of a mineral thread, or the gradual darkening of a cushion of moss after a week of heavier seepage. Even the dripping at the cliff’s lip has learned to fall at intervals so regular and so soft that the ear forgets to register it. The place therefore feels both enclosed and unfinished—an alcove still in the process of deciding what shape the next thousand years will give it.
