The place is a basin ringed by low basalt ridges that have been folded and lapped by an unseen sea for centuries, so the rock faces stand like the worn lips of a great bowl.  Rain seldom reaches the ground in sheets; instead it hangs as silver filaments that slip from cliff-top pores every late afternoon, turning the ridges into slow, sobbing walls.  Where the filaments meet warmer stone they bead, coalesce, and drop in irregular rhythms that sound like blunt mallets striking wet hide.  This is the dominant percussion of the city: not traffic, but water count.

Beneath the ridges lies a single terrace only a few hundred meters across at its widest, yet the city fills every square of it.  Streets are stone channels no wider than a person’s shoulders, roofed by iron grids whose gaps leak a perpetual rust-colored mist.  The buildings lean into one another like coral polyps; each floor juts a little farther than the last until the uppermost balconies almost touch across the lane, creating a false ceiling that further softens the light.  Glass is scarce.  Instead, panes are beaten from salvaged copper so thin the metal flexes and sighs when internal boilers vent.  The resulting glint is faint, the color of tarnished pennies seen under river water.

The heat is constant and close.  Pipes carrying super-heated condensate thread the masonry like bright veins; where they surface they bloom into hissing brass lilies that keep plazas above the dew-point.  Citizens walk through veils of their own condensed breath.  The air is so saturated that voices carry only a few body-lengths before they thicken into murmurs, then are overwritten by the metronomic slap of the weeping rock.

Vegetation grows in deliberate seams between the pipes and the stone.  Ferns with black, paddle-shaped fronds crowd the joints where humidity pools; their spores drift upward and adhere to warmer metal, becoming living insulation.  Rope-thick vines with milky, luminous sap climb the undersides of the iron roof-grids, dripping slow droplets that catch what little light filters from sodium-orange work lamps.  These droplets ignite for a fraction of a second—an ephemeral constellation—before falling onto the wet flags.

There is no fauna; the temperature, the continuous vibration, and the solvent-laced condensate combine to make the niche unlivable for bone and blood.  In their place, the city runs on a metabolism of servitor frames—jointed armatures of lacquered reed and salvaged bronze—programmed to scrub mineral scale from every weeping surface.  Their motion is constant yet oddly silent: felt in the bones more than heard, because each scrubber’s pads are soaked in oil rendered from the same ferns that lace the walls.

The industrial core sits at the terrace’s exact center: a single cylindrical hall four stories high whose exterior is a braid of water-mains and flywheels.  Inside, horizontal turbines catch the perpetual draft that howls down the encircling ridges; the wind itself is the product of heat differential between the sun-struck rock above and the cooler basin below.  The turbines drive drop-hammers that compact harvested condensate into dense, glassy ingots used as both coolant and currency.  Each compression stroke ends with a boom like distant cannon fire, followed by a high, crystalline ring as the ingot slides into brine gutters.  Workers inside wear copper mesh veils; the mesh sings in sympathy with the turbines, producing an ethereal overtone that braids with the hammer-fall until the hall feels less like architecture and more like the hollow of an enormous bell being struck.

Outside, time is marked not by clocks but by the gradations of wetness on the flagstones.  At dawn the streets are slick mirrors; by midday they steam and turn pewter; at dusk the mirrors cloud again as the rock resumes its weeping.  The basin rim catches the last direct light each evening—a brief red halo—before the lamps gutter awake and the city returns to its copper-colored dusk.  In that half-moment of contrast, every droplet suspended on a vine or a pipe seems to hold an ember, and then the lamps win and the ember goes out.

The settlement feels finite, almost claustrophobic, yet complete: an ecology whose every input (condensate, heat, vibration) is re-ingested by the next layer of pipes, plants, or servitors.  Boundaries are literal; the ridges admit only a single stair, wide enough for two abreast, its risers glossy from centuries of condensate.  Beyond that stair the land simply ends in sheer drop and open water.  Inside, nothing is still: pumps gasp, ferns unfurl, ingots slide, voices smear into the constant rain-clock of stone.
