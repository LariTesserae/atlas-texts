The air itself is water.

A fine, cold fog—almost rain that never quite decides to fall—hangs between the needles of the dark-leafed trees. Each breath condenses into a momentary cloud before it dissolves into the larger haze. Water clings to everything: the bark, the moss, even the tiny crystals that glitter at the base of cliff-like outcrops barely taller than a person. Where the stone has split, small streams thread downward, weeping from hairline fractures, collecting in depressions no deeper than a cupped palm. There is no standing pool, yet the ground never feels dry.

The terrain rises in a slow, deliberate swell. There is no summit here, only a sequence of shoulders that level into narrow ledges, each ledge perhaps wide enough for two or three people to stand abreast. The rock is young—still angular, its edges sharp enough to cut cloth. No rounded boulders yet, no smooth cobble beds; only jagged slabs stacked where the mountain’s slow pulse has pushed them apart. Between the slabs, thin mats of root hold the first soil. Nothing is settled; the ground is still deciding what shape it will keep.

On these ledges sit the dwellings. They are not houses in the usual sense. Each is a single chamber no larger than a modest room, formed by fitting flat stones into low walls and roofing them with overlapping cedar shingles soaked to a silvery grey. The walls are no higher than a standing adult’s chest; one steps over the threshold rather than through a door. Inside, the air is colder than outside because the stones store the night’s chill. There is no hearth—only a shallow basin where coals can rest without open flame, their smoke escaping through narrow gaps between shingles. A single person could live inside each chamber; two would crowd it. Twenty such chambers climb the slope in a loose spiral, never touching, each separated from the next by at least the width of a tree. The settlement feels half-empty even when every chamber is occupied. The spaces between them hold only fog and the slow drip from overhanging needles.

The trees are few but enormous. Their trunks are thick enough that two adults could not join hands around one, and they rise straight until their lowest branches begin ten or twelve feet overhead. The foliage is dense and dark green, almost black against the lighter fog. Each tree stands alone on its own patch of ledge; between them the ground is bare stone or a thin carpet of moss that gives underfoot with a faint, wet sigh. No undergrowth fills the gaps; there is simply not enough soil yet. The trees are the first arrivals. Their roots pry at the fissures, their fallen needles darken the stone, and in a few centuries—when the slope has gentled and the soil thickened—other plants will follow.

Life moves slowly here. A beetle the size of a thumbnail crosses a ledge in the time it takes the fog to shift from one side of the clearing to the other. Small, pale salamanders rest in the perpetual drip beneath the lowest shingles, their bodies almost translucent. Occasionally a raven lands on a roof peak, the sound of its talons on wet cedar carrying farther than its call. The ravens are the loudest inhabitants, their croaks ricocheting between stone and fog until the sound seems to come from everywhere at once. Beneath that croaking lies a continuous, soft percussion: water striking stone, water striking leaf, water striking the thin metal pans villagers leave outside to collect it. The pans ring with a single bright note when full; when half-full they give a lower, throatier tone. Villagers adjust them daily, not for water—they have all they need—but for the changing chord that marks the passage of days.

Nothing is hurried. A single plank left leaning against a wall will darken and silver before anyone thinks to move it. A crack that appears in a roof will be watched for a season to see whether the stone beneath is shifting; only then will someone fetch the mallet and wedges. The settlement grows by addition rather than replacement: when a family needs more space, they do not enlarge an existing chamber but begin a new one two ledges higher or lower, carrying flat stones one by one from the talus slope above. Over decades the spiral lengthens; over centuries it may meet itself again. The builders do not speak of completion.

Light reaches the ledges in pale, even sheets. The fog scatters direct sun into a general brightness without shadows sharp enough to define. Colors are muted: the stone is the grey of wet ash, the moss a blue-green, the cedar a charcoal that never quite dries to brown. At dusk the fog takes on a faint luminous quality, as if the water itself remembers daylight a little longer than the air. Night brings no true darkness, only a deeper grey in which the sound of water grows louder because nothing else moves.

The world here is still deciding its own rules. The stone has not yet agreed to stay in place; the trees have not yet agreed to multiply; the people have not yet agreed to remain or to leave. Everything waits, patient, while water continues its slow work of loosening and binding.
