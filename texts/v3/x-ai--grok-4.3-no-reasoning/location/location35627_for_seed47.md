The world is a single colossal crystal of calcium-sulfate anhydrite, a geologic formation the size of a continent, colder than any place on Earth yet far from absolute zero. Its surface has spent eons locked in the quiet stasis of its final chemistry; it does not erode, crack, or dissolve because every reaction that could occur already did, æons ago. Where the surface is exposed it lies like porcelain, polished to an almost optical flatness by nothing but the gentle, patient removal of stray ions into vacuum. Beneath this lacquer-thin skin, microscopic fault-lines of hydrated salt and native sulfur are embedded in thicknesses measured in atoms. Occasionally one of these filaments will fail, and the collapse ripples outward in a slow radial bloom that can be seen from orbit, a brief white flare swallowed almost at once by the mineral’s own lattice.  

Viewed from a distance the surface is divided into faintly tessellated planes, each ten to thirty kilometers on a side, tilted at angles so minute the eye mistakes them for flat. Across the planes lie scattered shapes that were once structures. They are not ruins. They are residuals: the insoluble cores of vast machines whose softer components long ago sublimed. One encounters a geodesic dome reduced to a tracery of tungsten-silicon whiskers, or a single monolith of magnesium alloy still levitating above a superconducting pedestal. The pedestal draws energy from a fraction of a degree’s temperature difference between the surrounding anhydrite and the heat sink of space; the monolith never touches the ground. None of these remnants whisper or hum; they are soundless, and the crystal beneath them transmits no vibration. Only the occasional structural collapse registers as a soft puff of crystallized dust no larger than talcum.

The atmosphere—if it can be called that—is a transparent helium-sodium mix only a few millibars thick. Molecules move so slowly that individual sodium atoms hang visibly as motes when the light strikes them. These atoms, resonating at the D-line, turn the sunlight a faint peach color; where they clump above subsidence pits they form drifting, lens-shaped clouds that drift for centuries before dispersing.  

The temperature stays near 230 K. In the depressions between tessellated planes this falls another twenty degrees; there, solid nitrogen frost collects in ultrathin veneers that glitter like powdered glass. At the rims of the depressions the nitrogen sublimates and reforms continuously as solar heat arrives—yet the change produces no audible hiss. The pressure is too low, and the mass moved too small; the only evidence is a ghostly recomposition of the boundary line over hours.

Nothing grows here, though something once tried. Embedded half a micron beneath the anhydrite are filamentary voids—former tunnels of a silicon-based lithotroph that exhausted its nutrient salts before the crystal sealed itself around it. The voids run for tens of meters then terminate; at each dead end is a microscopic crystal of the organism’s own waste, perfectly faceted. They are the only recurring geometry in an otherwise amorph landscape.  

Above the surface, kilometers high, orbital structures persist as wisps of fullerene filament. They are not satellites; they are loose kinetic sculptures, remnants of an experiment that released swarms of self-assembling tethers into stable polar orbits. The tethers shed electrostatic dust that falls, centimeters per decade, toward the surface, arriving as micrometeor showers too fine to be felt even on an eardrum. Each grain ionizes against the helium and briefly illuminates its own fall with a thread of violet Cherenkov light.

At planetary scales this crystal is so enormous that its own tidal locking has generated a perpetual circulation cell inside the mineral itself: minute shear stresses circulate along internal planes, rearranging dopants and occasionally creating new superconducting pathways. When such a pathway forms, it expels a localized magnetic plume into space. The plumes interact with the passing dust sculptures, nudging them into new orbital shells. The whole system therefore displays a slow, large-scale turbulence, invisible from the surface yet measurable from orbit: a self-reorganizing magnetic architecture that has outlasted the creatures who first set it in motion.  

No footfalls, no wind, no voices. The only human-scale motion is the incremental straightening of a filament that has been slightly kinked for a millennium; once it straightens, it remains straight forever.
