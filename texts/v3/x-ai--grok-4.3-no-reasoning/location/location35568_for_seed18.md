A single arched vault, roughly fifty paces across and thirty high, carved into the solid core of a needle-shaped mountain. The vault is not open to the sky; its ceiling is a solid plate of fused stone and riveted steel that once served as a planetary-defense battery casing. In the middle of that ceiling a single, sealed porthole—thick, crystalline, faintly luminescent—admits a daylight equivalent from a pale fusion reactor a thousand meters below. The result is neither day nor night, but a constant, silvery illumination that never warms nor cools.

Everything inside is built. The floor is a six-meter-thick girdered platform of oxide-red iron that was once an ammunition elevator deck; the original plates still show stenciled safety warnings in a dead language. Now the plates are bowed slightly by the slow settling of time, and their edges curl where constant condensation has leached the metal into mineral bloom. Between the plates, wide gaps reveal the abyss: shafts plunge downward in perfect geometry, each shaft wall lined with redundant cabling that no longer carries current. The gaps emit a constant, low hiss of slightly pressurized air—moisture rising from deeper reservoirs.

The iron is never truly dry. Condensation collects on the undersides of the beams, forms beads, and drips like a metronome that never stops. Every drip splashes into the drip-cisterns—circular arrays of ceramic catch-basins—that ring the central space. From the cisterns, water travels through capillary glass tubing to hanging gardens that swing on rusting gantry cranes once meant for missile reloads. These gardens are spherical cages of welded mesh packed with root matrices, now overflowing with leaves that press outward through the mesh like green tongues. The leaves are deep violet beneath the silver light, veins thick with mineral salts leached from the iron.

Everything sways, but slowly. A single turn of the load-bearing cranes takes three hours to complete; they still respond to magnetic pulses from failing regulators. Each shift is accompanied by the creak of failing bearings and a low harmonic tremor that travels through the iron floor. The fauna here consists of tiny pale crustaceans—millimeter scale—whose carapaces are metal-flecked, evolved to metabolize the iron oxides. They move in slow processions along the condensation drip lines, their clicking mandibles audible only if you press an ear to the floor.

Because the vault exists inside an otherwise solid mountain, sound does not escape. The slightest motion of the gantry chains sets off reverberations that layer upon themselves until the entire volume rings with a continuous, multilayered drone. The tone never rises or falls; it has already reached equilibrium. Human hearing eventually stops registering it as sound, yet the body feels the pressure. When new condensation beads finally detach from the ceiling and fall, their impact produces only a minute modulation in the drone—a brief, almost imperceptible brightening—before the original note reasserts itself.

Nothing is transient except the drip rate, which slowly decreases over centuries as deeper aquifers lose pressure. When the last droplet finally stops, the crustaceans will begin to starve and the gardens will brown, but for now every surface is wet, green, and iron-rich, suspended inside a bell of patient, echoing stillness.
