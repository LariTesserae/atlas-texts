The Watered City

It is a low, dense quarter of an older city that seems less built than grown through water. The streets have sunk beneath centuries of condensation and the steady weeping of the stone. Channels cut through the avenues—half-designed, half-worn—turning the paving into a grid of shallow canals. Runoff from neighboring districts arrives here through ducts of reddish brick that emerge from the masonry like roots; the water is never rushing, yet the surface never stills.  

The buildings stand shoulder-close, their faces mottled with lichen and salt. Where plaster has spalled, the underlying brick shows the teeth of repeated wetting and drying. Balconies, now inaccessible, are thick with moss that spills over the railings in slow cascades. Metal stairs—riveted iron, long oxidized to a deep umber—descend into water that mirrors their angles in a brown, wavering duplicate. The load-bearing pillars beneath those stairs are ringed with faint tide-marks no higher than a wrist’s breadth, as though the tide forgets to rise and yet cannot forget entirely.

Inside the ground floors, workshops continue. Belt-driven lathes and small presses clatter, their rhythms blurred by the muffling of saturated air. Steam pipes, lagged in canvas, leak thin plumes that rise, cool, and condense along the iron beams overhead, then fall back in slow drips onto the same bearings they left. Brass gauges sweat; each needle trembles, but only slightly, once every several seconds, registering pressures whose origins are already forgotten.  

Rain collects on every horizontal surface and finds its way through roof joints into tin gutters whose joints have been replaced so many times they resemble scales. The gutters themselves are now a secondary roof-scape, hanging above the streets at the height of a lifted arm. Because everything is kept in slow, constant motion by gravity alone, the sound is less percussive than respiratory—an overlapping hiss, the susurration of water over lips of stone, the deeper note of water meeting other water.  

Vegetation grows from every crevice the water can reach. Ferns unroll between bricks, their croziers the same color as the oxidized iron. A climbing fig has found its way along an old telephone conduit; its pale roots follow the conduit’s curve for three stories before vanishing into a ventilator grille. There is no soil in the ordinary sense. Where plants root they do so in the compacted silt left by successive floods, a fibrous matrix of leaves and brick dust that behaves like soft stone until stepped on, then collapses into a cool ooze that refills itself within an hour.  

No birds move through the filtered light. The dimness is not night; the canopy of ducts and catwalks keeps most direct sun away, yet the air is warm enough that breath feels weighted. The only movement against this stillness is the incremental migration of the condensation line on the window glass, rising a few millimeters each day as interior heat leaks outward. In the long workrooms, oil lamps have been replaced by bare carbon-filament bulbs whose filaments glow a sullen orange, reflections doubling on the water under the benches.  

The canals terminate in a settling basin no larger than a courtyard. Here the slow water pauses, thickens with suspended clay, and is siphoned upward through an arrangement of Archimedes screws turned by the same belt system that powers the lathes. The screws are wooden, their paddles swollen until they almost touch the housing. Every revolution lifts a cupful of water into an overhead cistern whose overflow returns, by separate channels, to the roofs—closing a loop whose only import is continuance.  

At the very center of the quarter stands a single civic clock, its face at street level so that those wading past can read it. The mechanism is housed below the water in a sealed copper drum. Once an hour the escapement releases a small amount of collected silt into the current; once a day the hour hand advances by its own weight along a notched arc. The sound of the silt sliding out is a faint, granular sigh, audible only because all other sounds are water against stone, water against iron, water against itself.
