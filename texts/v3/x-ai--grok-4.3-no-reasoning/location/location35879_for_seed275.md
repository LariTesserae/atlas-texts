Beneath the plain there is no aquifer, only a dry mineral lattice whose pores have been filled, grain by grain, with the living architecture of a single vegetative order. Root-chitin, leaf-ligament, and vascular scaffolding weave together into a city whose every surface grows and works. The metropolis is still warm only by the measure of bodies and slow, chlorophyll engines; its mean temperature keeps it just cool enough that the metabolism of its walls never tips into fever.

Seen from above, the plain is featureless—an acre of pale, unbroken dust. From beneath, the false ground is a vault whose ceiling is made of knotted, living pavers. These pavers are not laid stone; they are the flattened, sclerotized leaves of colossal, horizontal vines whose cambium has been trained to mineral hardness. The streets between them are gaps left by the vines’ natural spacing, corridors just wide enough for a freight tram or a single passenger. Overhead the “sky” is the underside of the next leafy stratum, translucent enough to admit a filtered, even light but never a view of the surface. The illumination is daylight caught and re-radiated by successive layers of cuticle-wax; it never brightens to glare, never falls to gloom.

Because the city is still being born, many districts show embryonic morphology. Some avenues are only half-formed hollow stems whose future load-bearing ribs are still thickening; sap still drips from the unfinished walls, and the scent is faintly sweet, like split green wood. Elsewhere, older streets have already lignified and contracted, pulling slight tensile arcs into the ceiling that collect the weight of later levels. These contraction joints are the only “architecture” imposed by the inhabitants; everything else grows itself.

Fauna moves through this half-built scaffold in strict accordance with the seasonless circadian rhythm of the vegetative city. Pale, many-limbed myriapods the size of cats patrol the leaf-pavers, rasping away bacterial films and reseeding beneficial symbionts with every pass. Small, transparent hexapods drift on convection currents created by the slow exhalation of the walls; they gather at the “streetlights,” where specialized phosphorescent glands pulse once every few minutes, providing micro-light for nocturnal pollinators. Larger fauna—slow, long-necked browsers—are restricted to the vertical greenways that rise, like living elevators, through successive strata; they descend only when fruiting bodies swell on the lower ceilings, and their feeding prunes the canopy exactly where clearance is needed for the next generation of tram rails.

Industrial processes operate not in factories but in specialized tissues. At regular intersections, clusters of heartwood knots harden into pressure vessels that metabolize atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia fertilizers; excess heat is vented through dark, flower-shaped chimneys whose petals curl open only after midnight. These chimneys release a faint white mist—harmless to the city’s inhabitants, deadly to surface microbes—that drifts downward and condenses on the cool mineral floor, forming transient pools no deeper than a fingernail. In those pools, motile rootlets drink and return minerals to the living streets.

Sound travels slowly here. The thick vascular walls damp most vibration, leaving only the periodic sigh of pressure release, the click of hexapod legs against leaf-pavers, and, once per local day, the low choral moan of the great horizontal vines as they relax after pumping sap to the upper tiers. Voices, when they occur, are muffled and close; no echoes linger.

Because elevation is everywhere negative, there is no horizon; instead, the city opens radially through successive horizons of living strata. The farther one walks, the older the tissues become. In the distance, kilometers away, an entire quarter has already matured into glassy black heartwood so dense that light is refracted, not transmitted. From that quarter a single, vast trunk—still growing, still embryonic at its core—rises toward the unreachable surface, a vertical promise that the city may one day press through the mineral crust and claim the sky.

Until then, the underworld remains a work-in-progress of cool, breathing architecture, sparsely inhabited by quiet engineers whose only raw material is life.
