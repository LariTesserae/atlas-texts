There is no water, yet the place is not arid. A transparent fluid—neither liquid nor gas—circulates in slow, imperceptible sheets between crystalline strata, carrying minerals the way blood carries oxygen. The mineral is bright gypsum laced with microscopic lattices that flex as the medium passes, shimmering like heat-haze even though the temperature is merely warm.

Above and below the gypsum veins, mats of vegetation grow sideways, stacking in horizontal canopies that press against one another with no visible gaps. The plants are broad, velvety fronds the color of wet slate; their undersides glitter with dew-like droplets that are actually tiny solid crystals, condensing the non-aqueous fluid into visible nodules. Because nothing changes, the crystals remain fixed, glittering at the same angle every moment. Their collective light bounces between the layers, creating a soft, multi-directional daylight that requires no sky.

The surface is low and recent: a single geologic event cracked the planetary crust only centuries ago, leaving tilted plates of gypsum that still carry razor-edged fracture lines. Over these planes the vegetation has risen like a plush tide, so the landscape feels simultaneously newborn and impossibly old. The whole region extends for dozens of kilometers, each fractured plate a few hectares wide, separated by seams a hand-span across where denser fronds interlock like fingers. Nothing built interrupts this quilt.

Because temperature and pressure are uniform, winds never form and sound is almost absent—only an occasional soft click when one crystal lattice grows an atom thicker and a neighboring frond shifts a fraction. Fauna exists solely as faint, translucent filaments that drift along the fluid sheets; they reproduce so slowly that centuries pass between generations, and they register as pale streaks across vision rather than living creatures. Their motion is the only dynamic element, yet it loops in identical curves, repeating the same vectors forever.

At the scale of a human stride, the fronds feel close enough to touch; at the scale of a valley, the plates tilt and rise just enough to make horizons waver. At the scale of the entire formation the pattern repeats without end, the same seams, the same glitter, the same soft clicks—a closed, time-invariant ecology engineered by physics no longer fully describable, where vegetation has replaced both sky and sea, and the only rule is the continuous, unchanging presence of growth inside stillness.
