Beneath the city of Greenmantle, the original forest still breathes.  

It began as a simple swamp where cypress and tupelo grew with their roots in black water. The city grew on top, then grew again, then grew into itself, layer upon layer, each new foundation using the one below as scaffolding until the whole metropolis was one unbroken tangle of living timber and poured stone. What appears on the surface as an ordinary river-side district is in truth a single organism many storeys thick, its ceiling a warren of brick barrel-vaults whose mortar cracks each season to release threads of root. Beneath those vaults the original swamp has never drained; water only rose, taking the weight of every added floor until the meniscus now kisses the keystone of every arch.  

The “streets” are actually rhizomatic corridors whose width was once set by ox-cart axles. Over decades the flanking pilings bulged, their cellulose grain re-orienting around each iron tyre rut until the passage resembles the inside of a petrified throat. Moisture films every surface; even the rust on the overhead tram-rails sweats droplets that fall in patient intervals, marking the seconds for those below. Condensation gathers along the upper flanges of the I-beams until it reaches critical mass, then streams down the webs in pencil-thin cataracts that disappear into floor-grates clogged with liverworts.  

No sky exists here. The only light arrives as a perpetual half-glow leaking through resin-sealed skylights—thick panes poured from the sap of the uppermost canopy that now forms the modern pavement. That glow is further softened by a haze of plant-borne steam; each leaf below exhales its own small cloud, and together they mute the illumination to the color of weak tea.  

Because every surface is either timber, bark, leaf, or the mineralised residue of leaf-fall, there is no gap for an animal body to occupy. The space is already full. The occasional movement is not locomotion but translocation: a vine re-threaded by a geared winch once every decade, a section of floor lifted on hydraulic jacks so new pilings can be grafted beneath. These procedures unfold at the pace of heart-wood deposition; the crews work in slow arcs, winches ticking once for every breath the surrounding tissue takes.  

Sound fills what volume the plants leave. Sap under pressure clicks in the xylem like distant knitting needles; entire avenues of trunks resonate together when a ventilator fan three storeys above changes speed, turning the corridor into an enormous stopped pipe. Condensation drips form an arrhythmic percussion on copper catch-basins whose overflow pipes moan at two hertz. Lift-platforms carrying replacement ceiling panels grind along tracks padded with cork; the friction produces a constant low C-sharp that never resolves.  

The temperature hovers at the edge of summer rain. Warmth rises from the marshy earth, but the constant mist strips excess heat, so the air is kept at the precise point where breath neither fogs nor scalds. Over centuries the balance has locked; thermometers set into living trunks have not moved more than a degree since the original survey.  

Every architectural detail grew from an earlier biological function. Door hinges are modified pulvini—swellings at the base of leaf stalks—irrigated daily so they stay supple. Lamps are photophores grafted from deep-ocean algae, their glow modulated by sliding palisades of bark. Even the drainage grates are stomata, able, once per lunar cycle, to iris shut if the marsh detects contamination in the run-off.  

Nothing arrives quickly; nothing leaves quickly. The city and the forest that became it continue their single, gradual metabolism—each new brick pressed into place by expanding wood, each new root allowed to thread an old conduit—until the distinction between infrastructure and organism is only a question of vocabulary.
