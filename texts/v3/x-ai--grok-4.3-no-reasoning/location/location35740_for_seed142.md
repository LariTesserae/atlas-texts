In the upper air, a vast plateau of fused mineral rises like the back of some long-submerged skull. The surface is not stone in any ordinary sense; it is a single, horizon-spanning slab of devitrified glass, its original volcanic flows annealed by eons of ultraviolet bathing until the once-molten rock cooled into a translucent, purple-tinged feldspar lattice. Between this skylight plain and the absolute black of the sealed sky above it, there is no water at all—only a transparent, nitrogen-rich medium whose pressure is just enough to transmit the faint warmth that lingers from deep geothermal reservoirs beneath the plateau.

Scattered across this vitreous table are the remnants of an industrial city that was never meant to be reached again. The buildings are cast in the same mineral glass, extruded rather than assembled, their forms flattened by age into low, polygonal domes whose once-sharp angles have slumped into gentle undulations. Inside them, networks of crystalline conduits still hold traces of the heavy oils that once drove pistons; the oils have polymerized into inert amber threads, frozen mid-circulation. Between the domes, wide, empty boulevards are etched with long, parallel scratches—legacy of the final, unmaintained freight sleds whose tungsten skids wore grooves that the slow regrowth of glass has only half erased.

Light is absent from above; the darkness is so complete that the glass itself has become faintly luminous, releasing a subdued violet glow wherever stress fractures catch the planet’s own faint geothermal radiation. The glow is too weak to read by, but it is enough to silhouette one rare form of life: slow-moving crystalline motes no larger than sand grains that migrate in patient caravans along the grooves, feeding on residual piezo-electric charge generated when minute temperature fluctuations flex the plateau overnight. They leave behind microscopic tracks that look, from a human distance, like static on an old screen.

Because the environment has reached chemical and mechanical equilibrium, nothing here accelerates or decelerates; even the motes crawl at a rate that is constant across centuries. Sound is limited to the occasional, almost subsonic creak of contracting lattices and the soft, continuous hiss of the plateau breathing out its stored warmth through hairline vents near the city’s perimeter. These exhalations carry microscopic flakes of the same glass, which settle again within meters, ensuring the surface never grows thinner, only smoother.

Farther out, where the city gives way to open plain, the glass is interrupted by low rings—collapsed factory shells—whose interiors now hold perfect discs of black, perfectly still shadow. If one were to lower a sensor into any of these shadows, the temperature would register a precise half-degree above ambient, a difference that has not varied since the last piston stopped. Nothing consumes that warmth; nothing adds to it. The city and the plateau have become one self-measuring system: heat, pressure, and surface tension locked into an unchanging ratio that will persist until the planet’s core cools—an eventuality that, on the scale of this place, might as well be never.
