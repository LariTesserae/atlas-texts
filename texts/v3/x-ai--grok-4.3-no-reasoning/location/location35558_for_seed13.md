Beneath a sky that holds its light without drift or temper, the land sits as if it has already finished arriving. The light is neither morning nor afternoon; it is the day’s measure, constant, and it touches every edge as evenly as breath touches stone. Across a ridge two thousand feet above the invisible sea, a city opens and closes on itself like a slow hinge.

The city is not compact. Wide terraces of unfinished stone—pale basalt and basalt-green slate—step back from one another in distances measured not by streets but by pauses. Between each layer, broad stretches of open ground lie empty except for the faint patterning of drizzle that comes and goes without schedule. The water never pools; it sheens and departs, leaving the stone slightly darker for a few heartbeats, then fading again to the same cool dryness. Only in the lower clefts, where the city’s foundations are split by narrow natural gutters, does moisture gather long enough to thread a runnel that whispers once, then silences.

Vegetation threads between the terraces like deliberate embroidery. High-canopy evergreens, each a solitary column, stand twenty or thirty paces apart. Their crowns brush but do not mingle; beneath them the ground is bare stone punctuated by low cushions of moss that hold, for the length of a stride, the memory of the last brief rain. Between the cushions grow clusters of slim, jointed reeds whose hollow stems, when the wind threads them, produce no sound. The space between each plant is deliberate, almost architectural; the city’s builders have left the vegetation the same measure of emptiness they leave between dwellings.

The dwellings themselves are single-storey, thick-walled, roofed with overlapping slate that has not yet been darkened by time. Doors and windows are cut square, unadorned, their thresholds raised a hand’s breadth above the terrace so that the occasional film of water can sheet away without entering. Inside each structure the light remains exactly the same as outside; there are no lamps, only the continuous outdoor clarity admitted through wide openings that face the open ground. The interiors are empty of furniture; niches cut into the walls hold folded lengths of woven reed, nothing more. The city’s population moves slowly, singly, or not at all. When a figure crosses a terrace it does so at the same measured pace the light maintains, leaving no footprint that outlasts a quarter of a circuit.

Above the highest terrace the ridge continues upward in a series of sharp, unweathered steps that look newly broken. No talus skirts their bases; any stone that falls is removed—by hand, by the same steady figures who carry slate from the quarry a valley away. The quarry itself is visible only as a clean vertical plane reflecting the constant light, its edges still angular, its floor damp from condensation that forms on the fresh fracture surfaces and then evaporates without running. From the quarry a single, narrow track climbs the ridge and meets the uppermost terrace; the track’s surface is compacted earth, not yet grooved by wheels.

Fauna is present but discreet. Small, six-legged browsers the size of open hands move across the open ground in ones and twos, their carapaces the color of the stone so that they appear only when they pause to crop the moss. They produce no audible step. Larger shapes—long-bodied gliders whose wings are translucent membranes stretched over reed-like bones—launch from the highest rooftops and ride the motionless air for long intervals before alighting on another roof two terraces down. Their landings are silent; the only sound they make is the soft, dry rustle of membrane folding.

No gradient governs the temperature. The air remains the same cool, stable medium whether one stands on the uppermost terrace or in the lowest cleft. Breath does not steam; skin does not chill. The city and its surrounding terraces exist at the temperature at which stone holds light without warming and water evaporates without residue. Because nothing changes, nothing accumulates. The moss does not thicken, the slate does not darken, the reeds neither grow nor wither. The only motion is the measured passage of the city’s inhabitants along their chosen paths, each figure spaced so that the distance between any two is always the same interval of open ground.

In this equilibrium the city continues, not as a process but as a condition: terraces, stone, solitary plants, silent animals, constant light, and the faint, intermittent sheen of moisture that appears and departs without trace.
