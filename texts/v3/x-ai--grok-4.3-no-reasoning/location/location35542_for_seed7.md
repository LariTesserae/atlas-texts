The chamber is a subterranean hollow the size of a modest cathedral, its ceiling scarcely thirty feet overhead, its floor an uneven disc of basalt perhaps two hundred paces across. The rock itself has the matte black of freshly congealed lava, its surface still so sharply jointed that one could shave a finger on the fracture planes. Yet these edges are already wearing: each shard shows the polish of slow condensation trickling down from the ceiling in perfect, separate beads. The ceiling is not bare stone; it is a dense weave of root-like cables—dense as rope but living—clinging to fractures with sucker-like discs. These roots sweat continuously. Every few minutes a heavier drop detaches, falls, and splashes against a pad of gray-green moss that rings each impact site like a tiny crater of living felt.  

Between the mosses, the floor is bare; no animal tracks, no burrows, only the patina left by the same slow drip. Here and there the cables descend farther, knotting themselves into slack lattices that dangle to within arm’s reach. The latticework carries a faint red luminosity—internal, photosynthetic yet subterranean—so that a traveler can navigate by the soft roseate glow without need of torch or lantern.  

Scattered among the roots are industrial relics, all small and hand-carried. A single riveted copper drum, half-buried in congealed mineral paste, still ticks: an antique pressure gauge whose needle creeps, a millimeter every hour, around a dial etched in some forgotten unit. Opposite the drum, a belt-driven ventilator no larger than a suitcase sits motionless, its vanes gummed by the same black lichen that grows wherever airflow stagnates. Occasional brass nameplates, each the size of a playing card, have been riveted directly into the basalt; the script is ornate, unreadable, and already half-erased by the trickling water.  

The air itself is warm—almost fever-warm—yet perfectly still. Humidity hangs in visible veils where the rose light intersects cooler rock. When a root bead finally falls, the splash is the loudest sound in the chamber: a brief, liquid chime that echoes three times and is gone. Otherwise only a minute, almost sub-audible fizz issues from micro-bubbles rising through thin films of water on the floor, a sound felt more in the teeth than heard.  

Nothing moves quickly. The moss pads expand outward perhaps one cell per day. The copper drum’s gauge needle will complete another full revolution sometime next year. The ventilator waits for an airflow that has not been felt since the last century. Even the temperature gradient from floor to ceiling is measured in fractions of a degree, the hotter strata lying a patient hand-span above the cooler pool of air pooled near the moss. In this slow world, the only abrupt change occurred long ago: when the lava cooled and the first root found the first fracture, and the slow work of living condensation began.
