The highlands rise in long, slow folds of black basalt, each ridge capped by a cloak of living matter that has been growing, dying, and regrowing for so many seasons the stone beneath is invisible. The vegetation is not simply “green”; it is a continuous, skin-tight organism that has learned to obey both gravity and wind. Fern-branches thicker than limbs roll down the slopes like fabric; palm-sized plates of moss trap the next layer of moss, which in turn traps the next, so that a traveler walking here walks across a stack of translucent carpets that yield, compress, and spring back, releasing a brief perfume of crushed chlorophyll. Where the slope steepens, the mass thickens into bulges and cantilevers; some of these vaults span from one ridge to the next, leaving dark, drizzling galleries beneath. In the largest of them, daylight falls through rents in the living ceiling in single, dusty shafts that illuminate drifts of drifting pollen as fine as smoke.

Moisture arrives twice each day. Just before dawn the air cools enough for dew to condense directly onto the upper fronds; by sunrise the film has already begun to slide downward in slow, branching rivulets that follow paths etched by generations of droplets. In the late afternoon, thin clouds push over the ridge-line and empty themselves in sudden, local downpours that last only minutes. The water never pools for long; instead it is immediately absorbed into spongy reservoirs inside the plant-matrix and later metered out through capillary networks, so the whole landscape is perpetually damp without ever becoming swamp. Where a rivulet finally reaches the edge of a leaf-shelf, it drips into the open air, striking the next lower roof hundreds of body-lengths below with a sound too faint to carry.

The cold is steady. Even at noon, exhaled breath hangs in front of the face for three heartbeats before the wind takes it. Because the vegetation is so dense and so thermally massive, it stores the night chill; every stem and root radiates the same even temperature, so there are no pockets of warmer air. The only warmth comes from the infrequent animals: six-legged, downy things no larger than a clenched fist that live inside hollow petioles. They emerge at twilight, move in straight lines from one feeding gallery to another, and vanish again. Their wings beat in perfect silence; only the faint displacement of pollen betrays their passage.

Farther up-valley the plant-matrix changes character. Here the stems have fused into vertical spars as thick as bridge pilings; they rise thirty or forty body-lengths before branching again into horizontal diaphragms. Between these spars hang thin sheets of translucent tissue—part leaf, part structural tendon—that vibrate at the edge of hearing whenever the wind slides across them. At intervals the sheets split and re-seal, forming temporary valves that admit or exclude drifting mist. The structure is not engineered; it is the accumulated compromise of countless growth seasons, each new layer answering the mechanical questions posed by the layer beneath.

Lightning occasionally threads between the spars, not because storms gather overhead, but because the constant slow release of water vapor and the continuous motion of charged pollen create micro-fields that discharge in thread-thin arcs. The bolts leave no scorch; the living matter reroutes the current along wet channels and uses the brief heat to trigger synchronized seed release. After each strike, the air fills for a few seconds with drifting capsules that glow like embers before they extinguish.

Nothing built by hand exists. No path has been cut; every route is a temporary deformation in the top layer of stems, already beginning to heal behind the passer. The only persistent sign of passage is a faint change in the local spectrum of reflected light, because the upper surfaces that have been compressed for a moment grow back a shade more blue-green than their undisturbed neighbors. Over decades these altered patches braid together into flowing maps legible only from above, and even then only when the sun is low and the angle turns every small difference into a long, slow shadow.

In the open intervals between ridges the vegetation steps down in wide terraces, each level twenty or thirty paces across. At the outer lip of every terrace the living mass overhangs by several body-lengths, forming a continuous dripping cornice. Water that falls from these edges travels a hundred paces before it strikes the next terrace; on windy days the droplets are caught and carried sideways, so the entire valley is cross-hatched with nearly horizontal threads of falling water that catch the light like harp strings.

The only sounds are the soft, internal creaks of stems lengthening, the liquid percussion of droplets moving through capillary galleries, and the almost inaudible sigh when a seed-case splits. Even the wind is muted; it cannot reach the open air directly but must first pass through successive filters of leaf and membrane, so what arrives at ground level is only the memory of movement, a pressure that rises and falls without ever becoming audible.
