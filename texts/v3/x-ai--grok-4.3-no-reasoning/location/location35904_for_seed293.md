The place cannot be called a forest because it is too small and too finished. It is also too wet to be called a ruin, too old to be called a city. It has settled at the edge of what the word “settlement” still means.

It is a basin that once held a shallow lake and now holds only the memory of that lake in the form of plants that are 95 % water by mass. They do not drink the ground; they are the ground, in the same way that a coral reef becomes the thing it grows upon. Roots and fronds, pressed together by centuries, have produced a continuous mat twelve to twenty meters thick. The upper surface is walkable; the lower surface still touches the original lake bed. Between them is a green matrix of living xylem so saturated that a dropped coin would take minutes to sink through it.

Across this mat stand the stumps of towers. They are not stone. They are xylem that has turned to heartwood and then continued to lignify long after the living cells died. Each stump is a cylinder two or three meters across, its cut surface worn by wind-blown grit into a shallow bowl that now catches rain. Inside each bowl a single broadleaf plant has rooted, sending a white filament down the entire height of the cylinder. At dusk the filament glows with the faint blue of bioluminescent sap. From above, the stumps look like a scatter of pale coins on a dark table.

Water moves without falling. After rain, the mat exhales a fine, continuous mist from millions of microscopic stomata. The mist drifts horizontally because the air above the mat is warmer than the mat itself. The vapor forms slow, knee-high rivers that travel between the stumps and eventually spill over the basin rim. The sound of this spilling is not like a waterfall; it is the sound of silk being torn, continuous, never loud enough to drown conversation yet never absent. Birds have evolved to hunt by listening for the change in pitch when prey disturbs the silk-tear.

Inside the mat, between the living strata, are tunnels left by earlier generations of xylem that later dissolved. The tunnels are now occupied by a single species of salamander whose skin is photosynthetic. During the brightest hours they lie against the upper membrane of each tunnel; at night they descend to the cooler, darker layer. Their bodies form moving constellations of green light visible from below when a person kneels and presses an ear to the mat. The vibration of footsteps is transmitted so efficiently through the water-filled matrix that the salamanders can feel a walker approach long before the walker is overhead.

The city that once stood here is remembered only in the alignment of the stumps. They form radial avenues exactly wide enough for two people to pass without touching. Between the avenues the mat has grown slightly higher, so the whole surface is a shallow, living topography of low ridges and shallow troughs. The ridges are not planned; they are the result of repeated human traffic compacting the xylem over centuries until the living tissue learned to grow away from the pressure. The effect is a city whose streets are raised and whose buildings have been replaced by the living pavement itself.

No machine larger than a hand can operate here for long. Nanoscale lattices woven into the xylem detect metallic ions and secrete oxalic acid that corrodes them. The lattices themselves are not circuitry; they are structural reinforcements that happen, as a side effect, to resonate with the background radio noise of the planet’s magnetic field. The resonance produces a faint, fluctuating tone—audible only if one lies flat and presses both palms to the mat—that the salamanders use for orientation when the mist is too thick for vision.

The basin is small enough that a fit person can walk from rim to rim in twenty minutes. Yet within it the humidity, temperature, and light gradient change so rapidly that three distinct ecological bands coexist. At the center, where the mat is thickest, the temperature is steady at 29 °C and the air is almost opaque with vapor. Toward the rim the mat thins; the temperature drops two degrees and the vapor becomes visible as slow, horizontal sheets drifting outward. The outermost band is only a few meters wide: a zone where the xylem mat has not yet sealed over the underlying clay. Here the surface is slick with condensation and the sound of the silk-tear is loudest, because the vapor escapes through a narrow gap between mat and clay.

Nothing decays quickly. A fallen leaf is colonized within hours by symbiotic fungi that convert it into new xylem rather than rot. The process is silent except for a barely audible click each time a microscopic crystal of cellulose is deposited. At dusk the cumulative clicking becomes a dry rain that lasts until the bioluminescent filaments brighten and the salamanders descend.

The place is finished. It has reached the oldest-stable-state possible for a warm, wet, low basin that once held a lake and later held a city. The only remaining instability is the slow, outward creep of the mat itself—millimeters per decade—as it colonizes the clay at the rim. In another few centuries the basin will be gone, replaced by a larger, flatter version of itself, and the silk-tear sound will have nothing left to fall over.
