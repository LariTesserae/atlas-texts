High on the plateau, the rock has only recently risen through itself. It is not old stone laid bare by time, but new mineral still expressing the moment of its arrival. The surface feels warm beneath a hand, not with the warmth of sun, but with a slow, internal glow that never quite reaches the visible spectrum. Where the stone is broken, it weeps; not water, exactly, but a condensate that beads like dew and evaporates almost as soon as it forms, leaving behind a faint crystalline residue that flashes once, like an after-image, before disappearing.

Between the outcroppings, the vegetation clings in tight, vivid clusters: fans of translucent leaves that ripple without wind, their edges carrying a faint electrical halo. Roots do not drill downward so much as they braid across the rock in raised, coral-like lattices, drawing whatever condenses from the air. In places the plants have pulled small stones together into low, hollow rings no wider than a person’s arms; inside each ring the condensate gathers more thickly, pooling in tiny, temporary basins that vanish by the time the eye returns to them.

The structures are not built so much as they are remembered. Scattered at intervals are low domes of the same living rock, their surfaces traced with hair-fine channels that pulse with a rhythm too slow to call a heartbeat and too regular to be random. Some domes are whole; others have cracked along these channels and fallen into precise, geometric fragments that continue to glow softly at the broken edges. Nothing suggests habitation, yet the placement of the domes follows an invisible geometry—each one set so that its sight-lines just miss the next, as if the builders had designed for an observer who moves between them without ever standing in two at once.

Above, the air is thick with suspended droplets that never quite become rain. They drift in slow, spiraling descent, refracting the ordinary daylight into momentary prisms that leave no after-color. Occasionally a droplet will touch a plant or a dome and vanish with a sound like a single plucked string, too soft to carry far. The plateau itself emits a low, continuous sub-sonic tone that rises only when the wind is absent, as though the stone were answering some question asked in a register no ear can hold.

Fauna is almost absent, but not entirely. At long, irregular intervals a single, translucent creature—neither insect nor vapor—will condense from the drifting droplets, hover for a few seconds above a plant ring, then dissipate again. Its passage leaves the plants momentarily brighter and the domes briefly darker, as though some small charge had been exchanged. When it vanishes, the plateau returns to its previous quiet, the only record of the encounter being a slightly deeper pool of condensate in that particular ring, already beginning to evaporate.

The scale of the place is intimate rather than panoramic: one could walk its entire extent in a quarter-hour, yet within those steps the geometry folds so that returning to a starting point requires deliberate attention to the angle of approach. The air smells faintly of ozone and warm stone; the light is ordinary daylight filtered through an atmosphere that is, at this elevation, still adjusting to the fact of its own newness. Nothing here is settled. Every surface is either arriving or remembering how to arrive, and the difference between the two is visible only in the way the condensate lingers a fraction longer on one than on the other.
