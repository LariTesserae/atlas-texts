The place is a basin the size of a moderate country, holding its shape exactly as it has for centuries—centuries measured in sediment layers, not clocks. It sits low between two long, blunt ridges of rust-coloured mineral that have worn down into blunt crests, their slopes streaked by slow trickles of condensate. No green anywhere; the surface is a mosaic of glassy sinter and pale siliceous crust, each sheet micro-laminated so that the terrain seems both new and impossibly ancient at once. The ground gives slightly under weight, a warm, chalky crust over still-warm hydrothermal pipes; the heat is steady enough that water flashes into vapour in some vents while a hundred metres away the same vapour re-condenses into milky beads that roll down the gentle gradient and collect in shallow, persistent pools. These pools never freeze and never boil away. They maintain a thin, skin-like meniscus that shivers when the air column above them shifts, but nothing disrupts the level itself.

Every hour or so a deep, single-tone thrum moves through the crust, originating from an unseen rotary apparatus deep beneath the sinter plain. The tone travels as a pressure wave; the crust amplifies it into a sustained, chest-filling drone that lasts a minute and then fades. In the interval between pulses the only audible motion is the slow pop of vapour bubbles rising through the pools and the muted hiss of renewed condensation trickling from the ridge faces. Fauna registers only as a momentary warping of that condensation film: a momentary tunnel of displaced droplets where something translucent and fast passes overhead, never twice in the same line. It leaves no track, no residue, and the film heals within seconds.

Nothing is growing; nothing is decaying. The minerals have already finished their reactions; they now cycle heat and dissolved silica without net loss or gain. The industrial element is the pulses themselves: an unseen engine whose rotation is locked to the local geothermal gradient, running without variation, exporting only pressure and a faint, iron-smelling vapour that rides each acoustic wave outward until it meets the ridgeline and disperses. The vapour does not seed new deposits; it re-condenses in the same pattern each cycle, falling back into the same shallow catchments. Scale is regional—the eye can follow the ridges until they blend into the same pale haze that fills the basin on every horizon—but nothing beyond that rim ever intrudes.

The atmosphere stays warm and still; no wind rearranges the surface film or shifts the pool levels. The light that reaches the basin floor has already been filtered through a permanent layer of suspended mineral dust at roughly three hundred metres altitude, turning noon and midnight into the same soft grey. The crust and the water beneath it remain in precise balance with the pressure waves, neither accumulating nor eroding, only vibrating in place. The landscape is therefore a single, self-sustaining chord: heat, mineral, sound, and the absence of change, all sustained at the same amplitude day after day.
