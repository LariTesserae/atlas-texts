What remains is a bowl-shaped depression perhaps six hundred paces across, where the dark, compacted mineral of the ground has taken on a faint sheen from the pressure of ages. No rain has ever reached the floor, no vapor has ever risen to form clouds; the only water here is locked inside crystal lattices, brittle sheets of gypsum and salt laid down when the stone itself was younger. The surface feels cool to the touch, the cold of its long absence of moisture, yet the air that presses against it is warm and utterly dry, the temperature held constant by whatever sealed this place away from wind or sky.  

A few, very few, stalks of vegetation stand upright: thin, jointed wands of dusty blue-gray that never flower and never seem to die. They grow where the mineral has cracked just enough to hold a handful of grains of dust, roots no thicker than threads clutching between crystals. Between them lies open space—meters of naked surface—enough that each plant feels alone even though the eye can count them all from a single standpoint. Nothing moves among them; no insects crawl, no burrows pock the crust.  

The silence is complete, save for the minute, high crystalline ringing when a stalk is brushed. Sound dies almost instantly; there is no air to carry it farther than an arm’s reach. Everything here exists in the same moment it has occupied for uncountable seasons: the mineral dust neither settles nor lifts, the stalks neither grow nor wither, the faint light—wherever it comes from—neither brightens nor fades. There is no edge to step across, no higher ground to climb; the bowl simply stops at the point where its walls curve upward into unbroken darkness. Within that darkness, the same temperature, the same dryness, the same mute equilibrium continues. The place holds itself together by never allowing a difference to appear.
