The world is a single, endless fluid. There is no surface here, only pressure gradients within the fluid itself; horizons dissolve upward and downward into the same green-black translucency. Light arrives as a soft, omnipresent field of photons: neither sun nor star penetrates, yet enough energy diffuses from countless microscopic nuclear pockets within the plant-matrix to sustain metabolism at every depth.

The vegetation is the substrate. Filamentous trunks the diameter of continents braid together into self-supporting rafts that drift at fixed altitudes within the fluid. Their leaves—each tens of kilometers across—form a continuous lattice of photosynthetic membranes. In the spaces between leaves, secondary vascular bundles extend like suspension cables, secreting buoyant silica microspheres that keep the entire superstructure neutrally buoyant at chosen depths. The plant matrix has no “roots,” only fractal holdfasts that clutch at one another; everything the ecosystem needs is harvested from dissolved minerals drifting through the fluid.

Between the immense leaves, volumes of open water hundreds of kilometers across are kept clear by the respiration currents of the mat itself. Within those clearings, megafauna graze. Filter-feeding leviathans the size of mountains move in slow, stately circuits, their internal bioluminescent symbionts pulsing in patterns synchronized to the plant lattice’s circadian rhythm. Smaller, schooling predators—each individual larger than any terrestrial whale—follow the grazer currents like living weather fronts. Sound travels far in this incompressible medium; the low-frequency groans of the leviathans, the click-train navigation of the predators, and the ultrasonic pruning-songs of specialized cleaning crustaceans form a permanent, layered roar that never changes.

The temperature is held uniform by the thermal mass of the living matrix: heat generated by constant metabolic work is immediately radiated into the fluid, and the fluid returns the same temperature day and night. No seasonal or diurnal gradient exists; the system has settled into an equilibrium where entropy production equals entropy export at every scale.

Industrial traces appear as living components of the mat: specialized vascular tissues have evolved into kilometer-wide osmotic turbines that extract dissolved ions and concentrate them into pellets the fauna later ingest. The energy harvested turns entire leaf-regions into slow-moving electrostatic fields that sort plankton by charge, concentrating edible particles for the next grazing cycle. The turbines produce no waste heat beyond the baseline temperature; every joule is either stored chemically or radiated as modulated bioluminescence that doubles as communication across the clearings.

Nothing is built; everything is grown. The densest sections of the mat can bear the weight of the largest leviathans without deformation, yet the lattice remains open enough that a drifting observer could travel horizontally for hundreds of kilometers before encountering another leaf surface. The only visible motion is the ceaseless respiration of the mat and the measured passage of the grazing herds—slow, relentless, and never changing.
