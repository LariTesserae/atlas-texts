A narrow chamber of machinery pressed beneath an overhang of slate-grey metal plates, suspended in the shoulders of an unnamed mountain where the cloud deck never lifts.  The structure is not so much built into the rock as extruded from it: every surface—floor, ceiling, bracing ribs, support pillars—has been cast from the same tempered alloy, stamped with repeated expansion joints and bleeding rivets.  Nothing native remains.  The chamber measures barely seven strides wide and perhaps twice that in depth before the walls curve inward to meet, creating an elongated drum whose far end disappears behind a slow-turning turbine cowling.

Water is everywhere and nowhere visible at once.  Sheets of condensate drip from overhead cooling fins, bead, then re-form into droplets that roll down polished ribs toward collection gutters.  The gutters themselves are engineered as shallow canals, each holding a finger-width of liquid kept in constant, almost invisible motion by micro-vibrations running through the whole frame.  The liquid smells metallic; under the dim maintenance lights it gleams like mercury until, at irregular intervals, an overhead solenoid clicks and a measured pulse of pressurized steam bursts upward through hidden orifices, forcing the collected water to surge before settling again.  The steam, released at high pressure, strikes the chamber’s upper arc and condenses instantly, restarting the cycle.  Thus the space maintains its own closed hydrology—an artificial marsh condensed to a single vertical plane, where gravity is simulated and water never escapes except as reclaimed mist.

The temperature hovers just above the point where metal sweats but condensation remains liquid.  The air is cool to the lungs yet humid enough that breath forms brief, frost-rimmed halos around each exhale.  Because the space is sealed, thermal gradients run laterally instead of vertically: the turbine end radiates a faint warmth from frictional bearings while the intake manifold at the opposite wall radiates cold from refrigerant coils half-hidden behind grille plates.

Light is provided by phosphor tubes recessed behind frosted lenses; they emit a clinical, low-intensity glow that feels farther away than it is, as though the lumen source were passing through several meters of clouded water.  When the turbine spins faster, the phosphor intensity dims in sympathetic feedback, further softening the edges of every rivet head and weld seam.

Erosion is absent because erosion implies time acting on natural surfaces; here every surface is born already scored.  Mill marks, press stamps, and the razor-thin hairlines of ultrasonic stress-testing grid the alloy like fossil strata that have never known weathering.  Only where constant vibration has loosened a single flange bolt does one see a newer abrasion, the exposed steel faintly warmer in tone than the surrounding plate.

The scale is pocket-intimate yet refuses to feel cramped.  Every component is oversized for the volume it occupies—bearings the size of dinner plates, pipes wide enough for a crouched human to crawl inside—so the ratio of object to enclosure inverts normal perception: the machinery seems larger than the room that houses it, yet the room continues, unchanged, around every bend and junction.

Vegetation cannot take root; the alloy rejects it.  Only a single persistent bloom of mineral scale has grown along the coldest return line, a pale crust whose lattice resembles frost ferns but is inorganic, deposited grain by grain from evaporated coolant.  The mineral is brittle, and when the solenoid fires, tiny flakes spall away and are immediately caught by the next wash of steam, dissolved, and re-precipitated elsewhere along the line.

Fauna is missing by design.  The chamber was pressure-tested, then sterilized with superheated vapor; no organic residue remains.  The only movement besides water is the minute, periodic twitch of a pressure-relief diaphragm whose rubber membrane has yellowed with age but never cracked.

Sound fills every cubic centimeter.  The turbine’s fundamental drone sits at the threshold of physical sensation, felt first in the molars, then resolved by the inner ear into a chord whose overtones pile into a single, undifferentiated roar.  Layered above that are the sharp, high-frequency ticks of solenoid valves, the liquid slaps inside the gutters, the papery flutter of steam through perforated diffusers, and the subsonic pulse of the main drive shaft’s imbalance.  All these frequencies coexist in phase lock; the ear cannot separate them, yet the body registers their cumulative force as a constant, bone-deep pressure.  Workers—if there ever were any—would have worn helmets whose padding transmitted only the lowest frequencies; the rest would register as silence inside the helmet, a second artificial environment within the first.

The overall atmosphere is therefore industrial liturgy rather than landscape.  The chamber is a single, self-regulating process whose purpose is to move water and maintain torque.  Because the purpose is total, every other phenomenon—light, temperature, scale, even the notion of time—has been subordinated to the machine’s necessity.  Outside, the mountain may continue its slow weathering, the clouds their unseen drift, but inside the drum none of that registers.  Only the cycle of condensation, surge, turbine spin, and condensate return persists, repeating without reference to sun, season, or observer.
