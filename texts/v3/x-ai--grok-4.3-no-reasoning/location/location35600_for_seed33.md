The Onyx Spine is a city that has never seen rain and never will.  
It occupies an immeasurable slab of obsidian tilted at thirty degrees above the absolute baseline of the world. The slab is five hundred kilometers in span, yet its width is only a few body-lengths of space—vertical becomes horizontal in the mind the moment one arrives, because gravity still follows the angle of the stone, not its declared “plane.” The city is therefore built on a perpetual incline, a single street that never bends back on itself but threads upward in a slow, widening helix until the uppermost dwellings hang like gargoyles above nothing.

Stone predominates; no solvent, no leaf, no drop of anything liquid has ever touched its surfaces. The buildings are extruded straight from the obsidian itself by some forgotten pressure that once flowed and then froze. Their edges remain knife-sharp where they have never rubbed against wind; elsewhere, centuries of slow settling have softened corners into dull facets that catch the faint light and return it as matte charcoal. Streets are merely negative space left between these extrusions, so narrow in places that a single person fills them shoulder to shoulder, yet the emptiness on either side of the city is so vast that even a shout dissipates before the echo forms.

Temperature lingers at the edge of frost but never crosses it. Breath does not condense; the air is already so cold and thin that moisture would have to be manufactured before it could freeze. The only thermal exchange is between the dark stone and the dim sky: the slab absorbs almost nothing and releases almost nothing, keeping the city in a constant, shadowless chill. When movement occurs—when a structure settles an imperceptible fraction of a millimeter—the released micro-vibration travels as a single infrasonic pulse felt in the soles rather than heard.

Nothing grows here. No lichen, no spore. Yet the stone itself has memory: wherever a surface has been walked upon for a millennium, it now bears a paler cast, a microscopic roughening that distinguishes path from wall, bench from floor. These ghost-trails function as the only map. A citizen learns, after a lifetime, that if the pale trace begins to spiral inward too sharply, one has entered a district whose buildings will soon collapse into the void beneath; the trace fades there because no feet return.

Light arrives from a single direction at all times: a low red ember fixed on the horizon-line that is not a sun but the exposed core of some deeper fracture in space. It provides neither warmth nor shadow; objects appear equally dark on every face. The ember also produces no ultraviolet, so there is no color beyond the achromatic range the eye can invent from texture alone.

Movement is continuous but almost imperceptible. The entire slab is still settling from an ancient tilt-event. Every century it drops another hair’s breadth. When this occurs, the city exhales a single, continent-long groan transmitted through the stone; windows that are merely gaps in the rock momentarily narrow, then ease open again. Citizens pause, wait for the vibration to pass up through their ankles and out the crown of the head, then resume motion. No one remarks upon it. The groan is older than language.

Fauna are rare because they must be small enough to survive the tilt without falling and hardy enough to extract minerals directly. Crystal-thread mites—translucent, six-limbed—travel along the pale ghost-trails, gnawing the roughened stone into nutrient dust. Their discarded casings, no larger than a grain of salt, accumulate in corners until a stray thermal current lifts them; the resulting dust devil is the only visible weather. It spirals once, twice, then collapses, leaving the mites to begin their slow procession again.

Sound exists only as an interior phenomenon. Citizens carry small tuning-forks forged from the same obsidian. Struck once, the fork produces a tone felt in the teeth rather than the ears; the vibration is used to test whether a new settlement-crack has opened beneath one’s feet. After the tone is spent, silence returns so absolute that the memory of the fork’s note becomes the loudest thing present.

No technology mediates any of this. There are no wheels, no lamps, no timepieces. The sole engineered artifact is the city itself, whose continuing existence is the only clock: each slow millimeter of descent is both calendar and obituary. When a block finally shears away and falls into the dark below the slab, the space it leaves is immediately occupied by the families who have been waiting generations for that precise vacancy. The new floor still carries the sheen of untouched obsidian; within a few centuries it will acquire its own pale ghost-trail, and the cycle of quiet occupation continues.

Above the highest dwellings the slope ends—not in a summit, but in an abrupt lip where the obsidian simply ceases. Beyond that lip there is no down, only a depthless absence that even the red ember refuses to illuminate. Standing at the lip, one can extend an arm outward and feel nothing at all—not wind, not pressure, not temperature gradient—only the certainty that if the arm continues forward it will never return. The citizens therefore build no railings. The absence itself is the only boundary they require.

In this landscape, every structure is both ruin and foundation; every footstep writes a map that will be walked again; every silence is the same silence that was here before the slab rose and will remain after the slab has finished its descent into whatever lies beneath the world.
