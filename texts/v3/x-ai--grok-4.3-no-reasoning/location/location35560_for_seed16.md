The basin sits within a single, waist-high stone ring no wider than two arm-spans, its low rim carved once from a single vein of rose feldspar and polished by centuries of fingertips. From outside the ring the space is nothing: flat salt pan stretching to a horizon of heat haze. Step over the stone and the atmosphere folds downward, enclosing everything within a shallow bowl of still, superheated air. The “sky” is the inside face of the ring itself, a pale, dust-rose ceiling only an arm’s length overhead; the ground beneath is a living mat of ochre lithops and dwarf barrel succulents no taller than a thumbnail, their swollen leaves sweating aromatic resins that shimmer in the dim, orange light. The light itself seeps upward through narrow vents between the polished blocks of the rim, angled so the glare never reaches the leaves directly yet still warms them to a steady 47 °C.

Inside this walled garden, every surface is inhabited by the plants. They root in pockets of wind-sifted mineral dust no deeper than a fingernail, yet they draw moisture from the air by nightly condensation on the feldspar, which beads, runs, and is captured by capillary spines. The air is saturated with a low, thrumming vibration: a chorus of expanding and contracting seed pods that pop at intervals like muffled kettle drums. The sound is constant and without echo, absorbed by the living carpet so that a listener feels it more in the teeth than the ears.

Because nothing moves but the plants themselves, the space feels crowded with stillness. Every few hours the largest succulents inflate bladders along their spines until internal pressure cracks them; the tiny fissure exhales a puff of steam that momentarily fogs the dim light, then collapses again, resealing. Each cycle contributes another layer of salt crystals to the stones, reinforcing the same architecture rather than eroding it. The “city” is this equilibrium: the ring is both the only structure and the entire settlement; the plants are both the only inhabitants and the infrastructure, maintaining temperature, humidity, and sound at the precise degree required for their own survival. Nothing arrives, nothing leaves; nothing hunts, nothing dies. The miniature desert exists in a permanent, resonant noon.
