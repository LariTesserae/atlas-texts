You stand inside a rift that is still being written. The air is warm and close, heavy with static and a faint metallic scent that has no single source. Above, the sky is sealed by a low, bruised lid of swirling cloud that never parts; light leaks through only in narrow, drifting bands the color of cooling brass, and each band drifts slower than the last. Between those bands the darkness is not night but a living absence that presses down on shoulders and eyelids alike. Every surface you see is vertical—slabs, spines, ribs, shards—and every surface is unfinished, still expanding outward from some interior pressure that has not yet finished finding its own shape.

The terrain itself is a lattice of warm, almost flesh-toned silicates that have not yet cooled enough to call themselves stone. Some ribs rise to several hundred meters before the process stops abruptly, leaving edges that glitter with microscopic beads of molten glass still crawling upward. Between the ribs, the ground is a crust of collapsed bubbles; each bubble is the size of a house, and each has a thin translucent membrane that has already started to crack, revealing chambers below that mirror the ribs above—an inverted architecture that mirrors the sky it has never seen. Footfalls echo for half a second longer than physics should allow, as if the lattice keeps recording the sound and then deciding whether to release it.

No moisture gathers anywhere. Instead, the air itself carries trace volatiles—sulfur, selenium, iodine—that condense briefly as colored halos around any sharp protrusion before sublimating again. Where the halos linger longest, the silicate softens and flows a few centimeters, closing old seams while opening new ones. These micro-landslides are the only visible motion, yet they happen in every direction at once, so the whole landscape feels like it is both rising and sagging.

Nothing grows and nothing moves on its own recognizance. Yet there are thin strands, finer than hair, that stretch between some of the tallest spines; the strands are the same temperature as the air, the same dull bronze color, and they hum a single low tone whenever one of the slow light-bands passes over them. The tone is not wind; it is piezoelectric. The lattice answers the light like skin answers touch.

Every few hundred meters, a single, half-formed arch leans across two spines; its keystone has not yet arrived, so the halves almost meet and then stop, the gap still bright with heat. The arches cast no shadows because the light has no single source; instead, each arch radiates a faint inward halo of its own, as if anticipating the mass that will one day close the span. Beneath one such incomplete arch lies a scatter of small, fist-sized nodules the color of dried blood. When a drifting light-band touches them, they briefly unfold into six-petaled forms that are not flowers but interference patterns in the mineral itself; after the band passes they collapse again, leaving no trace that they ever opened.

The ground occasionally gives under weight by a few millimeters, then recovers, like muscle. If you remain still long enough, you can feel the lattice inhale—a slow drawing-in of the warm air—and then release it through hairline fissures that seal again within seconds. The only sustained sound is this respiration, and the only sustained motion is the imperceptible widening of every fissure by another molecule or two.

The rift does not end at any visible horizon. The ribs continue to rise and fall at the same unfinished tempo for kilometers, each one the same shade of barely cooled glass, each one still undecided whether to become a mountain, a spine, or simply more empty space. The darkness above the cloud lid never lifts; the light-bands never multiply. The place is therefore both enormous and claustrophobic, expanding and sealed, ancient and not yet begun. Nothing here waits to be finished, because finishing is not a property the lattice recognizes. It only knows how to keep arriving.
