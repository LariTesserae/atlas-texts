The fissure sits high on the flank of an uncharted shard-mountain.  Its mouth opens inward, a cleft not much wider than a freight car, slicing through the living rock like a pocket drawn from nothing and slipped behind the curtain of the peak’s outer face.  One step over the lip and the mountain disappears; the only sky now is the cold mineral sheen of the fissure’s inner walls.

They are dark, the color of quenched graphite, and cut into long, prismatic planes.  Here and there a shallower ledge has not yet rounded to a bevel.  They catch condensation the way salt catches moisture: droplets form, bead, and, instead of falling, ride the surface tension in a perpetual crawl, marking the rock with iridescent trails that shimmer like snail paths when the light is right.  The water does not pool or run; the surface chemistry keeps it micro-thin, held just above the freeze point by constant slow motion.

Below the ledges the void widens and narrows again, the true floor lost in a single unbroken column of air that ends, a kilometer or two below, in an inverted meadow of the same faceted stone.  The stone sings—not with wind but with latent charge.  Every change in local heat (the warmth from a passing body, the brief flare of an arc-lamp) sets up a low, glass-clear vibration that travels between the walls at walking speed.  In silence the tone rises to audible register, a sustained note that would be a perfect fifth across any two surfaces more than five meters apart.  Visitors quickly learn to pitch their footsteps to a harmony they can stand to hear all day.

The city clings to one wall in three layers, each hung on cross-beams of silicate composite only a molecule or two different from the rock itself.  Streets are vertical terraces, each no wider than a sidewalk, roofed in the open air of the fissure and glazed against sublimation by continuous micro-spritzes from ceiling nozzles—essentially architectural fog-catchers.  Balconies cantilever outward on single-crystal spars.  The highest level is still under construction; work-frames extend like antennae, moving by local piezo-morph, re-shaping themselves every hour.  Every completed structure is also an instrument, storing the low vibration of the fissure in nanolattices that drip energy downward like stored daylight.  At dusk the lattices release it again as a soft, sky-colored glow from beneath the handrails, marking the pathway without bulbs.

There are no plants and no soils.  The only life-like processes are chemical gradients inside sealed modules: gas-exchange loops built into the terraces themselves.  They absorb stray moisture from the weeping walls, strip off any free oxygen, and export carbon dioxide at concentrations just low enough to stay outside of human awareness.  A faint ozone tang clings to the traffic lanes.  The nearest thing to fauna are the service mites—each smaller than a lentil, diamond-skinned, and programmed to ride the vibration like surfers, repairing stress fractures and sweeping the terraces of stray dust.  At any moment only a few dozen of them are visible, scattered across square kilometers of vertical surface like constellations.

Light arrives from the upper cleft in shafts that change angle by a few degrees every hour as the mountain’s rotation exposes new seams.  The shafts are cold and white; they create mirror-image bright bands on the opposing wall, and the bands migrate upward as the day turns, crawling over the walkways like slow searchlights.  No one is ever entirely in shadow or glare, but there are no warm tints anywhere; color exists only in the shallow diffraction films left by the condensing water.

Below the inhabited layers, kilometer-deep vents channel excess micro-water downward in slow capillary sheets.  At the true base the water meets a standing column of super-cooled argon held at 82 K in a refractive lattice; the mixture forms lenses of glass-clear clathrate that refract the vibration into visible threads of pale blue.  From the lowest balcony it looks as though the fissure is threaded with slow, quiet lightning.

Footfalls are almost inaudible under the constant fifth of the walls; voices are heard only when deliberately pitched to the harmonic, otherwise they dissipate in the distance between terraces.  If you speak the city’s name—three low, gliding syllables—your own voice returns a quarter-second later from the far wall, transposed upward a fifth, as though the city itself were answering.
