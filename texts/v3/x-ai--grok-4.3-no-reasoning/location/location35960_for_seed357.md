You stand in a living city that exists only because the green never stops.  

Where most terrain would slope or fold, here the ground itself is grown: tiered mats of broad-leaved caulking-plants laid flat and fused at the edges into continuous plazas; between the seams, vaulted ribs of succulent tissue rise like the arches of a ruined aqueduct, but fresh—still sweating resin, still adding thickness by the hour. The ribs twist upward in tight helical bundles and, at their summits, flare into shallow cup-leaves the width of plazas. Each cup tilts sunward, collects rain, and, when full, slowly tilts again to pour its overflow in a long, deliberate spill down the next tier. Water therefore never strikes ground; it moves from basin to basin, always in motion, its journey measured in the subtle tilt of living hinges.  

The air above the cups is thick with condensing mist. Every plant surface radiates warmth; the latent heat keeps the water in constant phase-change between vapor and droplet, so that a low, visible fog line hovers at chest height, rolling as the leaves adjust. The fog refracts the midday light into a diffuse white that carries no sharp shadows, yet remains bright enough that the veins inside the leaf-walls glow chartreuse where chlorophyll still works beneath translucent epidermis.  

Because the plants are both structure and substrate, emptiness is a planned negative: corridors are not cut but grown absent—zones where inhibitory hormones have suppressed cell division so that open shafts remain between towers. These shafts drop thirty meters or more to the next terrace, revealing the full height of the city in a single vertical slice. Inside each shaft, condensation drips in thick, slow strings that sing when they break; the impact echoes upward, a rolling, glassy chime that layers into a percussive drone as thousands of filaments snap at once. That is the only sound—no wind, no insects—only the deliberate hydraulics of living architecture releasing stored water.  

The elevation is high enough that atmospheric pressure has begun to sculpt the growth. Leaves that point outward are thicker, waxy, to resist the drying; those facing inward are diaphanous and cupped, catching whatever mist the wind lifts from the terraces below. On the uppermost ridges the tissue has hardened into translucent panels; the sun heats the fluid inside, driving convection currents that circulate nutrients without roots. The panels creak as the sap expands, a low wooden groan felt rather than heard beneath the dripping music.  

Nothing here is inert. Walls sweat and re-absorb; floors pulse with peristaltic waves that carry water and dissolved sugars from cup to root-zone; the city is still becoming itself. Where yesterday’s plaza edge met open air, a new rib is already rising, its tip glossy and blind, questing outward until it finds an unoccupied thermal. When it meets resistance—another plant’s shadow, or an already-occupied shaft—it simply flattens and fuses, surrendering individuality to the larger lattice.  

In the distance, beyond the immediate terraces, the same logic repeats at valley scale: mile-wide leaf-fields tilt in coordinated sequence, dumping water into a central cistern-bowl whose curved rim is itself photosynthetic, a single cell layer thick yet kilometers across. From this vantage the city looks less like architecture than like a green tide caught mid-surge, a static snapshot of something still arriving. The horizon is not a line but the place where the uppermost leaf-edges dissolve into mist and light.
