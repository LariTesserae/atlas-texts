The slope lifts like an unfinished paragraph—plateaus of lichen-cloaked talus stitched together by threads of brighter stone. There is no summit yet, only a rising that keeps deciding where to stop. Water finds every unfinished joint and stays there: sheets of it running inside cracked gabbro, trickling into basins still rimmed by drill scars. Mist condenses on the up-tilted faces and falls again, a second rain that never reaches the valley below. When the clouds thin, sunlight arrives like poured metal; every dripping face becomes a mirror for a moment, then the film evaporates and the rock returns to its dull iron colors.

The plants here have given up on soil. They braid directly into the fractures—black-green ropes no wider than a finger, anchored by tiny rootlets that wedge into quartz veins. Where one strand finds water it splits, weaving over its siblings until whole ledges are upholstered in one unbroken membrane of living tissue. Some stems are no thicker than wire yet reach the height of a person; their internodes swell into translucent bladders that catch the morning glare and then contract by noon, squeezing the excess moisture downward into the communal mesh. Because the temperature is only cool rather than cold, the bladders never freeze; instead they maintain a slow peristalsis that moves dew from sunlit crowns to shaded undersides without any animal assistance.

Between these living plates the rock is still bare, and the gaps are wide. Wind crosses them in long, uncomplicated sweeps. Occasionally a heavier gust knocks loose a spray of condensate that glitters, then vanishes. The emptiness between plant-masses is so consistent that the eye begins to read it as negative architecture: pale walkways left open for the next layer of tissue that has not yet arrived.

Scattered across the slope stand the incomplete frames of extraction rigs—scaffold towers of tubular steel, never fully clad, their bolts already beginning to rust where water migrates through the mesh. Conveyor housings sit half-buried in scree; only the upper rims protrude, their rollers locked by verdigris. Each structure is sited where a fracture is widest, as though the builders meant to tap the watercourses inside the mountain but stopped once the living mat proved quicker to harvest than the ore beneath. Power still reaches the nearest tower: an insulated trunk line snakes among the roots like a black vine, and a single sodium lamp flickers on its cross-arm, though its glow is pointless under a sun this relentless.

The only moving things besides the plants themselves are thin threads of vapor that rise wherever a bladder empties. They drift upward, coalesce briefly into low clouds, and settle again on the next rise. The sound they make is negligible beside the continuous low-frequency moan of wind being sliced by the open steelwork and the soft, almost sub-audible throb of the plant membrane expanding and contracting in overlapping rhythms. Together they produce a drone that seems to come from the slope as a whole, an industrial lullaby half-finished, like the rigs.

Nothing larger than a pebble stirs. Seeds arrive, roll a short distance, and are either absorbed into the mat or carried off again by the same wind that keeps the towers singing. The slope therefore remains at the same slow work it began long before the first scaffold bolt was torqued: the steady conversion of unfinished geology into a single, respiring skin.
