The space is small in human terms—a single long chamber, vaulted by plants rather than stone. The floor, walls, and ceiling are continuous layers of woody vines and thick, fibrous leaves, all of them braided together so tightly that the original rock is hidden beneath. Light arrives from above through a seam that runs the length of the chamber, a thin skylight of translucent membrane that turns the noon sky into a soft, white-gray wash. The light is ordinary, the air warm and heavy.

Water is everywhere but never deep. The vines themselves weep beads that collect in the cupped leaves of the understory, and the humidity condenses into a fine dew that rolls along the undersides of the higher canopy, following the curve of each leaf until it drops again in a quiet, steady rhythm. The smell is sweet and tannic, like cedar mulch after rain.

The only non-plant elements are a scattering of small brass engines—cylindrical, hand-sized—set into alcoves carved into the living wood. Each engine runs on the slow pressure of expanding and contracting xylem, its pistons ticking once every three minutes, enough to drive a single soft pump that moves water from the wetter base layer toward the drier upper leaves. The engines are warm to the touch, their copper jackets latticed by fine root hairs that grow straight into them.

Because there is no room, the fauna must be small enough to occupy the spaces that already exist. Minute springtails live between the leaf layers, and pinhead snails travel the condensation trails. All move slowly enough that a human observer will rarely see them in motion; their life-cycles are measured in seasons rather than days. No birds, no insects that buzz or sting.

Sound here is produced by the engines and by the water. Each three-minute cycle begins with a soft metallic knock as pressure builds, followed by the hiss of a single valve releasing a teaspoon of liquid into the leaf above. The water lands with a percussive tap that travels through the whole plant lattice, a single pulse passing through the chamber like a slowed heartbeat. Between knocks, the vines creak and adjust as they settle under their own weight, a minute, woody sigh repeated by thousands of fibers.

Time moves at the pace of the engines. A visitor arriving at dawn sees the same drip pattern at dusk; the change that occurs is only the gradual thickening of the light membrane as new layers of cuticle are laid down, or the slow widening of an engine alcove as the surrounding wood grows around it rather than against it. The space does not bloom or decay; it listens, adjusts, and keeps ticking.
