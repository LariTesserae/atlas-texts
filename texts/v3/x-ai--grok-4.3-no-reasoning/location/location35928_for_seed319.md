The city sits on a broad limestone shelf where the ridge folds into the cloudline. From above, the terrain reads like a dried sponge—grooves and sockets, long ribs of stone running level with the horizon, then plunging into narrow clefts that carry ropes of grey water down into the urban grid. The shelf is high enough that the air cools every few hours; condensation blooms across every exposed rock and metal surface, forming thin, persistent rivulets that glint like seams of mercury before they re-enter the stone.

Buildings rise straight out of the natural channels, bridging them with iron lattice walkways and open sluice-ducts. Some façades are cut from the same bone-colored limestone; others are grown on top of it—layer upon layer of riveted plating, corrugated sheeting, and ceramic tile. Because the limestone is slightly soluble, the water has already started to etch recessed channels behind the lowest storey of each structure; some older blocks appear to be slowly melting into the bedrock they once rested on.

The city’s machinery has settled into the pace of the water. Huge flywheels turn so slowly that their motion registers as a drifting shadow across the walls; piston arms rise and fall like the breathing of a large animal. Every quarter-hour a condenser tower exhales a bolus of warm vapour that rolls between the rooftops like slow fog. The vapour picks up the scent of hot brass and wet mineral dust before it descends, leaving a faint film on every rail and pipe.

Vegetation is limited to stubborn seams of salt-tolerant moss that grow in the narrow shadow line where a cornice meets a wall. In places the moss has trapped enough grit to support single, wiry ferns. These plants do not spread; they merely persist, vibrating when the wind changes. The city’s animal life is thinner still: pale, eyeless crickets move along the condensing films inside the sluice-ducts, and once per day translucent cave-swallows flicker through the vapour clouds, hunting condensation insects.

The human presence is measured in footsteps rather than voices. Because the machines are loud, conversation happens by slate and chalk, by the tap of code on railings, by the placement of coloured lanterns along the duct bridges. The noise itself is continuous but strangely unvarying: a layered roar of water on limestone, steel wheels on worn tracks, low turbine drones that rise an octave when humidity increases. The sound travels around corners, reflects off the high cloud ceiling, and arrives at the ear already softened, as though heard through heavy cloth.

Because nothing arrives quickly, the city records time in the slow accumulation of rust blooms on north-facing walls, in the millimetre-by-millimetre migration of the sluice channels deeper into the stone, and in the yearly ritual of replacing a single flywheel bearing that has crept one tooth out of true. The inhabitants speak of the city as already finished; they are merely present while it continues its long, damp negotiation with the ridge beneath it.
