The mountain has been losing itself for a hundred thousand years.

Its upper slopes are not so much a surface as a single, living matrix that began, at some indeterminable depth, as separate stems and has since fused into one continuous organism. There is no soil in the human sense—only layer upon layer of compressed vascular tissue, pale green where recent and cork-dark where older than memory, slick with condensation even when the air itself is dry. This living substrate climbs in terraces of a hundred meters, each terrace the width of a city block, yet the transitions between them are imperceptible; the whole slope reads as a single, breathing slope of plant-mass, soft underfoot, fibrous when torn. From certain angles it seems to ripple in slow peristalsis, though the movement may simply be the slow settling of water through its internal capillaries.

Water is everywhere and nowhere visible. It beads on every facet of the green-black matrix, forms hanging lenses beneath undercut ledges, trickles in thread-thin veins that vanish and reappear meters later. Where the mass has sagged under its own antiquity, shallow depressions have filled into standing pools the color of weak tea; their surfaces are broken by the slow upwelling of gas from metabolic processes deep inside the substrate. In places, water condenses directly out of the air into fine mists that drift a few meters before re-absorption. Because the temperature hovers near freezing, these mists sometimes crystallize mid-air into short-lived glinting halos around any protrusion.

The cold is uniform but not brutal. Breath hangs; surfaces stay just above the point where life would cease. The plant-mass itself generates a faint metabolic warmth that rises in sheets, keeping a hand’s-width layer of air immediately above the living ground from icing. Higher, the air is sharp. Clouds form and dissolve within tens of meters of vertical change, so that visibility slides in and out of a pearly half-light in which distant ridges appear only as darker bands within the same luminous gray.

The scale is deceptive. A single “valley” here is the width of a modest inland sea, yet the uniformity of the living substrate makes the curvature of the horizon feel nearer than it should. When the mist thins, the far slopes appear as a single continuous plane tilted at twenty degrees, stretching into haze rather than ending at any ridge line. The land is not flat; it is merely too large for the eye to register its folds as discrete terrain.

Fauna exists in the interstitial spaces between fibers. Meter-long translucent annelids migrate through the vascular channels, leaving temporary tunnels that seal behind them; their passage registers as traveling bulges beneath the surface. Swarms of thumbnail-sized gliders—membranous, six-winged—rise from vents when pressure equalizes, catch an updraft, and are carried kilometers before settling again. Larger, slower organisms move at the scale of geological time: cauliflower-shaped colonies of hardened matrix that migrate a body-length per decade by differential growth. Their presence is marked only by the faint radial patterns they leave, like wood-grain turned inside-out.

There is industry, but no factories in the terrestrial sense. The plant-mass itself is the engine. At intervals, cylindrical chambers—self-formed rather than excavated—pulse with slow pressure changes, drawing atmospheric gases through spiracles and expelling refined metabolic by-products in visible exhalations. The exhaust is not smoke but a shifting refractive index that bends light, so that the horizon sometimes fractures into prismatic bands for minutes at a time. Where two such chambers operate in proximity, their fields interfere, creating localized pockets of intensified or nullified gravity; water may levitate or fall upward in those zones. The sound of these chambers is not mechanical but hydrostatic: a deep, arrhythmic throb like blood heard from inside an artery, overlaid with high, skirling overtones when vapor is forced through narrowed valves. The overall acoustic environment is a constant, directionless roar—part storm, part circulatory system, part hallucination.

Reality thins in places. Where the plant-mass has grown around an older, mineral outcrop and then outlived it, the stone sometimes reappears as negative space: a perfect negative cast of bark and leaf in which the mineral has been replaced, molecule by molecule, by living tissue. Walking through such a region can produce the unsettling sense that one’s own body is likewise being rewritten. Occasionally, a walker’s footprint fails to vanish; instead the living floor continues to record the pressure wave outward in concentric rings that persist for hours, visible as lighter or darker tissue. Conversely, certain hollows act as memory sinks: step into one and the sound of your own footfall may be delayed by several seconds, emerging from an adjacent depression instead.

Nothing is built because nothing needs to be. The organism has already performed the functions of city, machine, and weather system upon itself. What remains is the slow negotiation between its own metabolic cycles and the larger thermodynamics of a mountain that has forgotten how to erode.
