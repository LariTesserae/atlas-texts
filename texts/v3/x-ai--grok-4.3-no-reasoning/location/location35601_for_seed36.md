It begins beneath the skin of the world.  

The surface is not a hard shell but a loose, porous ceiling of interlaced leaves and translucent, fibrous roots that allow a continuous, hammered daylight to filter through. Light reaches every stratum in brilliant, even sheets—no shafts, no shadows—because the plant mass itself seems to conduct the photons like a slow current, diffusing them so the entire volume below feels equally lit. Temperature remains fixed at a steady, humid warmth; nothing rises or falls, no convection currents disturb the equilibrium. The air itself is dense with moisture held in a perpetual state of near-saturation, yet never condensing into droplets that could fall or pool, never drying enough to crack or curl.

The plants have no distinction between growth and structure. Thick, planar sheets of chlorophyll-rich tissue stretch in horizontal planes like suspended strata, each layer offset from the one above so that vertical sight-lines are blocked at every half-meter. Between these planes rise columns of spiraled, woody stems that bifurcate at the same interval, forming an endless, self-similar lattice. The smallest gaps are narrower than a wrist; the largest clearings wider than a person lying flat. Within these gaps hang root-hairs so fine they read as mist until the eye adjusts. Moisture moves along these hairs by surface tension alone—intermittent, micro-scale transfer from one tissue to another—never accumulating, never evaporating. The sensation is of perpetual damp skin without the relief of sweat or dew.

Time does not announce itself. Nothing flowers, fruits, or decays at a scale perceptible to eye or ear. The vegetation is always the same density, always the same shade of saturated green that borders on black when seen against the uniform brightness. The only fauna are tiny, transparent isopods that adhere to the undersides of the larger leaves, motionless except for the faint peristalsis of feeding. They do not move between leaves; each generation is born, feeds, and dies on the same tissue, so the population map is as fixed as the leaf veins themselves. Their presence registers only as a slight local opacity, a faint gray stippling visible only if one lingers on a single square meter long enough for pattern recognition to emerge.

There are occasional built marks, but they are not architecture. At irregular intervals the plant mass opens into shallow, bowl-shaped depressions lined with a denser, harder cuticle that feels almost mineral. These depressions are always empty, always dry relative to the surrounding tissues. Their edges are raised no more than a finger’s width; they could be read as scars or as seed-scale templates for something that has not yet been seeded. No repeated geometric relation exists between them. They simply occur where the lattice thins by a single degree of branching, as though the plants have paused an ongoing act of self-intersection.

Sound is reduced to the barely audible: the constant, low-frequency flex of turgid tissue expanding and contracting at the cellular level—too slow to register as vibration, felt more as pressure change against the tympanum. No wind moves through the lattice; no leaves rasp. The only interruptions are the soft, arrhythmic ticks of an isopod occasionally repositioning a single leg. These sounds last less than a second and leave the equilibrium unaltered.

Scale asserts itself gradually rather than dramatically. Walking in any horizontal direction, one encounters the same density of planes and columns for distances measured in hundreds of body-lengths, yet never encounters a boundary wall or canopy edge. The space is open, but openness here is not emptiness; it is the continuation of the same saturated lattice beyond immediate perception. Vertically the situation is different: above the uppermost plane the light increases until it becomes painful, and below the lowermost plane the tissues thin and pale until the floor—if it exists—remains unreached. Both directions are effectively infinite within the logic of this place, yet they are not explorable; the same equilibrium that fixes temperature and moisture also fixes the gradient of tissue density. The system does not permit ascent or descent beyond its own current configuration.

Erosion, in the conventional sense, cannot occur. Nothing is wearing away; the geology is still forming, but its only material is living plant. The “raw” quality is not one of exposed rock but of an organism that has not yet finished deciding its final morphology. The occasional cuticle-lined depressions may be read as the first gestures of that decision, or as abandoned prototypes. They remain empty. They remain unchanged. The landscape is therefore not a finished habitat but a single, vast developmental stage held at equilibrium by mechanisms that do not, at present, include decay, succession, weather, or time.
