Beneath the world, in a place where rock was never finished becoming rock, a living membrane spreads itself like unfinished parchment between the upper crust and the forgotten strata. Nothing here has finished hardening; every plane is still deciding whether it is ceiling, floor, or breathing wall. The ceiling is a vault of milky, mineral-choked sky—a luminous lichen that condenses directly from diffuse vapors seeping through porous strata above. The floor is not ground but successive sheets of vegetable felt, each layer still knitting itself, so walking produces a slow yielding like treading wet suede that heals behind each step.  

All is vegetation, and vegetation is everything. Stems do not stand up; they braid sideways, interlocking at every scale. One moment the path is a knotwork of vines as thick as a wrist; a few body-lengths onward the same vines have fused into a single unbroken sheet so rigid it rings underfoot like seasoned wood. Where the sheets buckle upward they form temporary vaults, creaking softly as new growth pushes against yesterday’s growth. Nothing is inert. When a traveler rests against any surface it warms, then tightens, offering mild resistance—the entire matrix is one organism learning where its edges are.  

Temperature hovers at a steady tropical dusk: skin-warm, just below the point where sweating begins. Condensation pearls along every vein, but the droplets never fall; they are re-absorbed within moments, feeding back into the lattice. The air is dense enough to carry scent the way water carries color; every breath is floral, resinous, vaguely metallic, and immediately overwritten by the next exhalation of the surrounding mass.  

Light arrives in graded sheets from living skylights—rings of photoluminescent fungi that float at irregular intervals. They pulse gently in a staggered circadian rhythm, so no single area is ever fully dark; instead, brightness drifts like slow clouds across the ceiling. Where two skylights overlap, the spectrum turns faintly lavender; where none reach, deep-green shadows pool in which only movement betrays shape.  

Sound does not echo so much as multiply. Every surface is a drumhead. The slightest motion—blood pulsing in a wrist, air displaced by a turning head—transmits through the living felt and is answered by sympathetic tremors kilometers away. Louder events: the crack of an expanding seed-case, the whip of a contracting vine, the constant low thunder of millions of microscopic rootlets sliding against one another—merge into a single sustained roar. At times the din resolves into percussive waves that pass through the body like sub-bass, felt in molars and long bones. Hearing protection is useless; the organism itself is the medium.  

The ecology is almost entirely sessile yet hyper-mobile. What an observer might mistake for animals are emergent phenomena: dense clouds of pollinating mites that condense from vapor and disperse again; “browsers” that are actually mobile colonies of thorns riding along vascular expressways; temporary eyes—clusters of photoreceptive florets—that open for a few hours, track movement, then dissolve back into undifferentiated leaf. There are no fixed species. Individual forms arise where local needs are greatest, persist only while the task demands, and are reabsorbed once the configuration shifts.  

Scattered through this continuous plant matrix are artifacts of an earlier, simpler order: hand-worked stone bowls half-swallowed by encircling stems; lengths of braided fiber that have been adopted as structural tendons; a single corroded copper needle lodged in a vein like a splinter, around which the plant has built a scar-tissue knot. These intrusions remain because the living substrate has not yet finished deciding how to metabolize them. Occasionally a bowl or needle will vanish overnight, drawn deeper and chemically disassembled for trace minerals.  

Nothing here is permanent, yet nothing is wasted. A chamber that collapses under the weight of its own ceiling is within minutes colonized by pressure-sensing rhizomes that convert the rubble into nutrient slurry, which rises again as fresh lattice a day later. Caverns open where tensile stress exceeds photosynthetic output; they are filled not by water but by clouds of buoyant spores that drift until they lodge and begin the next cycle of growth.  

Elevation never rises above the level of a modest cellar, yet the sense of verticality is continuous: every surface is both ground and sky for the layer above. A traveler who insists on an absolute “up” soon discovers that the very notion is seasonal—six hours later the same plane may be experienced as floor. Gravity still functions, but the organism’s constant internal hydraulic redistribution makes directions feel provisional.  

Time is likewise overwritten. The only clock is metabolic: the pulse of the skylights, the dilation of transport vessels, the slow migration of nutrient fronts. A single human heartbeat registers here as an erratic seismic flutter, noticed, catalogued, and gradually damped by surrounding tissue until the matrix returns to its own overwhelming, constant music.
