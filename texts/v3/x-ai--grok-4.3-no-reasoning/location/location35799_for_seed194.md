The chamber that contains the city is the first living thing encountered. It is not excavated; it is grown—walls of ligneous tissue so massively thickened that their annual rings have fused into a single substance: smooth as horn, dark as slate, yet still faintly aromatic if one’s skin lingers on them long enough. The substance is neither wood nor rock but an indurate botanical polymer secreted in slow concentric shells over millennia. No tool can cut it. Every surface inside the cavity was once an interior of that organism; the only openings are the narrow, iris-like vents where the growth originally budded outward around pockets of trapped gas.

Within this hollow organ the city has settled, or perhaps the city is still settling. Its structures are woven, not stacked. Long, hair-thin tendrils—pale, almost translucent—have been coaxed into taut webs that span from one curved wall to another. Where the webs cross, they fuse; where tension is required, they thicken. Roofs are living diaphragms drawn tight by rings of contractile fiber; floors are compressed mats of the same tissue, now dead-white from lack of light yet still structurally sound. The settlement is one enormous, half-desiccated fruit whose internal septa have been repurposed by its later inhabitants. Over slow centuries the partitions have sagged, stretched, re-knit, until the entire arrangement reads as a single, extremely slow-moving membrane.

There is no water at all. Instead, the plants subsist on trace volatiles—hydrogen sulfide, ammonia, and the faint exhalations of their own metabolism—that drift upward from micro-fissures in the substrate. The air is cool, metallic on the tongue, and carries a constant low-pressure hiss. Every surface vibrates at a frequency just below hearing; the resonance is the city’s only weather. It rises from subsonic pulses of vascular peristalsis within the chamber walls—slow tides of nutrient gel that circulate once every several human lifetimes. Each surge produces a chord of muffled tones, like a colossal jaw harp stroked with a velvet plectrum. The city amplifies these tones through its suspended webs until the chamber sings without cease: a layered, harmonic drone that is both the power grid and the calendar.

Because density is near-vacuum, most of the volume is empty space. The webs occupy only the margins, leaving a central volume of utter darkness so large that a dropped seed would fall for hours before striking another strand. The only illumination is the faint phosphorescence of specialized parenchyma cells that line every tensile cable, triggered by mechanical stress; when windless tension fluctuates, the entire mesh momentarily glimmers like frost on cobweb. This light is dimmer than moonlight filtered through smoke, yet it is the only light the inhabitants have ever known.

Nothing moves quickly. Growth proceeds at the pace of lignification: centimeters per century. Repairs to a sagging floor are accomplished by one filament at a time, laid down and seasoned over generations. When a tension web finally reaches critical fatigue, it does not snap; it slowly loosens, droops, and finally rests upon lower cables that have already grown up to meet it. The resulting surface is slightly uneven—detectable only by fingertip—yet still perfectly habitable.

Ecology consists solely of the city itself and the parent organism that enfolds it. There are no animals, only symbiotes so reduced they register as organelles rather than species: chemosynthetic microbes living between cell walls, exchanging sulfur compounds for shelter. Their metabolism produces no heat detectable by touch; their only product is a faint, ozone-tinged sweetness absorbed again by the parent tissue.

The rules of this world are those of botanical time folded into civic time. A birth is not registered until the child’s first strand of hair can be wound into a structural splice and survive one full nutrient pulse. A sentence is commuted when the woven bars of a cell have grown so thick that the prisoner can no longer slip between them. Because light never changes, time is measured by shifts in the drone pitch, each microtonal slide corresponding to the passage of several human generations. In such a system, decay and construction become indistinguishable; both are processes of gradual, irreversible tightening.

The city has no name, because naming would imply that the chamber and the mesh inside it are separate entities. They are not. They are concentric layers of the same long, patient sentence, spoken once, still being pronounced.
