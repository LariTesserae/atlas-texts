High in a pocket of the mountains, where the rock has only recently torn itself upward from the buried mass of the earth, a narrow col holds a world the width of three long rooms.  The walls rise sheer, knife-faces of unweathered stone still striped with the percussion ripples of their own birth.  Between them, the floor is a mosaic of fresh scree: angular shards of quartzite and black slate, none yet rounded by frost or time.  The space feels smaller because everything crowds it.  Overhead, frost feathers and hair-thin icicles fringe the lip of the eastern wall; they glisten but never drip, because the temperature idles a hair above freezing, and every night the droplets flash-freeze again.

The ground is not dry.  A film of condensation pearls across every grain of rock, and faint rills—mere millimeters wide—thread between the broken stones, following grooves so new that they still echo the angles of the original fracture planes.  Here and there a shallow pan no larger than a palm holds a few spoonfuls of clear, cold water; breath across its surface raises a brief fog that rolls downhill and vanishes.

Vegetation is scarce and peculiar.  In the deeper crevices, fans of black lichen spread like spilled ink, each lobe stiff and matte.  Tiny liverworts root directly into micro-fissures, their thalli only a cell or two thick; they carry their own tiny beads of water at their centers.  Nothing stands taller than a thumbnail, yet there is a sense of crowding, as though every fissure already holds the maximum number of living threads it can support.

Light is almost absent.  The col lies beneath a perpetual cornice of wind-packed snow that sags from the ridge like a torn awning.  What illumination exists is dim and bluish, arriving second-hand after bouncing off blue ice walls farther up.  In that half-light the quartz facets glitter like wet glass whenever a body moves past them.

Sound is constant and low.  A fine, continual sift of frost grains slides down the eastern face, each grain tapping once on stone before vanishing into the accumulating powder.  Beneath that, a slower, almost inaudible groan rises through the rock itself: the massif still settling, joints realigning after the sudden uplift.  Occasionally a single shard, stressed beyond tolerance, lets out a note like struck porcelain; the shard falls, the note ceases, and the sift continues.

Fauna fills the remaining volume.  Thousands of minute springtails—translucent, wingless, each the size of a comma—drift through the air on imperceptible drafts.  They never land; they simply reorient, their furcula cocked, ready for the next small gust.  Pale, thread-like annelids coil inside every water pan; they move only when the water temperature shifts a fraction, stretching, knotting, then contracting again.  The cracks in the walls house colonies of feather-winged midges whose synchronized wingbeats produce a single, glassy tone just above human hearing.  When the tone swells, it masks the sliding frost; when it fades, the frost is audible again.  Nothing here preys in the usual sense; the creatures simply rotate through the same droplets, the same cracks, the same film of condensation, leaving traces so fine they re-freeze into the ice each night.

The slow dynamic expresses itself over weeks rather than hours.  Every few days a new hairline crack opens somewhere in the eastern wall; the fracture exhales a thread of warmer, moister air from deep inside the massif.  That breath momentarily thickens the condensation, allowing the liverworts to advance another cell’s width.  The springtails rise in a brief spiral, then resettle.  The crack seals again under nightly ice until the next slow exhalation finds it or another.  In this manner the entire col is engaged in a single, protracted rearrangement whose full measure will not be visible until decades after the uplift itself has stopped.
