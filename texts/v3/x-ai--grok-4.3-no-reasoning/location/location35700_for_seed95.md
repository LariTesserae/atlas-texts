The plateau is a vast shelf of warm stone, high enough that the air thins and the sky becomes a single unbroken bowl. Millennia ago the rock was still being written by water, but now it only receives the faintest of inscriptions: a thin lace of condensation left each night and burned off by midday. The resulting damp is shallow and transient, a silver film on dark lichen-crusts, enough to coax a few stalks of dull green from fissures but never enough to support anything that would call itself a forest.

Because the vegetation is so thin, sightlines remain long; yet each square pace of rock hosts its own crowded microcosm. Tiny moss-boulders, no bigger than a fingernail, crowd in overlapping sheets, each one a city of microscopic filaments drinking the evening dew before the heat erases it. Between them lie grains of wind-polished grit, a finer sediment that has already finished its long migration; it lies in shallow drifts, held in place by the slightest tackiness of nightly moisture. Nothing here is in a hurry to move on.

The plateau ends—not abruptly, but over distances measured in slow hours of walking—against a horizon of identical stone. That sameness makes scale deceptive: the eye can sweep what ought to be miles yet still encounter only the same faint texture of fractures and lichens, so the land feels both endless and intimately local. Scattered across this continuity are low domes of wind-sculpted basalt, their curves so gentle they might be mistaken for the natural sagging of the plateau itself. In their shallow lee sides, wind-borne dust has accumulated just enough to host a darker ring of drought-tolerant liverworts. From above, these domes look like the ghosts of buildings; from the ground they are simply warmer patches on an already warm skin of stone.

No tool has ever shaped any part of this surface, yet the geometry of the domes and the perfect radial cracks around them imply deliberation. In truth the pattern is the outcome of the slowest form of erosion: thermal cycling so slight that only after ages does the rock yield along invisible planes. When the occasional shower comes, water follows those same hairline seams, lingers long enough to darken them, then vanishes into vapor before noon. The only sound is the faint tick of contracting stone as temperature falls after sunset; otherwise the plateau keeps its silence, the air too thin and dry to carry the rustle of anything larger than a drifting spore.

Yet the quiet is crowded. At dusk, the temperature gradient draws tiny wingless flyers from the fissures—mites with crystal scales that catch the last light in brief prismatic flashes before they settle again. They never rise far, remaining within the boundary layer of warmth that clings to the rock. Their movements are synchronized to a rhythm so gradual that an observer must stay motionless for an entire change of light to notice them repositioning across the lichens. The same slow cadence governs the water: the plateau never floods, never dries to dust; it merely rocks between two states of damp, each state defined more by duration than by quantity.

Underfoot the stone is faintly resonant. A heel-fall produces a muted ceramic ring because the interior is veined with millions of microscopic air pockets, themselves relics of gas trapped when the lava cooled. These voids hold pockets of the previous night’s moisture; by morning the vapor diffuses outward through pores too small to see, re-condensing on the surface as a breath-fine dew that feeds the lichens until the sun climbs high enough to reverse the process. The cycle is so faint that it registers only as a slight deepening of color across the plateau—an imperceptible tide of darkness that arrives without clouds and departs without wind.

Here, erosion has done all the work it intends to do. The plateau is the finished sentence of an ancient climatic argument, its subject and verb worn down to a single noun: stone. All future events will be footnotes written in condensation and erased again by heat.
