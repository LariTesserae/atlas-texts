The place is a city that never knew the sun, yet never lost its leaves. Beneath a roof of living stone—ancient reef-stone, calcified into a single, unbroken vault hundreds of meters thick—the air is cool and almost motionless. Dim phosphorescence seeps from the walls themselves, shed by colonial algae locked in the rock’s pores. It is just enough to read the shapes of things, never enough to cast a true shadow.

The city fills a low basin once scoured by long-gone subterranean rivers. Every building has grown rather than been built. Thick, root-like trunks of pale heartwood spiral upward in helical flutes, branching outward into wide, flat canopies whose undersides are furred with gills that sift nutrients from the constant mist of spores drifting down from the vault. Between the trunks run bridges of fused mycelium, their surfaces ridged like dried riverbeds; they creak once every few hours, a wooden sigh that travels the entire span before fading. Walkways are padded with a living felt of moss whose cilia beat in slow, synchronized waves, carrying fallen debris toward the walls where it is reabsorbed.

Fauna is everywhere and nowhere at once. Armored slugs the size of freight cars glide along the undersides of the canopies, rasping off dead gill tissue; their passage sounds like distant surf. Smaller, six-winged creatures—more lung than body—drift between trunks on membranes that double as respiratory bellows; each breath they take adds a soft, wet pop to the air. The loudest residents are the cliff-striders: eyeless, many-legged brachiators that swing between the vertical root-trunks. Their footfalls on the mycelial bridges are thunderous; each stride lands with a boom that rolls for seconds, echoing off the vault until it merges with the next. The sound never ceases. It is the city’s pulse.

Water is absent, yet everything is lush. The plants draw what little moisture they need from the perpetual exhalation of the rock itself; the air tastes faintly of wet chalk. No droplets form. Instead, the atmosphere carries a fine suspension of mineral salts that settle as a pale rime along every horizontal surface by dawn-cycle. The rime is harvested each long day by the city’s sole animal domesticates: flat, many-mouthed grazers whose tongues rasp the salt into storage pouches along their spines. Their chewing produces a continuous, granular hiss that underlies the larger sounds.

Nothing here is sudden. The reef-stone above settles a few millimeters each century; the root-trunks add a single annual ring the width of a fingernail. When a bridge finally sags too far it does not fall; it puts down new anchor filaments into the basin floor and becomes a tunnel. The city widens by millimeters each year, the basin breathing outward as roots push against its sides. The only fast thing is sound, and even that is old when it reaches the ear—reverberations stacked upon reverberations until any footstep becomes a choir of its own past.

No tools exist because none are required. The living architecture maintains itself; the animals follow ancient grazing circuits laid down before the vault closed. A human might walk for days and never meet another, yet never feel alone—the low, constant thunder of cliff-striders and the soft hiss of salt-chewing are always present, near or far, like weather that learned to speak.
