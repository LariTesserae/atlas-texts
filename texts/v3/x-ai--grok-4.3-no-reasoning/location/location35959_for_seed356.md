The Mantle lies beneath itself—an unbroken integument of tissue and water so complete that the word “surface” has no purchase. What first registers is not green or brown but the trembling reflection of a planet’s own weight pressing down. The mass here is plant, but plant rendered motionless by the absence of time: every leaf, stem, root, and vascular bundle is locked into a single, globally coherent geometry whose smallest deviations are measured in planetary arcs rather than inches.

Water moves only where chemistry and pressure already agree; capillary films, so thin they remain invisible, condense continually on the micro-scarps of leaves that have never wilted. The liquid does not flow or drip; it assembles. A single droplet might remain suspended across an entire leaf-width for an epoch, then be absorbed again without trace, leaving only the faintest refractive shift in the leaf’s living glass. Where the tissue thickens into buttresses the size of mountain ranges, hydrostatic gradients create stationary lenses of pure water—subterranean seas, suspended within the lattice like tears within a single organism. Their surfaces mirror nothing, because every object that might reflect is itself the living mirror.

Because the entire volume is plant, every pore and channel has already become architecture. Curved galleries, tens of kilometers wide, rise and sink in catenary arcs whose skin is epidermis, phloem, xylem, and cuticle laminated into load-bearing strata. Light does not penetrate, but bioluminescence is unnecessary; the dark is absolute because no energy gradient remains to be expressed as radiance. The temperature remains a constant 26 °C across every height and depth, maintained by the slow, equilibrium respiration of a tissue mass whose total volume rivals the planetary crust. Any visitor—none exist—would register neither heat nor cold, only the unvarying pressure of living walls that neither contract nor relax.

No animal life, no weather systems of their own, and no manufactured artifact to mark passage, yet an industrial metabolism persists. Trillions of internal pumps, each a bundle of contractile tracheids no larger than a human wrist, cycle nutrients in an endless closed loop. The sound of these pumps is quieter than blood in an ear, a subsonic pressure cycle felt only as a faint thickening at the base of the skull. Where two galleries meet at kilometer-scale junctions, the flow divides without turbulence, preserving the same temperature and nutrient load on every branch; nothing is spent, nothing is gained. The system is a factory whose product is its own maintenance, sealed forever inside the darkness it has never left.

Erosion has not begun because nothing has yet moved. The youngest ridge or lowest hollow is still the same shape it assumed when the first coherent sheet of photosynthetic tissue locked the entire mantle into this single, self-supporting continuum. No scar, no sediment, no weathering rind exists. The landscape is therefore both infant and eternal: every surface retains the molecular sharpness of new growth, yet the growth has already finished. The result is a topography that feels simultaneously newborn and unalterably fixed, a landscape whose rules are the rules of equilibrium itself—perpetual saturation, perpetual silence, perpetual identity.
