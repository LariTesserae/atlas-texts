High on the world’s broad shoulder, the plateau rises like a single continuous table that continues farther than sight can follow. Its surface is not flat but gently buckled, a ripple made of stone that cools the air beneath its own weight. The stone itself is pale, almost colorless until moisture finds it, at which point faint iron-brown veining emerges and fades again when the air dries.

Moisture arrives in layers rather than showers. Night after night a skin of frost collects on every exposed grain and meltwater pools in shallow depressions no wider than an arm’s reach. During the brief mid-day the water sinks between the grains and then reappears the following midnight, a slow but constant redistribution. The land is never truly wet and never truly dry, only perpetually chilled and marginally damp.

Vegetation is scattered and determined. Low, tight cushions of silvery moss fill the sheltered lee sides of stones; elsewhere there are nothing more than thread-thin lichens that wind between cracks like faint writing. A few stunted shrubs grow wherever two slabs lean together to form a windless cleft, their branches flattened horizontal, bark rough as pumice. The root webs are shallow but densely interlocked, claiming every pocket of grit before the wind can steal it.

Stone architecture rises directly from the living bedrock rather than upon it. Massive slabs—some longer than sailing ships—were levered into place generations ago with the simplest of stone and timber tools. Their edges still show the shallow, regular pits left by pounding stones. Where the slabs meet, smaller stones were chinked into seams, each chosen for its slight taper so that weight alone locks it in place. These walls continue for distances that blur into the horizon; gaps left deliberately allow cold air to slide through the interior streets at ankle level, cooling the stone beneath so that frost can maintain its nightly grip.

Inside the walls, streets run straight for a few hundred paces before a new slab is placed at an angle that breaks the line of sight. The interruption is practical rather than decorative: it turns wind into slow eddies so the constant breath of frost does not shear away the thin layer of insulating grit that covers every roof. Roofs themselves are single stones, slightly dished to collect the nightly condensation and funnel it into stone basins carved into the interior corners. From these basins, narrow grooves no deeper than a finger carry water to root gardens that grow inside every household—lichen trays stacked on stone shelves that climb the interior walls like quiet staircases.

Fauna moves with the same deliberate patience. Large, low-bodied reptiles the color of cooled ash cross the open plazas only when frost has already set, their broad feet distributing weight so the thin ice does not crack beneath them. Between the paving stones, colonies of minute, translucent snails emerge at dusk to rasp the lichens; their trails remain as faint silver threads until the next wind. Overhead, the air is almost empty. Only at long intervals does a slow, high-winged glider pass, riding the plateau’s own thermal that rises by millimeters per hour. Its cry is a soft, extended whistle, rarely answered.

The built density is achieved not by crowding many structures together but by extending every structure downward and upward. Beneath the streets, low galleries follow the natural bedding planes of the stone, cool chambers used for food storage and for sleeping during the brief warm spell that occurs once each decade. Above the streets, flat terraces rise in staggered levels so that the shadow of one does not fall upon the next; each terrace holds another garden, another basin, another quiet household. The cumulative result is a city that occupies the same footprint as a modest town yet houses a population whose breathing can be sensed, if not heard, as a faint, shared warmth trapped between layers of stone.

Sound travels poorly through the chill air. Voices, footfalls, even the slow drip from roof gutters are absorbed within a few dozen paces. The only persistent noise is the almost inaudible grinding of frost expanding between grains of stone—an intimate, minute percussion that is felt in the teeth more than heard in the ear.

Time moves here in grain-by-grain increments. A crack that begins at the edge of a slab may take a century to travel its length; a basin may lose a finger’s thickness of stone to nightly dissolution over the same span. The inhabitants mark seasons not by changes in foliage but by the moment each winter when the collected frost first reaches the depth of a thumbnail, a measurement carved into the corner of every public basin as a single vertical line, left to accumulate until it must be recut.
