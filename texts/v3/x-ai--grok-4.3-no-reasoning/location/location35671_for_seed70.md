The water here has weight before it even touches you. It hangs above the world in a single, unbroken vault, a transparent ocean hanging from a ceiling that is no ceiling at all—just the underside of a vast, living sheet of liquid held aloft by some equilibrium that never quite settles. From that sheet, curtains of water descend in slow, ponderous ropes, each strand thicker than a pillar and slower than breath. Where they land, they meet other falling sheets, cross them, braid them, and continue downward until they are reabsorbed into the upward-drawing cycle again. The result is not rain; it is architecture. Towers, arcades, and long galleries of water stand and shift at intervals measured in years rather than minutes. Between them, the air remains breathable but is itself a thin film of moisture so saturated that every exhalation joins the living mass overhead instead of vanishing.

Because the land is high, these water-structures meet solid ground at angles rather than pools. The terrain is a broad, tilted plateau of pale, almost luminous stone still showing the original fracture planes from the moment it rose from the sea floor—an upheaval so recent that the rock retains the memory of pressure in its tight, glassy grain. Every ridge is knife-edged; every cleft is a wound still closing. Yet over these raw surfaces a skin of life has already begun. Thick mats of translucent, rubbery algae coat every plane that stays wet for more than a few hours. From the algae rise branching fronds the color of old bronze, each frond a single enormous cell whose interior circulates nutrient currents the way blood moves in veins. Where two fronds cross, they graft; where one frond touches stone, it dissolves a shallow groove and roots there. Over centuries these grafts will become a single organism that carpets whole slopes, but for now the process is still visible as a slow stitching of green across grey.

The warmth is steady rather than fierce, generated not by any visible sun but by the latent heat of the suspended ocean itself; the water aloft stores energy gathered during long, invisible days and radiates it downward in an even, golden light that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere. In that light the algae photosynthesize without shadow, their thalli glowing from within at dusk like slowly charging lanterns.

The plateau stretches for many kilometers in every direction, yet the same essential scene repeats with small variations: a forest of living water columns, a floor of glassy stone upholstered in ever-thickening bronze, and a ceiling that is also a floor. Between the columns, the air is hushed. The only sounds are the low, almost subsonic groan of rock still settling, the faint internal tides of the algae as their circulating fluids shift, and, once in many seasons, the soft, prolonged tear of a water-arch re-weaving itself after a fracture. No voice, no footstep, no wingbeat answers. Even when a frond reaches its maximum size and detaches—an event occurring perhaps once per human lifetime—the fall is soundless until it meets the stone; and even then the impact is muffled by the dense cushion of younger growth already waiting beneath.

Fauna is scarce because motion itself is expensive here. The only mobile creatures are microscopic pods that ride the internal currents of the largest algae, traveling from graft to graft over decades. Occasionally a pod will reach a column of falling water and be carried upward, re-entering the living ocean; the journey back to the plateau may take centuries. In the interim the surface remains empty except for the slow creep of bronze across stone, the patient rearrangement of liquid architecture, and the faint phosphorescence of photosynthetic tissues storing tomorrow’s warmth.

Nothing is built; nothing is used. The world simply continues its long, deliberate becoming—each element still half-emergent, still negotiating the terms of its own existence with the others.
