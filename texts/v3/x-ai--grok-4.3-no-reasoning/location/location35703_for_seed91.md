The Throat of St. Wisteria is not so much a place as it is a slow exhalation trapped between stone and leaf.  

Where the earth has cracked open, the bedrock has been swallowed—not by water or magma, but by an immense, slow-pressured mat of living tissue that closed over the wound like a scab. This mat is the only substrate; no soil remains. What appears at first glance as moss or lichen is, under closer inspection, entire continents of vascular tissue threaded through capillaries the width of a human wrist. The surface feels warm to the touch, a deceptive tropical fever that betrays the constant 1 °C air drifting overhead. The leaves, each the size of a banquet table, press together at their margins like the pages of a book left in rain. Between them, water beads perpetually form and migrate downward in rivulets so thin they glitter only when headlamps sweep across them. The water never pools. It is taken up again into the veins of the mat within minutes, so the entire layer breathes an almost imperceptible tide, expanding or contracting a hand-span over the course of a week.  

Above this living ceiling, the original mine shafts of the St. Wisteria company still intersect the stone, now repurposed rather than abandoned. Their steel rails are half-digested: steel oxidized to orange lace where it touched the tissue, yet the parallel wooden ties remain untouched, preserved by an unknown alkaloid exuded from the plant’s roots. The great extraction engines that once lifted ore cages are still in place, but their flywheels turn at one revolution per lunar cycle, driven not by steam but by the slow hydraulic creep of the surrounding growth. Every tooth on every gear has sprouted a single, hair-thin frond; the collective motion is imperceptible to human sight yet audible as a constant low-pressure sigh, the sound of millions of microscopic valves opening and closing in counter-rhythm to the breathing mat.  

Farther out, where the original galleries widen into an ancient limestone karst, the plant has developed vertical cantilevers—living buttresses whose cross-sections resemble the vaults of a cathedral seen from below. They grow downward at roughly a meter per decade, seeking the saline film that coats every wet stone surface. Each buttress is hollow; inside, capillaries pulse with a clear fluid that sometimes glows a dim bioluminescent green when disturbed. Miners once harvested this fluid for coolant; today, the remaining pumps run unattended, their electric motors throttled to a barely-turning idle by decades of layered insulation: first dust, then condensation, then new layers of waxy cuticle that the plant secretes across anything metallic. The resulting deposits resemble wax drippings frozen mid-fall, streaked with the ghosts of vanished tool marks.  

Sound in the Throat is paradoxical. The mat itself emits almost nothing, yet the resonance of every gallery concentrates the far-off industrial murmur until it becomes a single, crushing tone pitched just below the threshold of pain. It is the combined effect of flywheels, of water migrating through xylem, of limestone under stress from the inexorable outward pressure of root-tips that can exert several atmospheres. The noise never spikes; it never ceases. After half an hour the human ear begins to hallucinate counter-melodies—choirs, machinery idling down, the remembered ringing of picks against stone.  

Fauna are scarce because the mat is both food and trap. Its surface secretes a thin film of mucilage that immobilizes insects on contact; specialized pitchers, no larger than thimbles, form at every node and digest what they catch over months. The only larger creatures are blind, albino isopods the length of a finger that live inside the hollow buttresses, feeding on the constant rain of dead cuticle that flakes from the inner walls. These isopods in turn support an even rarer scavenger: a pale, slow-moving centipede whose mandibles have adapted to shear the metal lace of the rails, deriving trace minerals rather than nutrition.  

Scattered artifacts persist where the mat has not yet finished its patient reclamation. Near Ventilation Shaft 4, a miner’s helmet still hangs on its peg; the leather strap has vanished, replaced by a single vascular strand that now conducts a trickle of condensate from ceiling to floor. The helmet’s lamp lens is intact, but its reflector cup holds a miniature garden of liverworts that have evolved to tolerate constant cold and low light. A single boot-print in the hardened mucilage near Lift Station 2 has been preserved for decades; the print is now a negative space, the mat having grown around the vanished sole, leaving a perfect fossil of absence.  

Atmosphere moves in imperceptible currents generated by the temperature differential between the ever-warm mat and the cold limestone above. These currents carry a faint scent of chlorophyll and wet iron. In the deepest galleries, where the rock was quarried widest, the plant has formed translucent panels—single-cell-thick diaphragms stretched between living girders—that allow the distant industrial lamps to glow through like moons behind clouds. Their slow undulation creates the only visible movement in the Throat: the shifting of shadows that takes a quarter-hour to cross a human face.  

The rules here are patient ones. Metal rusts; organic matter mineralizes; the mat grows over every interruption at the rate of a glacier. Nothing is sudden. Even the occasional structural collapse—a wooden support finally yielding to the combined weight of tissue and condensate—is announced days in advance by a change in the continuous low-frequency drone, a subtle drop in pitch as tension redistributes through living architecture. To stand inside the Throat is to feel enclosed by two opposing clocks: one the geological patience of limestone, the other the biological patience of a single organism that has already outlasted the industry that birthed it and shows no sign of stopping.
