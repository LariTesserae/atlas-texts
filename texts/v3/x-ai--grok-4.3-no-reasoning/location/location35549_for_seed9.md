This is a single valley no more than two city blocks long—exactly the space between one factory’s back wall and the next, where the roofed-over gully between them has been left unroofed. Walls of patched brick and corrugated iron lean toward each other at slight angles, allowing condensation to drip in continuous sheets onto a floor paved with salvaged slate and steel plates. At the top of one wall a rain-gutter has been inverted to form a trough; thin rivulets run its length and tumble through weeping seams, keeping the air above the skin-damp level that registers 2.2 on the scale.

Because the valley is so narrow, every surface carries multiple functions. A ladder of riveted angle-iron climbs a quarter of the height, doubling as both maintenance catwalk and trellis for narrow-leafed vines whose leaves press against the metal so closely that they take its shape and keep it from rust. Below, rows of shallow crates have been bolted in staggered tiers to the brick; each crate is half-filled with nutrient gel kept warm by waste heat from steam-pipes threaded through the brick core. Roots trail from the crates, some dipping into the trickling gutter, some knitting into a mat that carpets the floor—green upon steel upon green. The vines never fully seal the gap; instead they form a translucent roof at a comfortable standing height, scattering the ordinary daylight into a dappled green glow that reaches only waist level.

The fauna here is few: transparent snails whose shells are no larger than thumbnail chips. They follow the condensation lines in slow, spiraling paths, leaving silvery trails that later become nutrient traces for the vines. Occasionally a single black beetle no bigger than a lentil drops from the canopy, lands on slate, and vanishes into a seam—its only presence a minute click, over before it begins.

Machines do not hum; they only sigh. A pair of piston-driven fans the size of dinner plates rotate once every fifteen seconds, timed by a pressure valve fed from a rooftop cistern. Their only noise is a soft chuff of air that stirs the leaves. Once an hour a solenoid clicks somewhere higher, releasing a measured spill of reclaimed condensate onto the uppermost crates; that sound, too, is absorbed by foliage before it can travel the length of the valley.

Everything is built, yet the built surface is still in the act of becoming geology. The slate and steel plates are already discolored in precise gradients: copper-green where condensation collects longest, rust-orange where the vines have not yet covered the metal completely. These colors migrate daily as the angle of ordinary daylight shifts and the drip lines adjust. In a generation the plates will look like river rock, and the seams between them like jointing in basalt; for now they are only half-finished strata, caught between salvage yard and sedimentary record.

The atmosphere is warm, moist, and still. Occasional updrafts from the factory vents rise along the brick, carrying a smell of warm iron and crushed chlorophyll that condenses again on the vines. From the open sky above, the occasional straight-edge of a roof joist intrudes, a reminder that the valley is a purposeful omission rather than an accident of terrain. The omission is deliberate, however, and the valley works according to its own slow clock: a metronome of drips, a breathing of fans, a patient layering of green over metal that will, if left undisturbed, eventually seal the last line of sight between the two factory roofs and turn this narrow space into a single closed, living vault.
