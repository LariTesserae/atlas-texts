A city-scale lattice of hardened sapwood is still knitting itself together across the shoulder of an enormous basin whose edges lie beyond sight. The ground is not earth but a single continuous floor of living timber, three to twenty meters thick, whose grain runs straight as rails yet bends in gentle arcs to follow long, slow curves of isostatic rebound. Between the planks, where new cambium is being laid, the wood is still pale and wet; where older tissue has finished curing, it has darkened to bronze and become resonant. There is no soil to speak of; everything that would have been topsoil has been metabolized into the living platform itself.

Over this platform rises an architecture that was never cut or mortared. Living trunks, each thirty or forty meters in circumference, rise straight as columns and then, at a height that keeps pace with the yearly thickening of the floor, they fork outward into horizontal boughs. These boughs meet their neighbors in knit-like unions, still green, still growing. From above, the canopy resembles a city grid whose streets have been roofed by the continuous, slow collision of crowns. Light filters through in long, soft shafts; the spectrum is full daylight, undiluted by haze, because the air column overhead is clean and dry.

Below the floor, roots have not descended into any abyss. Instead they form a second, inverted city of buttresses and tension cables that brace the living platform against the slight, continent-wide flexing of the basin. Cavities between roots—some large enough for a person to stand upright—are filled with spongy, pale mycelium that never fruits above ground. The mycelium respires quietly; the only sound it contributes is the occasional soft pop of a gas vesicle collapsing, a noise no louder than a knuckle cracking. Wind that passes over the lattice sometimes finds these cavities and sings a single sustained note through them, but the note is so low that most animals feel it as pressure rather than hear it as pitch.

Temperature stays within a narrow band of warmth that keeps the entire system metabolically active without ever tipping into heat stress. There are no seasons here, only the slow, millimeter-per-day advancement of new wood. Rain arrives as heavy, discrete cloudbursts once every several days; the drops are large and warm, and they are absorbed almost instantly by the living floor. Between rains the air carries the faint resinous brightness of terpenes, yet the overall impression is of olfactory quiet—scent present, but not insistent.

Fauna moves through the lattice at every scale. On the upper surfaces, herds of slow, six-legged browsers the size of tapirs graze the new growth that erupts wherever a crown fork has created a gap in the canopy; their teeth are specialized scrapers that remove only the outermost layer, stimulating the wood to thicken again. Inside the hollow trunks, colonies of social, flightless birds maintain continuous low whistles whose overlapping tones cancel into near-silence; the whistles serve as a living map of occupancy, each family keeping its own pitch so that the entire trunk complex functions as a single, self-updating census. Between these two strata—above and inside—swarms of tiny gliders with translucent wings ride the thermals trapped beneath the living roof, never descending to the open basin beyond. Their presence registers visually as a faint, constant scintillation, like dust in a sunbeam, rather than as individual animals.

Nothing here is manufactured. The lattice grows, self-repairs, and expands at its margins, where raw vascular cambium still meets the older, darker wood. Because the process is simultaneous at every scale, the city does not feel built so much as continuously becoming; one can stand inside a trunk whose interior surface is still tacky with fresh resin while, three hundred meters above, the same individual’s crown is already hosting the next generation of the bird colonies. The silence of the place is therefore not emptiness but the summed restraint of processes that are all occurring at once, each one loud enough on its own scale yet tuned, collectively, just below the threshold of human hearing.
