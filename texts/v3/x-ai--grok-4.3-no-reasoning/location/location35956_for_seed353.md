This is a world without water, where plant life has become the ground itself.

The substrate is living tissue—roots, rhizomes, xylem, cambium—layered and folded until every plane is verdant and warm to the touch. Each step is taken on plant. What look like walls are lignified trunks fused and planed into continuous surfaces, their grain still faintly moving beneath the cuticle. Where height is required, stems thicken into buttresses, press outward, and ossify into load-bearing buttresses; ceilings sag in woven mats of vine that continue to photosynthesize even when turned on their backs. The architecture never quite finishes; growth is the maintenance plan.

Temperature hovers near a constant 42 °C. The heat is metabolic, the slow oxidation of starches at planetary scale. Walls perspire a thin nectar that beads and evaporates, releasing a steam that never condenses because the entire volume sits above the dew point. Vapors travel along pressure gradients created by the perpetual expansion and contraction of living supports, moving like dry fog through the interior chambers.

No light enters from outside. The darkness is absolute, total, yet the space is not blind. The plants generate their own spectrum—chlorophyll fluorescence, faint bioluminescence in ripening tissues, and low-level chemiluminescence in the mycorrhizal networks that lace every surface. The glow is not enough for human vision; it is felt more as a directionality, an orientation toward sources of metabolic activity.

Sound travels poorly through the compacted cellulose; most movement produces only a muted creak of torsion in fiber bundles, a soft ticking of vascular valves opening and closing. Occasionally a whole trunk will resonate at a single low frequency when one of the industrial pumps hidden within its core changes state. The pumps themselves are biological—modified heartwood that contracts rhythmically, driving a sap-based hydraulic fluid through networks that terminate in articulated valves and pistons made of sclerified tissue. These pump-organs work in imperfect synchrony; at times their chorus forms a slow, three-beat pulse felt rather than heard.

There are no animals, no insects, no scavengers; decomposition has been internalized. When a section of plant architecture dies, neighboring tissues release enzymes that break it down into sugars, then reabsorb and re-form the space according to new load paths. The ecology is entirely self-consuming and self-replacing. The only waste product is heat.

Elevation is relative. The continuous mat of living matter rises and falls in long, shallow swells kilometers across. From one swell to another the difference may be only tens of meters, yet the absolute height above the planetary mantle is considerable. Because there is neither true rock nor true soil, the entire structure drifts, ever so slowly, across deeper strata of dead biomass compressed into something like lignite. The surface follows convection currents in this fossil plant-matter the way ice follows currents in a frozen sea.

Built and natural are indistinguishable. Every conduit, chamber, support, and seal is either still alive or was once alive and has been repurposed by still-living neighbors. There are factories, if one can apply the word to volumes where phloem is braided into cable runs and where entire chambers exist solely to concentrate starches into dense bricks that are then mechanically extruded and cooled. The bricks become new walls elsewhere; the process repeats on a cycle measured in weeks.

The turbulence is metabolic rather than climatic. Sudden surges of growth will occlude a corridor in a night; hours later, digestion will reopen it along a different axis. Regions can experience runaway lignification, becoming so dense that vascular flow stops and the entire zone must be broken down from the edges inward. Elsewhere, a sector may collapse into a soft parenchymatous foam that other tissues then colonize and stiffen. These events register as a change in the low-frequency pulse and as a shift in the already faint phosphorescence.

Over centuries the plateau continues its ancient settling. The average thickness of living tissue above the dead substrate increases by millimeters per year; simultaneously, the dead substrate beneath compacts under its own weight. Net elevation gain is negligible, but internal topology is never stable. The place is always mid-reconfiguration, its surfaces sliding past one another in slow vegetal tectonics.

It is hot, dark, packed, and without pause, yet it makes almost no sound louder than the slow flex of a billion fibers aligning themselves to new stresses.
