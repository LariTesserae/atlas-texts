The mountains here are not so much mountains as they are the last unfinished gestures of an older planet—an immense, shattered crust that never quite agreed on a final height.  Ridges of basalt still stand like ribs after an exhumation, but the spaces between them have already begun to surrender: long, fan-shaped aprons of talus have softened every saddle, and where water finds a joint it carves with the patience of centuries, leaving knife-slender canyons whose walls are no longer vertical but fluted and ribbed, the stone itself remembering each droplet that ever slid across it.

Water is not plentiful, yet it will not leave.  It arrives in sudden veils of cloud that press against the higher cliffs, condense, and slide downward in slow, glassy threads.  Some of these threads pause for decades in wide, shallow pans of polished rock, becoming mirror-still for a season, then vanish again in a single warm gust.  Others gather into thread-thin waterfalls that dissolve before they reach the valley floor, their spray caught and held by the wind so that the air itself is streaked with transient rainbows that last only as long as the eye can track them.

Vegetation answers the intermittent moisture with a stubborn exuberance.  Entire hanging gardens of moss and epiphytic orchid swing from the overhangs, their roots drinking only what the rock releases during the nightly dew.  On the broader ledges, forests of umbrella pine have learned to grow sideways, their trunks parallel to the slope, branches reaching outward for the brief hours when clouds graze the cliff.  The needles are a blue so dark they verge on black; when the wind accelerates, the sound they make is less a rustle than a low metallic shimmer.

Temperature hovers in the narrow band between warmth and fever.  During the day the dark stone stores heat until the air above each ledge shimmers; at night the same stone exhales it in slow, sweet-smelling plumes that smell of warm resin and iron.  Nothing freezes here; nothing quite cools.  The fauna is correspondingly sparse: ghost-colored rock goats with spiral horns that click softly against stone, and a single species of transparent-winged moth whose larvae drink only the condensation that gathers in the deep pits left by vanished waterfalls.  When a goat steps, the click is answered, seconds later, by another from a neighboring cliff—communication by echo that can travel two valleys before it fades.

The scale is deceptive.  Stand at the edge of any given ledge and the next ridge looks close enough to touch; walk toward it and hours disappear into the intervening gulf of air.  Distances are measured not in steps but in the number of cloud-streaks that must be crossed, and each streak carries its own small weather: a pocket of hail the size of millet, a curtain of warm mist, a momentary bloom of midges that live and die within the single thermal they were born inside.

Erosion has reached its final grammar.  Every surface that could be planed has been planed; every cliff that could be rounded has been rounded.  What remains are shapes so equilibrated they no longer suggest struggle, only the memory of it.  The rare human traces follow the same principle: low, dry-stone bothies tucked into wind-scoured hollows, each one no larger than a single body curled around a hearth of flat basalt plates.  They are empty now, but the soot on their ceilings records generations of watchers who waited out storms whose strength we can no longer measure.

Technology here never passed beyond the hand-axe and the fire-drill.  The axes are of the same dark basalt, knapped until their edges catch light like watered silk; the drills are of fire-hardened pine root bound with tendon.  The people who left them also left a single, indecipherable mark: spirals pecked into the cliff faces at intervals that do not correspond to any obvious measurement, yet whose spacing is too regular to be chance.  The spirals are deepest where the rock is softest from ancient seepage; they are shallow or absent where the stone remains hard.

Sound refuses to behave like an ordinary medium.  The constant turbulence—up-drafts, down-drafts, lateral shears—turns the entire massif into an enormous aeolian instrument.  Every fissure, every hollow, every needle cluster adds its own pitch; when the wind strengthens, the chords stack until the air itself seems to ring inside the skull.  At peak intensity the sound becomes tactile: molars vibrate, breath is stolen, vision briefly doubles.  Then, without warning, a downdraft may swallow an entire harmonic layer, leaving a comparative hush so sudden that ears ring with its absence.

The strangeness of the place is not in any single feature but in the way contradictory states are forced to coexist: extreme elevation and persistent warmth, lush growth and near-emptiness, deafening sound and vast empty intervals.  Time moves differently here; a single afternoon can contain the slow accretion of centuries in the deepening of a single water-pan, followed by the sudden erasure of that same pan when an updraft lifts it into mist.  The mountains are still becoming, yet they are already finished; the contradiction is what keeps them from ever settling into stillness.
