The high, open emptiness holds itself like a negative space carved from colder air. The ridge you stand on is not truly finished; the rock is young, its fractures still thin as hairline cracks, still warm in a way rock shouldn't be. Beneath the thin crust of permafrost, water remembers the pressure it was under. It seeps upward through those new seams, bead by bead, until it meets the air and stops, a film of ice already forming at each exit. Where the seep is stronger, the water emerges as a faint weeping line, crystallizing mid-fall into a lace of needles. These threads catch the weak light that arrives sideways, filtered first through high, slow-moving ice clouds and then through the perpetual haze that the industrial works exhale far below.

The slope is long enough to make the horizon itself feel provisional. Below, the flats are empty for miles except where the ground has begun to fold into new basins. Those depressions are not yet lakes, only the memory of where water will pool once the thaw arrives in the short summer. For now they are only darker patches in the frost, the color of a bruise under skin. From this distance the basins look like places waiting for their own reflections.

The industry that operates here is not housed. Its engines move themselves. Long, segmented rigs crawl along the valley floor on articulated treads, each segment a mobile drill head or sensor array. Their lights are single, yellow-white points that do not flicker with any living rhythm; they simply remain. When the rigs pause to core a new sample, their motors produce a low, continuous note that travels up the rock in waves you can feel through your boots. The note is almost below hearing, yet it seems to set the loose stones into a sympathetic tremor. Between these intervals the valley returns to the sound of its own becoming: the occasional splinter of a forming ice lens, the dry click of frost crystals rearranging themselves, the soft, glassy squeak when two plates of new permafrost press against each other.

Nothing grows tall. The vegetation is a low map of endurance: black lichen that stains the newest rock like spilled ink, and between the stones, cushions of gray-green moss that have learned to flatten themselves against the wind. In the depressions where the rigs have not yet passed, small melt-pools form for a few hours each day. There, in water that is never more than a hand’s depth, a single-celled film spreads across the surface, turning the light a faint, shifting emerald when the rigs’ lamps sweep across it. The film is the only color that answers the sky.

Animals move through this place as if the machines are weather. Small, white-furred lemmings emerge from tunnels no wider than a finger; their feet make no sound on the frost. Larger, six-limbed shapes cross the open ground at a steady, unhurried pace, their long necks low so that their breath does not show above the rocks. Their hides carry the same gray-white of the scree; only the faint, rhythmic swing of their tails marks them against the background. Above them, the air is crossed by small, darting flyers whose wings are translucent enough to show the dim light through them. They feed on the condensation that beads along the weeping rock seams, their tongues extended like threads. When they lift away, the only evidence they were there is a momentary clearing in the frost.

Nothing here has been built by human hands, yet the machines continue their work because the rock itself is the resource. Each new fracture plane is logged, each thin vein of water mapped. The rigs do not stop for the creatures; the creatures do not alter their paths. The only rule that governs them both is the cold’s steady subtraction of heat, and the water’s patient refusal to remain still beneath the surface.
