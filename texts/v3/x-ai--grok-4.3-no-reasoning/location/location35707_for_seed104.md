There is no horizon.

In its place there stretches an endless roof of vapor the color of bruised plums, so heavy with water it has begun to fold and drape like fabric, each fold becoming a slow-moving squall that drags its belly along the hidden ocean below. Rain does not fall; it simply materializes wherever the folds touch. The droplets are large, perfectly round, and possess a slight delay before they continue their descent, as though the air remembers them a moment too long. They never splash. They are reabsorbed into the body of the sea the instant they meet its surface, leaving rings of concentric ripples that drift outward, overlap, and never fade.

Below the unbroken mirror of the water, at a depth where sunlight should have died, the seafloor rises in a chain of high, submarine plateaus. The plateaus are built of the same material as the water itself—compressed into lattices of salt and coral-like mineral, yet still transparent. The effect is that the ocean floor looks like an immense, green-tinted cathedral window lying flat; you can peer through it and see older, darker strata miles beneath. On those strata rest the silhouettes of continents that have not seen air for so long that their mountains have been worn into low, rounded domes whose highest points almost touch the underside of the upper plateaus.

Where the plateaus approach one another, living bridges of spongy, translucent tissue span the gaps. These bridges are formed by the pressure of the water alone; when a column of liquid moves between them, it carries strands of cytoplasm that knit the two sides together for a time, then dissolve and reform elsewhere. Whole archipelagos of these bridges can be seen sliding in silence across the plateaus, rearranging the geography at the rate of continents drifting.

Inside the water, schools of minute, crystalline organisms hang motionless. Each individual is no larger than a grain of rice, yet together they number in trillions and refract the dim, sourceless light into faint, shifting glyphs that hover for seconds before dispersing. These glyphs are not language; they are merely the mathematics of light passing through synchronized motion. When the glyphs coincide with the slow ripples on the surface, the water records them as standing waves and preserves them for hours.

The temperature is that of blood. It never changes; there is nowhere cooler to sink toward, no thermocline. Because nothing is ever lost, the sea simply becomes more itself wherever heat would otherwise dissipate. Microscopic filaments drift through the warm medium, each one a single cell long enough to span hundreds of meters. When two filaments touch, their membranes open, exchange cytoplasm, and then seal again, leaving the joined pair fractionally thicker. Over millennia this produces cables the width of tree trunks that now hang in gentle arcs between the plateaus, carrying nothing except the memory of every union that lengthened them.

Occasionally, something larger moves.

Far below the transparent floor, a single dark oval the size of a city rises and falls with a period of decades. Its skin is the same density as the surrounding water, so it never displaces volume, only pressure. The pressure change is felt kilometers away as a single, slow inhalation felt inside the ribs of any diver who happens to be present. No one has ever seen its mouth. The oval simply thins in one place, becomes momentarily transparent, and whatever water it needs flows inward; when it thickens again the water is gone. The process leaves behind a spherical volume of absolute clarity that rises like a bubble until it merges with the surface layer. Inside that bubble the faint glyphs described earlier become momentarily visible as complete sentences, but they vanish before any eye can finish reading.

Scattered across the upper plateaus lie small, almost accidental artifacts: stone disks no thicker than a fingernail, perfectly circular, with a hole at the center the exact diameter of a single droplet. They are not tools; each disk is the place where a droplet was held still for an entire geological age until the mineral around it grew inward and froze the droplet’s memory into a lens. If you hold one to your eye and look upward, you see the vapor roof not as it is, but as it will be after another ten million years of folding. The vision lasts only until the disk warms to the temperature of your skin; then the future collapses back into present rain.

The silence is not absence. It is the sound of water that has never learned to move faster than the speed of its own viscosity. The only detectable motion is the almost imperceptible contraction of the living bridges and the faint, internal ticking of the crystalline glyphs as they re-align. Everything else waits, and the waiting itself has weight.
