There are no streets here, only the remembered idea of streets. Walls of petrified xylem rise twenty feet, their surfaces still faintly sticky where sap has not yet fossilized; the sap now grows fresh membranes of cellulose and lignin daily, so the walls themselves breathe. Overhead, leaves the size of canoe sails knit into a continuous canopy whose living edges brush and re-brush in ceaseless agreement. Each leaf is translucent with its own internal water channels; when rain comes the channels swell, turning the canopy into a glowing green lantern that drips on a five-second lag behind the sky.  

Between the walls, the floor is not earth but interlaced root plates, each root two hand-spans thick, laid down centuries ago in slow spirals. The plates have fused into a rigid membrane that nonetheless flexes when a heavy body steps across it; the entire hamlet-sized grove rings like the drumhead of a living drum. Beneath the membrane lies open air—interstitial caverns where condensation drips into slow, vertical rivulets that collect into mirror-black pools. The pools breathe; each breath is a faint mist of motile spores that rise and settle like dust.  

Across the pools, bioluminescent fungi the size of chandeliers hang from arching prop roots. Their light is silver and intermittent, pulsing once every seven heartbeats. Insects the length of forearms ride the pulses, laying eggs in synchronized rows; each egg hatches within minutes into a larva that consumes the parent on the spot, then drops to the pool surface and swims downward in a spiral that never closes. The swimming leaves a trail of momentary phosphorescence that lingers for three full seconds before the water forgets it ever existed.  

Raindrops arrive not from clouds but from a living fog that forms at knee height, thickens until visibility is measured in finger-widths, then condenses in sudden, local showers that last exactly one minute and twenty seconds. The shower intervals are staggered across different neighborhoods of the grove, so the overall soundscape is a rolling thunder of water on leaves, roots, and pools, never quite simultaneous. The thunder is so continuous that the arrival of silence (once every forty-nine showers) registers as a physical shock, like the floor suddenly stopping its slow drumbeat.  

At the center of this space stands a single, hollow baobab the height of a three-story building. Its bark is ridged with vertical slits that open and close on a four-minute cycle. When open, the slits inhale warm air from the canopy; when closed, they force that air down through a throat of xylem into the underground chambers, pressurizing the root-plate floor until it domes upward by the thickness of a page. The air that emerges from the slits carries the smell of crushed chlorophyll and ozone. Inside the trunk is a spiral stair of compacted pollen so fine it is almost liquid; any footfall releases a brief cloud of motile grains that seed the next generation of wall-plants wherever they drift.  

Fauna here moves in slow, deliberate densities. Large herbivores shaped like tortoises with fronded carapaces graze the leaf edges, their chewing the low-frequency component of the permanent roar. Smaller, translucent creatures the size of thumbs drift through the canopy on silk parachutes no thicker than spiderweb; they do not eat but instead fuse with any leaf they touch, adding a new translucent layer that thickens the roof by microns. Predators are auditory: a vine that grows in loose coils along the root-plate floor listens for the beat-pattern of footsteps, then tightens around the ankle with a sound like a wet rope snapping taut. The vine releases its prey only after the creature’s pulse has dropped below a threshold the vine can no longer track; the released foot is left numb for hours.  

Light reaches the space already filtered through four layers of living sail-leaves, so it arrives as shifting mosaics of jade and bronze, never brighter than late-afternoon sun yet never dim enough to read shadow as night. The light is accompanied by a secondary illumination: the walls themselves conduct faint electrical gradients between leaves and root-plates, producing a continuous, almost inaudible crackle that feels like the static that precedes lightning, though no bolt ever forms.  

Because the vegetation is substrate, every surface is potential architecture. When a seed lodges in a crack, it grows not outward but inward, hollowing a new chamber whose walls are its own body. Over decades the chamber may become a room, its ceiling a lattice of petioles that filter conversation into green syllables. The rooms connect in no fixed map; a person may enter one chamber at dawn and exit another by dusk having traveled no farther than the length of their own body.  

The only rule that holds across every scale is that nothing here remains still long enough to become a single image. The walls are growing while you watch; the pools are listening; the fog is rehearsing its next shower; the sound is remembering how silence felt, and is already preparing to forget it again.
