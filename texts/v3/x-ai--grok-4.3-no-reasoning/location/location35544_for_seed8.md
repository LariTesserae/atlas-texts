A wide, low plain laps against a veil of slow, stone-silted rivers that scarcely know where they end.  It is an endless sheet of warm, ankle-deep water—clear in some places, tea-brown in others—sliding in thread-thin channels between acres of polished gravel and long runners of dark clay.  Every evening the stones sweat; you can hear the first bead slide, then the drip, then the hundred others that follow, until the sound becomes one soft, unending rain that never falls from the sky.  The air is full of that moisture, kept close by low dunes of ancient coral-colored sandstone that have worn down to hips and ribs so gentle they barely cast a shadow at noon.

Between the water-ribs, the ground is pale, wind-packed silt—almost dust, but too cohesive to rise—and the silt itself is veined with ghostly white salt lines left by each retreating sheet.  Where the water lingers longest, it is dotted with sparse cushions of halophytic reeds whose stems are no thicker than thread, yet their roots knit whole islands no larger than a person’s stride.  Nothing taller grows here.  The reeds tremble at the edge of hearing, a dry whisper that is constantly interrupted by louder, slower sounds: the slap of a wide, patient fin against shallow water; the low, drawn-out creak of tortoiseshell carapaces grinding together when the herd of basin tortoises heaves itself across a bar of gravel; the faint popping of methane rising from buried leaf-muck; and every so often, the deeper bell-tone of a water-filled limestone cavity collapsing a millimeter at a time beneath the surface.

Nothing moves quickly.  The tortoises travel the breadth of a single meander in a season, leaving behind furrows whose walls crumble in slow avalanches of silt no louder than breath.  Tiny crabs the color of wet slate migrate only at the turning of the moon-tides, their march a muted clatter like distant rain on canvas; once they reach a feeding bed they remain for months, shifting the placement of a single claw by the width of a grain each day.

The sun is the ordinary golden disc of an ordinary afternoon, yet the air is so saturated that its light arrives in two layers—an upper brilliance that glances off the water, and a lower, coppery haze diffused by drifting pollen and microscopic evaporite crystals.  Together they make everything appear slightly doubled: reed against reed, tortoise shell against its own reflection, the low sandstone ribs against their mirrored undersides in the standing pools.

Over decades the channels shift by only the span of an arm.  Over centuries they return to nearly the same beds, having worn the rock beneath them into shallow, polished troughs whose sides are inscribed with concentric wave-rings, the signature of each long-vanished flood.  Nothing is built, nothing is carried here except the dissolved salts and the empty carapaces of drowned insects, and the land itself seems content with that economy.  The plain waits, the water waits, the tortoises wait, and the sound of waiting is continuous and loud and slow.
