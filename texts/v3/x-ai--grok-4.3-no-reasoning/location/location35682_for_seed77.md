The Slopeforms begin as faint corrugations in the planetary crust, too shallow to be recognized as slopes at all, mere swales where the heat index of the shell is fractionally different. Over the next hundred million years they will become an unbroken chain of linear basins half the circumference of a supercontinent, but at present they exist only as a potentiality. Between each pair of future ridges lies an interval of nothing—nothing except stone that is not yet stone. It is a paste of compacted, glassy grains that has never known pressure sufficient to force the grains to interlock; it will later be called sandstone, but today it is still “the substance that will accept the coming of water.”

Because the light is ordinary, shadows fall as they would anywhere. The difference is that the shadows keep changing shape though nothing is moving across the light source. Each shadow is the line where yesterday’s moisture, trapped beneath a paper-thin film of reprecipitated silica, evaporates a micrometer deeper into the substrate. The line migrates; the film fractures, reseals, fractures again. The cycle is measured in centuries. Nothing watches the movement, yet the movement continues.

Rain arrives infrequently, and never as a sheet. Instead, a single cell of vapor condenses directly onto the warm paste, forming an ephemeral droplet that sits for perhaps ten local days before the same heat that condensed it sucks it downward. The droplet does not roll; the surface tension is higher than the local gradient, so the droplet flattens and spreads until it is a skin one molecule thick. That skin sinks, pulling a filament of dissolved ions with it. Where the filament stops, a hairline fracture appears—the first articulation of what will become a joint set. The joint widens by the width of a single grain each time a new droplet follows the same route. In aggregate the process is called “erosion,” though no material has yet been removed; only rearranged at the scale of individual crystals.

Vegetation appears as microscopic crusts—no thicker than a scattering of pollen—whenever the evaporating droplet leaves behind a smear of nitrate salts. The crust is ochre on one face, verdigris on the other, and it survives only until the next droplet arrives. Then the salts redissolve and the crust disperses into colloidal suspension. The cycle is so rapid relative to the lithification of the substrate that no generation of the crust ever overlaps another; each is an independent experiment whose duration is measured in droplet lifetimes.

Because nothing consumes, nothing accumulates. The silence is therefore absolute: not the silence of absence but the silence of non-interaction. When a crust disperses, the sound is a single molecular click that never propagates beyond its own lattice. When the droplet evaporates, the vapor rises without turbulence because the air column above the Slopeforms has not yet learned to convect at continental scale. The only repeatable acoustic event is the soft, almost inaudible “tck” of a joint edge popping free after a century of ion migration—an event so rare that, across the entire future basin system, it occurs only a few times per local year.

The temperature remains high enough that the paste never cools below the threshold at which the glassy grains would recrystallize; they remain forever on the verge of becoming quartz. In this liminal state the material can accept any new environmental parameter without yet recording it as permanent structure. The Slopeforms therefore hold their shape as potentiality rather than fact, a vast unfinished diagram drawn in heat and moisture across which nothing yet moves except the diagrams themselves.
