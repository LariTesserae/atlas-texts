The world is a tower of iron and glass that rises five hundred fathoms from the sea-bed to the surface, its ribs braced by kelp forests thick as cables. The whole structure is open to the water, yet the water never touches it; between each girder hangs a membrane of still, unbroken surface tension, as though the sea itself has chosen to leave one corridor dry. The tower’s interior is a vertical commons, mile on mile of galleries and catwalks, all constructed from the same slow-weathered steel that has been painted, welded, replaced, and painted again for centuries until the latest coat is indistinguishable from the first. Overhead, an unceasing rain of micro-bubbles rises from every weld seam and valve, giving the illusion of daylight inside a glass column lit by its own slow boiling. The bubbles pass through shaft windows and emerge as silver coins on the upper surface, bursting with soft, industrial plops that mark the hours more precisely than any clock.

Every gallery floor is a grille of cross-braced beams over empty drop. Nets of hemp and polymer droop between them like slung hammocks; here the tower-dwellers raise kelp-bladders the size of melons, their skins taut with pressurized oxygen. The bladders rasp against the mesh when the vertical currents shift, producing a low, continuous thunder like distant rolling stock. The residents move between levels by way of counter-weighted lifts—steel baskets that descend only when a second basket rises—each descent accompanied by the long, ringing complaint of chains that have worn their own grooves into the pulleys. When two baskets pass one another, the rush of displaced water inside the membrane column adds a brief kettledrum beat, then fades.

Fauna is plentiful but never crowded. Flat-finned drifters the color of oxidized bronze glide in lazy figure-eights between decks; their wingspans equal a man’s reach, and they feed by brushing against the bladders, sipping leaked oxygen through fine maxillary combs. Below the tenth gallery, schools of glass-needle shrimp orbit in hypnotic spirals, their bodies catching the bubble-light and throwing miniature rainbows against the girders. At the very bottom, where the tower’s foot flares into a broad flange of riveted plates, colossal oysters the size of freight pallets have cemented themselves in concentric rings. Their shells are scored with factory stamps older than the present polity; each time a current surges, the valves crack open a hand-span and a single resonant click travels up the entire column, a bass note felt more than heard.

Light arrives refracted from the sky-membrane above, filtered through the constant rain of bubbles until it falls as a cool, even noon on every level. No surface glare exists; instead, the light has the mild opal quality of sunlight through canvas. Because the tower is narrower at its base than at its crown (an intentional design to shed lateral shear), shadows pool at the lower landings and must be swept away every watch by mirror-polished scoops mounted on slow-turning vanes. The scoops tick and hush, tick and hush, the loudest single sound in the lower reaches, louder even than the boilers.

The boilers themselves hang on external outriggers halfway down the tower—six iron eggs nested in a cradle of counter-rotating propellers. Each boiler burns compressed kelp-pellets to drive a single shared crankshaft that snakes upward inside a sealed oil-filled sleeve. From this shaft, secondary belts and worm gears branch off at every landing to run hoists, bellows, and the oxygen compressors that keep the vertical membranes inflated. When the crankshaft flexes under load, it produces a moaning note that climbs in pitch until the operator feathers the intake valves and the tone drops back to a contented growl. The operators, one per boiler, live in barrel-shaped cupolas riveted to the outriggers; they communicate with the galleries by bell-code and can feel every change in load through their bare feet on the deck plates.

Erosion, paradoxically, has not softened the structure but refined it. Every surface that once carried sharp edges has been burnished by the passage of water until the steel gleams like dark mirror. Rivets are flush; welds are invisible beneath centuries of overlapping coats of oxide and lacquer. Where the occasional stray current does reach an interior beam, it finds no purchase; the water simply slides along the surface tension membrane and returns to the open sea, leaving the architecture untouched. The only true sediment lies far below, on the sea floor itself: a thin, pale blanket of forgotten iron filings and oyster-spat that the tower’s shadow keeps from drifting.

Because the entire environment is built, natural rock exists nowhere inside the membranes. Instead, the “bedrock” of daily life consists of spare plates, spare chains, and spare valves stored in numbered racks along every gallery. When something wears through, the residents unbolt the failed piece and replace it with its identical twin; the old piece is lowered on a separate winch into the waiting jaws of the colossal oysters, whose nacre slowly incorporates it into new shell. In this manner the tower is both consumed and rebuilt at once, an engine whose moving parts are also its foundations.

The loudest moment occurs at shift-change. Klaxons mounted on the crown of the tower fire compressed air into the sea surface; the membrane above shivers, and a single perfect ring of whitecaps races outward for half a mile before collapsing. Below, every boiler answers with a steam-horn tuned a half-tone apart; the combined chord rolls down the galleries, setting every hanging net swaying. Then the chord fades, and the ordinary thunder of bubbles, chains, and ticking scoops resumes, each sound nested inside the next like the nested membranes themselves.
