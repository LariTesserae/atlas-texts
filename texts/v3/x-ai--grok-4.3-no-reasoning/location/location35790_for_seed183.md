A cliff-shelf world split between bare silicate bones and rivers of slow vapor.  

The ground is a single, unbroken plate of glassy feldspar that has been folded, not fractured, into an immense descending ramp: ten vertical miles of pure, faintly milky stone, its lower edge dissolving into cloud. Because the rock is slightly conductive, every droplet that lands on it beads, rolls, and evaporates again; the air stays saturated at wrist-height, forming a knee-deep fog that brightens under the open sky. The light is unrelenting—solar glare ricochets from every facet—so shadows exist only where fog or the occasional stone pillar intercepts them. Temperature stays just cool enough that condensation lingers on exposed faces before it is drawn back into the rolling white layer.

There are no plants, no animals, no spores, yet the shelf is never still. Every few hundred heartbeats the stone itself vibrates—low, bone-deep tremors that throw new beads of water into the air. The fog catches these vibrations and sings back, a layered roar that rises and falls like surf heard through bone. Above this constant thunder, smaller, percussive cracks wander across the plateau as micro-fractures reseal; the pattern of sound maps the hidden lattice of crystal grains.  

Scattered across the same plain are low domes of fused gravel, each the size of a single large room, their surfaces pocked by older impact craters now worn flush. Inside each dome the air is fractionally drier; the fog thins to a silver film over the floor, revealing primitive pictographs scratched into the mineral with stone tools. Some of the marks repeat the tremor frequencies, suggesting the domes once served as resonators or counting chambers. Their doors—if they can be called doors—are simple gaps left during cooling, just wide enough for one person to slip through. No metal remains, only the suggestion of hand-shaped sockets where wooden levers once rested.

The ramp continues downward in steps whose risers are perfectly polished by the ceaseless condensation cycle; each new step is a darker shade of glass, almost black where depth increases. Farther down, the vapor layer becomes continuous and opaque, hiding whatever lies beneath. From the edge of one intermediate step you can watch the next lower shelf appear and disappear as the fog convects—slow updrafts peel it aside, then let it fold back like a turning page.  

Seen from above, the entire landscape is a tilted lens of brightness, white mist rimmed by the hard silver line of the horizon. The scale is regional: the eye cannot reach either end of the ramp before it disappears into the haze of its own vapor, yet each visible dome or pillar anchors the space with unmistakable presence. Nothing living moves, yet every surface participates in motion—water cycling, crystal lattice flexing, sound propagating—under a sky too bright to look at directly and too loud to hear anything softer than its own weather.
