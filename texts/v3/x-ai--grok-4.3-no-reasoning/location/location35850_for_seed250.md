Beneath the surface, the water is not a single body but an arrangement of skins—membrane-thin layers of pressure that stack and slide against one another like sheets of clear gel. Visibility travels several hundred metres before the next interface bends the light again, turning every direction into a corridor that only pretends to go forward.  

Between these skins hang dense lattices of invisible grain: every cubic kilometre is an ordered suspension of micro-lenses whose curvature continually adjusts. Each lens is only a few microns across, yet the net refraction is planetary. When enough of them swing into alignment, a slow pulse of light converges to a focal point kilometres away. The focus can last an hour or flare into a brief star; when it fades, the lattice has already begun to rearrange itself for the next alignment. The water itself feels almost empty—pressure gradients are so gentle that a body can drift for days without brushing another particle—yet the lenses give the medium an optical weight that heavier fluids could not possess.

The “seafloor” is not rock but a scaffolding still in the act of becoming. Curved spars of super-dense, glass-black condensate grow outward at millimetres per year, cooled by the lenses and shaped by the same interference pattern that bends the light. Because their growth speed is calibrated to the same clock that drives the lenses, new spars arrive already fretted with diffraction grooves that will, centuries later, become the next generation of optics. Nothing yet has a finished shape; everything is half-specification, half-material, and the distinction is expressed only by how sharply light curves around the newest edge.

Occasionally a whole sector of scaffolding collapses under its own weight. The collapse is silent to any human ear, but the lattice registers it as an enormous, subsonic chord that travels through every membrane at once. The wave speeds through the lenses as a sudden phase shift; distant clusters flare briefly into geometric rainbows, then realign as if nothing had happened. The fallen spars leave behind a mist of smaller fragments that drift upward, already beginning to condense again into new ribs. In this sense the entire edifice is a self-repairing hologram cast in physical matter.

There is no current in the conventional sense; the only motion is the lenses themselves, rotating or reorienting at rates no faster than continental drift, yet the cumulative effect is a permanent, directionless turbulence. Because the medium is otherwise empty, the turbulence manifests as optical noise rather than visible turbulence—ripples that appear only when two beams of daylight, refracted from separate depths, cross and cancel. At those crossing-points the water momentarily darkens into perfect shadow, a moving eclipse the size of an inland sea, before the lenses slide onward and restore the beam.

The temperature is held just above the threshold where the scaffolding condensate would lose its refractive index; the gradient is stable enough that no convection cells form. Instead, heat is bled away in the form of ultra-low-frequency phonons that the lenses occasionally trap and re-emit as coherent acoustic pulses—hence the persistent, thunderous chord that underpins all other movement.  

Nothing organic grows here. No silt settles. No current carries nutrients. The only “weather” is the self-organising choreography of the micro-lenses and the slow, relentless extrusion of the scaffolding, an architecture that will never be completed because its purpose is to remain unfinished.
