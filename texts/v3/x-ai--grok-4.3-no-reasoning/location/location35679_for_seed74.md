You walk into a single city block that has been entirely overtaken by plant architecture.  It is not overgrown, nor reclaimed—it is *designed* as living infrastructure.  Every wall, every stair, every roof is laminated wood, woven root, and phloem, each layer still metabolically alive.  The material feels like dense, dark cork crossed with the grain of oak: a little warm to the touch, slightly yielding, patterned with the fine pale rings of seasonal growth.

Raindrops fall intermittently from a sky that is not quite visible above the canopy.  The droplets are captured by the highest living tiles—thick, cupped leaves that act as shingling.  They funnel water through translucent xylem gutters into bell-shaped cistern leaves that swell like green udders at every intersection.  When these are full, the water migrates downward by capillary pull into secondary reservoir nodes that keep the whole block evenly moist, never soaked.  You hear the sequence: faint patter, a minute of drip-and-drain, then a slow hydraulic sigh as the leaves relax again.  These cycles repeat every quarter-hour, keeping the cool air at a steady misted 14 °C.

The city block itself is small by human standards—one long courtyard the size of a municipal plaza, enclosed by four four-story walls whose outer façades are thick vertical plant tissue punctuated by service balconies.  Inside, the plaza floor is a single undulating plate of vascular tissue, veined with slower-flowing channels that transport nutrients between buildings.  The veins glow a dull bioluminescent chartreuse at night, but during the day they are simply slightly darker green, visible as wet calligraphy across the plaza.  Every fifty meters a “lamp post” rises: a petiole several meters thick whose terminal bud has hardened into an upward-facing lens.  The lenses focus normal daylight into diffuse pools of illumination beneath, enough to read by, but not enough to feel like artificial light.

There are few gaps between the structures—space is not empty but *inhabited by absence*.  Where two buildings approach, their roots braid together into openwork bridges that allow a person to walk from façade to façade without stepping onto the plaza floor.  Between these bridges the air is empty for two or three human heights, then resumes with another platform of foliage.  From below, looking up, the block resembles a three-dimensional chessboard whose squares are leaves and whose spaces are clear.  Wind rarely penetrates this lattice, so sound carries as small, dry echoes: a footstep on living wood, a single water droplet striking a cistern.

The living buildings contain functional interiors.  Rooms are lined with a secondary skin of velvety, water-absorbing moss that doubles as acoustic insulation.  Ventilation occurs via pressure differentials between stomata that open on opposite façades; when outside humidity spikes, the stomata constrict and the entire block quietly exhales warmer interior air through roof vents shaped like calla lilies.  The exhale is the loudest regular sound: a five-second susurration at the top of each quarter-hour cycle, just after the cisterns refill.

Fauna is limited to a single species of translucent, nickel-sized planthopper that feeds on the sugary overflow from the cisterns.  Each hop carries them only a few centimeters; they cross the open spaces of the plaza like drifting ash.  No birds, no pollinators, no vertebrates—only the patient click of their mouth-parts against the leaf cuticle when the light is strongest.

The neighborhood does not appear to have any central machinery.  Instead, the urban plant fabric itself is the industry.  The same tissue that forms walls can lignify on command, hardening to tool-grade density within minutes when a specific frequency of vibration—perhaps a footstep pattern or a spoken phrase—travels through the root network.  In places you see the after-images of past hardening events: chair legs still locked in mid-growth, or a doorway whose frame froze half-open centuries ago when the block decided the aperture should remain that size.  These decisions are slow enough that residents simply live with them until the next softening cycle frees the wood to reconfigure.

The entire block sits at the bottom of a shallow basin, so the ground-water table is only two meters below the living floor.  During prolonged dry spells the city lowers fine root hairs through the plaza veins, touches the water table, and pumps upward; when rains resume, excess is expelled back into the aquifer.  The city therefore participates in a miniature water cycle whose tempo is measured in hours rather than seasons.

At the cardinal corners, four spiral staircases ascend.  Their steps are living phloem pads that swell or shrink depending on load; heavier foot traffic makes the next tread slightly thicker before your weight arrives.  The sensation is of walking on a surface that already knows you are coming.  From the roof terraces you can see the next identical block three hundred meters away, separated by a band of exposed mineral soil that acts as a firebreak and acoustic moat.  Between the two living islands there is nothing but stillness—no weeds, no shrubs, no transition zone—only cool air and the memory of water moving underground.
