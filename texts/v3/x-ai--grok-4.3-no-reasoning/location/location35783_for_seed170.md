The place begins as heat made solid: a single, unbroken plain of whitish-rose crystal that stretches outward until the eye gives up trying to name its limit. The crystal is salt, but not ordinary table salt; it is a mixture of evaporated sodium, potassium, and rarer halides whose long-dried lakes have left a fractured pavement several fathoms deep. Fractures run in every direction, each one a capillary-wide groove packed with microscopic grains that catch and scatter the light so the surface appears to flicker without moving. Where the fractures intersect they create shallow, saucer-shaped basins; wind crossing them produces a dry, metallic rasp that never stops.

Overhead the sky is cloudless, but there is no true sky, only a high, stationary ceiling of faintly glowing mineral mist. The mist sits at a definite altitude; below it the air is thick, still, and searing; above it the atmosphere thins to vacuum in the space of a few hundred meters. Sunlight therefore arrives twice: once directly from a small, fierce white star already past noon, and again as a diffuse sheet of refracted light from the mist layer. The double illumination gives every surface two shadows—one crisp, one milky.

Nothing here grows or was ever grown. In their place, immense crystalline lattices rise from the plain like trees, yet they are not trees; they are ordered fields of electromagnetic activity that have condensed into mineral form. Each “tree” is a vertical column of hexagonal quartz-like prisms three to eight meters across at the base, tapering upward for hundreds of meters until they dissolve into the glowing mist. Their facets are alive with internal electricity—slow rivers of charge that circulate in pulses lasting tens of minutes. When a pulse reaches the surface, the prisms emit a brief ultraviolet glow visible to some eyes and felt by others as a fine, internal itch. Between pulses the lattices are silent, but the waiting is the loudest sound in the landscape.

Fauna here is not a population but a condition. The atmosphere itself is alive with numberless micro-organisms whose bodies are single protein chains folded around specks of metallic dust. Individually they are invisible; collectively they form slow-moving densities that look like heat haze until they cross a line of sunlight and ignite into brief gold filaments. The filaments last only seconds, after which the organisms re-form lower down. They move not by flight or crawling but by successive phase changes: portions of the colony evaporate, drift, and re-condense a few centimeters away. Over years these movements carve microscopic channels in the salt crust, contributing to the established erosion that has already leveled the plain.

Temperature never drops. The crystal plain absorbs heat so efficiently that the ground temperature hovers within a degree of the air temperature at all times; the lattice columns, being better conductors, act as heat sinks and remain marginally cooler, drawing faint convection currents downward along their flanks. The result is a permanent boundary layer a hand-span thick in which air is almost still. Farther up, slow horizontal winds slide between the columns at walking speed, carrying the dry rasp of salt grains and the faint ozone tang of the electrical pulses.

Nothing here is ever manufactured, yet the landscape obeys an industrial logic. The electromagnetic lattices behave like very large, very slow capacitors. Their charge cycles are synchronized across distances of several kilometers; observers standing on the plain can feel, through the bones of their feet, when a distant column initiates its pulse because the timing wave arrives as a minute tremor traveling through the crystal substrate. The pulses are not random: they form long, overlapping rhythms whose periods range from nineteen minutes to three days. Over centuries these rhythms have etched a geography of interference patterns—slightly raised ridges where waves constructively meet, shallow troughs where they cancel—visible now as faint, rectilinear scars running for kilometers in mathematically precise directions.

Light behaves strangely near the columns. Refraction through the internal charge fields bends incoming rays by a few degrees; thus a straight walk between two columns can bring the horizon into momentary view as though through a lens, then hide it again. At certain nodes where three or more columns stand at equal distances, the refractions overlap into stable mirages: inverted images of the plain itself, hung in mid-air, showing the same columns upside-down and lit from beneath. These images persist for hours and fade only when the charge distribution beneath them shifts enough to break the symmetry.

No water exists at any scale. The salt plain is anhydrous; the mist ceiling is a suspension of metallic aerosols, never condensing. Yet moisture is not missed, because chemistry here is mediated by charge rather than solvent. Trace metals dissolved inside the crystal matrix migrate along potential gradients the way ions migrate in water; the slow transport has, over eons, produced local enrichments that now appear as hair-thin veins of iridescent sulfide threading the otherwise uniform rose-white floor.

Time passes as heat redistribution. Each lattice column stores a measurable fraction of the daily solar input, then returns it to the plain at nightfall through infrared radiation. Because the columns are staggered in height and spacing, their collective thermal release creates a faint, continent-sized downdraft whose edge can be felt as a single, almost imperceptible drop in skin temperature when the observer crosses from one drainage cell to the next. The downdraft is steady, patient, and loud only in comparison to absolute silence; it registers to human ears as a subsonic pressure that builds behind the eyes and disappears the moment attention shifts.

At the farthest distances the plain appears perfectly flat, but closer inspection reveals that its level is an optical average. In reality the crystal is warped into extremely low-amplitude waves whose wavelength is measured in tens of kilometers. These waves are the fossil record of an earlier, now-dormant tectonic adjustment that occurred when the original mineral sea first crystallized. Their crests coincide with zones of denser lattice growth; their troughs are slightly more eroded, collecting loose hexagonal flakes that the slow wind heaps into centimeter-high drifts. The flakes are identical in composition to the substrate yet ring, when struck, at a different frequency; thus a traveler walking across a trough hears, beneath the constant metallic rasp, a secondary, bell-like undertone produced each time a flake is overturned.

Sound travels here as both vibration and light. When a lattice column completes its charge cycle, the released ultraviolet burst is accompanied by a minute thermal expansion. The expansion launches an acoustic wave whose frequency is too low to hear but strong enough to set loose salt flakes dancing. Observers with instruments record the event as a barely audible 7-hertz throb lasting four or five seconds; without instruments it registers only as a faint internal tremor at the base of the sternum. Overlapping cycles from many columns produce an infrasonic chord that modulates slowly across the hours, a music perceptible only as changes in bodily tension.

Because nothing is built and nothing decays, the place maintains its own permanence through the balance of these cycles. The electromagnetic trees neither grow nor erode; they simply translate sunlight into patterned electricity and back again, a closed transaction measured in centuries rather than seasons. The micro-organisms likewise persist by never quite dying: each evaporation event is matched by an equal condensation elsewhere, so the total living mass remains statistically constant. The salt crust itself is replenished grain-by-grain as deeper brines, still liquid kilometers below, migrate upward along micro-fractures and flash-dry at the surface, adding new facets to the existing pattern.

In such a landscape the only motion that can be called rapid is the ultraviolet pulse itself, traveling the length of a column in less than a second. Everything else—the drift of organism densities, the migration of metallic ions, the slow reorientation of distant mirages—unfolds on timescales that make human attention feel like lightning. The eye therefore learns to see not change but arrangement: the precise spacing of columns, the exact angle at which refracted light grazes a facet, the mathematical regularity with which infrasonic chords resolve into new chords. The landscape does not invite exploration so much as prolonged standing still, until the observer’s own pulse rate aligns with the nineteen-minute rhythm of the nearest lattice and the surrounding emptiness begins to feel, paradoxically, densely inhabited.
