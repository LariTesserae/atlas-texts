A city that never cooled hangs at the edge of a broken continental shelf, itself lifted long ago into rainless altitudes by slow tectonic folding. The streets, though paved in the same volcanic glass that once flowed here in sheets, are now fissured and veined with condensed water; each fissure acts like a capillary, drawing vapor from the humid layer that permanently clings a few hundred meters above the plain below. In daylight the glass is dark—nearly black—but every crack glimmers pale blue when it holds its daily ration of moisture, as though veins of captured sky were running through the city’s skin.

The towers themselves are not stacked from separate blocks; each is a single extrusion, a tower of cooling rinds where successive lava flows froze against one another like the growth rings of a mineral tree. Their surfaces are slick from centuries of condensation and from the gentle, ceaseless rain that falls only at night—mist that forms on the undersides of high silicate clouds and drips for three hours before dawn, then ceases. The droplets are almost hot; when they strike the glass they bead instantly into pellets and roll away, leaving momentary comet-trails of steam.

Because the city sits above the altitude at which most water would linger, every flat surface has been engineered—by whatever hands built this place—to tilt just enough that condensation will sheet rather than pool. The runnels converge at the tower bases into thin, luminous canals that follow the avenues, each canal no wider than a forearm. They empty at the city’s rim into a hanging delta of glass threads, each thread no thicker than a rope; the water follows these threads in slow, silent falls to the marsh three hundred meters below, where it rejoins the planetary cycle.

The only movement comes from the city’s own machines—low, cylindrical engines set flush into the glass at intersections. At irregular intervals one of them exhales a single breath of super-heated vapor; the puff momentarily thickens the ever-present mist, then vanishes. Each exhalation is followed by a soft metallic tick, the only sound louder than the faint ticking of cooling glass. No birds, no insects, no voices: the silence is architectural, not accidental.

Between the towers the avenues remain almost empty; the scale of the city is human only in the width of its walkways, yet nothing walks them. Occasionally a lone maintenance crawler—six articulated limbs, each tipped with suction glass—descends a tower face to re-seal a fracture with fresh molten silicate drawn from some subterranean reservoir. When the crawler passes, its feet leave momentary prints that cool into shallow, mirror-like depressions; by the next night-cycle those depressions have filled again with their private lenses of water.

The light here is perpetual dusk. High, thin clouds of volcanic dust perpetually veil the sun, diffusing it into a uniform ochre glow that never quite reaches the strength of true daylight and never quite relinquishes the city to night. In this amber gloom the glass reflects almost nothing, so the city does not double itself; only the faint gleam of the water-veins gives any indication of depth.

Beneath every tower a hollow resonance chamber remains, left by the withdrawal of magma after the outer rind cooled. These chambers still hold residual heat. Occasionally the heat finds a new fissure and pushes a sudden sigh of steam up through a sidewalk grate; for an instant the nearest canal bubbles, then the pressure equalizes and the city settles again. The temperature never drops below the threshold that keeps the glass pliant; if it did, the whole conurbation would ring like struck crystal. Instead it stays just warm enough to remain mute.

Far below, where the marsh meets the coastal plain, the condensed water finally cools and slows, spreading into broad, shallow sheets that mirror the city’s silhouette in fractured ribbons of light. Up here the reflection is lost; the city sees only its own breath, its own ceaseless condensation, its own slow, industrial respiration.
