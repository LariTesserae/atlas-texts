Beneath the city of mirrors the ground never dries. The avenues lie across a drowned bedrock that sweats its own aquifers; streets are paved with quarried water-stone whose joints bead constantly, so that every footfall leaves a wet print that evaporates before the next is taken. From those pavements rise black ribs of still-living coral, some shaped by masons into low walls, some left free to thrust straight through cellar ceilings as though the city were merely a reef that kept its roof on. Between the ribs the groundwater pools in glass-clear sheets, reflecting ceiling-lamps that are themselves only clusters of slow-swimming photophores—creatures the size of clenched fists whose skins seal against any air pocket, glowing a soft lunar white until shadows from above drift across them and they darken in unison.

Water travels here without rivers. When rain reaches the surface vents, it falls only a body-length before it is drunk again by felt-thick patches of sky-moss that blanket every horizontal plane. The moss drinks until its fronds turn translucent, then releases the surplus through hair-thin capillaries that lace the undersides of every street. The release is so fine it cannot be heard; the only audible sign is a faint ticking when condensation gathers and lets go in a single droplet onto slate, the droplet swallowed by a waiting patch before another forms. All other life has learned the same measure. The city’s great cellar gardens exhale mist in measured clouds that rise three hand-widths, swirl once around each blossom, then sink again, threading between ankles like low, obedient weather.

No surface wind penetrates. Instead a steady updraft travels through vertical shafts bored by root-tongues of the coral; each shaft widens into bell-shaped listening chambers where pressure equalizes. When a person steps into such a chamber the air shifts, cool as river stone, and the coral walls answer with a subsonic sigh that trembles the small bones behind the ears. Those chambers open onto wider plazas whose paving stones are drilled with thousands of fist-sized holes—old breathing pores of the coral itself. Through every hole rises a steady ribbon of droplets that burst at the surface into spheres held by surface tension long enough to mirror the ceiling before they burst and return to the stone. The rain, inside-out.

All movement is slow enough to register as stillness until seen from a slightly later vantage. Ferns rooted in the pores unfold one pinna per hour, their motion visible only as a gradual brightening when they catch the steady light that filters through high slits; the slits themselves are roof vents filled with panes of isinglass harvested from the inner valves of enormous freshwater clams. The panes are never perfectly clean: thin films of algae stain them rose or sulfur, so that the light below travels in shifting veils that wash across the coral ribs and then vanish, an hour’s worth of miniature dawn and dusk repeated throughout the day.

Fauna negotiates the same economy of space. In the films of water between paving stones live minute copepods whose synchronized leaps produce a sound like a single plucked string whenever a larger predator’s shadow crosses them. The predators are spindle-bodied water-striders whose tarsi rest on four separate films at once; when they strike, the surface tension collapses in a perfect circle and the copepods scatter downward into the next, safer layer. Amphibious mice with wet velvet fur slip between the legs of benches carved from petrified sponge, pausing to drink from leaf-bowls left by the moss. Their teeth leave crescent marks in the bowls that refill within minutes, the rim thickening again until the mark is only a faint discoloration and then gone.

Because the city cannot expand outward—bedrock walls rise sheer on every side—the streets stack. Staircases are living coral risers whose treads are planed flat by centuries of bare feet; the risers themselves secrete fresh calcium carbonate, erasing the wear each night so that the edge always remains knife-sharp. Between stair flights hang bridges of braided root, tense as harp strings; when a foot lands the bridge exhales a spore cloud fine as steam. The spores drift until they find a patch of damp stone already occupied, then burst against an invisible membrane and fall back as dew, food returned to the parent organism.

Sound travels only as far as the next layer of water film. A dropped coin rings once, the note absorbed before echo can form; a voice, if raised, condenses on the nearest cool surface and slides downward in rivulets shaped like the words themselves until the letters lose their edges and become indistinguishable moisture. The only persistent voice belongs to the tide doors—massive valves of nacre that open twice daily when hydrostatic pressure reaches a certain threshold. Each opening produces a single low note that rolls the length of the avenue, shaking loose droplets from every hanging root. After the note fades, the valves iris shut with a soft inhalation, and the city holds its breath until the next pressure cycle begins.

Above the tide doors, on every available vertical face, the coral grows thin garden ledges where bladderworts dangle their traps. Each trap is a clear bladder the size of a thumbnail; when a copepod or infant water-strider brushes the trigger hairs the bladder inverts in one-fifteenth of a second, too fast to see but slow enough for the city’s time to register. The bladders refill at the same pace the city exhales, so that the gardens seem to pulse in sympathy with the larger breathing shafts. No citizen trims these gardens; the plants belong to the same metabolism that built the streets.

At intervals the paving opens into wide cisterns whose bottoms are invisible, the water kept at constant level by the breathing shafts. Schools of glassfish hang motionless at the precise depth where light still penetrates, their flanks aligned with the surface so that they appear to be holes in the water itself. When a cistern lid is lifted, the fish fold their bodies in unison and vanish into the pores of the stone; seconds later the surface is featureless again, only the reflected ceiling remaining.

The city never floods and never empties. Every drop that arrives is already promised to a waiting surface, every surface already crowded with the next waiting drop. The equilibrium is visible in the meniscus that stands proud of every crack, trembling but never spilling, held by nothing more than the city’s refusal to offer space for anything else.
