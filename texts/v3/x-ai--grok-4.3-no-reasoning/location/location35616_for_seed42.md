A green cathedral under a roof of water

The city is not on an island; it is the submerged rib of an extinct seamount that has been grown over, not merely occupied. A single colossal vine—its girth the width of a municipal hall—corkscrews from the basalt floor up through a shaft of limpid water. Along that vine the inhabitants have built walkways, terraces, and arched bridges, all woven from aerial roots that the people keep pruned into load-bearing beams and slender lattices. These root-scaffolds darken to charcoal where they meet the sun-filtering surface, then pale again in deeper shade, giving the impression of a city half-painted in dusk.

Coolness seeps down from the thermocline above, keeping water temperature just above the range that would favor coral bleaching. Sunlight arrives filtered to the quality of late-afternoon skylight, a soft greenish wash that slides across every surface every time the canopy overhead sways. Plant tissue replaces mortar and glass; when a terrace needs reinforcement, elders sing a resinous secretion from a specialized cultivar of the central vine, and the structure “knits” the new material into itself within a single lunar cycle.

Because the population is low, the living masonry is rarely crowded. Individual dwellings appear as seed-pod chambers about the size of small barges, suspended along the vine at irregular heights. Between them hang long, empty distances crossed only by drifting mats of free-floating duckweed—horizontal gardens tended by schools of slow herbivorous tetras that graze without hurry. The duckweed patches are kept from coalescing into a choking mat by a steady downdraft of cold, oxygen-rich water that the seamount’s porous core exhales in patient sighs every second or third day.

Sound here travels differently than in open ocean. Every time one of the great seed-pods swells with the next generation of fruit, the pod walls resonate like wooden drums; the throb carries through the root system and is answered, hours later, by a similar pulse from the next nearest pod several hundred body-lengths away. Meanwhile, the surface above is occasionally scored by squalls. Each rain needle punches the water in needle-thin lines of bubbles that descend, pop, and leave momentary motes of turbulence that the duckweed rearranges around. After a storm the city seems newly rinsed; leaves glisten, and the resin on the load-bearing roots smells faintly of camphor.

Fauna passes through rather than resides. Once a month a parade of comb-jellies the size of wheelbarrows drifts along the vine’s lee side, each pulsing a slow electric violet; the people mark their passage on strips of bark as one would a lunar calendar. Between these events the only constant companions are translucent shrimp that farm microscopic algae gardens on the undersides of terraces. They click their claws against the root walls when disturbed, adding a dry, percussive layer to the lower frequencies of the pod-drums.

No metal exists here; knives are shark-tooth, chisels are fire-hardened timber, ropes are braided from discarded vascular fibers. The only “technology” is selective pruning: elders know by touch which aerial root will thicken into a beam and which will stay supple enough for lashing. This knowledge is stored in communal song-lines rather than ledgers, and is updated each generation by comparing how the city sounded in yesterday’s water against how it sounds after the newest terraces have set.

The city’s boundary is the reach of its own roots. Beyond that perimeter the seamount slopes into colder, darker water where the vines never took hold; the people speak of it as the place where sound forgets itself. Inside the perimeter, every surface is either growing or waiting to grow, and nothing is allowed to rot except the deliberate sacrifice of old, hollowed pods. Those are left in place, becoming nurseries for infant tetras and, later, hollow bell-rooms where adolescents practice the resonance songs that keep the greater vine healthy.

On the scale of a single afternoon nothing appears to move, yet nothing is static. Each root’s lignified grain lengthens by a few cell-widths, the water column above the canopy exchanges a thin skin of rain for evaporated salt, and the resonance pitch of every seed-pod drops by the barest quarter-tone. Over decades these microshifts amount to the slow cartography of a living municipality that has forgotten, or never learned, the difference between architecture and organism.
