The world is called only by the absence of water: Naqheth. It stretches continent-wide, a single continuous substrate grown, poured, spun, or extruded across millions of square kilometers until even tectonic memory was overwritten. Nothing remembers an original surface. Every meter has been re-cast at least once in materials engineered for permanence; the oldest strata now lie a hundred meters beneath transparent shells, faceted crystalline slabs that weather cannot scratch and whose composition is measured in picometers rather than molecules.

Seen from orbit, Naqheth appears as a field of opalescent polygons, each between two and six kilometers across. Their edges are barely perceptible seams of darker luminescence, the meeting lines where different material generations fused. The entire plane is warm to the touch—hovering near 34 °C—because every facet is threaded with micro-tubules that ferry heat from distant fusion wells. At this temperature the air immediately above the surface becomes a thin, perpetually rising layer that never cools enough to fall, creating a motionless atmospheric lens that magnifies the sun into an unblinking eye overhead.

Rain never falls; instead, what little moisture the builders once imported is now sequestered as microscopic droplets held inside sealed polycrystalline nodules that stud the plane like seed pearls. Occasionally a nodule will rupture under stress, releasing a single silver bead that rolls, evaporates almost instantly, and leaves behind a transient scent of iron and ozone before the surface reseals itself. The scent lingers only seconds, then vanishes into the general quiet.

Sound is attenuated to a low tonal whisper. When a human steps barefoot across a seam, the contact produces a note between middle C and D that travels thirty meters through the material before being absorbed. Conversation carries no farther than arm’s length; breath, clothing rustle, and the faint interior hum of circulating heat are the only audible phenomena. Fauna therefore consists only of what can survive without hearing—mostly microscopic symbionts that live in the tubules. They manifest externally as shifting iridescence where colonies thicken, resembling oil on water but without any liquid present.

Vegetation is reduced to engineered fronds no taller than a thumbnail that grow outward from the vertices of each polygonal plate. Their chlorophyll is replaced by a metallic-organic lattice that converts the perpetual glare into minute electrical currents; they quiver at frequencies too high for human hearing, maintaining structural tension so the plates never drift out of true. At dusk, when the ambient light drops from painful to merely luminous, the fronds briefly fluoresce cyan before powering down for the night cycle.

Erosion, in the conventional sense, has long finished; the surface instead experiences “phase smoothing,” a controlled atomic rearrangement that erases micro-fractures over centuries. The overall topography is almost flat, yet its micro-topography is labyrinthine: capillary ridges and inverted dimples only a few atoms deep that steer thermal currents the way continental divides steer rivers. The eye perceives only a single calm plane, but instruments record continuous surface migration at rates of one micrometer per decade.

Built density is total, yet habitation remains unseen. Dwelling spaces exist as negative geometries: vast pressurized voids whose walls are lined with phased-array transducers that can become perfectly reflective or perfectly absorptive at will. From within a void, one can look upward through a ceiling that is simultaneously transparent and opaque—light and radiation pass inward while waste heat is radiated outward along different spectra. The effect is an inverted sky in which stars glimmer only when an occupant wills them to.

Tech permeates everything. Walking across Naqheth, a person’s mass slightly deforms the uppermost crystal lattice; the deformation propagates downward through stacked layers until it reaches a depth where computational nodes read the signature, calculate the traveler’s trajectory, and pre-rotate the next polygon by a fraction of a degree to meet the expected footfall. If the traveler pauses too long, the lattice beneath their soles will gently extrude a low seat shaped to their posture. When they leave, the seat withdraws without trace.

Fauna, already rare, is further constrained by the silence. The only macroscopic organisms are mirror-skinned flyers that exist as flat sheets of nanoscale foil, propelled by Casimir-gradient sails. They glide centimeters above the surface, feeding on the sparse electrical trickle from the metallic fronds. Each flyer is effectively a single enormous molecule whose edges are programmable; on the rare occasions when two meet, they merge or split along programmable seams, exchanging fragments of memory encoded in the lattice itself. Their movement is slower than breath; only the minute change in reflected sunlight betrays them. When one lands on a traveler’s shoulder, it weighs less than a sigh and stays motionless for hours, performing micro-adjustments in albedo to maintain the body’s temperature within 0.01 °C.

At night the plane itself becomes faintly alive. Embedded photonic corridors carry light from the distant fusion wells across the continent; the light leaks upward in slow, cycling patterns that resemble the breathing of an enormous animal. The polygons modulate brightness in sequences too gradual for human attention to register. Over years, these rhythms encode weather information for the autonomous systems managing thermal equilibrium, but to any short-lived observer the effect is simply a vast, soft shimmer that never quite repeats. The light is still bright enough to read by, yet it feels like an after-image rather than illumination.

Naqheth’s scale asserts itself only in duration. A single lifetime is insufficient to walk from one edge of a polygon to another if one honors the intended path, which spirals inward along the surface’s computational grain. Travelers who ignore the grain and march straight report arriving exactly where they began after an interval whose length cannot be reconciled with the measured distance; the lattice beneath them simply lengthened the metric while they moved. No one knows whether the world is expanding outward, whether the traveler contracts, or whether the distinction has lost all meaning inside a substrate built to outlast both.
