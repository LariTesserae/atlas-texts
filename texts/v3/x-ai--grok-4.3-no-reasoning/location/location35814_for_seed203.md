The Hollowcanopy is a continent-wide cavity beneath a crystalline crust whose ceiling, at most places two hundred meters thick, is not stone at all but a single, living lattice of whitewood—millions of interlaced xylem towers whose uppermost layer has mineralised into something like glass. Light that touches the surface is split into spectra no human retina can hold; inside the Hollowcanopy the result is an opalescent glare that seems to arrive from every direction at once, bleaching even the smallest shadow into a pearlescent haze.

Because the ceiling is biomass, water never falls as rain; instead it condenses inside the xylem and trickles downward along filaments finer than hair. The filaments braid themselves into slow-moving rivers suspended in mid-air—transparent, gelid braids that undulate with the breathing of the lattice above. When a braid bursts against a fracture in the crust, the released droplets drift rather than fall, held in tension by electrostatic fields generated by living cellulose. The result is a drizzle that hovers at chest height for minutes before re-coalescing elsewhere.

Temperature is a paradox: the air itself is cold enough to set breath into tiny crystalline lattices, yet every surface radiates a faint metabolic warmth. Where the two meet, hoarfrost forms in lacework patterns that melt within minutes and re-form elsewhere, etching temporary maps across leaves as wide as plazas.

The floor, ten kilometres beneath the crust, is not rock but an older generation of the same whitewood, long petrified into a pale, pumice-like stone that still remembers its cellular architecture. The floor is therefore porous on a microscopic scale; every few hundred metres a vertical shaft opens into deeper, lightless strata, but these shafts are quickly colonised by downward-growing root-fibres that seal them again within decades. Thus the Hollowcanopy is not truly subterranean; it is an enormous, living sponge forever in the process of devouring and regurgitating its own foundation.

Vegetation is not ground cover; it is the ground, the walls, the sky. At every scale larger than a human stride, the only solid material is plant tissue differentiated into tensile cables, photosynthetic diaphragms, and storage organs the size of cathedrals. Some diaphragms are so thin they function as sails, trembling with the slow convection currents that the cold air and metabolic heat set in motion. Others are thick enough to walk upon, yet they are not passive; they undulate on a roughly nineteen-minute cycle, pumping interstitial fluids and thereby driving the entire weather of the cavity. When two such diaphragms brush, they discharge stored bioelectricity in silent white arcs that briefly illuminate the suspended droplets like lightning behind frosted glass.

The emptiness is not absence but interval. Between any two rooted masses lies a gulf of fifty or sixty metres; distances that on the surface would be crowded with shrubs or undergrowth are here kept void so that the diaphragms can flex without tearing. The rare fauna have adapted to this sparsity. The only animals larger than an insect are slow-moving, gas-filled cephalophores that drift between diaphragms, grazing on airborne spores. Their bodies are almost entirely transparent; only the faint shimmer of metabolising cellulose betrays their passage. When one dies, its collapse seeds a temporary garden of fast-growing vines that race across the empty space, then wither once the nutrients are exhausted, restoring the interval.

Built presence is even rarer. The only artifacts are occasional “knots”—dense, hand-worked nodules of plant tissue where earlier generations learned to persuade the whitewood to form load-bearing vaults or rainwater cisterns. These knots are centuries old, yet still alive; their surfaces are covered in tool marks that have healed into smooth callus. Primitive copper awls and adzes lie wedged inside healed scars, the metal slowly transmuted by the plant’s chemistry into organometallic crystals that the living matrix uses for electrical signalling.

Sound is spare. The diaphragms flex with a low, sub-audible thrum; condensation droplets occasionally strike one another with the tick of glass beads. Only during a “surge”—a chaotic reconfiguration that overtakes the cavity every eleven local days—does the soundscape become violent. Entire braided rivers reverse direction in minutes; diaphragms buckle, tear, and re-knit; the whitewood ceiling sings in frequencies that travel through bone rather than air. After each surge the cavity is subtly rearranged—new shafts opened, old knots abandoned, fresh intervals established. The inhabitants (if any still remain) must navigate a geography whose landmarks rearrange themselves while they sleep.

Scale warps perception. Because weather is generated internally, clouds can form, drift, and dissipate within a single chamber no larger than a town square; yet the same circulatory engine powers continent-spanning fronts that travel for weeks between opposite walls. Standing in one such chamber, a visitor can watch a miniature cyclone spin a hundred metres overhead while, simultaneously, a pressure wave rolls through distant diaphragms like the swell of an invisible sea. The effect is of being inside an organism whose respiration is measured in centuries yet whose heartbeat arrives every nineteen minutes.

The only constant is the glare—bright, sourceless, and faintly violet at the edges—under which every surface seems both newly grown and already fossilised, every interval both empty and pregnant with the next rearrangement.
