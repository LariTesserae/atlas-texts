The place exists as a single, uninterrupted mass of warm, iron-rich stone that has been carved and sealed into a single cathedral of occupied matter. Its ceiling and walls and floor are not layered surfaces; they are a single, dense, old carving in which every linear measure that a hand can span has been occupied by some working or relief. The mass is scaled to the span of three arm-lengths in any direction—large enough to walk through, too narrow to get lost in, so close on all sides that a person standing in the middle can touch the opposite surfaces by turning the body, not by stretching.

No air moves inside it. The atmosphere itself is a mineral vapor that has thickened over millennia until it behaves like an extremely slow liquid; it is warm to the lungs, carrying no moisture, carrying only the faint metallic bite that the stone itself exhales. Nothing grows; no spores, no films of oxidation, no salts blooming on surfaces. The stone is finished—polished in some tracts, left in precise facet cuts in others—so that the light, which enters only through narrow slits cut high on the long axis, strikes flat faces and rebounds at shallow angles. The illumination is always the same dim amber; the slits are aligned to a source whose angle never changes, and the color of the stone never shifts under it.

Sound fills every interval between stone and skin. The noise is produced by the slow, ceaseless motion of countless tiny countersunk weights—disks of the same iron-bearing rock, each the diameter of a thumbnail, each suspended on a short axle whose bearing has been carved directly into the walls. Because every surface is occupied, the axles sit flush; the disks turn under their own weight in staggered sequence, each rotation striking the next disk in line. The contact is soft stone on soft stone, yet because the chambers are packed, the sum is a continuous, low, grinding roar that fills the bone cavities and makes speech impossible. The sequence of strikes never accelerates or slows; it has been locked for so long that the pattern is indistinguishable from a single tone.

No channels, no ducts, no open volumes exist that are not also stone. Every alcove that might have been a niche has been infilled with additional carving until the recess disappears. Even the entry threshold, which was once an open arch, is now a maze of offset stone teeth that interlock so tightly that a person entering must turn sideways and slide through one body-width at a time; once through, the threshold is visually closed behind them. The seams between teeth do not admit light or sound except at the same fixed amber pitch.

No trace remains of any earlier use. No sockets for tools, no wear hollows where feet once rested, no soot on ceilings, no pigment in grooves. The carving is so complete that later hands would have had nowhere to begin work. The last available volume is the space immediately around a standing body; even there, the stone has been incised with shallow negative arcs whose spacing matches the breadth of shoulders and hips, so that a person standing still finds the surfaces almost touching them on all sides. When they move, the motion simply follows the next negative arc already provided; there is no improvisation left.

The temperature never varies. The warmth is stored in the mass of the iron-rich stone itself and leaks outward so slowly that the difference across any measured span is smaller than can be felt by skin. The same stability governs the sound: the turning disks produce exactly the same chord because nothing has altered their mass, their friction, or their angle of contact since the carving was finished. The chamber holds itself in an equilibrium that began before the species that made it developed writing and has continued without modification into the present.

To leave, a visitor reverses the sequence of the entry teeth; to remain is to become another counted interval between strikes, another surface already shaped for occupancy. The place has no other state.
