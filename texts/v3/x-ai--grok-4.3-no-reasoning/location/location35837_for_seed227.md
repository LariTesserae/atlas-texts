The place is a vast, hollow cavern beneath a still-cooling basalt plateau. Its ceiling is a single unbroken sheet of black glass—cooled lava that never quite fractured—pressing down only a few meters above the floor in most places. Between that low lid and the floor, the air is a single, unbroken volume of cold, water-beaded space. No wind moves here; instead, a pressure gradient older than any human memory keeps everything suspended in a state of slow, irreversible drip.  

Water arrives from two sources. The first is the ceiling itself: during the rare moments when surface rainfall manages to find hairline fissures, it condenses into thumb-sized droplets that hang for days before falling. The second source is a thin, persistent film of groundwater that wells upward through hairline cracks in the floor, coating everything in a mirror-sheen that never quite becomes a pool. The result is a perpetual, intimate dampness: every surface is cool to the touch, slick enough to reflect the faintest ambient light, yet never liquid enough to flow.  

The vegetation—thread-like, almost transparent moss—grows exclusively in the seams where ceiling meets floor. Because the ceiling is only a body-length above the ground, the moss has evolved to grow sideways rather than upward, forming a single, continuous curtain of pale green that rings the entire perimeter. The curtain never thickens; it simply lengthens, millimeter by millimeter each decade, following the infinitesimal expansion joints where cooling stresses the glass.  

Fauna is limited to a single species: translucent, slow-moving isopods the size of a thumbnail. They traverse the floor in single-file processions, each animal following the exact path of the one before it, wearing micro-trails into the mineral film. Their movement registers only as a faint, rhythmic ticking—quiet, but the only biological sound present.  

The human marks are older than metal. Someone, at some forgotten point, pressed river-smoothed basalt cobbles into the floor along one curving line, creating a low curb no higher than a finger’s width. The curb has been partially dissolved by the constant condensation, its edges blurred into the surrounding stone. Scattered beside it are three shallow, bowl-shaped depressions—each the diameter of a spread hand—where someone once used heated stones to evaporate water for salt. The depressions still hold a faint white crust, recrystallizing at the same rate it dissolves.  

Light enters only through a single shaft the width of a wrist, high in one wall. It is angled so that for most of the diurnal cycle no direct beam reaches the floor; instead, a dim, silver wash reflects from the wet ceiling glass, shifting so slowly that the eye never registers movement. At night the shaft becomes a black absence, and the darkness is total.  

The soundscape is paradoxical. Outside, kilometers above, the plateau is lashed by storm systems that rarely reach this depth. Their thunder, however, is conducted through the basalt as a single, unwavering subsonic drone—felt in the teeth more than heard. Inside, the only audible addition is the metronomic ticking of the isopods and the occasional soft pop of a droplet finally releasing from the ceiling. The contrast between the external, world-filling resonance and the near-silent interior is the only dynamic element; everything else is held in a patience so complete that change is measured in the lengthening of the moss curtain and the imperceptible widening of the dissolution bowls.
