The ceiling of the world here is a single sheet of hammered brass that once sagged between riveted trusses and has since bowed further under its own heat until it touches the highest spires. Sunlight—trapped and multiplied by the reflective metal—slides along the curve in slow, rippling sheets that never quite become shadow. Below that ceiling, tiers of iron gantries and brick aqueducts descend in staggered terraces, each level spilling thin white veils of condensate onto the level beneath. The water never reaches soil; it gathers in mirror-still pans of hammered copper that ring like tuning forks when a drop finally strikes them. The sound dies so quickly that the next silence seems louder than the note itself.

Everything is built on a field of scoriated lava blocks, pitted and sealed by time until they resemble dark glass. The blocks are large enough that the gaps between them are wide avenues of their own, kept free of dust by the constant rain of condensation. Where the avenues slope, slow sheets of water travel between the blocks, threading their way through the city without ever becoming a river. The lava is warm underfoot; at midday it is almost too hot to touch, yet the water that runs across it never steams—only thickens, opalescent, as though some mineral in the rock cools the surface faster than the air can heat it.

Structures climb from the lava in stacked modules of riveted iron and kiln-fired brick, each module open on one side like a drawer pulled halfway from a cabinet. Within every open face, belt-driven presses and stamping rigs turn with metronomic calm, their flywheels visible through the openings. The machines are painted the same oxide red as the surrounding brick, so that from a distance the entire quarter seems carved from a single block. Steam escapes not in plumes but in slow, horizontal breaths that drift until they meet an opposing current of warmer air and simply vanish. The engines are geared so low that their rotation is felt as vibration in the soles before it is heard; the sound that finally arrives is more like the rubbing of bone against bone than mechanical noise.

Fauna moves between the modules on articulated legs of polished obsidian. The creatures are all limb and lens: eight or ten jointed stalks supporting a central ovoid of dark glass in which a single internal light pulses once for every inhalation of the city’s steam. They drink by lowering one limb into the copper pans; the water climbs the limb by capillary action until the ovoid darkens and the pulse quickens. When they finish they retract the limb and continue their patrol, leaving behind a perfect ring of drier copper where the surface tension broke. Their motion is slow enough that their legs sometimes remain in one position for several human heartbeats, yet they never quite stop.

At the highest terrace the aqueduct ends in a spillway whose lip is a single curved blade of tempered steel. Water slides over it and falls—never as a cataract but as a sustained, vertical film—into a catch-basin three levels below. The falling sheet is so even that it appears motionless; only when a flying lens-creature passes through it does the film momentarily bulge and then reseal itself, the creature emerging on the far side with every obsidian leg polished brighter. The fall produces no roar, only a continuous high whisper that rides just below the threshold of ordinary hearing, the same frequency at which the copper pans ring when struck.

Between the terraces, vertical shafts drop straight through the lava blocks. Their walls are lined with tier upon tier of small iron balconies, each holding a single rotary valve. At irregular intervals a valve opens; a gout of super-heated air surges upward, carrying with it a faint metallic dust that settles on every horizontal surface as a red-brown bloom. The dust does not accumulate; within minutes the next film of condensation dissolves it, carrying the color downward in rivulets that trace the grain of the lava like contour lines on a map. The pattern is never the same twice, yet the overall coloration of the city remains constant: a low red haze seen through water.

Farther out, beyond the last tier, the lava field continues as a gently tilted plain whose blocks grow farther apart until each stands as an isolated pedestal. On the pedestals, solitary stamping rigs continue their work, unattended. Their flywheels turn against nothing; their anvils receive no stock. The machines are present only because stopping them would require an intervention the city has forgotten how to perform. From this distance the rigs look like clockwork insects frozen mid-gesture, their motions the only visible activity on an otherwise empty plain that extends until it meets the downward curve of the brass ceiling.

At the very edge of sight, where the plain finally tilts out of view, a single line of darker blocks marks the horizon. The line is not a wall but a seam where the lava was poured against an older, colder stratum. Heat still leaks along that seam in slow, invisible waves; the air above it shimmers though no water vapor rises. Lens-creatures occasionally travel the seam in single file, their internal lights synchronized so that from a distance they resemble a slow red pulse traveling the length of the city’s boundary. When the last one passes out of view, the pulse continues as an after-image against the brass, fading only when the eye finally looks away.
