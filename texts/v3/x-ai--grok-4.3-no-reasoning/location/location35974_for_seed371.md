The place is a gorge no wider than a trading hall, yet it drops three hundred feet in a single, knife-cut stroke, its walls so recently sheared that the newly exposed stone still sweats grit into the air. The same convulsion that opened the cleft also released an intermittent, pressurized stream that now leaps from ledge to ledge; each time the flow stutters, the spray that remains turns instantly to frost feathers on the rock faces. Moisture therefore appears and disappears in sudden cold pulses, leaving the stone in a perpetual half-dry, half-glistening state.

At this cathedral-scale depth, light has no direct path; what filters down is a bruised, bluish twilight that barely distinguishes edges. The only reliable illumination comes from the sporadic splashes of bioluminescent bacteria that have colonized the wettest overhangs; their faint green pulses last exactly long enough to silhouette the next arriving wave of water before the dark swallows them again.

On the narrow floor, vegetation crowds upward in frantic layers. Ferns the size of window-shades overlap one another; above them, trailing vines spiral toward the single crack of sky, their leaves overlapping so closely that individual fronds lose identity in a continuous green veil. Between the leaves run capillary threads of condensed vapor that fall again as miniature rain whenever a gust funnels down the slot. The growth is so dense that the air is always moving somewhere—either the slow respiration of leaves or the constant shiver caused by the nearby industrial heartbeat.

That heartbeat is the sound: six vertical piston arrays bolted directly into the gorge walls, each driven by the intermittent water. When the stream surges, the pistons slam downward; when the flow pauses, coiled counterweights drag them back up again with a racketing clatter that echoes and doubles in the tight stone throat. The repeated collisions keep the rock faces newly fractured; freshly sheared mineral glints in the momentary green light before the next downstroke smashes it to powder again.

Above the pistons—on ledges that widen only as far as a man’s shoulders—rank upon rank of moss hang in curtains whose weight bends them into dripping arcs. The moss is so saturated that the pistons’ thunder vibrates droplets loose in miniature, meteoric showers. Nothing stirs except the vegetation and the machinery; no insects crawl the stems, no birds call from the narrowing sky. Only the green pulses, the mechanical thunder, the silver flick of water, and the wet, clean scent of stone that has not yet learned how to weather.
