The location is a single excavated vein of stone, no wider than a school corridor, that runs vertically through a cliff face like a splinter of dark ore. From a distance the opening is almost invisible, a hairline fissure between two vast slabs of basalt, yet once inside the passage yawns outward, becoming a self-contained world that never quite touches the open air above or the cave system below.  

The rock that walls the vein is quartz-rich, still glistening with microscopic growths of hydrothermal glass. Fresh ridges and shelves jut like newly broken teeth; there are no smooth faces, only razor facets and angular ledges where stone sheared from stone a geological moment ago. Where the vein widens, the walls are shored with hand-poured concrete ribs mottled by the orange of curing iron salts, each rib exactly one stride apart, as though an industrial skeleton were grown inside the fissure to keep it from collapsing inward. Between these ribs, pipes and conduits snake in tight bundles—some polished copper, others rough-hewn ceramic—carrying chilled nitrogen and powdered reagents rather than water.  

A perpetual cool fog of sublimating dry-ice crystals drifts just above ankle height, kept from escaping by the surrounding pressure curtain of heavier argon. The crystals click softly when they touch metal, but otherwise the passage is hushed; the loudest recurring sound is the low, dry ticking of thermal regulators cycling open and shut every few minutes.  

Against this quiet, movement is constant. Teams of segmented, crab-like servitors no larger than a human palm scurry along narrow ceiling rails, each one clutching a cargo bulb filled with chemical paste. The paste is the only “soil” here: a slurry of silicates and nitrogen-fixing microbes engineered to digest the native rock and extrude thin crystalline filaments that function as both root and cable. The filaments glow with a faint cobalt light, giving the passage its dim illumination and painting every fresh mineral edge a deeper indigo.  

In places the filaments have been coaxed into freestanding lattices that function like partition walls; behind them, small foundries no bigger than kitchen cabinets spit out replacement bolts and wire spools, their ejection ports glowing dull red before the pieces cool. Spent casings drop into hoppers where the servitors recycle them by melting and re-extruding the metal on the spot.  

Overhead, the ceiling occasionally heaves—a controlled seismic thump that shivers loose a dusting of powdered quartz. At each thump the servitors freeze for exactly three heartbeats, then resume their errands. The quakes are the signature of the vein itself lengthening, still prying apart the cliff in imperceptible increments. A newly opened fracture at the far end already displays the same fresh, glass-slick walls, already hosts a single glowing filament and a first, tentative servitor crawling along it.  

The ecology is a closed loop of mineral, machine, and engineered microbe, sealed by argon, chilled to just above freezing, and in a constant low-level riot of production and disassembly. Nothing here drinks water; nothing here sleeps. The only clock is the measured rhythm of the servitors and the vein’s own slow, tectonic heartbeat.
