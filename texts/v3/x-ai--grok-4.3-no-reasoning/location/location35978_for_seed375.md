The valley is no larger than a single avenue in a ruined city, bounded on every side by sheer, newly-risen walls of glassy basalt that catch the faint twilight and fling it back as cold indigo. These walls are still shedding their birth-heat; thin, gray coils of steam lift from hairline fissures and vanish at once, leaving the air as dry as kiln dust. Between them lies a low basin of pale, compacted salts, their surface etched with hair-thin polygonal cracks that look hand-drawn. Nothing has had time to polish those edges yet.

A few dozen plants persist here, each one a solitary, thorned obelisk no taller than a standing person. Their skins are translucent, revealing slow pulses of milky sap that circulate through veins no thicker than thread. Every so often a tiny internal valve clicks—almost too soft to register—sealing moisture against the punishing dryness. Their roots, fine as lace, cling to the shallow crevices of the salt, feeding on the trace minerals that the cooling basalt still sweats. When the wind finally moves, it does so without sound; the thorns merely incline a fraction, adjusting their angle by degrees.

Between the spires the ground is almost empty: smooth flats of wind-sifted salt interrupted now and then by a single shallow footprint of some night-roving thing, edges already blurring. No larger creatures linger; any that arrive must leave again before the air’s chill deepens. Small, translucent beetles the color of skimmed ice patrol the cracks, each one dragging behind it a filament of exhaled vapor that condenses and vanishes before it can be seen. Their legs make the barest rustle against the salt—inaudible unless an ear is pressed directly to the ground.

High above, the basalt walls hold the only motion that persists without interruption. Minute crystals of volatiles still grow outward from the cooling stone, branching like frost; their incremental expansion sends hairline fractures through the glass. The sound is sub-audible, but felt in the teeth as a faint, rhythmic ache. Each new fracture eventually exhales a microscopic gout of vapor that rises, cools, and immediately re-deposits as a dust of micro-snow too fine to settle; it lingers in a permanent, motionless haze a hand-span above the valley floor, giving the twilight its strange, depthless flatness.

Nothing here is built by hands; everything is still in the first hour of its own becoming. The salt, the thorns, the beetles, and the micro-snow all obey the same law: whatever moisture is captured must be spent at once or hoarded for the next slow pulse of the planet’s cooling skin. In the hush between those pulses the valley simply waits, a sealed chamber of sharp new rock and evaporating glass, no larger than a single street, yet already older than any living creature that has crossed it.
