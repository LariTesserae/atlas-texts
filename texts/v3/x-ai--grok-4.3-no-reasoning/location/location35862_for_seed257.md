The Lantern Vaults

Far beneath a collapsed mountain range, a city of black salt and pressure-formed glass occupies the last pocket of dry air beneath an ocean that has not seen light in a thousand generations. Its scale is not that of continents or even of neighborhoods; it is a single, self-contained circulatory system—five linked chambers, each large enough to hold a cathedral yet small enough that sound, when it exists, travels wall-to-wall within a heartbeat.

Everything is fabricated. The floors are unbroken plates of iridescent obsidian, laid so flush that one cannot tell where one segment ends and the next begins. Walls swell outward in bulbous ribs, the stone having been coaxed, not carved, into its present curvature; centuries of slow crystallisation have left pale salt-beaded veins across every surface, as though the whole place sweats salt instead of water. No living plant anchors itself anywhere; only a faint, refractive dust—finely powdered glass and halite—swirls in patient, almost stationary clouds, stirred by the measured rise and fall of warmer upper layers meeting cooler lower layers.

Temperature sits just above human skin. It is not oppressive, yet it prevents any moisture from lingering on the skin, so the air itself feels parchment-dry despite the immense press of water outside. The boundaries of this pocket are invisible: a sheer membrane of compressed brine, held at bay by an ancient pressure differential engineered when the first artisans still hammered copper. Behind that membrane the ocean waits, dense, perfectly still, utterly lightless.

Fauna moves in deliberate, unhurried pulses. Basket-sized, translucent creatures—each a colony of mineral-digesting polyps—drift between ribs of the architecture. Their interiors pulse with faint, rhythmic phosphorescence, the only native light. They feed on the slow exfoliation of the salt-glass walls, grazing millimetres over decades, excreting new crystalline nodules that thicken the ribs in infinitesimal layers. Occasionally, larger, eel-like organisms—long, jointed chains of the same polyps—traverse ceiling channels, dragging threads of that same soft glow. Their motion is so languid that individual contractions are almost imperceptible; one notices only that a shape which was in one corner is now in another, hours later.

Sound is minimal. The city breathes—once every several minutes a soft, subsonic pop as an interior gas bladder equalises pressure across a partition. Otherwise there is only the friction of polyp chains against crystal, the faint hiss of warm salt air sliding over cooler surfaces, and, on rarest occasion, the distant thunder of the true ocean beyond the membrane, transmitted as a single, long, felt vibration rather than audible noise.

Because the environment is fully made and fully inhabited, every surface serves a function. Curving ducts the width of forearms rise and descend through the ribs; inside them, warm updrafts carry the polyp spores to new grazing sites. Low stone benches, their tops polished by generations of reclining bodies, ring the largest chamber; these were not furniture for rest, but condensing surfaces, their undersides designed to collect trace atmospheric water that precipitates when night cycles chill the outer membrane. The benches are empty now, yet still perform their task—tiny, perfectly round droplets forming and sliding, one by one, into collection grooves that have not overflowed in centuries.

Light, where present, is strictly local: the interior bioluminescence of the polyp colonies, the occasional glint of salt crystals catching that glow at precisely the right angle. The darkness is otherwise total. One learns distances not by sight, but by the slight increase in warmth against the skin when one nears a living wall, or by the infinitesimal change in air pressure when a duct inhales.

Time moves here like the polyps—imperceptibly, yet without cease. Walls thicken, benches collect their patient droplets, the membrane flexes by the width of a fingernail each decade, and the city grows, grain by grain, toward whatever final equilibrium its makers intended before they vanished into the black water beyond.
