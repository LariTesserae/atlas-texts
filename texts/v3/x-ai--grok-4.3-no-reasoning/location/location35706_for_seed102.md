The place has no wetness in it at all, yet it is thick with growth. Every surface—floor, wall, ceiling, the very air—displays its own mineral signature. Green-grey quartz fibers grow in tufts like coarse hair; translucent cinnabar plates jut outward at odd angles; veins of native copper braid through gypsum columns that have dripped downward instead of upward. All of it is dry, powdered with salt so fine that a fingertip leaves no moisture behind, only the ghost of glitter. Because elevation is negative and the chamber is pocket-small, the entire ecology sits inside an ellipsoidal cavity roughly the size of a modest ballroom. From the single, eye-level entrance the space curves inward to a low central basin that has never held liquid, only a patina of evaporated brines now crystallized into a floor that crunches like new snow.  

The temperature sits just below the threshold where sweating begins. A visitor feels it as warmth on the backs of hands and across the temples, though nothing in the cavity moves to distribute that heat. The light that does exist leaks through thread-thin fissures high in the vault—enough to silhouette the tallest mineral pillars but never enough to cast a true shadow. Sight, therefore, is peripheral, guessed at, corrected by sound and touch.  

Life is abundant, if not diverse. The fauna are burrowing, low-slung, and largely eyeless. Dozens of arm-length, segmented velvet-worms drag crystalline setae through the mineral hair. Their footfalls produce a continuous rustle like distant brushfire, amplified by the hard walls until it becomes an arrhythmic drumming that never repeats a pattern. When they cross veins of copper, the setae ring like tiny chimes; when they cross gypsum, the sound is a dry exhale. Together the worms constitute a single, living resonance chamber.  

Scattered among the worms are fist-sized crustaceans whose carapaces are made of living halite. They do not move so much as vibrate, grinding their legs against the floor to produce a high insect whine that carries farther than any voice. Because nothing ever changes—no seasons, no inflow, no decay—the whine and the drumming coexist without interference or dominance, each always at the same volume, always the same pitch. The effect on a listener is first disorientation, then a strange calm as the ear learns to inhabit two simultaneous tempos without resolution.  

Vegetation—though lush—does not photosynthesize. Instead, needle-shaped chemolithotrophs mine sulfur from the gypsum and vent tiny jets of hydrogen sulfide that smell sweet, almost floral. The gas diffuses slowly enough that a visitor who remains still for long minutes begins to taste metal on the tongue. The plants themselves are sessile, anchored by root-hairs that split mineral grains at the cleavage planes, a process audible as intermittent, minute detonations. Over centuries the cumulative effect is a floor that grows slightly higher while the ceiling grows slightly lower; yet because dynamic equilibrium forbids gradients, any given moment appears identical to the next.  

A few artifacts survive from an earlier, forgotten hand. Near the entrance, a single band of hammered native copper circles the throat of a mineral pillar like a torque. Its surface is patterned with shallow punch-marks—concentric circles meant, perhaps, to map the cavity itself. Elsewhere a scattering of quartz flakes lies where a knapper once worked; some flakes still bear the bulbs of percussion. These fragments have not been moved, nor will they be; they sit exactly where they landed, preserved by the absence of water and the absence of time.  

Rules here are few and absolute. Nothing grows wet. Nothing rots. Nothing rises or falls. Sound propagates without loss. The chamber can be entered, examined, even inhabited, but its occupants and its mineral occupants alike remain in their fixed proportions, as if the entire pocket has already finished happening and is simply continuing to happen.
