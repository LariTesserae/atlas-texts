The place is called the Feather Hollow.

It is not a valley in the usual sense. It is an enormous, shallow bowl whose floor is not stone but a continuous membrane of dark, living lichen—jet-black, slightly iridescent, like cooled obsidian streaked with slow pulses of indigo light. The lichen is everywhere, covering a terrain so flat that the eye travels for kilometers before a ripple or ridge interrupts it. At irregular intervals, long fissures like the veins on the back of a hand crack the surface, each fissure a meter across and filled with pale, faintly steaming water. These cracks are only a few meters deep, but their water never drains; it seethes gently, releasing threads of vapor that drift upward in sinuous columns no thicker than a finger. At dusk the vapor glows. At dawn it condenses into a fine, persistent dew that beads across every surface, even the air itself.

From horizon to horizon the bowl is pocked with thousands of circular cisterns—perfectly round, each ten to thirty meters across—cut into the lichen like shallow wells. The cistern walls are not built; they are grown, ridged with the same black membrane that forms the floor, yet the membrane inside the cisterns is translucent, revealing labyrinthine networks of root-like fibers pulsing in slow peristalsis. Rain falls only once every eleven days; when it does, the water arrives not as drops but as a low, singing fog that condenses directly into the cisterns, raising their levels by centimeters before the fog lifts again. Between rains the water level never drops, because each cistern is connected by subsurface arteries to every other, forming one vast, hidden circulatory system.

Scattered among the cisterns are the ruins of a city that is no longer ruinous. The buildings never collapsed; they were re-absorbed. What remains are tapering, helical towers, each composed of thousands of overlapping, wafer-thin disks of living ceramic that shift and reorient themselves to follow the slow passage of whatever weak light penetrates the constant overcast. The disks are so thin that wind passing between them produces a continuous, layered drone—thousands of microtones that braid together into a single, deafening chord that seems to come from everywhere at once. The towers never repeat the same orientation for more than a few hours; their motion is the only visible movement in the entire expanse, and yet the motion is so slow that the eye registers it only as an after-image, a ghost of where the tower was moments before.

Nothing grows taller than the lichen except the towers, yet life teems. The air is warm, almost sticky, and carries a low-frequency pressure that makes breathing feel like swimming. Swarms of translucent, ribbon-shaped organisms—each no wider than a fingernail—drift in loose, ever-changing lattices at shoulder height. They are not insects; they are living interference patterns, sustained by the constant drone of the towers. When two swarms intersect they do not scatter; they merge momentarily into a single, larger organism whose edges shimmer like oil on water, then split again. Beneath the lichen, in the dark water of the cisterns, larger creatures move in slow, deliberate circuits. Their presence is announced only by the way the surface tension of the water momentarily warps, forming a dimple that travels ahead of them like the bow-wave of an invisible ship. Occasionally one will surface just enough to reveal a single, enormous eye the color of oxidized copper before sinking again.

The low overcast never breaks. The light that reaches the bowl is filtered through three distinct atmospheric layers: the first scatters high-energy particles into a faint auroral wash; the second converts that wash into polarized bands of pale violet and green; the third layer—composed of microscopic, self-replicating machines—absorbs most of the remaining spectrum and re-emits it as a soft, directionless glow that never quite reaches the brightness of ordinary daylight. The result is a perpetual twilight in which shadows have no edge and colors are suggestions rather than facts.

Because the scale is regional yet the density of visible objects is nearly zero, the mind keeps trying to correct for distance. A tower that seems a hundred meters away may be three kilometers distant; a cistern that appears an arm’s length across is actually the size of a stadium. This mismatch between apparent and actual scale creates a permanent low-level vertigo that visitors describe as “falling upward.” The only reliable measure of distance is the sound of the towers: their drone dopplers slightly when one moves toward or away from them, but the change is so gradual that the ear learns to read it the way a sailor reads the shift of wind across water.

There is no soil, only the living membrane. There are no true rivers, only the connected cisterns. There are no seasons, only the eleven-day rhythm of the singing fog. The rules that govern the place are not the rules of geology or biology but of resonance: everything here—water, architecture, organisms, even the light—is tuned to the same fundamental frequency emitted by the towers. When that frequency shifts, even by a fraction of a hertz, the entire bowl responds at once: the lichen brightens or dims, the cisterns briefly overflow, the ribbon-swarm reconfigures itself into new geometries, and the towers themselves rotate a single additional degree. These micro-adjustments are the only weather the Feather Hollow knows, and they are constant.
