A half-drowned foundry in perpetual twilight. Its ceilings are the under-sides of other ceilings— tier after tier of steel walkways and catwalks that once carried carts, pipes, and personnel above the floor. Water has risen steadily through every level until only the topmost vaulted hall still has a measurable air pocket; most passages have simply become tunnels of cold, silty brine.

The architecture itself is not ruined. It is merely submerged.  
Every rivet, beam, and riveted stair-tread still holds its geometry; only the intended purpose has changed. Motors and drives, designed to lift ingots or turn rollers, now turn sluggish turbines in the slow current, their copper windings humming faintly against the pressure. A grid of overhead rails still intersects the vault, and from many of them the original chain hoists dangle like limp tentacles, each hook festooned with schools of silver fingerlings that dart in perfect formation when the current pulses.

The sound is industrial thunder muffled by leagues of water. Hydraulic rams, still pressurized from reserve reservoirs, fire every twenty-three minutes; each cycle sends a low-frequency concussion that rattles the remaining air bubble and sets loose flakes of rust drifting like red snow. Air that escapes from cracked pistons rises in deliberate chains of shimmering spheres, each the size of a child’s head, and bursts against the steel roof with the sound of wet canvas tearing. Between these detonations there is the quieter, constant percussion of finned bodies striking the undersides of gratings—fish whose lateral lines have learned to read the trembling metal as a reef.

Light arrives only as a dilute olive dusk filtering through glass-brick skylights that have not cracked under the centuries of hydrostatic load. The illumination is enough to reveal, but not enough to warm. Thermometers bolted into stanchions read 4 °C year-round. The same skylights are colonized on their inner faces by colonies of pale, radially symmetrical anemones; they open when the pressure wave of a passing ram hits them, then close again like slow eyelids.

Because the structure is sealed, no natural sediment has filtered in; the floor is the factory floor—diamond plate steel now upholstered in a thin membrane of anaerobic bacteria that glows an almost imperceptible umber when disturbed. A boot-print left on it five centuries ago is still visible, the pressure having driven the microbial film into the checkering so that the tread pattern remains a permanent scar. Around each print the film heals by slowly drawing dissolved minerals from the water and depositing them as a varnish of iron-oxide, thickening the record year by year.

Fauna moves along predetermined lanes. Eel-shaped service drones, their original batteries long dead, have been colonized by chemosynthetic microbes; the once-copper coils now sprout filamentous mats that draw power from the weak galvanic cells still embedded in the chassis. The drones patrol the same inspection routes they followed when the plant was dry, scraping filter grilles and occasionally dislodging a loosened bolt. When two drones meet they perform a slow orbital dance, the living mats on their surfaces exchanging ions, then continue. No new machines are built, yet none decay to dust; every component is locked in an equilibrium that treats metal fatigue as a renewable resource.

Ventilation shafts, still driven by flywheels geared to the rams, exhale warmer water in scheduled plumes. Around each vent a halo of turbulence keeps the water slightly less dense; fish aggregate there to spawn. Their eggs drift upward until they lodge in the mesh of catwalks, where they hatch weeks later and the fry ride the rising bubbles back into the open halls. Predators—larger, armoured forms that might once have been salvage submersibles—circle the perimeters of these spawning clouds, their hull numbers still legible beneath layers of tube-worms. When one strikes, the concussion wave travels the entire lattice; every loose chain clinks once, in sympathetic time, a single note sustained by the architecture itself.

In this place the concept of “abandonment” is meaningless. Nothing has been left behind; every process has simply translated its former medium—air, lubricant, current—into water. The factory and the ocean have become the same continuous object, a single slow machine whose moving parts are fish, rust, and pressure.
