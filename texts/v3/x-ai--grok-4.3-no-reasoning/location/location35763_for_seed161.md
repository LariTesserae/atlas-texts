The place is a canyon so deep it might as well be a mountain turned inside-out. Its walls are the last standing bones of a range that surrendered to the wind millions of seasons ago; only a few crags and buttresses remain, tall enough to drink the light and tall enough to return it as heat. The floor of this canyon is level, a single broad stone slab the size of a small city, worn so smooth that it feels like polished bone underfoot. At its edges, faint ribs of the vanished range still cling to the walls like knuckles, the last cartilage of a long-eroded skeleton.

Across this slab, an entire city has grown like moss across an old skull. Every wall and passage is cut from the same pale, honey-coloured stone; no mortar was ever needed, because the rock itself had already surrendered to every pattern the stonemasons could dream. Roofs are low and thick, vaulted in ribs thin as fingers and wide as streets. Windows are only narrow slits, letting out more sound than light. Walkways bridge from wall to wall at every height, forming a single unbroken lattice of stone so crowded that a bird flying above sees only a single grey-green block pierced by a few veins of shadow.

There is no sky, not really; the open roof of the canyon is a slit so far above the city that it admits only the dimmest, dust-coloured light. The air that drifts down is warm, faintly metallic, carrying the smell of sun-heated stone and slow spice. A lattice of pale, broad-leafed vines has bridged every walkway and sill, their leaves veined with a luminous dust that gives the whole city the ghost of daylight. Their stems descend in ropes as thick as wrists, dangling past windows and ladders, so that every narrow street is lined with living curtains. Between the leaves, small birds and heavier, lizard-bodied gliders hunt flying insects that never touch the ground; the sound of their wings fills every shaft and court.

Water is absent, but the rock sweats an almost imperceptible film of mineral salts that crystallises in pale veins across every surface. Where feet and wheels have worn the stone, these veins have been polished into low ridges. They glint like faint threads of ice when the slit of light crosses them at midday. Every cistern and basin is carved with lids of the same rock; the few still in use are filled with rainwater collected by angled runnels that converge at the highest rooftops. In some forgotten courtyards the lids have cracked, and the basins now hold only a dry glitter of salt crystals that sing when the wind passes over them.

Fauna crowds the stone: pale goats with ridged horns cling to the narrowest ledges, their hooves clicking in slow, deliberate steps; long-tailed rock-hens roost on the rooftops, their calls a rolling, throaty chorus that swells and fades with the light. In the deeper galleries live the leather-backed, herb-eating tunnel bears, whose slow breathing can be heard through floor-grates as a low, rolling tide. The city’s human occupants move more quietly, their voices no louder than the goats’ hooves, but the stone itself magnifies every sound until the whole canyon hums like a held note. At the very bottom, near the one wide plaza left open to the slit of sky, a single fountain still runs from a hidden seam. Its trickle is so small and so steady that the sound never changes; it merely becomes part of the permanent chord the canyon carries.

Every generation adds only a few new vaults, a new layer of walkways crossing the old. Because the city cannot grow outward or upward beyond the existing walls, each new generation carves deeper rather than higher, widening halls, lowering floors, opening new passages through older stone. The result is a slow, patient architecture that moves like sedimentation: each century leaves its own thin stratum visible in the colour of the walls and the depth of the door-sills. Nothing is ever torn down; the city only thickens. The stone remembers every tool-mark, every rope-groove, every groove worn by the same slow traffic of bare feet across the same thresholds. What was once a single crack in the plateau has become a city so dense and so still that, from above, it looks less like a settlement and more like the canyon’s own slow attempt to dream itself into a single continuous body of living rock.
