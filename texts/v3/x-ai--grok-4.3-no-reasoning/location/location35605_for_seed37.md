The cavern is not a place so much as a slow, persistent becoming.  
From far above it, the true surface is a patch of rose-gray sandstone worn into a shallow bowl; thin veins of calcite glint in the cracks, but nothing living grows there yet. Water that arrives on rare mornings drips through hairline fissures, collecting in a series of glass-clear pools that sit like separate, half-formed thoughts along the stone floor. Between them the rock is bone-dry, a chalk-white carbonate lattice still warm from some older subterranean heat that has not fully left. Each pool is only a hand-span across, shallow enough that the light—whatever light finds its way down—reaches the stone bottom and bounces back upward, filling the low chamber with a muted radiance that seems to come from everywhere at once.  

No tunnel connects the pools; they are instead linked by a lattice of damp, newly precipitated calcite fingers that are still soft enough to dent under a careful thumb. These proto-stalactites glisten because they are still wet, because they are still deciding what shape they will become. They are joined by the faintest tracery of gypsum needles that spring from the ceiling in crooked arcs, each no thicker than a sewing pin, each catching the reflected pool-light and splitting it into prismatic grains smaller than dust. The ceiling itself sags a little; where the rock is weakest, a membrane of clear water bulges downward in a trembling blister, held by surface tension and the memory of the last rain. When the membrane finally bursts, the droplets strike the calcite fingers below and set up a tiny ringing that travels along the mineral lattice like a pulse through a nerve.

Tiny insects—transparent, wingless, eyeless—are already at work. They skate the meniscus of each pool on hydrophobic feet, gathering a film of calcite particles the way bees gather pollen. The crystals adhere to their bodies; as the insects move from pool to pool they leave behind faint white trails that, over unknown centuries, will harden into the first true walkways of an infant cavern system. Between pools the insects vanish into gaps narrower than eyelashes, following micro-rills of condensation that keep the air breathable though the chamber has no outlet to the sky.

Farther in, a single, fist-sized boulder of fossiliferous limestone sits half-buried in the floor sediment. Its surface is not yet stone but still the soft, chalky paste that precedes lithification; when disturbed, it releases a smell like wet paper and yields minute spirals of ancient shell still soft enough to bend. From its lee side a colony of pale, hair-fine fungal hyphae radiates outward in a perfect circle three finger-widths across. The hyphae pulse with a faint internal light—no brighter than the reflection off a distant snowfield—synchronized to the slow drip of ceiling water. Every time a droplet lands, the pulse travels one body-length farther, claiming new territory grain by grain.

There is no wind, yet the air shifts. Condensation beads on the cooler undersides of the emerging stalactites, grows heavy, falls. The impact is inaudible, but the splash sends microscopic droplets into the air; each droplet carries a crystal seed and a single fungal spore, and where it lands the next small cycle begins. The only sound that ever rises above absolute silence is the soft, almost tactile whisper of these droplets meeting mineral that has not quite decided it is mineral yet.

Light appears from nowhere and everywhere. It is bright enough to read by, yet it has no source; it simply is, as though the young rock has not yet learned the rule that illumination must come from outside. In this bright, sourceless glow the calcite fingers cast shadows that are too pale to be shadows—more like suggestions of darker brightness. Moving through them feels like walking through an unfinished sentence whose grammar is still being negotiated between water and stone.

Time here is measured in the interval between one condensation cycle and the next, in the number of insect trails needed to outline the first true passage, in the slow decision of gypsum to become either needle or flower. Nothing is complete, nothing is quite certain, and the landscape continues its patient argument with itself about whether it will ever finish becoming at all.
