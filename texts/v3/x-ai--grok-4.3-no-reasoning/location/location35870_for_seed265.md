The darkness here is not night. It is a condition, a medium that has become permanent. It begins at ground level and rises with the terrain rather than falling from above.  
Where water meets the stone, the darkness condenses. Vapour lifts from shallow, lens-shaped pools and, instead of evaporating, gathers into slow sheets of warm mist that roll along the ground like oil. These sheets fold over themselves in long, sinuous drifts, never fully dispersing. At the edges of the pools the rock is sharp and unrounded—newly extruded, its surface still puckered with gas bubbles from the moment it first touched air.

A single pipe, waist-high and riveted, runs along the low slope, its joints weeping rust-coloured condensate that drips into the nearest pool. Ten metres further on, another pipe emerges from beneath a cracked concrete pad, disappears into the darkness, then surfaces again farther away, the intervals between exposures growing shorter until the pipes intersect like the exposed veins of an unfinished engine. Somewhere inside the metal, fluid moves—one direction only, the same direction it has moved for decades—yet the flow is too slow to produce audible turbulence. The only sound comes when a drifting sheet of mist brushes a joint and the temperature difference makes the steel tick, once, like a cooling kettle.

Between the pipes the ground is a jigsaw of low ridges and shallow pans. In the pans, water stands a few centimetres deep, its surface unbroken except where a slow bubble rises and bursts without sound. On the ridges, the stone is bare, still warm from whatever process last shaped it. Nothing grows taller than a single hand-span. Where vegetation occurs it takes the form of flat, translucent discs no thicker than paper, their edges anchored to the stone by hair-fine roots that drink the condensing film. The discs breathe a faint phosphorescence that registers only as an after-image against the darkness; close inspection shows each disc is etched with a repeating hexagonal fracture pattern that matches the crystal habit of the stone beneath.

Animals move in the same measured rhythm as the water. Pale, eyeless crustaceans the size of thumbs crawl between the pans on jointed legs that leave no prints; they feed on the bacterial film that forms on the underside of each disc. Larger, segmented bodies—each segment carrying its own bioluminescent stripe—drift through the mist at the speed of a falling leaf. Their passage momentarily parts the vapour, revealing the next pipe or the next ridge, then the mist folds closed again. None of these creatures hurry; they have only just arrived in evolutionary time, and their metabolisms still run on the residual heat of the rock.

Scattered among the pipes are the remnants of human intention. A single concrete footing, corners still sharp, supports the stump of a lattice tower whose upper half has vanished into the darkness. Its bolts are factory-stamped with a date only six years past. A hundred metres farther, a metal cabinet stands open; inside, circuit boards have grown a uniform grey fur of condensed salts. The printed labels on the boards are still legible, but whatever language they once recorded has been overwritten by the slow crystallization of moisture into angular white dendrites that branch along every conductive trace. The light they emit is not generated by the boards; it is the same cold phosphorescence that outlines the vegetation, only refracted through salt instead of living tissue.

Overhead, the darkness itself has texture. Veins of warmer vapour rise until they reach a temperature inversion, then flatten into an invisible ceiling thirty or forty metres above the ground. Against that ceiling the outlines of larger shapes sometimes appear: ribbed ducts the diameter of rail tunnels, suspension arrays of catenary wire, the silhouettes of cooling towers whose fans have not turned in living memory. None of these structures touch the surface; they exist in a suspended industrial stratum that will descend only after another few centuries of condensation adds enough mass to overcome whatever holds them aloft.

Time here is counted in the interval between one bubble rising from a pan and the next. In that interval the mist drifts another body-length, a crustacean advances the distance of its own antennae, a crystal of salt lengthens by a single molecular layer. The only measurable change is the gradual thickening of the mist sheet where it meets a new pipe joint, and the only rule is that whatever emerges must emerge slowly enough for the darkness to accommodate it without revealing its source.
