The place exists as a single, unbroken ocean-bed of mirror-glass basalt that has been polished by the combined actions of an ancient tide and a permanent, invisible rain. The surface is not level; it tilts in smooth, endless dunes whose gentle rises and valleys stretch beyond any horizon the eye can hold. Every facet is fractured into identical hexagons a hand-span wide, and each fracture line is filled with a filament of liquid mercury. These filaments refract the dim overhead light into needles of pale turquoise that drift across the glass like slow comets. Because the glass is so old, its edges have slumped into one another until the overall shape of the dunes has almost flattened; the next million years will erase them altogether, yet that erasure will take more time than the world itself will be allowed.

The rain is water only in name. It is a heavy, refractive medium that never falls as drops; it forms vertical columns of slow, glassy descent—tall, unbroken streams that touch the surface, spread outward in a thin film, and are instantly re-absorbed into the rock. The columns shift laterally without warning, carving fresh channels across the mercury veins and sealing the old ones within moments. Where two columns merge, the local temperature drops a further fraction; the glass contracts, emitting a single glassy tone so faint that it registers more as pressure against the inner ear than as audible sound.

The entire plain lies at a height equivalent to a high coastal shelf, yet it is completely submerged beneath an inverted pressure-dome of the same glassy rain. This dome is not sky; it is simply water whose surface tension has become permanent, so that the upper boundary of the world is a taut meniscus under which the sparse inhabitants drift or glide. Because the rain-columns descend from that meniscus, they leave vertical tunnels of near-vacuum behind them. Fauna—small, ovoid bodies the color of carbonated pearl—move along these tunnels by rapidly crystallizing and dissolving successive internal shells of ice. Each crystallization emits a brief, static pop of released gas; taken together these pops form a slow, continuous crackle rather than discrete noise. The creatures spend most of their lives in suspension, rising or falling only when the rain-columns migrate; their migration is the only choreography the plain permits.

No material exists that is not the same mercury-infused basalt. The rock has absorbed every particle that once lived or was manufactured, so that the memory of past biology is encoded only as faint internal streaks of rust-brown within the hexagons. The world therefore obeys a single rule: every change must be expressed as a rearrangement of those streaks. The rain-columns are the agents of rearrangement; the fauna are the witnesses. Nothing else is possible, and nothing else occurs.
