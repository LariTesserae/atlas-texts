The chamber is no larger than a root cellar, yet it has been stacked wall-to-wall with a single, continuous mound of fibrous, translucent mineral. Each strand is no thicker than sewing thread and lies parallel to its neighbors, so the floor feels like a single unbroken velvet. Light pours in from a high slit no wider than a letterbox; because the walls angle sharply inward, every surface faces the slit at almost the same acute angle, making the whole mass glow with a white, shadowless brilliance. There is never a variation in that glow. Nothing moves—neither dust nor breath—so the chamber looks the same when you close your eyes and reopen them.

Water exists here only as a breath-held condensation. Microscopic droplets coat each filament. They gather, tremble, and are absorbed back before they can fall, repeating in an endless cycle too fast for the eye but slow enough to keep the chamber perpetually dewed. The air is cool; the droplets are colder than the air itself, so the only sensation of temperature is a faint, continual chill that rises from the floor.

Nothing grows in the usual sense. The filaments themselves are the only vegetation, and they do not advance or retreat; they have simply always occupied this precise space to this exact density. They do, however, respond to pressure: when touched they darken from white to opalescent blue, then fade back over a period of perhaps ten thousand heartbeats, as though resetting a memory.

The only inhabitants are sessile, pinhead-sized crustaceans whose carapaces are exactly the same refractive index as the filaments they cling to. They exist in such numbers that each thread supports a living sleeve of them, yet because they never move and never alter color, the chamber never looks more crowded than when it is empty. Their only activity is a collective, imperceptible vibration that produces a continuous high chime, like an enormous tuning fork that never decays. That sound is the only sound, and because nothing else varies, it seems louder than a waterfall heard from its basin.

A single human-scale artifact lies among the threads: the partial rim of a ceramic bowl, half-buried, its glaze cracked into thousands of tiny facets. Around its exposed edge the filaments have been displaced by exactly the thickness of one strand, as if the bowl appeared first and the chamber then grew around the interruption without ever touching it. The rim is oriented toward the light slit so that it casts no shadow of its own; it functions less as an object and more as an additional facet of the same radiant surface.

Time inside the chamber has no gradient. The filaments neither lengthen nor decay. The water droplets neither accumulate nor evaporate. The only way to tell that duration occurs at all is the bowl rim’s slow return to its original color once a hand has brushed the threads away from it. Even that change is too gradual to mark an arrival or a departure; it only reminds a visitor that whatever is inside the chamber was already inside it before they entered and will remain inside it after they leave.
