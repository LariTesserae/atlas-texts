There are no plants at all in this valley, not even the memory of moss on stone. The only green is mineral-green, a faint verdigris that stains the undersides of the quartz outcrops where condensation has lain longest. Everything else is the muted spectrum of silt, chalcedony, and obsidian: pinks turning gray where light fails, gray-blue where a faint film of water clings, iron reds deepening to maroon where the rock is still warm from the day’s absorbed heat.

The ground is never truly dry, yet never liquid. The water lives inside the rock—between grains, along hairline fissures—brought up from the shallow water table and then quickly re-absorbed when the air cools toward evening. Small, pearl-sized beads appear on the undersides of the boulder shelves and then vanish again, as though the stone were gently sweating. Footfalls leave no prints; each slight displacement of sediment is followed, within minutes, by a capillary re-settling that erases the mark.

The space itself is intimate. From the high point of the central rise you can see both of its containing walls: the northern arc, a single slab of rhyolite two hundred paces across and no higher than a tall person; the southern arc, broken into stepped blocks the size of low altars. There is no sky in the conventional sense, only a low gray ceiling of vapor that presses down between the shoulders of those walls, diffusing any light that might arrive from above. The dimness is permanent but never absolute; it is the half-light of an overcast afternoon that has forgotten how to become night.

Within this hollow, the built remnants are few and stubborn. Three low cylinders of unmortared stone sit half-embedded in the floor, their tops flush with the surrounding silt. Their interiors are packed with layered discs of the same pale stone, each disc no thicker than a thumbnail, each one slightly offset so that a thin radial gap spirals inward to a central void the width of a wrist. Nothing grows inside those voids; nothing is stored there. They are simply present, as the walls are present, as the constant film of water is present.

No wind moves here, so the only sounds are the minute, almost inaudible clicks of stones settling after a drop of condensation falls, or the soft, arrhythmic tapping of heat fractures expanding and contracting along the northern slab. These noises occur at such long intervals that the silence between them feels continuous rather than interrupted. When a rare visitor steps from one disc to the next, the only new sound is the faint compression of water between sole and stone—gone again before the next step lands.

Temperature remains in a narrow band just above skin warmth. The warmth has nothing to do with sunlight; it seeps upward from the saturated substrate, a steady diffusion that keeps the surface stones from ever chilling below the dew point. Because there is no gradient—no slope of terrain steep enough to drive flow, no significant temperature differential between day and night—the water never drains away and never accumulates into pools. It simply cycles through the pores of the rock, rising, condensing, descending again, an equilibrium so complete that the valley registers as a single, unchanging organism whose pulse is too slow to be perceived within a human lifetime.

Fauna is limited to occasional threads of what might once have been called nematodes, now reduced to translucent mineral sheaths that slide between grains of silt like animated glass fibers. They leave no tracks. When one passes beneath a condensation bead, the bead darkens for a moment as the fiber draws a fraction of its moisture, then brightens again as the fiber moves on. The exchange is so minute that it registers only as a fluctuation in the light that reaches the eye—there, then gone, then there again at a different angle.

In this place, change has already happened; what remains is the condition after change. The structures, the water, the warmth, the silence, all hold one another in a closed loop whose only evidence of time is the slow, uniform deepening of mineral stains where condensation has lingered longest.
