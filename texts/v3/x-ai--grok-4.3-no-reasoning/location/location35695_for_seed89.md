The cave begins as a vertical throat. The passage plunges through basalt like a gullet worn in a single breath of time, though the breathing has been constant for a million years. Condensation beads the rough walls, each droplet catching the faint light that falls from a pinprick above. That single opening is the only source of illumination: a diffuse, star-pricked disc far overhead, no wider than a closed fist from the floor below. Moisture runs in patient rivulets, following the spiral grooves carved by countless drips that fall at intervals measured in minutes, then hours, then days.

The air is warm and thickened by respiration. It moves slowly, stirred only when a body larger than a human presses against the walls. Where the throat widens into a chamber the size of a nave, the floor rises in irregular terraces formed by evaporating pools. These terraces are slick with a thin film of water that never quite drains; it lingers long enough to foster micro-algae whose dull blue-green glow outlines every contour when all other light is absent. The glow pulses with the slow exhalation of the rock, a biological tide that coincides with temperature fluctuations of less than a single degree over hours.

In that half-dark the floor is never empty. Beetles the length of a forearm scuttle in dense, overlapping carpets, their elytra a muted lacquer that reflects the algae. They feed on a felt of fungal hyphae that threads between grains of sand and fractured basalt. The fungi exude a faint vinegar scent that rides the slow convection currents upward. Occasionally the beetles pause, antennae lifted, as though listening to the faint percussion of water meeting stone above.

Larger than the beetles—but still smaller than a crouched person—are the salamanders. They drape themselves across the terraces like living ropes, their skins a translucent ochre through which internal organs are dimly visible. The salamanders move so infrequently that a casual eye might mistake them for root thickenings until one opens a gill slit with a wet click. Their breathing is measured in minutes per cycle; each inhalation draws the humid air across vascular membranes that glow the same algae-blue as the walls. They feed by positioning themselves across beetle highways, waiting days for traffic to pass beneath their jaws.

Over everything hangs a lattice of fungal fruiting bodies no thicker than threads, strung between ceiling projections like abandoned rigging. These strands tremble at frequencies too low for human hearing yet strong enough to dislodge condensation. When droplets fall, they strike the threads and produce a continual patter—loud inside the enclosed space—each impact releasing a minute cloud of spores that spiral downward through the humid air. The spores catch in the algal film and begin their own long germination.

Nothing here is hurried. The water film advances and retreats across the terraces by millimeters per day. The salamanders shift position by centimeters per week. The beetles clear corridors through the hyphal felt and then allow new growth to reclaim the paths, a slow negotiation measured in generations of insects. Even the slow seep of mineral-rich water lengthens the stalactites by fractions of a millimeter each year, depositing rings like the growth layers of submerged trees.

Far above, the single aperture occasionally receives rain. The drops descend the entire height of the shaft in a matter of seconds, exploding against the terraces with a report that momentarily silences the beetles. The reverberation rolls upward again, amplified by the cylindrical walls, until it dissipates into the greater darkness. Afterward the water film thickens for a time, the algae brightens, the salamanders stir and reposition. Then the system settles back into its patient rhythm: condensation, evaporation, slow traffic of many small bodies, and the ceaseless, almost inaudible negotiation between rock and water.
