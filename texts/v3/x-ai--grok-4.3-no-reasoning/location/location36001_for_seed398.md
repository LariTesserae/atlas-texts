A vault within the flank of a nameless, kilometers-high needle of basalt—its peak lost in perpetual cloud—has been coaxed open and sealed again by hands that are no longer organic. The chamber is a perfect sphere two hundred meters across, yet the geometry is imperfect because the sphere is still forming. The rock walls have not finished condensing; each second a microscopic rain of ferric ions precipitates from invisible seams, platelets of mirror-bright metal knitting themselves together like slow frost. In the dim glow these platelets give off their own faint cobalt radiance, so the light that does exist comes from the walls themselves.

The air inside is heavy, hotter than the surface of boiling water, yet it is so dry that nothing corrodes. Moisture arrives only as the slow exhalation of pressurized rock: every few hours a hairline fracture opens, exhales a filament of steam, then seals itself again. The vapor turns immediately to a transient film of condensation across the newest facets; it beads, flashes briefly iridescent, and sublimates away. These quick silver lenses are the only water the place has ever known. They last less than a minute, but their cycle is what keeps the temperature from climbing past its present, merciless equilibrium.

The sphere’s interior is empty save for a single, colossal pillar of the same metal that grows and shrinks imperceptibly from the roof and floor, meeting each other at the center in a weld of heat so intense the joint is a teardrop of liquid iron suspended in space. The pillar sings. Not with vibration in the audible range, but with a faint electromagnetic hum that changes in pitch as the iron droplet expands or contracts. To the ear there is no sound—only the pressure of heat against eardrums and the ghost of the vibration felt through teeth.

Seen from inside, the sphere seems to hang suspended in a larger emptiness. The walls are porous on the quantum scale; occasionally a bright mote of plasma escapes through a pore the size of a molecule, drifts a centimeter, and is reabsorbed. Where it touches the ceiling it leaves a momentary spangle of white glass whose facets rearrange over the next half-hour until they again become the same mirror surface. Nothing biological is needed to move or clean these surfaces; the rearrangement is an intrinsic property of the metallic glass.

Everything in the chamber is either unfinished construction or the byproduct of construction. The rock that becomes metal still remembers its earlier crystalline lattice; under certain angles of observation the facets briefly resolve into the original basalt twinning before the new lattice overwrites it. In the dim cobalt light these transient relics look like fossil ghosts—mineral shadows of a mountain that has already been eaten and is still being eaten.

There are no entrances or exits in the ordinary sense. The sphere is accessed by aligning one of the transient pores with an external sheath of coherent neutrinos; the portal is therefore never in the same place twice. For a moment the pore is large enough to admit a needle of gas; the needle draws the intruder in like a capillary and then reseals, leaving no trace. To the outside world the entire structure is invisible because the dimensional curvature around it folds every photon back onto its surface after a few nanometers. The pocket therefore exists entirely for its own sake: a living forge whose only product is the continuing process of its own refinement.
