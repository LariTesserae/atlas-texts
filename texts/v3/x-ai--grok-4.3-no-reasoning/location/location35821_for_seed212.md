The city does not begin at a gate; it begins as a membrane of living matter pressing against the skin of the air. Where two vines meet, a doorway occurs—an opening produced by tension, not cut by tools. Beyond it the living mass thickens into a cathedral of roots and fronds, the ceiling a tangle so high that light arrives already filtered through three canopies at once. Every step deeper meets another layer: shaggy moss-boles thirty meters across, each ribbed like whale bone and warm to the palm; sheets of translucent leaves the size of bed-sheets that sag under their own dripping weight; thorns like glass filaments hanging in curtains that part when warm currents pass through them and close behind.

The heat is not oppressive because the vegetation itself is cooling. Condensate pearls form along every down-facing vein, slide into the main stems, then slide again into open gutters lined with absorptive bark. Water is never still. It moves in slow arterial pulses, driven by the continual expansion and contraction of tubular leaves that inhale and exhale on a cadence slightly faster than a human heartbeat. In the low basins where stems bunch, the excess collects into shallow, sky-reflecting ponds whose surfaces tremble with falling droplets. Above these ponds the air hangs in a thin, iridescent veil—spores, pollen, and the finer mist that never quite settles.

Nothing here is separate from anything else. Walls are also floors; floors are also lungs. When the basin beneath a district exhales, whole neighborhoods rise a few hand-spans, producing a soft, tectonic groan that rolls outward. When the breath returns, districts sink and water rushes inward through the same corridors, sluicing away the pale spores that threaten to clog breathing pores. This cycle repeats every quarter-hour, a living tide in which buildings themselves become wave-forms. The sound is continuous: a layered roar of sap accelerating through xylem, of fibrous membranes snapping taut, of seed-pods popping open like muffled corks. Underneath all of it lies a sub-sonic thrum—the mass of roots grinding against one another as the city repositions itself millimeter by millimeter, forever rearranging the map.

No animal voice breaks the texture because no animal can survive long enough to make a voice. The air is saturated with compounds that fix nitrogen and scar lung tissue; the surfaces secrete enzymes that turn chitin into sugar. Only the plants remain, and they do not mind the chemistry. They metabolize what would kill an insect, and they metabolize the dead versions of themselves just as readily. Where a stem is crushed by the rising of a neighbor, the crushed tissue liquefies overnight and is drawn back into the circulatory lattice, recycled before the next breath-cycle.

Elevation is minimal but never flat. The entire mass is a shallow bowl whose center lies five meters below the rim. In the lowest zone the water has pooled into an irregular lake whose surface is tiled with floating pads the size of plazas. Each pad is anchored by a single transparent stalk that plunges into the deeper mud; when a shift in buoyancy occurs, the stalk contracts, dragging the pad under for a moment, then releasing it again so it bursts upward trailing curtains of bubbles. From above, the effect looks like a slow green heartbeat.

Raindrops fall continually from the upper leaves. Because they fall from varying heights and are broken into smaller and smaller droplets by intervening fronds, the precipitation arrives as a continuous fine spray rather than discrete showers. The spray is warm—slightly above skin temperature—so condensation gathers on cooler surfaces and runs downward in rivulets that look like molten glass. Where these rivulets meet an upward-facing leaf, they pool until surface tension fails; the water then pours in a silver rope into the next level, producing a chain of miniature waterfalls that sound, collectively, like distant applause.

Light behaves strangely. The upper leaves are engineered by growth rather than selection to refract certain wavelengths; entering the canopy, a walker is surrounded by columns of lemon-yellow air alternating with columns of violet shadow. As the breathing cycle lifts and lowers the leaves, these columns slide across one another like slow stage lighting. The result is a moving map of brightness that never quite settles on any single surface long enough to bleach it.

There are moments—perhaps every third cycle—when the entire city shudders in what might be described as a sneeze. Every leaf stiffens, every stem contracts, and a wave of vapor is expelled through the uppermost pores in a single white plume that rises two hundred meters before it disperses. For the space of a held breath the sound drops from deafening to merely loud; then the exhalation finishes, the city relaxes, and the roar returns. During that brief silence the vegetation settles into slightly new positions: stems that had leaned left now lean right; doors that had been open become closed. The map has shifted again.

The city has no center and no edge. Where the vegetation thins, it does so gradually, becoming first a loose net of vines over open water, then scattered rafts of leaves, then a slick of spores on the surface tension. Beyond that, nothing can be called city or even land. Yet the same circulatory pulse continues beneath the water, connecting the settled zone to an unseen perimeter of floating green continents that drift, merge, and split again under the warm sky. The city is therefore not a place so much as a state of continuous emergence—vegetation inventing itself faster than any map can record, water remembering every shape it has ever been asked to hold, and sound taking the place of both memory and future.
