The cavity is not a room so much as a seam: a hairline crack between two compression-fields that refuses to close. Inside the fissure the scale is only a few body-lengths in every direction, yet the perspective refuses to settle—sometimes the walls appear arm’s reach, sometimes they blur into kilometer-deep gorges of themselves. Matter here is matter under protest; the minerals that line the crevice are not ordinary rock but folded lattices whose atomic registers have been re-tuned. Each facet catches the dim, sourceless light and folds it into sub-millimeter rainbows that last seconds before collapsing back into grey.

A single, unbroken slab of colorless crystal runs the length of the cavity floor. Its surface records eons of almost imperceptible subsidence; ghost-thin layers have settled, one after another, until the whole sheet is bowed like a page left too long in humidity. Tiny, perfect cubes of a darker, metallic mineral stud this slab at irregular intervals, spaced as widely as lone buoys on an empty ocean. Each cube has grown outward in minute filaments that stop just short of touching its neighbors, as though the available crystallizing ions had run out mid-thought.

Above the slab, the opposing wall is nothing so solid. It is a standing interference pattern: a curtain of overlapping probability shells that hold vacuum on one side and a whisper-thin neonate atmosphere on the other. Every few decades the shells drift a micrometer, exchanging packets of gas; the exchange is soundless. The temperature is constant, locked just above the threshold where the last molecular motion could be mistaken for warmth. The air—if it can still be called air—remains a single, motionless column, too thin to carry scent or vibration.

No biology. No motion visible to eye or instrument. Only the slow re-alignment of those probability shells, ticking forward like a clock built of forbidden transitions. The cubes on the floor accumulate charge in increments too small to measure; over centuries the difference will amount to a single additional electron, enough to nudge the nearest filament a single lattice step outward. When that step is complete, two cubes will finally touch. The result, whenever it occurs, is not recorded in any surviving description of the place.
