The Swell is a planet-scale enclosure whose floor and ceiling are a single, continuous green. There is no soil. There is no stone. Every surface is tissue: lignin lattices thicker than mountains, phloem columns wider than cities, mesophyll membranes that billow and re-knit. The whole interior—floor, walls, roof—is alive and industrially maintained by circulatory turbines the size of moons that pump nutrient broth through vascular ducts the width of straits. From any altitude above a few thousand meters the landscape appears as a uniform emerald; close inspection reveals everything to be either leaf or stem, nothing else.  

Temperature is held at a breathable chill, a constant 12 °C, by the slow expansion and contraction of the huge vascular ducts: every few hours they constrict, heat radiates outward, the temperature drops by one degree, then the ducts relax and the cycle repeats. There is no weather in the meteorological sense; only this planetary pulse and the occasional pale fog of transpired water vapor that condenses briefly into drifting droplets before it is reabsorbed.  

Light is constant, generated inside the leaves themselves by engineered chloroplasts whose fluorescence spectrum is tuned to human-visible wavelengths. The result is an even, shadowless brightness—white-green, almost clinical—yet it never dazzles, because the same membranes that emit the light also absorb its excess.  

Nothing here was grown from a seed. Every cell was printed, then instructed to iterate according to schematics whose tolerances are measured in micrometers. The built surface is so complete that the concept of “ground” has lost meaning; walking across it is indistinguishable from walking inside a living machine whose smallest tolerances are biological.  

Erosion here is metabolic. Over centuries the tissues shed thin epidermal layers that are immediately reclaimed by phagocytic cells, keeping dimensions stable while allowing microscopic renewal. No tectonic drama occurs; stresses are absorbed by elastic sclerenchyma fibers woven through every load-bearing structure. The only visible motion is a faint contractile shimmer that travels across distant ridges—vascular peristalsis on the scale of continents.  

Fauna is rare because competition was designed out: the vast green substrate supports no niches for mobile consumers. What animals remain are archival drones, soft-shelled and transparent, drifting through the air like motes to monitor cell-wall integrity and report via sub-sonic pulses. Their occasional clicks are the only sounds. Otherwise silence prevails, the sort of silence that amplifies the faint thrum of pressurized sap moving beneath the surface.  

Weirdness manifests as curvature anomalies: straight corridors that seem to narrow the farther you look, then suddenly expand when you turn your head. Gravity is locally warped by dense chloroplast clusters that double as optical computers; they bend space slightly so that light from the ceiling can reach floor-level cells without loss. Walking through these warped zones produces a mild vertigo, like the world is folding its tissue over itself.  

The atmosphere is nitrogen-oxygen with trace volatiles released by the leaves—green aldehydes and micro-doses of glucose vapor—so that each inhalation carries a faint sweetness that never clings. Pressure is stable, the entire volume held within an exoskeleton of xylem armor kilometers thick whose outer surface is exposed to vacuum. Inside, the ecosystem has its own closed-loop meteorology: transpiration rises, condenses against the ceiling membrane, drips re-absorbed through capillary veins, and begins again.  

Nothing here is wild. Nothing is accidental. Yet the scale is so large that entire weather systems of slow chemical exchange play out across horizons measured in days of travel, and the steady expansion–contraction pulse of the living architecture gives the impression of breathing on a timescale geological rather than biological.
