The plateau-city of Itharr sits like a single, living organism that has forgotten where its bones end and its habits begin. It does not spread; it thickens. Basaltic terraces rise three hundred metres above a black sea of vapour, each terrace edge a broken lip where the cooling lava once folded back on itself. From far below, the mists never clear. They arrive every evening as low, ribbed banks and sleep there all day, leaving the upper faces filmed with condensation that never falls far enough to become rain. Instead it beads, rolls, and re-clings, an inch above the stone.  

The city itself is not stone any longer. Over generations the buildings have absorbed the living minerals that continue to sweat upward through hairline cracks; the streets feel warm through thin-soled shoes, and the door frames hold a faint, internal glow the colour of weak tea. No plants grow, but colonies of glassy filaments—slow, hair-like accretions of condensed salts—climb the shadowed angles of alleys, recording, in their branching order, the pressure of every footstep taken in the last century. They are harvested once a generation and fed back into the city’s foundries as flux.

Itharr’s engines lie beneath everything. Deep shafts drop into the plateau’s living heat, where turbines the size of grain silos turn without cease. The sound they make never stops; it is not a roar but an immense, steady drone that vibrates the molar roots of anyone who lingers too long on the lower levels. Citizens adapt by speaking one register lower than they would elsewhere, so that conversation rides above the drone like a thin boat above a river’s bed-noise. At night the turbines shed excess heat through vents cut like arrow-slits; the vents exhale white plumes that roll upward, become new mist, and drift back down, completing the loop.  

Fauna is mostly mechanical and self-replicating. Insect-sized crawlers—each the size of a thumbnail—scuttle along the mineral veins, tasting dissolved metals and excreting microscopic lattice repairs. Larger, crab-like tenders patrol the vent arrays, their carapaces made of salvaged turbine plating bolted together with ceramic rivets. They move slowly, one deliberate joint at a time, and the sound of their passage is a sequence of soft, ringing taps, like spoons testing glass. They do not breed; they are assembled in batches whenever the city census records an unexplained loss.  

The streets do not run straight. They follow seams that the original lava flow left behind, seams that have since been widened into thoroughfares by the weight of traffic and the patient settling of foundations. Some streets narrow to single-file gaps; others open into plazas large enough to hold three foundry halls side-by-side. The plazas are roofed with tensile canopies of woven alloy mesh that turn the daytime glare into a parchment-coloured wash. Beneath these canopies the air is two degrees warmer than the open terraces, because the mesh traps the upward-welling heat and the exhalations of the crowd.  

At the plateau’s rim, the land drops away into a vertical kilometre of weather that is not the city’s own. From the lip, watchers can see whole strata of cloud sliding past at different speeds: high, fast cirrus above; lower, slower stratocumulus below; and, at eye level, the dense, city-bound vapour that never escapes. The contrast makes the city feel both anchored and adrift, a fixed place inside its own slow-moving sky.  

Nothing here is sudden. The turbines turn, the filaments lengthen by microns per day, the tenders replace a single bolt every decade. Even the mist cycles so gradually that residents mark its phases by memory, not measurement: “when the salt on the north balustrade turns from grey to white, the long rains will begin again.” The city and the mineral plateau remain distinct yet interdependent; one feeds the other, one shelters the other, and together they occupy a span of time too wide for any single lifetime to cross.
