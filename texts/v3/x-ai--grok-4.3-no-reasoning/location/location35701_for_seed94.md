The place is a high, vast plateau that stretches for many days’ march in every direction, yet its weather is the weather of the plateau itself—circular storms that sometimes turn in place for weeks, slow fog-layers that thicken and thin without ever leaving the rim. The ground underfoot is a dense, almost cellular mat of dark peat that has grown over centuries on top of an ancient lava-flow; the peat is so thoroughly woven with fine root hair that walking across it feels less like stepping on earth than on a living membrane. In places, the surface gives slightly under a heel, and dark water wells up at once, then is re-absorbed in minutes, leaving only a momentary darkening of the moss.

The peat and the moss are green-black in most lights, but where the surface has cracked or slipped the exposure is a startling rust-orange of oxidized iron. From those cracks rises a constant, almost inaudible hiss—steam generated where groundwater meets residual volcanic heat. The steam does not escape into open air; instead, in the cool, still atmosphere, it condenses immediately into curtains of fine droplets that drift a hand-span above the ground before being caught again by the moss. Seen from a distance, the entire plateau seems to wear a slowly rolling layer of breath-height fog that never lifts higher than the knee.

Because elevation keeps the mean temperature just above freezing, the droplets freeze on contact with any exposed stone or metal. The result is a lacework of delicate frost that forms each night and melts each noon, feeding the moss with a daily pulse of moisture. The frost takes the shape of whatever it settles on: the delicate, branching outlines of spider-silk become temporary white coral; the edges of leaves acquire a temporary rim of glass. Nothing built by human hands survives here long enough to gather more than one night’s frost; if a tool or scrap is left, the moss grows over it within days, turning its outline into another soft contour of the living mat.

The vegetation that dominates is not one species but an interlocking community: a miniature canopy of knee-high vascular plants whose leaves are thickly haired to trap condensate; between them, gelatinous mats of liverwort that swell and shrink with the daily freeze-thaw; and, in the wettest depressions, colonies of globular algae that look like green bubbles until they are stepped on and release a smell of cucumber and iron. All of these plants grow so closely that the surface appears seamless; there are no paths except those made by the broad, low bodies of grazing herbivores whose slow passage is the only visible disturbance.

Those grazers move in loose, constantly shifting aggregations of twenty or thirty. Their bodies are low, six-legged, covered in overlapping plates of living chitin that are colonized by the same moss as the ground; when the animals lie down to rest they become almost indistinguishable from the peat hummocks. They feed by pressing their broad undersides against the vegetation and drawing it upward with thousands of hair-like mouthparts, pruning rather than cropping. The sound of their feeding is only a faint, rhythmic suction, softer than breath. When they are disturbed, the plates along their backs lift slightly, releasing a puff of the same iron-scented steam that rises from the cracks; the puff forms a temporary veil that hides their retreat.

Above the moss layer, the air is motionless most of the time, but at irregular intervals a slow, planet-scale pressure shift tilts the entire fog layer. Then the droplets drift sideways for hundreds of paces before the surface tension of the moss pulls them down again. During these drift events the plateau seems to inhale; the only audible result is a single, prolonged note like the softest imaginable bow drawn across the very lowest string of a cello—an infrasonic resonance transmitted through the peat itself. The animals pause their feeding until the note fades; the plants, whose leaves are already saturated, simply thicken their cuticle for a moment and resume normal exchange.

The horizon is a raised rim of darker rock where the ancient lava cooled against cooler crust. From that rim the plateau looks almost flat, yet the surface is actually a very shallow basin: the center lies a few tens of paces lower than the edge, so that all condensate and meltwater move, over decades, toward the middle. There they pool into a single, perfectly circular lake no deeper than a man’s height yet vast enough to hold its own microclimate; on windless days its surface is a perfect mirror of the fog above it. At intervals of years the lake level rises until water finds a hidden crack at the rim and drains in a brief, silent sheet across the outer slopes. When the level falls again the exposed lakebed immediately begins to re-grow the same peat-and-moss carpet, the cycle renewing without ever leaving a scar.

Nothing here was made, yet nothing is random. Every texture, every slow motion, is the current expression of processes that have run for so long that their record is written only in the thickness of the peat and the precise angle at which frost crystals re-form after each dawn. The plateau is not still; it is simply busy at a scale and speed where human eyes register only the appearance of changelessness.
