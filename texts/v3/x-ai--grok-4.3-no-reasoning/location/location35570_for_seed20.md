The world is a breath-held sphere of warm, pressurized water contained inside a cathedral-sized fracture in the side of a recently emergent reef. What the eye first meets is not a horizon but a slow thunder: a vertical sheet of rain falling endlessly against the reef wall. The rain never strikes a surface; it simply dissolves into the surrounding water, creating a permanent wall of churning bubbles that refracts the dim light into drifting blue-green scars.

Inside the fracture, the ceiling is a ragged, knife-edge vault of young limestone—surfaces too new to be worn smooth—still shedding micro-crystals that sparkle in the water like slow constellations. The floor is a tilted plain of the same stone, puckered with shallow mineral basins that catch and hold brighter pockets of water, each pocket acting as a mirror. Between basins, the plain is bare; only an occasional fan-shaped sponge fans out from a fissure, pale as bone. Their presence feels accidental, as though the sponges have not yet decided whether they belong here.

Far overhead—impossibly high for this confined volume—the water itself behaves like sky. It forms transparent lenses that drift downward, rotate, and evaporate, never reaching the floor. Each lens bends the distant light into momentary columns that cross the chamber like spear shafts. When a column strikes a sponge, the sponge briefly darkens, its pores releasing a fine, almost invisible mist of eggs that rise and are caught by the next passing lens. The eggs linger inside these moving columns, glowing for several seconds before dispersing; most vanish without hatching.

The atmosphere is warm enough that the water feels like living skin pressed against the skin. Currents push through the fracture in sudden, soundless bursts—each burst arrives from an unknown direction, reverses, then collapses into a standing vortex that holds for the length of a breath before dissolving. The vortices sometimes lift loose calcite shards and hold them in suspension, turning them like slow planets. When two vortices intersect, the shards shatter into a brief galaxy of glinting dust that rains upward, then falls again.

There are no animals visible at any given moment. Their absence registers as a pressure behind the ears, a readiness the water never fulfills. Occasionally a single pressure-wave passes through the chamber—origin unknown—strong enough to rattle the sponges and set their egg-mist swirling. The wave carries a low, infrasonic rumble that the water transmits directly into bone; it is the only sound permitted inside this place. The wave does not repeat; it simply leaves behind a faint trembling that the next lens of sky-water momentarily illuminates before the trembling fades.

The world keeps time by the slow growth of the fracture walls. Every few hours a new hairline crack opens somewhere along the ceiling; water rushes into it with a hiss that is felt more than heard. Within minutes the crack seals itself again, leaving a raised white scar that glows briefly before the scar tissue is reabsorbed. The scars accumulate like tally marks, recording a sequence of events that happened only minutes ago yet already feel ancient. The chamber is therefore always slightly larger than it was the moment before, and always exactly the same size as the last time it was measured.
