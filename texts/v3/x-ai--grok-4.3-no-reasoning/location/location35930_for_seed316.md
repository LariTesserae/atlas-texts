In the planetary-scale lowlands of a world whose atmosphere will not forget water, the land lies so flat that weather itself has become terrain. Vast slow drifts of vapor move like tectonic plates, each layer a different temperature, each collision a new city of cloud. The ground is never truly dry; even the shallowest depressions hold a film of condensation that moves from footprint to footprint as though eager to be stepped on. Where the earth rises a handspan above the general level it weeps, thick-beaded moisture collecting on black crystalline plates that ring faintly when touched, a tone that travels through the water table and answers elsewhere.

Life here is not absent but spaced as though obeying rules of exclusion. A single colossal slab of half-buried basalt the size of a mountain range bears, along its entire upper surface, a felt of mustard-colored lichen so thin it could be mistaken for rust; this is the only vegetation for hundreds of kilometers. Elsewhere, the soil is sterile dark glass, cracked into polygons so immense their centers are invisible from their rims. Only along the cracks do threads of pallid moss venture, each thread the ancestor of the next, never quite meeting. When the wind lifts a fragment of this moss it becomes a kite that refuses to fall, sustained by the constant upward press of evaporating film.

Temperature hovers in the narrow band where breath feels heavy and skin never quite cools. The air is saturated; every exhalation adds to a haze already thick enough to blur horizons into ghost ridges. The sky above is not black but a deep slate, pierced at long intervals by rents through which a paler, faintly violet light leaks; beneath each such rent the ground steams, and the hissing columns of vapor spiral slowly as if stirred by a continent-sized finger.

Sound is not an overlay here but the medium itself. The entire basin resonates at the frequency of its own shifting cloud masses. A permanent infrasonic growl—too low for ordinary ears yet felt behind the sternum—marks the collisions of vapor fronts the size of archipelagos. Layered upon this is the continuous, granular roar of water films shearing across glass polygons; each fracture acts as a capillary flute, producing a note that rises and falls with barometric pressure. Occasionally a sharper percussion arrives: a crystal plate shears, its ringing tone traveling through the saturated ground faster than through air, arriving at the skin before the ear can register it.

Because there is no emptiness, every location already contains the next event that will occupy it. A vapor front rolls forward; behind it a temporary lake forms on glass, lasts hours, and drains through hairline fissures into buried galleries whose vaults are lined with the same weeping black crystal. The galleries themselves are not empty: they hold, in suspension, a clear gel that has not moved in geological time, yet every molecule within it is in slow, turbulent orbit around micro-eddies created by the planet’s faint magnetic drift.

Artifacts are almost absent, yet not nonexistent. In certain depressions, half-sunk into the glass polygons, lie curved ribs of a material paler than bone and colder than the surrounding stone. They are spaced too regularly to be natural, yet too eroded to suggest purpose. When mist condenses upon them they grow beaded fringes that drip inward, feeding the same slow circuit of moisture that circulates through the weeping rock. Nothing grows on the ribs; nothing grows because of them; they are simply another saturated presence in an already saturated volume.

At this scale, change and stasis have collapsed into one another. The landscape is not becoming flatter; it has already achieved the final flatness that admits no further planation. Vapor continues to rise, condense, fall; the basalt slab continues to host its single film of lichen; the glass polygons continue to ring. The turbulence is therefore not a symptom of unfinished creation but the very signature of completion: every process has found its place, every place its process, and the resulting density of occurrence is indistinguishable from absolute stillness except for the ceaseless, continent-wide roar that is the sound of saturation itself.
