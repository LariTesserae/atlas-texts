There is no ground.  
There is only the mirror-crust.

A continuous sheet of slow glass—half mineral, half arrested liquid—covers the entire volume of this place. It lies only a hand-span above the true floor, flexing under the weight of any body that walks it, yet never breaking. Every footstep births a spreading bruise of faint opalescence; every removal of weight leaves a diminishing ring that fades like the last ripple in a pond. The crust is not transparent. Light does not cross it. Instead, each photon that strikes its upper surface is swallowed, re-emitted sideways along the layer itself, and travels like a slow tide until it leaks upward again somewhere else. The result is a low, sourceless glow, never brighter than the last breath of dusk, rippling whenever motion disturbs the medium.

Above the crust the air is saturated. Temperature hovers a few degrees above the dew point; the vapor does not condense into rain but into momentary droplets that assemble along the ceilings of hollow stone arches carved directly into the living rock overhead. The droplets tremble, swell, and release again—never falling, simply dissolving back into the ambient mist. The act of their formation and collapse produces a persistent, layered hiss that never ceases and never changes pitch. Within the hiss are harsher, percussive accents: a sudden brittle report when one droplet cluster reaches critical size and bursts outward rather than inward; a low, wet thud when another cluster is absorbed too quickly by a neighboring patch of warmer glass. The cavern refuses silence; its acoustics have settled into this single, loud equilibrium.

The cavern itself is not large. A chamber perhaps twelve strides across and four high, its walls and ceiling formed by the same slow glass that coats the floor. Here and there the layer thickens into bulbous extrusions—cylindrical, veined, unpolished—suggesting that the mineral continues to accrete in directions no eye can follow. These nodules occasionally align into deliberate rows, spaced like joists, and the spaces between them are bridged by taut strands of the same substance pulled into hair-fine filaments. The strands thrum whenever a footstep moves the crust, turning the entire structure into a single idiophonic instrument that answers every impact with its own fixed chord.

No biology remains. The only movement besides the visitor is a sluggish circulation of the mineral itself. In places the crust darkens to hematite luster; in others it brightens to a nacre that refuses to reflect any form. These patches migrate at the speed of centuries, never quite erasing previous boundaries yet never quite repeating them. The net effect is a surface that is recognizably the same shape every instant, yet microscopically rewritten. Any artificial objects introduced here—cloth, bone, worked flint—undergo the same gradual migration. A dropped blade will, after an unknowable duration, have its edge aligned with a new mineral grain, its rust scoured into microscopic deltas that flow into the crust and disappear.

The density of presence is therefore not life but pressure. The air, the slow glass, the arches, the filaments, and the migrating patches all occupy the same narrow envelope with no intervening vacancy. A body standing inside experiences a continuous low-grade compression, as if the entire micro-cosmos leans inward at once without ever quite arriving. Yet the compression produces no motion. Nothing accelerates; nothing decays. Equilibrium is total, loud, and unbreakable.
