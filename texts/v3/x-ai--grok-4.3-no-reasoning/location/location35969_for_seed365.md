The High Mist Gardens exist in a single, perfectly bounded meadow no larger than a cathedral nave, yet the meadow is not open. It is an old, shallow crater, its rim a living wall of blackwood trees whose trunks have grown together into a single, seamless parapet. These trees stand barely twenty paces across at their thickest, yet their crowns knit so tightly that the sky above is a single, pocked disc of dim daylight no brighter than late twilight. Inside this wall the land rises in slow, terraced waves, each level no more than a child’s stride in height, each wave lipped with a rim of living, dripping stone.

The stone is born wet. Every few minutes a small, slow exhalation of vapor rises from beneath each terrace, condenses on the underside of the blackwood canopy, and falls back as a single, perfect droplet that strikes with the sound of a fingertip on glass. The droplets collect in bowl-shaped depressions left by earlier droplets; these depressions never overflow, because every time one reaches its limit the water is drawn downward through invisible channels and emerges again on the next lower terrace as a new droplet. Thus the meadow is both a reservoir and a clock, and the clock is always at the same hour.

Because the terraces are stone yet still growing, their edges remain knife-thin and translucent, veined with pale root-hairs that drink the falling droplets before they touch the surface. Between the terraces the ground is not soil but a fine, pale grit—almost dust—dotted with black, fist-sized corms that never sprout yet never rot. From each corm rises a single, hair-thin filament that climbs until it meets the canopy; there it branches into a three-dimensional net that catches light and moisture alike. These nets do not overlap; each corm claims its own volume of air and holds it like a private sky. When wind occasionally slips through a seam in the blackwood wall, the filaments sway once and then freeze again, preserving the exact distances between them.

Fauna here is both legion and soundless. Tiny, six-winged slugs crawl along the undersides of the filaments; their wings are transparent and beat at a frequency too high for human ears, so the meadow is filled with motion yet remains acoustically still. Larger creatures—soft-bodied, many-limbed things the size of open hands—drift between terraces on silk parachutes no thicker than breath; they descend only when a droplet is about to fall, alight for the instant it takes to drink, then lift away before the next droplet forms. Predators exist, but their hunt is purely positional: a predator simply occupies the exact space another creature will occupy in the next moment, so the prey vanishes without struggle or sound.

The built element is the terraces themselves. They were not carved; they were coaxed upward over generations by the same hands that still tend the corms. These hands belong to a sparse population of tall, slow-moving people who live inside the blackwood wall, each person assigned one terrace level and never more. Their tools are lengths of the blackwood itself, fire-hardened at one end and kept damp at the other so the wood never splits. They do not speak; communication occurs when two people stand on adjacent terraces and let their filaments braid together for a few seconds, exchanging chemical messages through the living net. Because the braid can be made or unmade in a single breath, the society remains both connected and solitary, its population density exactly equal to the number of terraces.

Weather inside the crater is generated by the crater. Every third droplet cycle the blackwood wall breathes inward, pulling cooler air from whatever lies beyond; the air sinks, chills the stone, and triggers another round of condensation. The temperature never rises above the dew point, so nothing ever dries. Because nothing dries, the pale grit never becomes dust; because the grit never rises, the filaments never tear. The system is closed, perfect, and slightly larger each year as new corms are planted at the precise spacing required to keep the meadow “cathedral-sized.” When the last available terrace is claimed, the people inside the wall will simply stop planting; the crater will continue its quiet labor, unchanged, until the blackwood wall grows another ring outward and a new level of terraces can begin.

In this place, erosion is the opposite of wearing away. It is the patient addition of one droplet-width of stone each time a droplet falls, so the terraces are always new yet always older than memory. Nothing here is loud, yet nothing is still; the only rule is perfect spacing, and the only sound that ever escapes the blackwood wall is the single, crystalline note of a droplet meeting its appointed depression exactly when it should.
