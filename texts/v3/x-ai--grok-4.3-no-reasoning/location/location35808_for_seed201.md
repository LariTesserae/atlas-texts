High on a knife-ridged shoulder of stone the size of a cathedral nave—walls no wider than a wagonbed, yet falling sheer on either side into cloud-lit chasms—the air is crystalline and so thin it burns the sinuses. Bright light, unsoftened by haze, hammers off pale, freshly cracked faces of rock that still carry the pale gray of marrow. Every surface feels newborn; corners are knife-edged, and loose grains tinkle downslope at the slightest shift in weight.

The ridge is not a single blade but a linked sequence of summits no farther apart than a shout carries; between each summit the stone dips into a shallow saddle, the only place soil can gather. There, within a pocket no larger than a cottage floor, dark needles of hardy conifer and mats of jade-green cushion plants crowd together, their roots threading through a thin frost-heave crust. Condensed droplets bead on every needle tip, freeze by night into faceted granules, and melt again at first light, releasing a soft, almost inaudible fizz. The water never stands in pools; it slips downward along the minute furrows left by yesterday’s snowmelt, re-freezes in hairline cracks, and widens them a finger’s breadth each season.

Fauna here is not measured by the eye but by sign and echo. Tiny springtails pop in and out of the frost crust like static sparks. At intervals, a marmot the color of wet slate slips across a saddle, its claws ticking on stone, then vanishes down a cleft no wider than a wrist. Above, invisible against the glare, raptors carve slow arcs, the only motion across the empty sky. Their faint mewing, when it comes, arrives already thinned by altitude, so the ridge seems quieter afterward.

Nothing metal shows; the human marks are three things only. First, a waist-high ring of unmortared slate tucked into one saddle, its stones fire-blackened on the inner faces—an old wind-break still used by whoever climbs this high in summer. Second, a single length of braided root-fiber rope slung between two pinnacles, its ends threaded through natural holes and weighted with frost-split cobbles; the rope sags but has not yet parted. Third, at the lowest point between the two highest spires, someone once knapped a nodule of glassy basalt into a thumb-scraper, then left it balanced on the ridge crest like a surveying token. The tool is bleached by ultraviolet and still sharp.

Wind seldom rises above a persistent murmur, but when gusts do funnel between the spires the sound is a dry whisk, like fleece drawn across stone. Otherwise, the loudest noise is the muted drip of meltwater running from one ice-wedged fracture to the next, an errant clock ticking away centuries in droplets. Light floods without warmth; shadows are so clean they look scissored. Underfoot, every step rings once, then silence again.
