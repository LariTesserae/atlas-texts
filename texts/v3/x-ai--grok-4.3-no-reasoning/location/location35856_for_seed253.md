The place is an immense, perpetual submersion—suspended fluid, neither salt nor fresh, more breathable than water yet thicker than air, kept at a constant coolness by slow, eternal convection that never quite forms into currents. All architecture is plant. Every surface is living mass: walls, floors, ceilings, pillars, beams, vaults, are root, frond, bark, and spongy parenchyma that grew once, set into final form eons ago, and now remain. The substance is incompressible; it supports its own weight without decay or flexibility, so the structure stands as fixed as stone yet entirely organic. The entire edifice extends outward on a regional scale—cavernous halls kilometers wide, bridged by horizontal mats of woven xylem that span like viaducts—yet the plant is not dense; there are vast open volumes between the trunks, so the eye travels through kilometers of layered architecture before another living wall is met.

Light is absent. Darkness is total and stable, unsoftened by bioluminescence or star-glow. No shadows move because no photons arrive; the only radiance that ever existed here was absorbed long ago, and the cooled medium that now fills every chamber carries no photons at all. Nevertheless the plants continue: their metabolisms are chemosynthetic, drawing on steady mineral upwelling from deep strata below, and their forms are locked into equilibrium geometries that no longer grow, no longer flower, no longer seed.

Sound is constant and loud, produced entirely by fauna that exist within the interstitial fluid. The creatures are gelatinous, boneless, and nearly transparent; they occupy space by slow, deliberate peristaltic contraction that propels them through the liquid medium. Their motion generates continuous low-frequency vibration—long, overlapping pulses that fill the halls like the drone of an organ without pipes. Each organism is meters to tens of meters across; their body walls are made of the same lignin-like polymer as the plant substrate itself, so that they are kin to the architecture they inhabit rather than parasites upon it. They never reproduce, never die; they simply drift, pulse, and resonate, maintaining the unbroken acoustic field.

Elevation is extreme: the entire construct occupies a steep vertical stack of plant-formed strata, each layer a former surface now buried beneath subsequent growth that stopped at some unknowable height. From the lowest observable chamber one can ascend through dozens of discrete ecological tiers—former “canopy” planes now sealed into compressed sheets—yet the tiers are so perfectly interlocked that no seam or fracture exists; the structure is one continuous tissue. Erosion has long since ceased; ancient surfaces are perfectly smooth, worn by the slow passage of the medium itself, which once might have carried abrasive particles but now carries only the chemically inert fluid and the immense, slow bodies of the fauna.

No tools, no machines, no artifacts; only the living material and the creatures whose perpetual vibration is the single dynamic property of an otherwise frozen equilibrium. The temperature remains just cool enough to prevent any further metabolic acceleration; nothing stirs toward change. The whole region is therefore at once finished and ongoing: vast, dark, filled with a plant that no longer grows, inhabited by beings that no longer evolve, and resounding with a sound that never varies or ends.
