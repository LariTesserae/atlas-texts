The first thing to know is that none of this is still water in the ordinary sense. The entire landscape sits beneath a single unbroken body of cold liquid that reaches many hundreds of meters above the highest peaks. In places the water is almost perfectly transparent, tinted only by distance; elsewhere it carries swirls of glassy particulates that look like suspended ice. These currents never settle; they are driven by a planetary-scale exchange in which cooler, denser water sinks along the western horizon and warmer water rises from vents deep beneath the eastern cliffs. Because the medium is everywhere liquid, the sky and sea are the same continuous volume; what humans would call “weather” occurs as moving fronts of temperature and particulate within the fluid itself.

The terrain that exists beneath this liquid is a plateau of basalt and compressed sedimentary stone, its surface carved into long, undulating terraces rather than sharp peaks or valleys. The rock is scoured smooth by millennia of drifting particulates—fine black glass, crystalline calcium needles, and threads of olivine that have abraded the basalt into gentle curves. Where the terraces break, they form broad shelves rather than cliffs; the drop from one shelf to the next is never more than a few body-heights, so the landscape feels like an endless stair left open to the currents.

Across these shelves grow vast meadows of sessile organisms that resemble enormous ferns, each frond a single translucent blade many meters across and only a few centimeters thick. The blades are anchored by holdfasts of living stone—calcite lattices secreted by the plant itself—that rise from the rock like low, circular walls. Between the holdfasts the basalt is bare; the meadows are therefore sparse, each cluster of blades separated by wide lanes of polished stone through which currents flow freely. The fronds themselves are cold to the touch; their metabolism slows dramatically below a critical threshold, so they remain nearly motionless for long periods, then pulse once, expelling a visible cloud of micro-spores that drift until they settle on another shelf.

Scattered among the meadows are low, hand-shaped shelters built from the same calcite the plants secrete. They are not houses in the usual sense; each is a single curved slab, open on one side, just large enough for a person to sit inside with legs drawn up. The slabs are grown rather than quarried: the inhabitants (who have long since vanished) encouraged the plants to lay down extra layers of calcite in prescribed curves, then harvested the shelters whole. Some still stand intact, their inner surfaces spiraled with shallow grooves where tools once rested. Others have fractured; the broken edges are rounded again by the same drifting particulates that polish the rock.

Light here is faint and directional. Far above the plateau a perpetual ceiling of high-pressure cloud—dense, super-cooled mist—absorbs almost all surface illumination. What reaches the terraces is a diffuse, blue-gray radiance that seems to arrive from everywhere at once and nowhere in particular. In the distance, where the plateau drops away, this light is interrupted by slow-moving pulses of brighter luminescence: colonies of photoluminescent bacteria that ride the currents like schools of fish, blooming and fading on a timescale of hours. Their light travels in long vertical shafts, sometimes caught and bent by temperature gradients so that the shafts appear to bend around unseen obstacles.

Sound fills the liquid continuously. The dominant note is a low, rolling infrasound produced by the flexing of the fern-like blades as they pulse; each flex releases a pressure wave that travels for kilometers before attenuating. Layered above this is a sharper, irregular clicking: calcite crystals suspended in the currents colliding with one another and with the rock. At irregular intervals a deeper boom arrives from the east—the venting of super-heated fluid from fissures in the plateau’s buried foundation. The boom is followed by a visible shimmer as the temperature front races outward; the fern blades nearest the vent curl inward for several minutes, then slowly unfurl again.

Nothing with nerves or muscle moves through this landscape. The only life is the vegetation and the microbial light-colonies. Their presence, however, is sufficient to maintain an unbroken metabolic rhythm: the plants fix dissolved minerals into calcite, the microbes oxidize trace organics released by the plants, and the currents redistribute both the minerals and the organics across the terraces. The system is therefore active in the biological sense even though it contains no animals; the pace is simply so slow that a human observer might watch for days before noticing any change.

At the western edge of the plateau the terraces give way to a steeper descent—a submerged escarpment whose face is striped with horizontal bands of darker and lighter rock. The bands record ancient changes in current strength; each darker layer is richer in suspended particulates that settled when the water was colder and denser. Because the escarpment is open to the full force of the planetary circulation, the rock here is never still: faint vibrations travel through it at all times, a sub-audible counterpart to the infrasound above. The vibrations keep the calcite holdfasts from overgrowing the cliff face, preserving the stripes as permanent records of the water’s memory.

To stand anywhere on these terraces is to be inside a single, slow-moving organism whose organs are rock, plant, and current. The cold is constant, the darkness almost total, yet the space feels neither empty nor dead. It feels instead like a vast, half-lit library in which every surface is a page that has been written and rewritten by the same slow hand for longer than any alphabet has existed.
