The place is a chamber no larger than a lecture hall, yet the room is the entire world that can be visited. Its ceiling is a single low vault of poured stone, its walls and floor a continuous skin of riveted iron plates held together by brass tabs the size of palm leaves. Every surface has been machined to within a millimeter, then coated—again and again—in successive skins of green verdigris, brown rust, and mineral lacquer until nothing original remains except the conviction that nothing was ever un-made here.

Water is everywhere, yet it refuses to pool. It beads upon the iron, runs in hairline troughs that have been milled into perfect, repeating arcs, and is drawn by capillary threads into the vertical quilting of the walls. The same water reappears seconds later as steam that lifts from thousands of microscopic vents, re-condenses against the cool ceiling, and falls again in silver droplets the size of millet seeds. There is no reservoir, no inlet, no drain; the cycle is closed, endless, and perfectly balanced. The temperature never varies because the same steam that warms the air is what keeps the iron from overheating; warmth and moisture are the same current running through different phases.

The chamber contains almost nothing. Three colossal flywheels hang on massive axles that vanish into the far walls. Their rims are slotted, their spokes lattice-thin. Each wheel turns at the identical, unchanging speed; their teeth mesh with a system of fixed racks that line the ceiling like the ribs of some inverted creature. Because the racks are perfectly rigid and the wheels never accelerate or slow, their meshing produces a single, unwavering tone—an iron chord that sits inside the bones rather than the ears. Between the wheels, the volume is empty for twenty paces in every direction. No catwalk crosses it; there is no reason to walk where the sound already lives.

The only vegetation present has been cultivated in narrow grooves milled along the base of the walls. Rows of mirror-bright leaves, no taller than a knuckle, push upward through slots in a perforated apron of steel. They are fed by the same cycling condensate, pruned daily by an automatic arm that travels on a fixed track; their growth is measured, stunted, and uniform. They do not spread because there is nowhere else for roots to go, and they do not flower because flowering would alter an established equilibrium.

The only living things that move under their own power are a pair of eyeless, silver-scaled eels that travel inside a single horizontal conduit at ankle height. Their circuit is three body-lengths long; they circle it once per minute and then rest for the next fifty-nine. Their motion disturbs nothing and registers on no instrument; they exist only to keep the water slightly agitated so that surface tension never locks.

Nothing erodes because nothing is free to move against anything else. The wheels turn, the leaves grow, the eels orbit, but every vector is already matched by an opposing vector of identical magnitude. The chamber is therefore ancient not because it has lasted, but because it has nowhere left to change. The sound never rises or falls; the temperature never spikes or dips. The space feels crowded by its own completion rather than by objects. A visitor standing inside would sense, rather than hear, that the deafening chord is not a product of the machinery but simply the only remaining note that the whole system is still able to produce.
