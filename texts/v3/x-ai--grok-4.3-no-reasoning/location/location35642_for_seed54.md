The Dwelling

High above the ordinary world, a mineral city is being etched into existence from the same black basalt that forms the colossal stack beneath it. The rock is not solid in the conventional sense: it grows outward in thin, overlapping plates—each plate only a few centimeters thick and separated from its neighbors by a hairline film of condensed water that never quite becomes ice. The plates flex and rearrange themselves on a scale of days, so that streets widen or narrow, entire avenues tilt, or staircases migrate one riser at a time while inhabitants move about their business. Because the plates are porous at the microscopic level, water seeps continuously outward, coats every surface in a mirror-like skin, then slides inward again through capillary action. The city therefore breathes its own humidity in and out, creating an atmosphere that is never exactly still but never exactly turbulent either.

At this altitude the light is an absence. Permanent twilight comes from the cumulative glow of uncountable small bioluminescent nodules—each nodule a single engineered bacterium encased in a glassy cyst. The nodules are arranged in dense, overlapping clusters along every fissure, every ledge, every free-standing fin. They produce a soft, directional luminescence that rolls across surfaces as they pulse, making the city look as though it is slowly rotating inside its own darkness. Farther down the basalt stack, the nodules thin out; at greater distances they vanish altogether, leaving only the cold red-black of the rock.

The city’s inhabitants are audible before they become visible. They are not animals in the usual sense. They are resonant lattices—self-organizing, soft-bodied networks of crystalline fibers suspended in sacs of super-cooled brine. Each lattice resonates when the ambient pressure fluctuates, and the pressure fluctuates constantly because every movement of the basalt plates changes the volume of the interstitial water films. The lattices therefore produce a low, sustained chord that shifts microtonally as they drift through corridors or drift across plazas. When many of them gather in a single volume, the combined chord becomes loud enough to shake droplets loose from the weeping rock. The droplets fall, strike the lower plates, and ring like tuning forks, adding percussive accents to the drone. The result is an unbroken, city-scale sonority that changes only when the plates themselves migrate—something that happens on the order of seasons, not minutes.

Nothing grows here that can be called vegetation. Instead, the surfaces are colonized by mineral excrescences that resemble frost but are actually pressure-formed silicates extruded from the rock by the same flexing action that moves the plates. These excrescences branch and re-branch in fractal filaments until they meet and weld into larger structures. Over years they may span an entire plaza, forming canopies whose weight is supported by the continuous adjustment of the underlying plates. When a canopy reaches a certain mass, the plates beneath it slowly buckle downward, creating a shallow basin that fills with the water already present in the films; the basin becomes a shallow reservoir whose level changes only when the city rearranges itself again. The reservoirs are therefore the closest thing the city possesses to permanent geography.

Fauna—overwhelming in density—live in the space between the plates. The resonant lattices occupy the larger intervals; smaller motile forms occupy the micro-channels where water films are thinnest. These smaller entities are clusters of mineral grains held together by electrostatic fields that they modulate themselves; they move by alternately attracting and repelling the surrounding grains, effectively crawling along the walls of their channels. When enough of them gather, they create localized pressure drops that draw additional water from deeper within the rock. The water arrives already supersaturated with dissolved silicates; the grains therefore accrete new mineral mass as they move, leaving faint, glistening trails that harden within minutes. Over decades these trails become the fine capillary network that feeds the larger plates, so that the movement of the smallest organisms literally writes the next layer of the city.

The temperature is low enough that any water reaching the outer surface should freeze, yet it does not; the engineered bacteria keep a boundary layer of liquid through localized heat pumps that operate on quantum-tunneling principles. The same pumps run in reverse on the interior faces of the plates, chilling the brine that contains the lattices and thereby keeping the resonant chord stable. Because these temperature gradients are maintained by continuous, low-amplitude work distributed across the entire volume of the city, they do not register as mechanical activity; the city appears static to any observer whose attention span is shorter than a decade. Only when a lattice colony decides to migrate across a plaza can an outside witness see movement at all—and even then the migration takes the better part of a local year, the lattices drifting in single file while the plates beneath them inch aside to clear a path.

Nothing is finished. The basalt stack is still accreting new plates at its upper margin; older plates at lower levels are slowly dissolved back into the water films and redeposited elsewhere. Streets that existed last decade may have become vertical shafts; yesterday’s plazas may have contracted into narrow flutes. The resonant lattices sing whatever configuration they currently occupy, and the droplets falling from the weeping rock mark the rhythm of the next rearrangement. In this place, permanence and change are the same process observed at different speeds.
