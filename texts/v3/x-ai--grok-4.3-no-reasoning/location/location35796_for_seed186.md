In the high, wind-scoured strata where the air grows thin enough to sting the lungs, a mountain range stands so ancient that the living rock remembers its own slow breathing. Here the peaks are not jagged teeth but smoothed shoulders and shallow troughs, each contour worn by a million quiet rains and the dust that drifts down from upper glaciers no eye has ever named. The stone itself—banded slate shot with feldspar—has taken on the texture of wet, salt-crusted leather.

Between these shoulders, stone basins collect the moisture that falls only when the clouds sit low enough to brush the rock. The water gathers in shallow pools no wider than a human stride, their surfaces velveted with a thin film of oil exuded by a lichen so pale it almost looks like frost. The same lichen veins the surrounding rock faces in silver threads; when a pocket of sun breaks through the dark, these threads turn briefly incandescent, then fade again without reflection, as if the light were swallowed rather than bounced back.

From every lip of stone the vegetation drops in dense, hanging gardens. Needle-leaved shrubs no taller than knee-height cluster around each pool, their foliage the color of oxidized copper yet vivid with chlorophyll. Their roots, thick and cable-like, worm along cracks in the slate until they reach the next small reservoir; they drink, swell, and push new growth sideways instead of skyward, knitting a horizontal canopy that is almost a single organism across the entire slope. Between the shrubs, tiny white mushrooms fruit continuously from the damp inner bark of dead stems; they glow faintly blue when the dark is absolute.

The cold is not fierce enough to freeze standing water, but it chills every surface that the wind can reach. Breath condenses into momentary lace that hangs, collapses, and is replaced. Where the wind cannot reach—inside deep crevices or beneath the living root mats—the air grows almost warm and smells of wet stone and sweet, fermenting leaf matter. Condensation drips from the root undersides into the pools below, producing the only regular sound: a soft, unceasing patter like distant fingers on taut hide.

Everything is spaced by emptiness. The valleys between the shoulders are hundreds of meters across; a person standing on one rim sees only the next rim, blurred by mist and distance, the space between them so hollow that sound dies before it travels the full span. Occasional gusts of air move through these gaps without carrying odor or temperature change, merely displacing the mist that clings to the vegetation. The result is an acoustic hush broken only by the drip, the faint creak of contracting lichen, and the occasional sigh of wings too large and too slow to belong to birds.

Those wings belong to the gliders—creatures whose membrane spans are wider than a human height yet whose bodies weigh less than a loaf of bread. They ride the thin thermals that rise only inside the stone basins, spiraling upward until they vanish against the slate-dark sky. When they descend they do so without a sound, folding their wings at the last instant so their arrival on the water is a gentle dimpling rather than a splash. Their diet consists of the surface algae that thrives in the pools; they graze by skimming, never fully landing.

Deeper in the root labyrinth, beetle-like organisms the size of thumbs tunnel through the spongy wood. Their carapaces are translucent, and through them the faint luminescence of the mushrooms shows as moving constellations. They move slowly, pausing for long intervals as if listening. When two meet they exchange packets of spores rather than mating; the spores travel inside them for days before being deposited on a new stem. No single generation lasts more than a season, yet the tunnels they leave behind become permanent highways for water and later generations of the same insects.

Above all of this, the sky is not merely dark; it is the dense, bruise-colored dark of perpetual high-altitude twilight. No sun is visible, only a gradual brightening and dimming that never crosses into true day or true night. The rhythm is not twenty-four hours but something closer to four slow pulses—each marked by a slight warming of the air and an increase in the drip rate from the roots. During the brightest of these pulses the lichen threads briefly fluoresce, the pools shimmer with micro-currents, and the gliders rise in coordinated spirals that look, from a distance, like pale script being written and unwritten across the emptiness.

Nothing here was built; everything was persuaded into shape by the long negotiation between cold, stone, and the patient downward pull of water that arrives only when the clouds remember to descend. The mountains continue their imperceptible breathing, the pools keep their small inventories of moisture, and the emptiness between them remains wide enough that even a shout would be swallowed before it reached the next living rim.
