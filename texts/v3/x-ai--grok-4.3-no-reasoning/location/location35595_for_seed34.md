The **Vell** is a high continental basin where water never falls far.  Layer upon layer of pale limestone, polished by ten thousand rains, rise in shallow shelves like an immense stair.  Each tread is only a hand-span higher than the last, yet the entire flight stretches farther than the eye can travel before haze takes it.  Between the shelves, long clefts hold slow curtains of water that sheet downward without noise, threading from one basin to the next so steadily that the rock underneath is never truly dry, only cooled to a permanent slate-blue sheen.

Because the land lies well above the usual cloud deck of the world, moisture arrives not in storms but as an almost constant descending mist that forms on every exposed face and drips, strand by strand, into the clefts.  Walking along the rim of any single shelf you can look downward through thirty, forty, fifty translucent sheets of falling water, each catching a little of the overhead light and turning it into a gauze of milk-blue.  The air stays cool enough that the droplets do not bead into heavy rain; they remain minute and weightless until they strike stone, so the only audible sound is the muted plick of individual drops meeting pooled water somewhere far below—too faint and irregular to disturb the greater silence.

Vegetation is present yet meagre: a dark, resinous lichen spreads across the upper surfaces in sprawling medallions whose edges lift slightly where mist collects underneath, giving the impression that the rock itself is breathing.  In deeper clefts, bundles of pale moss hang like inverted sheaves; each bundle is anchored at a single point and sways when the slow currents of cooled air drift past.  There are no trees, no shrubs taller than a finger’s length—just the lichen and the mosses, enough to tint the stone but not enough to break the monochrome stone-and-water palette.

Life moves at the same measured pace.  Small, translucent snails traverse the lichen medallions in paths no wider than threads; their shells catch the light and momentarily become prisms before they slip back into shadow.  Now and then a larger shape, perhaps a flat-bodied amphibian, glides between two sheets of falling water and is gone again, leaving only the concentric quiver of the surface where it passed.  Nothing calls; the water itself supplies the only rhythm.

The scale here is continental, yet everything is local at once.  A day’s walk covers perhaps three risers of the immense stair; the next basin ahead seems identical to the last, yet the temperature of the mist drops a fraction of a degree with each tread downward, and the hue of the rock turns imperceptibly cooler.  Cities of a sort exist: human-scale clusters of dry-laid limestone blocks fitted into the natural clefts so precisely that the walls read as continuations of the geology.  Inside each cluster, walkways of the same stone cross the thread-falls on slender bridges, and small courtyards are left open so that the descending water can sheet across them at ankle height.  No fires burn; the air is already too laden with moisture, and any warmth is supplied only by the slow radiation stored in the stone.

Above all, the basin behaves like a single body.  When the planetary winds strengthen far beyond the horizon, a pressure wave travels through the entire system; every water curtain quivers at once, sending a faint, continent-wide ripple through the basins.  The lichen darkens a shade as extra mist condenses, and the snails pause until the motion passes.  Then the stair settles again into its balanced, endless descent of water over water over stone.
