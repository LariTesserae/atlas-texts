The place is a single vast bowl of stone, no larger than a college courtyard, yet the walls rise sheer for a thousand feet of layered, glass-like obsidian. All daylight that enters arrives from a narrow slit far overhead and is multiplied and sharpened by the curved facets; every surface throws reflections of that slit into every other surface, so the chamber never grows wholly dark. At intervals, sun-bright highlights roll across the obsidian like slow ripples of mercury, catching in hidden grooves and then draining away again as the angle changes.

The obsidian is cold—near frost-point—yet so dense it still radiates a residual warmth from its long-ago forging; cold droplets of condensation bead along its fluted ridges, gather weight, and fall. Each drop lands in one of the shallow, bowl-shaped hollows worn into the floor. Those hollows are no deeper than the spread of a hand, and they are all that remains of an older, slower erosion: the rock has been eating its own edges for centuries, rounding grain by grain until the surface beneath your feet is a single continuous lacquer of slick black glass.

Nothing grows here, but the stone itself is busy. Microscopic veins of quartz push upward through fractures like clear needles, crystallizing out of dissolved minerals that trickle down the walls whenever a condensation droplet finally reaches them. The quartz sprouts in tight, branching fans that catch the light and fracture it into tiny rainbows; after a few hours each fan collapses back into the bowl, dissolved again, only to re-form elsewhere. The floor is stippled with these momentary fans—dense constellations of refracted light that drift, reform, and vanish again.

At intervals the slit overhead funnels in a thin veil of mountain cloud. The cloud condenses against the chill glass into a brief, cold fog that drifts downward in sheets no thicker than a linen curtain. As each sheet settles, it deposits a rime of minute frost crystals that crackle underfoot, then melt into the waiting stone bowls before the next veil arrives. Between these quiet fogfalls, the chamber is almost silent: the only steady sound is the tiny, glass-bell note each falling droplet makes when it lands. The note is so faint that the space between notes seems longer than the note itself.

Scattered across the lower walls are the remnants of an earlier, deliberate presence—hand-chiseled notches spaced at shoulder height. Each notch contains a single smooth river pebble lodged like a plug; the pebbles are foreign here, water-rounded granite. When the cold contracts the obsidian enough, the plugs loosen and drop into the bowls below, striking that same bell-note before rolling to rest. After each fall, the notch refreezes shut. A handful of pebbles still wait above, minute accretions of the builders’ intent.

Nothing moves on its own here except water, frost, and quartz. The human sense of scale keeps trying to name the room a ruin or an altar or a cistern, but the obsidian refuses the label; every few minutes the light shifts and the chamber becomes, again, only itself: a condensed, hard place of glass, cold, and slow returning water.
