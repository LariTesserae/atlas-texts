A marsh that has been entirely translated into iron.

The ground does not exist beneath the pipes; it has been replaced by them. Thick conduits, some as wide as freight cars, others no thicker than wrists, lie stacked and dovetailed until their collective top-surface becomes the only level one can walk upon. Their metal has been allowed to oxidize for centuries—first ochre, then charcoal-black, then the blue-green verdigris of a final decay state—so the entire plain reads as one continuous patina. Water moves through the hollow veins of this infrastructure at a deliberate crawl, barely audible inside each tube but always present, a slow arterial throb rather than a flowing stream. Occasionally a joint fails or a flange weeps, and thin curtains of condensate hang for days between the pipes like translucent skin.

Above this endless grating the air is heavy with mist that never lifts. It forms where the warm interior of the pipes meets the persistently cool exterior; every exposed seam becomes a miniature weather system, a bead-string of droplets that grow, fall, bead again. The temperature hovers just above the dew point, low enough that breath fogs instantly yet never cold enough for frost. No natural substrate is visible anywhere; even the occasional vertical strut or catwalk is bolted through older pipes rather than soil.

Light arrives in isolated, mechanical pulses rather than day or night cycles. High on skeletal towers—towers themselves assembled from decommissioned pipe segments—sodium-orange beacons rotate in fixed intervals, casting conical beams that sweep the mist into momentary amber corridors before darkness swallows them again. Between sweeps the only luminance is the faint bioluminescent bloom of algae thriving inside translucent inspection windows; the glow is cool, steady, and utterly without warmth.

Sound is continuous yet shifting in texture rather than volume. The dominant note is the low-frequency thrum of pumps buried somewhere beneath the pipe plain, felt more than heard. Layered over it are the staccato bursts of pressure-relief valves, the long metallic moan of thermal contraction, and, every few hours, the resonant toll of a colossal bell—actually an inverted disused surge tank struck by its own counterweight. These events overlap rather than alternate, so at any given moment the sound field is a composite drone with sudden, chest-deep accents. The scale ensures that these noises never localize; one is always inside them, not near them.

Vegetation survives only in the most accidental pockets: a fan of salt-tolerant lichen on the underside of a horizontal run, a single fern rooted in the sediment trapped inside a cracked expansion joint. They appear almost curated, each specimen tens of meters from its nearest neighbor. No birds, no insects, no larger creatures; the only motion not mechanical is the slow migration of condensation across every surface, droplets sliding along pipe gradients like minute, directionless traffic.

Rules of the place reveal themselves gradually. Because every surface is engineered, every surface is also a potential route; one does not step off the network, merely chooses a different gauge of pipe. Water finds its own equilibrium across the web, filling low points until they overflow into the next segment. Temperature differentials are maintained by deliberate design—warm fluid runs through some pipes, chilled fluid through others—so the mist never dissipates. Repairs, when they occur, are performed by autonomous rigs that crawl along dedicated service rails; they move with insectile deliberation and never acknowledge a human presence, because there has never been one since the final maintenance crew walked away centuries ago.

In this engineered marsh the distinction between landscape and machine has collapsed; erosion here is the patient flaking of oxide, succession is the accrual of sediment inside inspection chambers, and the only ecology is the slow negotiation of pressure gradients inside an infrastructure that has outlived its operators yet continues, in perfect automatic obedience, to circulate water it will never be asked to deliver.
