The place has no water—none at all—and no possibility of it ever arriving. Instead, a tissue of translucent, near-living mineral occupies every available space: a vast, slowly pulsing lattice of organo-silicate sheets whose surfaces shimmer with the faintest hint of verdigris. Where the sheets meet the air they produce a low, irregular rattle, like thousands of transparent coins slid across one another; that rattling is the only sound water would have made, but here it is performed entirely by friction between plant-like glass.

The vegetation (or the thing that fills vegetation’s place) is the substrate itself. Fractal filaments of jade-green chlorosilicate root through the mineral sheets, threading them into colossal, open-weave towers. These towers rise in a grid that is less a city than an archive: every five or six hundred meters a narrow vertical cleft opens like a codex spine, and between clefts the towers fan outward in buttresses hundreds of meters wide, each composed of millions of paper-thin blades still growing, still breathing faint exhalations of oxygen that never become liquid. The towers are not built; they have simply never finished becoming plant.

Temperature sits just above freezing. The photosynthetic reactions are slow enough that the lattice’s color deepens to emerald only at midday, then fades to the blue of thin ice when night approaches. Because the air is so thin and dry, the chill feels sharper than its measurement, the way metal at five degrees bites harder than wood.

Elevation is considerable. The entire lattice is draped across the shoulders of an ancient shield whose granite heart has long since been replaced molecule by molecule. Nothing remains of the original mountain except the ghost of its curvature—a gentle convexity that still stretches twenty kilometers in each cardinal direction before the horizon begins to curve away. From any cleft one looks outward across tier after tier of similar latticework, each horizon slightly lower than the last, until distance dissolves them into a single pale plane.

Erosion ceased in some inconceivably remote epoch. The mineral sheets have settled into their final thickness; the towers do not shed fragments. Instead they add, by microns per century, a new layer that is chemically identical to the layer beneath. The surface therefore carries no rubble, only a fine, talc-like dust of powdered silica that records the footfalls of whatever moves across it and then slowly heals the impressions.

The built environment is indistinguishable from the living mineral. Streets are simply the intervals where the towers chose not to grow. They are perfectly straight for hundreds of meters, then bend according to crystallographic logic no human surveyor ever laid down. Bridges are spans where two opposing towers grew together and then fused, the seam still visible as a darker seam of deposited manganese. Lighting is absent; the plants themselves store photons in electron traps and release them over hours, giving every vertical surface a faint internal glow that moves like the second hand of an immense clock.

Technology here exceeds any catalog of current physics because it is not applied; it is the ground state of the matter. The chlorosilicate lattice maintains quantum-coherent domains centimeters across, allowing signals—chemical, photonic, electrostatic—to propagate with negligible loss. The towers therefore act simultaneously as dwellings, processors, archives, and skins. When an organism moves inside a cleft, the surrounding filaments reconfigure their internal band gaps to keep a pocket of slightly warmer air around it; that pocket is not climate control, it is metabolism.

Light reaching the surface is dim, arriving from a swollen orange primary whose disk never quite clears the perpetual haze of airborne pollen-minerals. Shadows are long even at noon. Yet within the lattice the stored light leaks outward again, so the interior of each cleft is paradoxically brighter than the open air. The contrast creates an optical moiré: one step forward and the world darkens; one step sideways and the walls ignite with moving veins of stored noon.

Fauna is abundant but weightless. The primary animals are clusters of buoyant gas-cells—hexagonal mylar-thin membranes filled with hydrogen they liberate from the mineral itself—tethered to the lattice by single filaments of contractile chlorosilicate. The clusters drift from cleft to cleft, grazing on the slow exhalation of sugars, their passage marked by a soft, continuous tearing sound as filaments are reeled in and out. Occasionally a cluster meets another and the two exchange membranes in a bright stutter of static; the encounter can last minutes or hours before they separate again.

Weirdness accumulates at the level of identity rather than appearance. The lattice is not a single organism, yet every filament contains the entire genome of the whole. When a tower collapses—an event so rare it has never been recorded in the present eon—its genes are already distributed; the fracture plane begins to regrow from both sides within hours, the two new faces sliding toward one another across the gap like living bookends. Because no water is required, the process never pauses.

Sound is loud because the lattice is never still. Every temperature shift causes the silica blades to expand or contract by fractions of a percent; the cumulative motion produces a continuous chorus of chimes, each tone corresponding to a different tower’s resonant length. At night the stored light cools and the chimes drop half an octave; at noon they rise again. The gas-cell fauna add harmonics: their hydrogen bladders ping softly when over-pressured, and their filament tethers scrape across the lattice in irregular arpeggios. The total effect is a single, evolving chord that fills every cleft and every open interval between towers.

The world is active at a geological tempo. The shield beneath the lattice still rises, a millimeter per thousand years, as residual heat from the planet’s core finds its way to the surface. That imperceptible uplift keeps the entire structure under slow tension; every cleft is a crack that is simultaneously trying to close and trying to widen. The tension is expressed in the lattice as piezoelectric micro-discharges that flash between blades too quickly for the eye, illuminating the interior of a tower for an instant like summer lightning behind cloud. Each flash slightly alters the local band gap, so the stored light in the affected region brightens by a single perceptible lumen for the next several hours—an after-image of tectonic motion expressed in photosynthesis.

Nothing here is ever finished, yet nothing ever arrives. The landscape is the record of an equilibrium so ancient it has forgotten how to end.
