The chamber opens thirty steps across and twice that deep, its ceiling lost in a haze of warm, milky light that arrives from above without any visible source. Condensation forms constantly on the rock overhead and falls as slow, almost reluctant drops that collect in shallow mineral bowls along the floor; the water does not pool or run, only re-seals the porous stone after each fall. The air is gentle, neither steam nor mist, but the memory of moisture.

At the scale of this single hollow, the stone has already finished becoming itself. Every surface is rounded and silky, pale as old bone where the water has lingered longest, darker where dryness rests longest between rains. No sharp fracture remains; edges have been returned to curves so long ago that the chamber feels less carved than breathed open. The floor dips in a slow, shallow basin—never more than ankle-deep at its center—yet holds only scattered pearls of water that shrink and re-form on cycles longer than an afternoon.

Nothing grows here in the usual sense; the vegetation rating is carried by minute, salt-tolerant crusts that bead across the driest patches like a second mineral skin. In the dampest angles a single variety of hair-thin, glassy filament extends from cracks—slowly, millimeters per decade—feeding on nothing visible and retreating when the light intensity changes. The crusts and filaments share no color; both are the shade of the stone itself, only glossier, distinguishable only by touch.

Fauna arrives in the pauses between droplets. Minute, pale, soft-bodied creatures the length of eyelashes emerge whenever a drop touches stone. They drink once, then return to threadlike burrows no wider than their own bodies. The sound of their movement is a single, dry whisper per emergence, quieter than breath. Larger, faintly translucent grazers, no bigger than thumbs, drift across the floor on invisible currents of warmer air, pausing at each crust to rasp it with minute, glassy radulae. Their passage is slower than a heartbeat; they might cross the chamber in the time it takes a human thought to finish.

Because nothing is built, boundaries are felt rather than seen: the light refuses to enter the side alcoves beyond a certain depth, the temperature refuses to rise above a single, constant warmth no matter how long one lingers. The light itself seems to be the rule-set; it keeps moisture cycling, keeps the slow fauna moving, keeps the mineral filaments from retreating entirely. Any sudden change—an unexpected dryness, a louder sound—fails to register; the chamber simply waits until the anomaly is reabsorbed into its own duration.

The quiet is not absence but pressure. Every sound that does occur—the faint tick of a droplet striking an already-wet surface, the whisper of the thumb-sized grazers, the minute scraping of radulae—remains inside the chamber exactly as long as it takes the next slow cycle to begin, then folds back into the same milky brightness that never quite becomes glare.
