A vast, warm plateau floats at the very fringe of a gas-shell planet, where atmosphere thins to an almost perceptible edge. Its surface is an alloy of compressed sulphur-tinted regolith and smooth sheets of obsidian-like slag that together form a single horizon: seamless and gently curved, as though the whole world were the dome of a slow forge.

Scattered across this floor are city-fragments that once touched. Walk between two of them and you might spend days crossing open ground so empty it seems to pull colour from the sky. Each fragment is a lacework of sintered stone, poured-metal arches, and hollow towers that taper like thermals. Their surfaces carry the faint polish of centuries of dust-wind, yet the metal under that polish still hums—subsonic and hardly audible—with residual heat. No human has added to the structures in living memory; they simply settle, warp, and re-anneal under their own patient gravity.

Fauna here moves in geometric pulses. Herds of spindle-legged mineral mites migrate between shards of city like chess pieces repositioning across months rather than minutes. Each mite’s carapace is a tessellation of crystalline plates; they click once, faintly, when the temperature gradient between sun and night-side stone balances. When a herd halts, their plates tilt in unison, catching the light so that the plain briefly glitters like a field of dark mirrors.

There are no plants, but in their place rise slow, self-assembling lattices of metallic lichen. The structures extrude from the slag in hairline needles that bloom, over years, into shelf-like plates. The plates align themselves with the planetary magnetic lines, then discolour to burnt umber, shedding a fine dust that the wind tugs into long, horizontal veils.

Temperature remains temperate because the plateau itself is a vast heat reservoir. Below its skin, capillary veins of molten sulphur circulate at a crawl, reaching the surface only through hairline vents that stain the sky with threads of pale yellow smoke. These threads dissipate so slowly that they appear painted. The city-fragments collect this smoke in their open plazas, letting it settle as a translucent sediment that later sinters into new paving.

Daylight is ordinary—yellow-white, filtered through thin high haze—but the geometry of the plateau’s edge bends it into slow-moving pools of brightness. A single hour of dusk may stretch across several terrestrial weeks because the entire landform is drifting along a tide-locked orbit. When the edge finally passes into night, the city metal cools by degrees so minute that the sound of contraction travels only as infrasonic tremors felt underfoot.

At the plateau’s outer perimeter, the terrain drops sheer into absolute nothing—an atmospheric cliff where gas density falls so fast that breathable pressure is gone within a few metres. Along that cliff, wind curls in long, slow rotors; the mites sometimes gather there to drink dissolved ions the rotor mixes out of the upper sky. The city-fragments nearest the drop-off lean outward, as if listening to the quiet thunder of the gas-shell’s deeper currents, yet they have remained poised for millennia without collapsing.

Sound travels reluctantly. The ambient drone of mites and lichen is so subdued that a single metallic fracture somewhere across the plateau registers as the only event loud enough to carry, arriving hours after the fracture itself. By then, the event is already ancient to its own small world; the plateau simply folds the memory into its slow erosion, another settled layer on the long-cooled skin of an industrial afterlife.
