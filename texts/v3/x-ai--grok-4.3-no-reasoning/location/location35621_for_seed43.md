The memory of soil is gone, so they carved soil from other places and carried it down in baskets. They have been doing this for longer than any living person can remember.

The hollow that contains the village is not a cave. It was a mine once, or perhaps it is still a mine, only the ore they sought became the space itself. The ceiling is a shallow inverted bowl of packed clay, supported by a forest of wooden columns that were once living trunks. Each column has been scorched black on its south-facing side by generations of lamp-flame. Between them hang catenary nets of woven root-fiber, heavy with condensation; water beads along the strands and falls in deliberate, spaced drops that mark the hours more reliably than any clock.

The floor is not level. It is a single continuous surface of fired brick laid in overlapping herringbone patterns, each brick stamped with the thumbprint of its maker. Where one course meets another the bricks are staggered so that a person walking must place their feet exactly where the previous walker’s feet have worn shallow depressions. The brick is always faintly warm, even when the lamps are banked, as though the earth beneath it still remembers how to be molten.

The air is thick with the smell of wet clay, heated resin, and the fatty smoke of rendered fat used for illumination. Every surface sweats. The walls are covered in a continuous bas-relief of overlapping handprints pressed into the clay while it was still soft; the prints are of every size, and they overlap until they form a kind of second skin. When the lamps gutter, the prints seem to move. No one can say whether they were made by living hands or by the living hands of the dead.

Water moves through the village without visible pipes. At irregular intervals thin copper-colored roots push through the packed-clay ceiling. Each root ends in a bulbous nodule the size of a fist; every few minutes the nodule contracts and releases a measured pulse of water into a shallow brick channel that has been cut into the floor. The channels intersect and branch according to rules no outsider has ever been taught. Sometimes the water is clear; sometimes it carries fine particles of red clay that settle in the lowest concavities of the herringbone and stain them the color of old blood. Children are taught to read these stains the way other children read clouds.

The village is loud because everything is small and close and nothing is still. The brick floor conducts footfalls so that a single person walking produces a soft thunder that travels the entire bowl. The condensation nets sing when the temperature drops; each fiber has a different tension and therefore a different pitch, and the combined sound is like a slow rain on taut hide. The nodules in the ceiling make a soft percussive exhalation when they release water, always three rapid beats followed by two slower ones, as if they are answering each other across the space. Somewhere deeper in the network of chambers a larger mechanism turns; its rhythm is slower, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

Fauna here is mostly invertebrates that have learned to live on the brick and the root-fiber. Pale, soft-bodied isopods the size of thumbs move in slow caravans along the warm mortar lines, grazing on the thin films of algae that grow where the lamps are brightest. Larger creatures—something between a salamander and a cricket—live inside the hollow columns; they drum against the wood with specialized legs, producing the steady background rhythm that newcomers mistake for their own pulse. The only flying things are small moths whose wings are the exact color of the clay; they never rise higher than the lowest net, and when they land they fold themselves into the handprints until they disappear.

The people move with the sound. Their tasks are timed to the drip cycles and the percussive nodules. At the third beat of every release, a group of women begins grinding clay in stone mortars; the sound of the pestles joins the existing noise until it is impossible to separate one rhythm from another. Children carry baskets of spent lamp-fat to the lowest point of the floor, where a slow drain carries it away; the fat travels in a thin gleaming ribbon that catches the lamplight and makes it flicker.

Nothing here is natural in the sense of untouched, yet nothing feels artificial because the materials remember their former lives. The wooden columns still smell of resin when the air grows warm. The brick still holds the ghost of the straw that tempered it. The handprints in the walls are still the size of the hands that made them. The only thing that has been fully transformed is the idea of surface; down here, surface is simply whatever is nearest to the skin at any given moment—brick underfoot, clay overhead, the warm breath of a neighbor an arm’s length away.

The lamps are simple clay bowls filled with rendered fat and fitted with wicks of twisted root. They are never allowed to go out completely. When one begins to gutter, the nearest person takes a spill from another lamp and tends it, the movement as automatic as breathing. In this way the light travels through the village without any single person being responsible for its continuity.

When the temperature rises, the clay walls expand; when it falls, they contract. The sound of this movement is a low, continuous creaking that underlies everything else. The people have learned to sleep through it, or perhaps they have never known silence long enough to miss it. The village breathes with the temperature, and the breathing is loud enough to be heard from the surface if one stands in the right place and listens for the difference between wind and work.
