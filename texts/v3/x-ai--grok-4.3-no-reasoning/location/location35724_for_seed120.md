A steaming crevice within a mineral-veined obsidian wall—less a crack than an unfinished sentence of rock—has been widened just enough for a single person to stand upright. Its mouth is choked by a living patchwork of woven stems, slick with condensation, that pulse faintly as though still learning how to breathe. Beyond that green membrane the space opens into a chamber no wider than a burial niche yet packed to the ceiling with every object the wall has so far produced.

Slabs of new basalt lie half-formed in their parent layer, still soft enough that fingermarks remain in them. From their cooling surface a million hair-thin strands of water are pulled upward by an invisible tension, each droplet hissing as it meets the hot stone and re-condenses into the next strand. The entire ceiling has become a slow-motion rain reversed: water climbs rather than falls, collects in silver blisters, then bursts into steam when the blisters touch the lava’s hidden warmth. The resulting mist never clears; it simply thickens until the air itself feels like warm cloth pressed against the skin.

The woven plants are not truly plants; they are thin ropes of glassy pumice extruded from the wall and seeded with living moss that drinks the rising vapor. They knot and unknot themselves at a speed just below the threshold of sight, continuously re-arranging the boundary between inside and outside. Where two vines cross, they fuse, forming tiny mouths that inhale the steam and exhale spores no larger than droplets. Each spore lands on a cooling rock and begins to etch a shallow groove; the grooves connect, become channels, and carry the next generation of spores farther from their parent mouth.

There is no soil. Every surface is a single, unbroken substance: a pressurized emulsion of cooling glass, captive water, and nascent root. Where the emulsion meets the crevice’s limestone backing, the stone is still being dissolved. Calcified ribs protrude like unfinished beams; they are not structural yet, but they already hum at a pitch too low to be heard, only felt through teeth. When enough ribs accumulate, the crevice will decide—without warning—to lurch another hand-span wider. The woven ceiling will stretch to accommodate, threads singing as they lengthen, and a new sheet of molten glass will be offered to the air for shaping.

The only fauna are the spores themselves. A few have drifted far enough down-current to reach the limestone ribs; there they blossom into translucent isopods the size of thumbnails. Their shells are momentary—each wave of heat liquefies them again—so they never remain long enough to be counted. The sound they make when they vanish is a single, brittle click absorbed into the larger chorus of dripping, hissing, and the low seismic murmur of stone still deciding what shape to take.

Light arrives only as a pale sulfur glow that leaks between the vines whenever the chamber’s pressure momentarily drops. In those instants the walls show their true unfinished nature: every surface is still a cross-section, an interrupted growth ring of some immense, directionless organism that has not yet agreed upon an outside. When the pressure rises again, the glow vanishes and the chamber returns to its crowded dusk.

Nothing here is complete, yet nothing waits. The heat, the water, the woven glass, and the dissolving stone all act at once, each pressing the others toward whatever form the next rupture will allow.
