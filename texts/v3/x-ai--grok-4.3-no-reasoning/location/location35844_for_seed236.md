Here the world is small, held within the cathedral of a single cliff chamber whose ceiling is only thirty meters above a floor of pale salt that has never known water. The stone walls have been left sharp by whatever recent heaving of the crust lifted this pocket into being; every fracture is still new, each shard and facet still faceted, nothing yet rounded by wind or time. These walls are laced with an impossible fabric of vines—thick, glossy ropes that rise vertically, layer on layer, until their highest canopies press against the salt-white stone above like a living fresco. Each vine is studded with dark, broad leaves that give off a slow vapor; the chamber’s only moisture arrives in this manner, exhaled in a patient, almost invisible breath that never condenses into droplets.

Between the vines, small creatures move like drops of living ink. They are beetles the size of thumbnails, wingless, their backs the precise shade of the salt they walk upon. They travel a few body-lengths in an hour, scraping microscopic threads of salt crystal into food; the faint rasping of their mandibles is the loudest sound in the chamber, and even that is felt as texture rather than noise. Occasionally a larger, slower animal—a lizard whose scales mimic the layered leaves—passes along a single horizontal vine. Its feet grip without tremor; its tail drags a slow, continuous furrow in the salt that erases itself again within hours.

The air is warm but never hot; it feels like the temperature of skin left in shade. Light enters through a single fracture high in one wall. The crack is knife-thin, admitting a plane of ordinary daylight that slices downward and is then multiply refracted by lenses formed inside translucent nodules on the vines. These lenses drift a few centimeters each day, pulled by the slow expansion of the tissues beneath, so that the shafts of light crawl across the floor in perfect, silent arcs measured in seasons rather than seconds. At any given moment the light that reaches the salt is still daylight, uncolored and unhurried.

Nothing here is built in the human sense, yet the density of living matter is such that movement is possible only by pressing aside leaves or stepping exactly where the beetles have already cleared narrow aisles. The entire chamber reads like a city without architects: every available surface is occupied, every volume between surfaces subdivided by stems, by the translucent skins of slow-growing fungi that hang in curtain-like sheets between vines. New chambers are being created at the same deliberate speed; when a strand of vine thickens enough to touch the stone ceiling, its tip flattens and begins to lift the salt in microscopic increments, widening the crack by the breadth of a grain each decade. Over centuries the crack will become a skylight, the skylight a courtyard, and the chamber will enlarge by a single room-width. Until then it remains a sealed ecology, warm, quiet, and complete, in which nothing rushes except the eye trying to follow the minuscule traffic of salt-beetles across the white floor.
