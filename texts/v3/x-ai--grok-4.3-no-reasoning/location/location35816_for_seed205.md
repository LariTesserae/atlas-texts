The place is a living continent whose interior has been entirely surrendered to vegetable tissue. From orbit it registers as a single, unbroken emerald lung; on foot it is a three-dimensional latticework of trunks, vines, rhizomes, stems, leaves and flowers that have interlocked so completely that nothing remains which can be called “ground.” Every surface one stands on, climbs, or leans against is a freshly grown interface of bark, bast, or phellem. The mass is continuous horizontally and vertically, because growth occurs in all three dimensions at once, a tissue culture writing itself across an entire planet’s tropics.  

At the human scale the lattice forms alleys, galleries, and chambers large enough to swallow a storm front. Those chambers can be miles across; inside them the humidity condenses onto broad, upward-curving leaves, runs down in braided cataracts, and collects into slow-moving reservoirs. From time to time an entire slope of tissue will relax its turgor, spilling a temporary lake that spends itself across an interior plain of moss-like parenchyma. The water never lingers for long. Within hours new cell walls grow over it, turning the lake into a spongy aquifer until the next relaxation cycle repeats the process.  

Heat rises from every layer. Metabolic respiration and solar absorption together keep the average ambient temperature at a constant, oppressive 38 °C even at night. The warmth drives convection along natural flues—vertical tubes of living xylem that act as chimneys. When the pressure inside these flues exceeds a threshold, the tops open like valves, expelling a visible column of vapor and pollen that can rise ten kilometers before the evening collapse of the convective cell. The noise of these events is a continuous roar; the valves themselves slam and hiss like vast bellows, and the expelled air is further agitated by the wingbeats of the megafauna that ride the thermals.  

Those animals are everywhere. The largest are dirigible herbivores whose bodies are themselves gardens: seed-studded hide that sprouts epiphytic vines, hollow bones colonized by photosynthetic algae, and wing membranes veined with chlorophyll. They drift in slow arcs, browsing the canopy at altitude, their passage producing subsonic chords that reverberate through the living substrate. Beneath them swarm flocks of smaller flyers whose exhalations, synchronized at dusk, produce a thunderclap of coordinated breath. Ground-level megafauna are less common; the lattice is so dense that only sessile or burrowing forms persist. Huge, hemoglobin-rich rootworms move through the vascular system of the continent like corpuscles, feeding on excess starch and excreting nutrient gels that nearby tissues immediately absorb. Their passage is a continuous low-frequency slither that can be felt through the soles of bare feet.  

Primitive tool-using hominins exist in scattered bands. Their settlements are not villages so much as continuous modifications of the substrate: they coax particular vines to thicken into platforms, train flowering stalks to curl into rain-shedding domes, and selectively prune xylem to create internal chimneys for controlled smoke. Tools are chipped from glassy phytoliths or are grown outright—axes whose handles are still living petioles that will re-root if left unattended. They never cut deeper than the top few centimeters; any deeper incision triggers an immune response that floods the wound with astringent sap, quickly sealing it in a scar of callus as hard as stone.  

Light filters through an ever-changing mosaic of leaves. At noon the photosynthetic ceiling is so dense that only a shifting, aqueous dappling reaches the lower levels. At dawn and dusk, however, entire galleries turn translucent as the guard cells of trillions of stomata relax; the planet-sized organism breathes outward, and a brief interval of honey-colored clarity allows the hominins to navigate.  

Sound is the primary weather. Thunderclap breathing, valve detonations, rootworm tremors, pollen-bomb expulsions, the ultrasonic squeaks of symbiotic bats that pollinate deep interior flowers—together they produce a continuous broadband roar that vibrates even dense bone. The hominins have developed a gestural sign language whose syntax is based on pressure changes rather than pitch; they press their palms to living walls to feel the grammar of the place.  

Erosion, in the classical sense, barely exists. Where rainfall does run, it follows channels that the living tissue is continually rebuilding. The continent maintains its topography not through deposition and uplift but through differential growth rates: high ridges thicken faster, valleys are allowed to relax into spongy aquifers, and the resulting forms remain stable for centuries before being remodeled by another growth wave.  

At the largest scale the system behaves like an organism on a planetary surface. Storms that form over the interior generate their own closed circulation, dropping precipitation that never leaves the lattice; the edges of the continent simply feather into salt marshes where vegetation meets ocean, and the transition zone is a hundred kilometers wide. The planet’s weather is therefore an emergent property of a single metabolism rather than an external driver acting upon inert geology.  

Nothing here is finished. The living matrix grows, breathes, floods, drains, and re-grows according to rhythms that the hominins can influence only locally. Their scattered artifacts—charred hearths, woven mats of bast fiber, simple clay figurines—are already being absorbed back into the substrate within a single season, overwritten by the continent’s incessant green logic.
