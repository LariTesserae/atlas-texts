A high shelf of land, wide enough for a village, narrow enough to feel like a single place, perches just below the weather line on a long granite ridge. The rock is pale and fine-grained, its joints opened by frost so many centuries ago that every crack has softened into a channel no wider than a wrist. In the channels lies dark dust that was once lichen; when the sky lowers, the dust darkens further and gives the impression of moisture without ever actually being wet for long. The village—forty-eight low, single-roomed houses—stands with its backs to the ridge wall and its faces toward a slow, shallow stream that threads between two parallel ribs of stone. The stream is never more than ankle-deep and is fed less by distant snow than by nightly films of condensation that bead along the underside of overhanging ledges and roll downward at dawn. By midday the films are gone, the ledges are dry again, and the village returns to its faint mineral smell of cooled stone and cold bark.

The houses themselves are assembled from the same granite, split along natural bedding planes and dry-laid without mortar. Over each doorway a single timber—often twisted juniper—has been set to carry the weight of a roof made of thinner granite slabs. The wood is dark with age but shows no rot; the high, dry air and the nightly chill preserve it. Inside each house the temperature stays nearly constant, so that an earthenware bowl of water left on a bench at dusk still has a skim of ice on its surface when the door is opened at mid-morning. Smoke, when any fire is lit, does not eddy or pool; it rises straight through a roof-hole and is immediately taken by the prevailing wind along the ridge, leaving the interior air sharp and scentless.

Vegetation clings where the joints of the granite happen to trap a little more dust and a little more shadow. Low cushions of silvery alyssum, barely ankle-high, flower for three weeks in early summer and then turn into brittle, translucent seed heads that keep their shape through the long quiet season. Between the cushions are stretches of bare stone wide enough to lie down on without touching a plant. A few gnarled pines stand singly or in pairs, their trunks no thicker than a forearm, their canopies flattened by the steady wind into disks no taller than a person. They cast almost no shade; their needles are so stiff that even a moderate gust produces only a single dry note, like the click of a latch, rather than a rustle.

The fauna is even sparser. A pair of rock ptarmigan nest each year beneath the same overhanging slab; their eggs are speckled with the exact shade of the lichen dust. A small, solitary fox with pale guard hairs patrols the stream at twilight, stepping from one dry stone to the next without breaking the film of water. Its prints remain visible for weeks in the dust, then disappear all at once when a rare shower rinses them. Once in a decade an eagle may circle the ridge at dusk, but its shadow crosses the village in silence and leaves no sign.

The light here is ordinary daylight—bright enough to read by at noon, soft enough to lose the edges of things at the edges of the day—yet because the surrounding ridges block the lowest angles of sun and moon, every morning and evening the village receives a period of clear, shadowless illumination during which textures flatten and distances seem to shorten. It is during these intervals that the inhabitants perform the few tasks that require communal effort: resetting a slipped roof slab, carrying the year’s harvest of pine nuts to the central store-room, or simply walking the stream to confirm that its course has not shifted by so much as a hand-span.

Nothing grows taller, nothing is worn away further, nothing new is begun. The streambed, the roof-lines, the fox’s trail, and the positions of the pines are all where they have been for longer than living memory and are expected to remain exactly so. The only variation is the nightly condensation and its morning disappearance, a rhythm too small and too regular to disturb the larger equilibrium. In this place time passes, but place itself does not.
