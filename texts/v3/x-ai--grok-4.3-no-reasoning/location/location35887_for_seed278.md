The place is not one landscape but a chain of them strung together like beads on a single strand of mist. Each bead is a mesa-island, tens of kilometers across and separated by gulfs of air so wide that the next mesa can be seen only as a darker blue against the constant haze. These mesas have no names; they simply appear when the cloud-tide lifts and disappear again when it falls.

Stone here is fractured yet never broken away: long, slow tectonic breathing has lifted and settled the same plateau so many times that the fracture faces are burnished smooth. Their color is the muted red of old brick soaked in iron, gone almost gray under centuries of condensed ice-crystal etching. Because the rain is infrequent and light, every crack is a miniature greenhouse: inside each cleft the air is a degree or two warmer and a single black-green thorn-vine spirals upward, its leaves no wider than a fingernail. These micro-ecosystems exist only where two fracture planes meet at the correct angle; elsewhere the rock is naked.

Between the mesas the atmosphere is a permanent slow fog. It is dense enough that distant rock faces blur, yet not dense enough to obscure the next nearest mesa if one has patience. The fog does not drift; it settles, then lifts, on a cycle measured in weeks rather than hours. When it settles, surface moisture beads on every exposed plane and then trickles down the stone in threads so fine they do not cut channels. When it lifts, it leaves a temporary dew that lingers longest in the vine clefts.

Temperature hovers just below freezing except for brief intervals near local noon. During those windows a film of meltwater forms on the uppermost faces of each mesa. The water is quickly wicked into hairline fractures and refreezes, wedging the rock further apart—yet the process is so gradual that millennia pass between observable shifts in the surface.

Fauna is the most present element, though every animal appears to be working very hard not to be seen. On each mesa a single herd species exists: something like a long-bodied hexapod whose pelage is the same fractured-brick gray as the stone. They move only when the light drops another fraction; their footfalls are muffled by pads of compressed frost. Their respiration releases visible steam, yet they arrange themselves so that each exhalation merges into the background fog before it can mark their position. Predators—smaller, black, with compound lenses that function in starlight—drift between the mesas on membranous sails that act as both wing and net. They hunt by silhouette: any break in the constant low cloud is tracked until it resolves into prey. The interaction between the two groups is so infrequent that a single successful kill on one mesa can support an entire drifting predator population for years.

Sound is nearly absent. The wind, when present, moves at the same pace as everything else; the edges of the mesas are rounded enough that no edge whistle occurs. The animals do not vocalize above the threshold of hearing for humans. The only consistent source of acoustic energy is the slow fracturing of ice within the rock—tiny internal snaps, irregular but almost inaudible except as a pressure change felt rather than heard.

No structures exist, yet the mesas themselves carry a kind of memory of industry. Across the largest of them lie long, straight depressions—kilometer-wide, perfectly straight, and older than the current erosion regime. The depressions contain faint striations parallel to their length, as though something massive and metal once moved along them at a walking pace. Whatever created the grooves left no residue and no tools; the stone has healed itself so completely that only the geometry remains as evidence. In the dimness, the grooves read as deeper shadow rather than cuts, and they function now as drainage channels for the infrequent condensation that collects at higher elevations.

Light itself behaves differently here. Because the fog is perpetually present, illumination is omnidirectional and colorless. Shadows do not have edges; they feather into the general gray until they cease to be shadows at all. The day-night cycle is still measurable, but only as a further reduction in an already low baseline. During the long night the light is not black; it is the blue-gray of an overcast sky seen from underwater.

Ecology is therefore a matter of patience rather than abundance. Each vine cleft supports a thin crust of microbial life that photosynthesizes at extremely low photon flux. The hexapods graze these crusts once a decade, moving across an entire mesa in a single season. The drifting predators follow the hexapods at the same pace. The whole system appears static only because the observer is too short-lived to perceive the motion.

At the very center of the largest mesa a single circular depression, thirty meters across, has collected ice for so long that the surface is a single crystal with no internal fractures. From certain angles this lens reflects the weak ambient light back into the fog, creating a faint, diffuse glow that moves slowly across the rock as the light source itself travels. No animal approaches this lens; the predators steer their sails around it, the herds detour by kilometers. The glow is the only feature that can be said to draw attention, yet nothing here moves quickly enough to be called drawn.
