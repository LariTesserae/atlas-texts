Here is the place:

It is a single chamber, a deep cavity roughly forty paces across and perhaps twelve high, entirely filled with cold, clear water that is almost perfectly still except for the slow creep of a great pressure-driven circulation. The rock that contains the chamber was raised in some ancient orogeny, now so long settled that the ceiling sags under its own load, tilted in a gentle dome whose highest point is only a body-length above the water surface at its shallowest. The stone is a fine-grained, blackish basalt that formed under enormous pressure; it still carries microscopic cavities left by the gases that escaped from its matrix while it cooled. From these tiny pores, minute streams of bubbles rise every few minutes, each bubble the size of a pea, traveling in lazy, ruler-straight lines before flattening against the ceiling and popping without a sound audible through the water.

The chamber is both dense and small. The water is saturated with dissolved minerals and with life. The life is not plant-life; it is an extremely slow metazoan lattice made of braided, spongy colonies that adhere to every vertical surface. Each colony is a finger-thick rope of translucent, flexible tissue that oozes tiny, glassy spines for grip. They grow in radial patterns, branching outward like frost-work on a window, never quite touching but crowding so close that a human arm could not pass between them without bending them. The tissue itself is the color of pale flint; when a colony is disturbed, it releases a single pulse of cold bioluminescence, an amber flash no brighter than the glow of a dying ember, that lasts exactly three heartbeats. After the flash the colony stiffens for a full day, becoming rigid as a branch of coral before relaxing again.

Overhead, where the ceiling meets the water, a thin film of floating mineral oil has collected—an iridescent slick a single molecule thick in most places, thicker in seams that drift like slow, rainbow-colored lightning. The film is the residue of a geological seep that emerges from a hairline crack at the highest point. As the seep adds new oil, older oil is pushed down the dome slopes in imperceptible sheets; it takes roughly seven years for any given molecule to travel from the center of the ceiling to the lowest corner of the chamber. The same seven-year clock governs the feeding cycle of the sponge colonies: the oldest parts of each rope are shed at one end, sink, and are consumed by smaller motile clusters that live in the sediment at the floor. The motile clusters move at the speed of a shadow cast by the sun across stone. Their bodies are no more than aggregates of ciliated cells, each cluster the size of a coin, yet there are thousands of them, and their movements stir the water just enough to maintain a shallow, persistent current along the floor that returns dissolved nutrients upward.

The temperature is constant at six degrees above freezing; the stone conducts heat away from the water at exactly the same rate that the slow, mineral-rich seep adds warmth. Any warmer current that might ever enter from the narrow fissure at the bottom is instantly chilled and diluted. The fissure itself exhales a hiss so low that human ears cannot register it until they are directly above the crack, and even then it is felt more as a vibration through the soles of boots than heard. Once every nine days, a pulse of denser water arrives from deeper strata, carrying with it a faint metallic tang. When this pulse reaches the chamber, the motile clusters rise a hand-span from the sediment, each cluster rotating like a coin on its edge; the rotation produces a brief, dry scraping sound that is the loudest noise the chamber ever makes—an abrasive whisper that travels the length of the room and dies against the opposite wall.

Because the chamber was lifted so recently in geological time, every surface retains the micro-fractures of its birth: hairline edges, no thicker than a fingernail, that catch stray light and fracture it into minute prisms. The light that does penetrate from above arrives through a single shaft bored by a long-vanished surface spring; at the present epoch the shaft is roofed by a plug of clear ice that grows and melts on a cycle of centuries. When the ice thins, a shaft of ordinary daylight falls in a perfect cylinder to the floor, illuminating a patch of sediment and turning the motile clusters into a field of tiny, shifting mirrors. The light lasts only hours at a time, and centuries pass between illuminations, yet the geometry of the reflected prisms on the wall above is so exact that an observer who stayed long enough would see the same sigils repeat at intervals shorter than the life span of the colonies themselves.

The basalt contains veins of native metal—threads of native copper so fine they appear as green-blue hairlines when wet. Over centuries the slow current deposits a dusting of iron-manganese particles along each vein, and the combination of copper and manganese produces a faint, constant electrical potential between any two points separated by more than an arm-span. The motile clusters exploit this potential: each time one crosses a vein, it stores a charge in its cilia and releases it later in a series of microscopic sparks that help it dislodge food particles from the sponge ropes. The sparks are visible only in darkness and last for the duration of a single blink; collectively they raise the background light level by less than a single candle, yet they are the only active illumination the chamber possesses during the centuries when the shaft above is sealed by ice.

Because nothing larger than the motile clusters exists here—no predators, no scavengers, no visitors—the slow abrasion of the sponge ropes against the stone produces the only mechanical wear. The sound of that abrasion is a soft, continuous hiss, not unlike the noise of sand against glass, pitched so low that it registers in human hearing only as an almost imperceptible pressure on the eardrums. It never rises or falls; it is simply present, an ambient constant against which every other faint sound—the bubble bursts, the pulse-driven scrape, the electrical crackle—is measured.
