The seamount chain that splits the plateau is so ancient its basalt has been planed and re-planed until it no longer resembles rock at all. It has become a single, continent-spanning plate of polished onyx, glossier than glass yet still faintly rippled with the ghosts of former ridges. From a distance the plateau appears level; closer in you see that every surface is under a finger-width film of perpetual condensation, a film kept in constant undulation by thousands of tiny convective cells that rise, burst, and collapse again, each one lifting and dropping the skin of water like the surface of a sleeping lung.

Above that water a sky’s worth of vapor hangs in horizontal sheets that never quite turn to rain. The sheets are themselves liquid—dense enough that shafts of normal daylight refract into slow-moving curtains of green-gold. Between the sheets, pockets of pressurized steam punch upward in sudden pillars, each pillar fringed with hundreds of ephemeral, downward-dripping stalactites that evaporate before they reach the ground. The sound of this process is continuous: a braided roar of high-pressure hiss, the hollow gong of vapor domes collapsing, and the brittle tick-tick of condensing droplets that strike the glassy rock and rebound rather than splatter. All of it is amplified by the vertical faces of the remaining basalt needles—hundreds of slender, kilometers-tall spires left standing because no erosion could reach their interiors fast enough. Water streams down their sides in braided veins that shine like fiber-optic cables; when the pillars of steam pass between them they glow momentarily, and the whole range appears briefly lit from within.

Vegetation grows wherever the rock is not quite glass. In every seam and micro-crack the same ultramarine algae has taken hold, sending up translucent blades no thicker than eyelashes yet stiff enough to stand against the steam. These blades are coated with beads of oil that keep them from dissolving; each bead acts as a miniature lens, and under the refracted daylight the whole plain glitters with shifting constellations of cobalt points. Between the algae rise occasional, waist-high domes of coral-glass—ancient colonies whose polyps mineralized into perfect hexagons—now inhabited by colonies of pale, wingless moths whose bodies are filled with pressurized hydrogen. When disturbed, a moth releases a soft pop of the gas and drifts upward until it collides with the vapor ceiling; the collision is audible as a muffled bell-note that adds to the soundscape and also seeds the next generation of condensate domes.

Scattered across the onyx surface, every few hundred meters, sit the remnants of the plateau’s industrial past: low concrete rings no taller than a human shin, their interiors filled with slow-cycling turbines whose blades are entirely encrusted with the same blue algae. The turbines still turn, driven by the pressure differential between the surface water film and the steam vents below; each one emits a dull, subsonic thrum that travels through the rock like a heartbeat. Occasionally one of the rings fractures under the weight of centuries; the algae that spills out forms a temporary bright-blue delta that is quickly reabsorbed by the larger mat. No human hand maintains these machines, yet they have not stopped; the plateau is so flat and the pressure gradient so steady that the cycle has become self-sustaining.

Fauna follows the same logic of permanence under motion. Vast shoals of translucent, disk-shaped creatures—each the size of a cartwheel—drift just above the water film, grazing on the algae. They have no mouths; they absorb nutrients directly through their ventral cilia, which beat continuously and produce a collective white-noise hiss. When two shoals meet they merge and separate without pause, individual disks drifting between groups like slow sparks. Higher in the vapor layers live the skimmers: meter-long, paper-thin invertebrates whose bodies are stretched into perfect aerofoils. They ride the steam pillars upward, then fold their edges and drop like falling leaves until the next updraft catches them. Their passage through the vapor sheets is loud—a rapid whip-crack each time they break the surface tension—and this sound repeats in such dense overlapping waves that the overall loudness becomes a single, unvarying note pitched just below the threshold of pain.

The scale is deceptive. From any one basalt needle the horizon still appears open: the plateau extends in all directions, but because the surface is so reflective the horizon is also visible directly beneath your feet—an inverted mirror image of the sky above. Standing on the onyx is like standing inside an infinite vertical cylinder whose walls are the reflections of clouds and spires. The turbulence of steam and the steady churn of turbines make the cylinder appear to rotate slowly, an illusion reinforced by the migrating constellations of algae-light and the orbiting disks of the grazing shoals.

No structure rises more than a single story above the rock except the needles themselves, and none of the rings or algae domes are arranged according to any human plan; each is where local pressure happened to allow it. Yet the overall impression is not of ruin but of equilibrium—an industrial ecology whose factories, farms, and weather systems have interlocked so completely that the boundary between them has dissolved. Water that evaporates from a turbine blade may condense on a skimmer’s wing, fall as a droplet onto an algae frond, be re-ingested by a disk-creature, then exhaled as steam that drives the next turbine. The plateau has become a single organism whose metabolism is audible, visible, and continuous, its extreme age expressed not in stillness but in the perfect, self-correcting turbulence of its final steady state.
