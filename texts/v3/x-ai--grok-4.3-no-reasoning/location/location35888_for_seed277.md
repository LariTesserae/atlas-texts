The plateau is not a single thing. It is a stack of eras written in stone that once lay flat and now lies in great, gentle sheets that sag and tilt against one another like worn leather. Walking across any one band of strata is to cross a country already old when the next band above it was still sediment; the rise from one to the next is only a few meters, yet it feels like crossing an entire geological province. The angle is seldom more than a walking slope, so the plateaus flow into one another without cliffs, but they remain unmistakably separate worlds of color and grain.

Water never stays. Rain falls in sudden, local curtains that darken a quarter-kilometer of stone and then wander on, leaving the wetted rock to exhale mist until the next thermal. Most nights a dew so heavy it counts as precipitation collects along every concavity; in the morning it becomes a million bright threads that race downslope until they drop into one of the enormous sink-holes—perfect cylinders bored by ancient phreatic water—that perforate the plateau like the holes in a flute. From a distance the sink-holes are simple absences; closer, each one exhales a cool, steady breath of 100-percent humidity that supports hanging gardens of moss and liverwort on its shaded walls. At the bottoms lie circular pools, each an isolated sea no larger than a village square, so perfectly still that the condensation dripping from the overhang sounds louder than any river.

Because the water never lingers on the surface long enough to carve a drainage net, the plateau keeps a strange arithmetic: for every kilometer of open stone there is perhaps a single thread of vegetation, and for every thread a handful of animals. The vegetation itself is sparse not from poverty but from spacing. Individual plants are large, long-lived, and very far apart—columnar succulents whose trunks are ribbed basalt-grey and whose crowns are a single, living parasol of translucent, blue-green plates. Between them the stone is covered only by a living varnish of lichens so thin it reads as color rather than texture: rust, verdigris, sulfur, violet. In the rare depressions where dust has gathered, tiny annuals appear after each shower and vanish within a week, their entire life cycle compressed between two dawns.

Temperature on the plateau is not a gradient but a vertical stack. At ankle height the air is almost cold; heat rises from the stone during the day and pools a meter above the ground, so standing still one feels a warm belt across the chest while the feet remain chilled. Every few hours a convective cell forms—sun-warmed air lifting, cooler air sliding in from the sink-holes—and the entire layer flips. The motion is silent; only the slow drift of pollen and the sudden lift of a thermaling raptor announce it.

Fauna follows the same layered logic. The stone is patrolled by herds of low, six-legged grazers the color of cooled lava; they move in a single-file procession so spaced that from above they look like stitches in the rock. Their feet never quite touch the surface; instead they skate on a micron film of condensation that the animals themselves generate by scraping the living varnish with specialized mouth-parts. Above them, in the warm belt, translucent gliders—half insect, half sail—ride the thermals with no more energy than the tilt of a single vane. They never land; they sip moisture from the updrafts and lay eggs that drift like dust until chance drops them into a sink-hole pool. In the pools themselves live dense communities of eyeless, filter-feeding worms whose castings form the only true soil on the plateau.

Sound on the plateau is mostly the sound of nothing moving. When rain arrives it falls in separate, local storms that announce themselves by the change in color of the stone a minute before the first drop strikes; the drops themselves are large, slow, and musical, each landing with a distinct, almost metallic note on the resonant rock. Between storms the quiet is not silence but a very slow, very low susurrus—the collective breathing of a hundred thousand columnar plants opening and closing stomata in near unison. Once every few days a glider colony passes overhead and the air fills with the crisp, papery sound of thousands of membrane wings tacking; then the quiet returns, deeper for the interruption.

Light never comes from above. The plateau lies beneath a perpetual deck of high stratiform cloud that scatters the sun into a uniform silver glow with no discernible source. At night the clouds luminesce faintly from within, not enough to read by, enough to keep absolute darkness from ever arriving. The only true shadows are the vertical shafts of the sink-holes, where light simply stops. Inside those shafts the darkness is not empty; it is occupied by slow, downward-drifting motes of condensed vapor that glitter when a glider passes overhead and momentarily blocks the glow. The shafts therefore contain their own miniature weather: a perpetual, gentle snowfall of water that never reaches the pools below, because the rising breath from the pools turns it back into mist before it can fall the final meters.

No structure larger than a single plant exists. Yet the plateau is unmistakably industrial. The living varnish is not passive; it is an unbroken, continent-scale reaction surface that fixes atmospheric carbon at a rate comparable to any factory. The columnar plants do not merely grow; they perform a slow, distributed electrolysis whose waste gas is vented through pores at the base of each trunk—minute, perfectly regular bubbles that rise, burst, and leave behind a ring of precipitated salts. These salts form geometric crusts that record the daily cycle of production; viewed from above, the plateau’s surface is tessellated with millions of such pale rings, a visible ledger of an ongoing chemical harvest that requires no hands and leaves no ruins.

The only rule is spacing. Nothing is allowed to be where something else already is; if a new plant begins to root within ten meters of an existing trunk, the older plant exudes a surfactant that lowers local surface tension until the condensation flows away and the seedling desiccates. The grazers observe the same interval; when two herds approach, both stop at the precise distance where the next footfall would bring them within the forbidden radius, and wait. The gliders never cross paths; they tack in a perpetual, three-dimensional braid whose knot points are empty cubic meters of air. The sink-hole pools are likewise spaced so that their humidity halos never quite touch; each pool therefore maintains its own micro-atmosphere, its own distinct community of worms, its own faint, private note in the chorus of dripping water.

The plateau is therefore not empty. It is occupied by an absence so carefully maintained that the emptiness itself has become the primary ecological resource. Every presence—plant, grazer, glider, pool—is defined by the exact volume of nothing that surrounds it, and the quiet is not the absence of sound but the presence of that carefully allocated space, large enough for weather, small enough to remain one single, self-regulating organism whose metabolism is measured in centuries rather than heartbeats.
