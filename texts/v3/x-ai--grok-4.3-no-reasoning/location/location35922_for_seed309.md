The place is a buried chamber-city named Můrovník. It exists twenty meters beneath the skin of a dry plateau, accessed only by a single vertical shaft whose walls are wet with condensation yet never flood. The shaft arrives in a circular plaza where light falls as a single dusty beam from above and is immediately swallowed by a canopy of living fabric.

Everything here is plant except the memory of machinery. The chamber’s ceiling is a continuous membrane of pale, vein-patterned leaves that exhale a slow, warm mist; the mist turns to droplets on the cool pipes that still run along the original girders. Those pipes once carried steam; now they carry sap. At regular intervals the sap-pressure pushes open valves shaped like orchid mouths, and a fine spray drifts down, feeding the hanging roots that have threaded themselves through every bolt-hole. Where roots meet iron, the metal has been drawn into living lace; where roots meet older concrete, they have softened it to chalk and woven it into new joints.

The floor is not floor but an undulating mat of broad, rubbery pads the size of feast tables, their edges feathered with tiny white flowers that close when stepped on and reopen after a count of twenty. Between these pads lie narrow channels of dark water that smells of iron and bruised mint. The water is never still: every few minutes a pulse travels through it, bulging the surface into a momentary dome before the next trough follows. The pulse begins somewhere deeper in the warren of tunnels and travels outward until it reaches the central plaza and rebounds, sending smaller ripples toward the walls. The ripples carry seeds—black commas no larger than lentils—that lodge in the pad edges and begin to swell at once.

Walls are layered histories. The outermost stratum is industrial brick, its mortar replaced by a tough, translucent gel that the local mosses secrete. Next inward is a lattice of salvaged train rails now held apart by woody struts the color of dried blood; sap beads at every weld. Innermost is a living wicker of aerial roots that have braided themselves into load-bearing cables; they flex with temperature change, tightening at night, loosening by day, producing a constant low creak that never stops.

Temperature hovers at the upper edge of warm. The air feels like breath held inside cupped hands. Condensation forms on every upward-facing surface and drips in patient intervals; each drip is answered by a soft percussive thud as it lands on the mat below. The plants drink the drip in a visible gulp, the pad surface dimpling then rebounding.

Sound never drops below the threshold of “loud.” There is the hydraulic sigh of valves, the drip, the creak of cables, the friction of pads sliding against one another when a large animal passes, and beneath all of it a deeper vibration—like a subway car heard through many meters of soil—that arrives and departs on a cycle of roughly seven minutes. When the vibration peaks, the white flowers along the channels snap shut in unison, then slowly reopen; the interval between closing and opening is exactly the same duration as the vibration itself.

Fauna is continuous and overwhelming. Pale millipedes as long as forearms flow along the water channels, backs encrusted with seedlings that ride them to new planting sites. Basket-winged moths the color of dried blood hang in motionless clusters from the ceiling membrane; when the vibration arrives they beat their wings once, producing a single bass thump that rolls through the chamber. Between the pads live colonies of hairless rodents whose incisors are translucent and veined like young leaves; they gnaw the mat edges into perfect circular clearings, then abandon them to the water when the next pulse arrives. Larger shapes drift overhead—gliding, membranous bodies the size of small boats—that never touch the ground and seem to breathe through the same pores as the ceiling itself.

Nothing is truly still. The plants grow visibly if watched long enough: a fresh shoot will reach the height of a child in the time it takes condensation to collect and fall from one pipe to the floor. The machines—whatever they once were—continue to turn, their gears now cushioned by living packing that must be pruned back daily by whatever hands still tend this place. The water pulses, the flowers close, the animals migrate, the roots tighten and loosen, the ceiling breathes mist, and the single beam from the shaft above tracks slowly across the living floor until the light fails for the night.
