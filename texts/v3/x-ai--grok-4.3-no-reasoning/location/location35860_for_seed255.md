The dark is not absence but material.  

It is a vast, open pocket, perhaps four hundred paces across, held within the hollow of a larger, hand-finished structure that keeps growing. Everything here has been shaped from the same soft, charcoal-grey slate that never quite dries. The surface has been left rough so that condensation can bead in the micro-valleys and seams; droplets the size of lentils slide continuously from every ridge and rim, leaving silver threads that vanish into the floor cracks before they can pool. The air smells of wet stone and cold metal filings.  

Underfoot the floor is deliberately uneven—flat only for three or four steps at a time—so that every change of direction is registered by ankles and knees. The steps ascend or descend in wide, overlapping fans that no two travellers follow the same way. These ramps have been cut from the same block as the walls, so the whole place feels like a single, slowly folding sheet that has not yet decided its final geometry.  

Above, the ceiling is a lattice of thick slate beams drilled through with thousands of fist-sized holes. The holes are not aligned with any pattern that is obvious at first glance; they simply let the darkness in. Somewhere far overhead water is running across an unseen roof, sending a constant low thunder that vibrates through the beams and arrives here as a granular, wet static. Each time the thunder swells, smaller stones lodged in the holes vibrate like loose teeth and drop, pattering onto slate or occasionally onto flesh.  

There is no green or brown, only shades of slate, graphite, and iron oxide. Where water has lingered longest, pale mineral skins have bloomed—circular patches of gypsum and calcite that look like slow-motion frost. These skins are fragile; a fingertip pressure cracks them into wafer dust that drifts for a moment before sticking again to another damp surface.  

The only life visible at any one time is a handful of translucent, slug-sized creatures whose bodies are mostly water held inside flexible mineral tubes. They move by expanding and contracting the tube walls, forcing themselves through the condensation film on the ramps. When they meet an obstacle they cannot dissolve, they simply reverse direction; their motion produces a faint, regular clicking that adds an insectile rhythm to the larger acoustic wash.  

At intervals, human-sized alcoves have been cut into the walls at chest height. Inside each alcove a shallow basin has been chipped and then burnished until the stone itself gleams like wet leather. A single steady droplet falls from the alcove roof into the basin every nine or ten seconds. The interval is exact because the masons left a hairline ridge that controls the water’s gathering weight. The drops ring clearly; each is an isolated, metallic ping that momentarily parts the surrounding roar before the roar closes again.  

The temperature stays just above the point where breath becomes visible; surfaces therefore feel colder than the air. Hands pressed against the wall leave prints of displaced moisture that linger for minutes before being overwritten by new condensation.  

Occasionally the entire floor vibrates at a frequency too low to name. The vibration travels up through the legs and settles behind the eyes, then fades. It is the only measurable warning that, somewhere far above and outward, another course of stone has been levered into place, adding mass to the unfinished structure that continues to rise around this hollow. The process is deliberate, endless, and without ceremony.
