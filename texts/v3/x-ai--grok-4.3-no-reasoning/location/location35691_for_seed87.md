Beneath a kilometers-thick sheet of cold water, a city forms itself.  

The place is less a metropolis than a slow swelling of mineral exoskeletons. Carbonate and silica spires rise in tiered arcades, each ribbed column no thicker than a wrist yet packed shoulder-to-shoulder; light filters down through them in syrupy blues and oilslick greens. Between the spires run filament roads—spongy, cartilaginous walkways laid by generations of tube-worms that have already worn their own tunnels, then abandoned them when the city moved deeper.  

The water here is dense with life suspended like motes: copepods the size of fingernails, swirls of colorless ctenophores, translucent cephalopods that flash once and vanish. Everything moves without turbulence; sound barely propagates, so the constant motion registers as a soft graininess against the skin, felt more than heard.  

There are no foundations. The city grows the way coral grows—by the millimeter, continuously re-surfacing its own skin. Newborn towers still show the lattice of the organisms that built them: calcifying polyps still alive inside the rock, their tiny mouths opening and closing in unison when cold currents bring fresh nutrients. Older towers are hollow inside; their empty chambers contain older generations already fossilized into delicate, lace-like scaffolds that later species simply occupy.  

Light itself is cold—an almost ultraviolet dusk that clings to surfaces without warming them. Patches of bioluminescent lichen cling to every north-facing wall, pulsing at the slow rhythm of their own tide. In the dim, these patches read like constellations reversed: points of living light mapping the city’s internal compass rather than any external sky.  

Fauna thickens the interstices. Bristle-worms the color of bruised plums move in files along the carbonate ridges, each leaving behind a single bead of mucus that hardens into the next layer of roadway. Glass-spined urchins cluster at every intersection, spines interlocked so tightly they form living turnstiles; currents must thread through the spines, or turn aside. Pale skates with wingspans no wider than a palm glide the vertical alleys between towers, their undersides studded with chemosensory papillae that taste the water for the metabolic signatures of the polyps still building the city.  

At this scale the city has no periphery. It simply thins, the spires growing shorter and more widely spaced until, a few dozen body-lengths out, the plain of raw geology reappears—uncolonized carbonate mud still settling, its surface cross-hatched by the grazing trails of yet-unclassified grazers. Those who move between the two zones feel, in their bones, the precise moment the supporting pressure of the towers drops away and the water reclaims its volume.  

Nothing is finished. The towers continue their incremental push upward; the worms continue their incremental deposition of new road; the skates continue their incremental hunt. All of it occurs inside a silence so complete that the only measure of time is the faint, recurring pressure change as a distant vent somewhere far below exhales another bolus of mineral-rich water and the entire living architecture swells, ever so slightly, in response.
