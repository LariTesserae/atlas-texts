The city that never quite grew up lives folded into a cleft high in the mountains, where the wind has been gnawing the ridgelines for whole geologic eras.  From a distance, the cleft looks like a single black tooth broken off the skyline; up close it reveals itself as a maze of ledges so ancient that their outer edges are already rounding back into the mountain’s own shape, the stone softly dissolving at the rates that only water can manage over a million winters.  At this height the air is cold enough to sting the lungs, yet pockets of condensation gather under every overhanging slab; each dawn a thin scrim of frost melts and re-forms, feeding the rivulets that thread downward in fits and starts.  They do not become rivers; they simply bead the stone and disappear again into the next crack.

The built part is nothing more than a single stratum of stone rooms and stubby corridors—three broad terraces, one above the other—each terrace a cluster of chambers whose walls once rose higher and then stopped when whatever builders had been moving them onward found another ledge that suited their needs better.  The masonry is fitted without mortar, each block planed by the centuries until the joints are almost invisible; in places, water has insinuated itself behind the stone and frozen, prying single blocks outward until they now hang like loose teeth above the abyss.  Mosses the color of oxidized copper have colonized the sun-facing sides, turning the architecture into a half-submerged garden whose only geometry is the slow downward drift of the plant itself.

Vegetation here is lush but miserly with space.  Knee-high tufts of blue-black sedge sprout wherever a ledge holds a finger’s depth of soil.  They catch the light like velvet and release it in the next gust, so that the whole façade seems to ripple.  Between the sedge, cushion plants the size of dinner plates form coral-like colonies; their tiny white flowers open for a single week each year and then spend the rest of their lives turning to black, seed-laden threads that the wind keeps braided into the cracks.  Trees are absent; the temperature and the exposure make them unnecessary.  In their place, a single kind of vine—almost leafless, almost luminous—threads from terrace to terrace in pale green ropes no thicker than a child’s finger, collecting condensation on the undersides until droplets fall with the sound of a distant metronome.

Fauna is rare and mostly audible.  Wind-scorpions no larger than thumbnails move inside the dry inner courses of the masonry, their claws ticking against the stone like minute castanets.  Once in a generation a white raptor descends from still higher ledges, lands on the uppermost terrace long enough to pluck one of the cushion plants entire, and lifts away; the only evidence is a sudden darkening of the sky and the brief concussion of wings against the cliff.  Everything else—moths, jumping spiders, even the tiny midges that hatch from the condensation films—stays below the threshold of sight.

Sound, however, is constant and contradictory.  The loudest element is the drip-work: each condensation bead that detaches from the vine or the ledge-edge falls either into the open air (producing a single clean note that travels the width of the cleft) or into one of the many shallow stone basins left by the long-gone builders.  The basins, carved perhaps for ritual or simply by accident of erosion, are shallow enough that they never overflow; instead they ring, one after another, like a dispersed carillon whenever the wind shifts and more droplets come loose at once.  Beneath that music runs the dry, glassy abrasion of wind carrying ice crystals across the exposed upper surfaces of the terraces, a continuous high scrape that modulates when a gust funnels between two remaining wall-stubs and becomes a brief, hollow whistle.  Occasionally the stone itself adds a voice: a block that has spent ten thousand winters at the brink finally shears free and drops, striking the ledge below with a muted, almost wooden thud that reverberates for several heartbeats before the cleft swallows it.

Light is ordinary mountain light—clear, high-contrast, shadow-heavy—yet it behaves strangely here because of the cleft’s proportions.  At midday the sun strikes the uppermost terrace at a steep angle, sending reflections skittering across the wet inner faces of the lower rooms like fugitive mirrors.  By late afternoon the sun has dropped behind the opposite ridgeline; the cleft fills with a cool blue indirect light that makes every wet surface appear lit from within.  At night there is no artificial illumination, only starlight so intense at this altitude that the mica flecks in the granite glitter as though the whole city were dusted with ground glass.

Nothing here contradicts the rules that governed its making.  The cold forbids large animals or fast metabolism; the intermittent water forbids forests yet permits the tough, slow-growing cushions; the ancient erosion has already planed the mountain almost flat, so the terraces cannot grow outward, only inward, cell by cell, as frost continues its microscopic quarrying.  The city, if it can be called that, is therefore not expanding or decaying so much as settling—its empty volumes filling, measure by measure, with the only material that still moves at this scale: water turning to ice, ice returning to water, and the faint percussion of that endless exchange.
