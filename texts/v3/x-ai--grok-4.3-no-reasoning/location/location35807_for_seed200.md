The Heat that Grows, the Heat that Stays  

It begins at ground level and refuses to climb or fall. The elevation is modest—only a few dozen feet from the lowest puddle to the highest mound—yet the impression is of a continuous, low-slung skin stretched over an immense slow body. Any small rise one meets seems more like a fold in that skin than a hill, a place where the hot earth has exhaled once and then decided not to move again.  

The wetness is constant but never floods; every surface is merely the outer face of water that refuses to sink farther than a hand’s span. When a heel presses, a pale film breaks and immediately reforms, threaded with tiny bubbles that do not rise. Ferns and vine-like things lean low enough to trail in these films, their undersides coated with a living condensate that glitters even in the dim light.  

Light itself is a kind of broth: a heavy, tannin-colored glow that arrives from nowhere in particular and simply stays. The source—sun, sky, or something else—cannot be located; the air is saturated with suspended droplets that catch and hold whatever illumination there is, turning every direction into a hazy wall of warm brown. Objects farther than a stone’s throw dim quickly into suggestion; beyond that they vanish, leaving only the sense that the same pattern continues. The result is an open field that feels enclosed, a contradiction that the land holds without strain.  

Vegetation grows in stacked planes. At ankle height there is a tight, springy mat of spore-bearing mosses whose surfaces are never dry. Knee-high, broad-leaf creepers form a second story, their veins running with slow sap that beads at the edges and drops back into the moss. Waist-high, translucent stalks rise at irregular intervals; each stalk ends in a translucent bladder filled with heated gas, keeping it just buoyant enough to stand without swaying. Above these, still within reach, thicker fronds angle together to create partial canopies whose overlapping edges drip continuously. Because nothing ever flowers or seeds in the ordinary sense, the foliage looks less like growth and more like an unfinished weaving that the land continues to add to at its own pace.  

Temperature never varies enough to be felt as change; it is simply the medium in which everything exists. The air on the skin, the water film on every leaf, the faintly metallic scent rising from the ground—all carry the same even warmth, like air inside a sleeping animal.  

Erosion has not begun; instead, the land is still deciding its own mineral arrangement. Pale, half-formed nodules the size of eggs push up through the moss, their surfaces still soft enough to take a fingerprint yet already patterned with minute ridges that look geological even though they are only hours old. These nodules will perhaps become stones, or they may dissolve back into the water film; either outcome appears equally possible and equally postponed.  

Sound arrives from everywhere at once and never stops. The chief source is the breathing of the bladdered stalks: each bladder contracts and expands in its own rhythm, producing a low, wet click when the membrane meets itself. Thousands of these clicks overlap, forming a steady, directionless roar like the sea heard inside a shell. Beneath that is the hiss of continuous condensation as droplets gather on the undersides of leaves and fall again, multiplied by millions. The moss itself produces a barely audible, almost tactile vibration when walked upon, each footstep answered by a faint fizzing release of tiny bubbles. There are no distinct calls, no sudden silences; the acoustic field is saturated and constant, indistinguishable from silence only because it never alters.  

Fauna is so numerous that individual bodies lose definition. The eye perceives motion as texture rather than event: a slow seething across every surface, a continual readjustment of positions rather than arrivals or departures. Most visible are translucent, slug-like organisms that glide along the undersides of the creepers, grazing the condensate; their movement is a faint, local brightening, as though the light itself were being rearranged. Within the moss live thread-thin worms whose bodies glow dull orange, visible only when they happen to cross one another; the intersections create brief nets of light that dissolve again without pattern. Larger, vaguely insectile shapes drift between the bladdered stalks; they possess no wings yet remain aloft, buoyed by internal gas the same temperature as the air. Occasionally one drifts low enough for its ventral surface to brush the canopy; the touch releases a soft sigh of displaced droplets, instantly replaced.  

Nothing arrives, nothing leaves, and nothing appears younger or older than anything else. The entire field maintains an unchanging distribution: the same number of bladders, the same density of threads in the moss, the same spacing of nodules pushing upward. This equilibrium is not fragile; it is absolute, and the only evidence that time exists at all is the memory of having entered the place. Once inside, that memory itself begins to fade, absorbed into the continuous, loud, wet, motionless present.
