The place is a single, airless chamber cut from the side of a high mineral ridge. It is not much larger than a cathedral nave, yet everything inside it is pressed close, as if the ridge itself had once folded and then refused to unfold again. The walls are a crystalline carbonate laced with veins of darker oxide; their surfaces have long ago lost all sharp angles. Heat radiates upward from fractures that thread the floor, the only water present locked inside the crystal lattices as hydroxyl groups—chemically present but unavailable. No wind moves, and nothing breathes in the conventional sense, yet the atmosphere feels dense: a continual faint hiss of out-gassing salts keeps the pressure slightly above the vacuum outside.

Across the floor, dense clusters of geometric growths rise and slump in slow rhythm. They are not plants; they are salt-hydrate columns that grow at the vapor interface where warm subsurface fluid meets the cold ceiling. Each column is packed shoulder-to-shoulder with the next, their facets interlocking like soap-bubble films. Where one column subsides, its neighbors lean inward within minutes, closing any gap; where one swells, it nudges its neighbors aside without ever leaving a visible seam. The motion is soundless except for an almost inaudible ticking—individual crystals popping into new alignments.

High on one wall, a seam of darker, sulfur-stained mineral has been worked into a shallow alcove. Inside it rest three small, perfectly round nodules the color of oxidized copper. They show no tool marks, yet each bears a shallow equatorial groove as though something once fit around them. They are the only unmistakably artificial objects present, and they are already half-swallowed by the slow encroachment of the salt columns.

Fauna here means temperature-dwelling mineral organisms that propagate by fracturing and re-knitting. They appear as hairline seams of brighter carbonate that travel the surfaces of the columns at the speed of a slow breath. Where two seams intersect, a node bulges outward; within minutes it cracks, sheds a dusting of powder, and the daughter seam continues on the adjacent column. These seams never occupy the same face twice; they are perpetually migrating across the packed forest of columns, an unceasing but almost silent traffic.

Light is absent except for the thermoluminescent glow of strained crystal. When a seam passes over a stressed lattice plane, the plane emits a brief, directional spark no brighter than a struck match. Each spark lasts only a few heartbeats, yet the cumulative effect is a moving star-field that travels in waves from floor to ceiling and back, always pacing the migration of the seams. Observers inside the chamber perceive these sparks as silent, cold fireflies drifting through an otherwise perfect dark.

There is no true erosion in the hydraulic sense. Instead, the mineral forest has reached a steady state in which growth at the vapor interface exactly balances subsidence at the base; the entire structure breathes upward and downward over a cycle measured in hours. Temperature gradients across single columns are steep enough that their lower third can glow a sullen orange while their crowns remain black. Heat escapes the chamber roof through a hairline network of micro-fractures that leads, eventually, to the vacuum beyond. No other exit exists.

Because the space is sealed, every process is local and self-contained. The copper nodules, whatever their origin, are now just another substrate for column growth; within days their grooves will be bridged and their surfaces will carry seams like any other surface. The chamber will remain cathedral-sized, packed, hot, dark, and alive with the inaudible ticking of mineral life.
