A pale, cobalt-limestone plateau tilts slightly toward a horizon that is not so much distant as merely elsewhere; distance itself feels flattened, like a sheet of hammered tin.  Because water has never existed here, the stone is without polish: every surface retains the chalky roughness it received when it first crystallized, and every crack is filled with microscopic shards of the same material.  From this floor rise slender, shoulder-wide spires—kilometers of them—close enough to touch if you stretch an arm sideways.  They are the same cool stone, yet their summits taper into something like petrified ash, as if every ridge had been rubbed for eons by the world’s single, unending dry wind.  Nothing else moves.

Above the spires the atmosphere is perfectly transparent and perfectly still; light arrives neither bright nor dim but precisely enough to reveal every ridge and seam.  Where two spires lean, a wedge of permanent shade forms; inside those wedges the temperature drops by the width of a hand-span, enough to coat the air with a barely visible haze of condensation—vapor that was never liquid water, simply the atmosphere’s own trace elements momentarily slowed enough to cluster.

Scattered among the stone columns are squat cubes no larger than a person, fashioned from the same cobalt limestone but weathered to a lighter, almost pearlescent gray.  Their edges are perfectly square only where they were first chipped; afterward, time itself eroded them until each angle reads like a graph of long decay.  Nothing grows from or on them.  Occasionally, in the interstices between columns, one finds a single black mineral sphere the size of a fist, resting exactly where it first cooled.  No wind has rolled any of them; they simply occupy the one place left after everything else had already settled.

Fauna fills the open space the way silt fills a slow river: not movement, not sound, but sheer presence.  From a distance the air looks clear; step between two spires and the bodies appear.  They are colorless, nearly translucent, shaped like flattened bells or the skirts of ancient lamps, each one the width of a palm and no thicker than a page.  Their edges touch; where they overlap, their outlines remain distinct because no fluid medium ever allowed them to merge.  There are millions—billions—yet the pressure they exert is even, so the space between you and the next spire feels no heavier than the space behind you.  They do not vibrate; they do not produce heat.  They are the fauna that replaced noise.

Nothing is in transit; nothing has been, or will be.  Even the light appears fixed: every shadow holds its angle the way a fossil holds its pose.  The plateau extends until extension ceases to matter; if another terrain ever existed, it finished becoming this one so long ago that memory itself is older than any name for it.
