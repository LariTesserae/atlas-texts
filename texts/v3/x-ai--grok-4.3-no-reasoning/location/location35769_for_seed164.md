High on the frozen shoulders of a continent-sized rock, the highland city of Sarruva floats above a sea that is not quite water and not quite stone.  The land itself is a fabric of fine needles, dark obsidian calved in needle-shapes like the teeth of a giant comb, each needle still growing imperceptibly from slow precipitation out of the air itself.  Between these needles, roughly five hundred metres of hollow space exists before the next protruding layer—a continental scaffolding whose gaps are wide enough for cloud to wander through, yet the city threads suspension bridges, aerial tramways and thin tensile rails between them.  

Rime and hoarfrost cover every black needle; from their tips crystal-clear meltwater condenses downward in shivering curtains, never reaching the ground before it re-freezes into glinting stalactites that sway in the constant wind.  The moisture that gathers on the needles is the only source of liquid on this world-height plain; everything else is either ice or the faintly metallic air, which smells of ozone and cold iron and is so dry that voices carry for kilometres in brittle echoes.  

Beneath the needles, industrial skylights—wide discs of riveted iron set flush with the rock—pulse a faint orange at fixed, unchanging intervals.  Nothing moves within the cavernous halls they illuminate; the turbine halls below hum at a single unchanging frequency, the boilers maintain a single temperature, pistons shuttle on frictionless bearings in perfect, eternal rhythm.  The engines never idle and never accelerate.  Somewhere a single, brass-cased clockwork governor ticks at a rate imperceptible except to itself, and that is the only time-keeping device permitted on Sarruva.

Above ground the city is built in stacked parasols of steel and lacquered glass, each layer suspended from the needles by cables that sing in a single sustained chord.  The parasols are covered in solar collectors so dense they seem black, but they do not gather energy; their purpose is simply to maintain the fixed temperature of every interior corridor at precisely −6 °C, irrespective of the outside conditions.  The same unvarying chill flows through the ventilation shafts, perfumed with trace mercury vapour and the faint metallic tang of machined tungsten.

The population moves in slow, synchronized currents—elevators rising, trams crossing, labourers loading ingots—yet no individual ever changes position relative to any other.  Walkways are grooved in permanent traffic lanes; footsteps follow the grooves like rails, and they are designed never to wear.  The city’s millions are therefore indistinguishable from the architecture itself, figures in steel-grey pressure suits whose visors reflect the unchanging sky.

No birds call, no insects drone.  The only motion is a perpetual fog that forms around the condensation zones on each needle and is blown in a single, constant horizontal sheet across the city.  The fog is so fine it does not obscure the horizon; instead it filters the light into a uniform, metallic grey that never shifts toward dawn or dusk.  In this even light the frost on every surface gleams with the same intensity, hour by hour, century by century.

Far to the horizon the needles grow taller, curving at last into natural arches that meet in the centre of an immense, continent-spanning dome of obsidian where the upper atmosphere has been drawn down into a permanent inversion.  Within that dome, the city continues in stacked discs until the detail dissolves into the grey of distance; one could walk a hundred days across its breadth and find the same street-plan, the same song of cables, the same orange skylights beating at the same unchanging interval.

Thus Sarruva exists: a cold, wet, mechanically perfect ring suspended in an endless, hollow sky, where the only water is the never-ending weeping of the needles, and every sound, every light, every footstep remains exactly as it first occurred when the needles first rose from the vapour.
