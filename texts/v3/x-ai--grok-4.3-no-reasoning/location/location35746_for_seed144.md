The mountain does not slope so much as cantilever. You stand inside the verticality rather than beneath it:  a vast wall of heat-shocked gabbro and banded gneiss tilted almost to perpendicular, its long-parallel fractures holding pockets of shadow no wider than a child’s forearm.  Upward, the rock keeps arriving—two thousand meters without a true bench or meadow—until the cliff face becomes the sky’s other edge.  

Because water is scarce yet never absent, every fracture sweats.  Condensation beads along cooler veins of quartz, swells, drips; by noon the drops have already been claimed by the heat, but by midnight new ones have collected.  Between the veins, rust-red iron stains map the slow capillary route each droplet took before vanishing.  

Nothing grows tall here; the vegetation that exists hugs the rock like lichen tattooed in place.  Pale crusts of *Rhizocarpon* and brittle tufts of *Draba* speckle the sun-struck exposures.  In the deeper cracks, a few finger-width ferns raise fronds no longer than eyelashes, their stipes anchored in dust fine enough to drift if you exhale across it.  Even these minute gardens are spaced so that a human stride can pass between them without touching either.  

What little soil there is has been winnowed into long vertical grooves, each groove ending at a ledge just wide enough to cup a shallow basin of grit.  In those basins the color shifts from mineral red to a fugitive olive; the tint marks where the daily pulse of condensate gathers, dissolves a trace of soluble salts, then is drunk again by rock or air.  

At intervals of roughly three hundred meters, small platforms of poured concrete—each no larger than a café table—project from the cliff on rust-streaked cantilevers.  Most support only a weather-faded instrument mast or a solar panel canted like a single dark wing.  A few still wear a chain-link cage around squat diesel generators; the cages are open now, doors frozen at angles by blown grit.  The engines themselves are silent except at twilight, when one or two cough awake for ten measured minutes, then fall quiet again.  

The soundscape is not silence so much as narrow-band quiet:  the tick of expanding gabbro in late morning, the answering tick of contraction at dusk; the thin, dry lisp of a rill of mica flakes sliding two meters then stopping; occasionally, the parchment flutter of a thermalling kite-bat whose wingspan equals a human hand.  Larger rumor travels upward from the valley floor three vertical kilometers below—a mutter of industrial ventilation fans or the faint, continual hiss of a slurry pipe—yet by the time it reaches this altitude the sound has been sieved to a rumor.  

The abundant fauna here is almost entirely aerial.  Swifts too small to cast measurable shadows comb the cliff for stirred insects at dawn and again at the last bronze of evening.  Pale scorpions the size of house keys occupy every horizontal crack wider than their carapace; they track moisture rather than prey, drinking the night-breathed film off stone.  Higher still, colonies of hardy tardigrades encyst inside biofilms that survive weeks of oven heat, re-animating each time condensation pearls return.  

Wind rarely arrives as gust; it is instead a steady laminar flow that follows the cliff like water over a spillway.  The flow sorts loose particles:  coarser sand stays tucked into the grooves while basalt dust skims outward in tawny banners that catch the light and vanish within a body-length.  At certain elevations these banners align with the generator platforms, veiling them for minutes at a time, then revealing them unchanged.  

No roads switchback the face; instead, tensioned cables—each the color of oxidized copper—run diagonally between platforms.  Small gondolas the size of packing crates slide along the cables when counter-weights are released.  The gondolas carry batteries or sample cartridges downward, ride back empty, or sometimes dangle for days until a new descent order is logged.  Their passage is nearly silent except for the soft, glassy ring when a car crosses a cable joint.  

Because elevation and heat coexist, the daily cycle is a single, large breath.  By mid-morning the entire wall is exhaling stored moisture upward; by late afternoon that moisture has condensed against whatever portion of the cliff has already slipped into shadow.  During the two hours of true darkness the temperature drops only four degrees, yet that is enough for a second distillation:  a dew so brief it leaves no footprint except a momentary darkening of the rock.  The darkness fades before the eye can be sure it was ever there.  

At every scale the cliff therefore functions as a slow wick:  it lifts water a few centimeters, loses most of it, lifts what remains a few centimeters higher.  Over centuries the rock has stained itself in vertical stripes that mark the height to which capillary films regularly climb before surrendering to the heat budget of an average day.  The stripes are the landscape’s only calendar.
