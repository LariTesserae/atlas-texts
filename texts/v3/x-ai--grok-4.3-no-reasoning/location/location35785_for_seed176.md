A basin has been cut into the side of a cliff so recently that the rock still remembers the path of the blade: thin, knife-bright stripes of quartz and basalt run up the rim like tool-marks, still shedding grit into the air. The basin itself is only a hundred paces across—cathedral-scale, ringed by sheer walls that rise another two hundred straight into the mist before the mountain continues upward and vanishes. Water never stops arriving. It weeps out of every crack, collects on every ledge, trickles down the vertical face in mercury-bright veins that merge, separate, and re-merge again. The air inside the bowl is saturated; every breath tastes metallic and warm. Vapor condenses on lips and eyelashes and on the warm, humming housings of the turbines that line the lower perimeter.

Those turbines are the only built things present—fourteen of them, each the size of a small shed, each turning without visible blades, their housings dark with condensation and faint orange rust. They hum at different, deliberate pitches so the basin never falls silent; the chord they make is the loudest single presence in the space. Power cables, slick with moss, droop from one turbine to the next like wet rope. Where the cables cross the weeping rock faces they are already sheathed in mineral scale—an inch of pale carbonate already hardening around the insulation. The scale grows visibly, grain by grain, in the half-light that filters through the permanent fog above.

Inside the cliff wall, water flows along ancient faults and new fractures, sometimes spurting in fine jets when the pressure behind a plugged seam finally overcomes the plug. The jets fall as curtains that are caught, funneled, and returned to the turbines through carved stone channels no wider than a forearm. The channels are newly cut—edges still sharp, the stone bright where the cutting tool passed—but every edge is already furred with emerald algae that drinks the warm spray. The algae forms a continuous, almost rubbery mat wherever light can reach; at night the mat will luminesce a low, sourceless green, though the turbines’ orange indicator lamps are always lit.

A single concrete pier juts from the northern rim, its aggregate still showing fresh aggregate sparkle. At its end, a rusted winch drum and a coil of wet cable wait beneath a tarp weighted with stones. The pier is the only flat surface in the basin; everything else slopes or stands vertical. On the pier’s lee side, water pools ankle-deep in a depression shaped like the hull of an overturned boat. Inside the pool, fine brown sediment has settled into a perfect, motionless disk the size of a tabletop. The disk grows a hairline thicker each day as dust from the turbines and dissolved limestone rain into it. Around its margin, minute white threads of crystal climb toward the surface, forming a delicate, ever-rising fringe.

Because the basin is so high and so recently opened, the air pressure is lower than a human body expects. The combination of warmth and low pressure causes the saturated air to feel oddly buoyant; exhaled breath does not disperse but lingers as small, slow-moving clouds that drift until they touch a wet wall and collapse back into rivulets. Birds do not enter the bowl—too little room to maneuver, too much water in the air, and the sound of the turbines seems to confuse their sense of direction. The only regular visitors are pale, translucent salamanders the length of a finger that live inside the scale that forms on the power cables. They move with the same patient rhythm as the algae, laying eggs in the hollows of the mineral crust and emerging weeks later, still translucent, already the same size as their parents.

The cliff above is still collapsing in microscopic increments. Each night a single quartz grain loosens, falls, and is caught by the algae mat before it can roll into a turbine intake. Each grain adds one more almost-imperceptible layer to the soft green carpet. In a few centuries the basin will be choked; in a few millennia it will be overgrown into a hanging garden that has forgotten it was ever machined. For now the turbines still turn, the water still sings down the walls, and the salamanders continue their almost-motionless patrol across the warm, luminous cables that thread the weeping stone.
