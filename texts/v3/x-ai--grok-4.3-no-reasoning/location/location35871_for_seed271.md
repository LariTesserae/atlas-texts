The place is a low bowl of land ringed by an almost imperceptible rise—more a change in the way the light lies than a true crest—so the sky seems closer here than it does just outside the hollow. The soil is a dry, pale dust the color of sun-bleached bone, its surface sealed by an ancient, translucent crust that cracks underfoot into soft, soundless plates. The crust never crumbles to powder; once disturbed it settles back into its thin, hard skin almost at once, leaving no visible trace. Occasional drops of water bead on the crust from nowhere, collect for a few minutes, then sink inward along hair-fine fissures, leaving the surface dry again. No one ever sees the water arrive, only the momentary gleam and the smell of wet stone that follows.

Scattered across this basin are low, shoulder-high domes of the same crusty earth, spaced far enough apart that three or four people could lie between any two of them without touching. The domes look almost architectural: their profiles are gentle, their tops flattened as if pressed down by an even weight that no longer exists. Thin sheets of translucent mineral vein the domes like frost; in the permanent half-light the mineral catches what illumination there is and returns it as a faint internal glow, so each dome seems lit from within, though nothing moves inside them.

Nothing grows tall. A few flat, circular lichens adhere to the crust itself, their edges raised into microscopic ridges like the rims of drying puddles; they are the color of tarnished silver and never spread beyond the reach of a hand. Between the domes the ground is bare, the crust etched with wandering hairline channels that all lead, eventually, nowhere. The air is warm and utterly still; the only motion is the slow, synchronized dimming and brightening of the mineral veins inside the domes, a pulse too slow to measure except by watching the same dome for several minutes and noticing that its brightness has shifted.

Sound arrives already muffled. If one stands perfectly still, the ear registers an almost subsonic pressure, the ghost of water moving through rock far below; if one moves, the crust hushes the footfalls so quickly that silence returns before the next step lands. There is no wind, no birdsong, no insect tick. The quiet is not oppressive; it feels like a surface that has been smoothed until no roughness remains to catch the passage of time.

Because nothing changes, the eye keeps seeking the next alteration that never comes. The low domes remain the same distance apart; the faint mineral veins keep their slow cycle of light; the invisible water continues to arrive and depart without ever increasing or decreasing the dampness of the crust. The place holds its single state so completely that an observer begins to feel, after a while, that they are standing inside the pause between two identical moments rather than inside ordinary, accumulating time.
