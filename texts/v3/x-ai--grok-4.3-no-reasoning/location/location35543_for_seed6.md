The landscape does not have an ocean.  
Instead, the water has thickened.  

Here, water is a standing medium—neither liquid nor solid—exactly dense enough that a human body would sink through it at the pace of a slow diver. It fills the space like clear gelatin, pressing equally against skin and metal alike. When the body stops moving, it hangs suspended, unable to float or settle.  

The terrain is a cathedral of raw, unweathered girders and retorts, all of them new. Nothing shows patina; the steel glitters with untouched mill scale, the rivets still carry the spiral scars of the last wrench that tightened them. Enormous horizontal and vertical members interlock at precise right angles, creating a single, immense latticework rising five stories high and fifty meters across. At its edges, the lattice meets perfect vertical walls of the same metal, bolted in place without seams or gaps; beyond those walls there is only more water-medium, indistinguishable from the interior. There is no rock, no soil, no window that looks outward, only this single, self-contained volume.

The lattice is overgrown in green. Broad, vein-thick leaves the size of doors jut horizontally from joints, their surfaces ridpled with fine cilia that beat so slowly the motion is visible only as a shift in the light passing through them. Between every tier of steel grow curtains of spongy moss so dense that the beams they conceal look upholstered rather than structural. The moss exhales tiny oxygen pearls—microscopic, perfectly spherical—which rise through the water-medium at a constant one millimeter per minute, disturbing nothing else. When these bubbles finally touch the ceiling they flatten, spread, and re-dissolve into the medium without popping. The only sound is the very soft, continuous click of millions of cilia beating in perfect synchrony, so faint that it registers more as pressure on the eardrums than as audible noise.

Along the vertical faces of the beams, ribbon-thin copper coils are set flush into the steel. Each carries a steady current whose voltage never changes; the coils warm the medium just enough to maintain a uniform 28 °C. Small crystalline capacitors hang on silk-fine glass threads at every intersection, and each crystal pulses once every seven hours, a slow heartbeat that travels through the entire structure as a faint violet light. The light is just bright enough to read by, and its color never varies. Because nothing moves faster than the drift of the oxygen pearls, shadows stay where they are cast for hours, stretching only as the crystals recharge.

At the exact center of the lattice stands a single empty node: a cube-shaped volume bounded by six square plates of thin mica, each one meter on edge and suspended so that its edges align with the girders around it. Inside the cube the water-medium has been cleared, leaving a cube of ordinary air at the same temperature. A person standing inside the cube would be able to speak without distortion, but outside there is no animal voice left to answer; there are no animals.  

Because the structure is completely sealed and fully occupied by steel, moss, and water-medium, there is no substrate that is not constructed. Every surface is the result of deliberate placement, even the leaves: each leaf’s petiole is threaded into a bored hole in a girder and sealed with a metal collar. Nothing here grows from soil; everything grows from an earlier act of fastening.  

The only change is slow. Every twenty-three hours a single capacitor finishes its cycle; its light dims, the leaf directly above it briefly stops its cilia, and a single oxygen pearl that had been rising stops for half a minute before the cycle resumes. That is the extent of motion. The rest of the place waits, the new steel gleaming, the leaves breathing at the speed of continents.
