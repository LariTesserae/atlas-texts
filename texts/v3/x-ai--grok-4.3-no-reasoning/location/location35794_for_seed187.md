A place that has become one thing for so long that time has almost stopped noticing it.

At its heart is a single cathedral-sized hollow, its walls made entirely of living wood—vascular towers of black-kneed willows and split-trunked yews that have long since ceased to be trees and have become the architecture itself. The hollow is roofless. Its upper edge is a ragged ring of foliage so continuous that, from within, the light seems to arrive through a single vast skylight, a cool, silver-grey wash that never quite shifts from morning to dusk. The wood is damp. Not with rain, but with the slow exhalation of the plants themselves: each bole and slab leaks a fine, constant mist that beads along every surface and pools in shallow, glassy depressions carved into the horizontal growth-layers. These depressions are the only water the hollow possesses—pocket basins no larger than a cupped hand, connected by hairline channels etched by capillary action. They never overflow, never empty.

The floor is a single living membrane, an unbroken sheet of spongy root-felt that has replaced soil. Nothing moves across it; there are no tracks, no trails, no fallen leaves. The surface rises in long, low undulations so gradual they are felt more as pressure changes underfoot than as visible slopes. Embedded in this mat are the silent engines that keep the hollow alive: fist-sized turbine-nodes of compressed xylem, each turning with glacial slowness, circulating the mist through an internal lattice of veins. The sound they make is constant and soft—an overlapping chorus of wet, fibrous friction—like thousands of pages turning in a library that never closes. This sound fills the space so completely that silence has become impossible; yet the sound does not vary, does not rise or fall, so it reads as a kind of acoustic stillness.

Fauna exists, but is almost motionless. Slow, translucent salamanders the color of wet bark drift along the vertical walls, their movements measured in hours rather than seconds. Pale moths with wings like torn parchment hang in the air, wings beating once every several minutes. Their presence registers more as weight changes in the mist than as visible flight. Nothing preys; nothing flees. The animals simply occupy space at the same equilibrium the plants have reached.

The scale of the hollow is deceptive. From the outside, the mass of vegetation would appear modest—a dense but unremarkable stand of ancient trees. Inside, distance stretches: the far wall is farther than the eye wants to accept, the vertical growth-layers recede into a soft, featureless grey. Yet no human construction marks any boundary; the transition from outside to inside is only a thickening of the living membrane until it becomes continuous. There is no entrance, only the moment when the last gap between branches seals behind a visitor and the sound of the engines asserts itself as the new atmosphere.

Nothing here is growing toward anything. The vegetation is not young or old; it is simply finished. The engines turn because they have always turned. The salamanders drift because they have always drifted. The mist condenses because condensation is what the air does here. In this place, stability has replaced succession, and the loud, wet hush of the engines is the sound of a system that has outlived change.
