A cold, high-altitude wilderness where thickets of enormous, trunkless plants rise directly from rock and then fan outward into soft, living scaffolds that support their own weight. The plants are all one living surface: their leaves overlap and braid into continuous sheets that slope, bridge, and vault across kilometres of fractured ground. Between these sheets the space is nearly empty—vertically vast voids filled only with cold, motionless air. Water arrives in sudden bursts: steep, narrow showers that fall as fine needles, freezing mid-fall into soft glassy filaments that lace the leaf-sheets and then drip again hours later, collecting in shallow pools rimmed by the same living tissue. The ground beneath the canopy never forms true soil; the youngest rock still shows knife-edge ridges and sudden clefts left by the last tectonic pulse. Because the vegetation is the substrate, every vertical drop or rising column is already lined with spongy, translucent membranes that collect condensate and slowly transfer it downward; condensation is louder than footsteps, a perpetual soft spatter that travels through the leaves as resonance, then is answered, after a short lag, by an explosive crack as a frozen filament shatters and cascades into a lower pool.

Temperature is steady near 0 °C, yet the canopy is internally warmer by only a few degrees, enough to keep liquid water moving along vein-like tubes that contract and dilate like slow peristalsis. Within this tepid humidity thrive dense populations of micro-fauna—thread-thin, transparent worms that migrate between pools, and clusters of spindle-shaped arthropods that skate along the underside of the leaves, their legs producing a faint crackling like static whenever they accelerate. Larger fauna occupies the vertical space: ribbon-shaped gliders with translucent wings the colour of frost, each the length of a person. They launch from one membrane wall, cross the open void in seconds, and impact the far surface with a wet thud that shakes droplets loose; the membranes absorb the shock by bowing and then springing back, sending a second ripple of sound across the chamber.

Human-scale traces are sparse: a single vertical post of pale stone set into a cleft, carved with shallow rings; a shallow ring of stones arranged around a cold fire-scar on one leaf surface; a few discarded tools of chipped obsidian lashed to wooden shafts. The tools themselves have already begun to be absorbed—the wood grain pulled apart by living plant fibres that re-knit around the blade as though the artefact were an intrusive seed. Light reaches the uppermost layers only as a dim, pewter wash that filters through several kilometres of overlapping translucent leaves; deeper levels are absolute night. Bioluminescent streaks—thin veins of cold green—mark routes between pools, pulsing once every several minutes like a slow heartbeat.  

The loudness of the place is spatial rather than violent: every droplet impact, every glider landing, every snap of ice transmits through the continuous leaf architecture until the entire volume resonates at low frequencies that human ears translate as a constant low roar, punctuated by brief, higher-frequency bursts when membranes rupture or animals vocalise. Because the vegetation spans such a scale, weather systems form inside the voids themselves; downdrafts of cold air, funnelled between parallel sheets, generate small vortices that carry leaf fragments upward, only to drop them again when the thermal rises stall. Each cycle redistributes moisture and seeds new growth across otherwise empty vertical distances. The world is still young, and the sharp edges of newly uplifted stone continue to slice through the living canopy, creating fresh wounds that bleed sap for weeks before the plants seal them again.
