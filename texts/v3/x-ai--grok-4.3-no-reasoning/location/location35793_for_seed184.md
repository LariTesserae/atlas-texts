The Oxbow Tank

There is a single sunken basilica—its nave four hundred meters from the ruined narthex to the broken apse—whose original sandstone walls have been dressed in younger coral, the coral grown so thick that the blocks have softened into pillow-like shapes. The roof is gone; the nave stands open to the water that fills it from the crushed floor to a fluctuating mirror surface fifty metres overhead. A mild current drifts in through the clerestory gap on the eastern wall and out through a rent in the western transept, a slow clockwise gyre that never quite empties the room. The current is weak enough that motes of silt spin for minutes before they settle; the same slow rotation polishes the coral into faint helical ridges.

Inside this cathedral-space the factory lives. Two colossal pistons—each the thickness of an oak trunk—rise from the nave floor and disappear into the water above. They do not move quickly; the uppermost flanges of the pistons carry a pale bloom of bacteria that brightens, dims, and brightens again in cycles of roughly three days, timed by pressure changes in the air pockets trapped inside the pistons themselves. When the bloom brightens, thousands of minute fans embedded along the cylinder walls engage, drawing water through slotted vents and through sintered-metal scrubbers that hiss in a tone too low for any but a drifting ear to register. The filtered water is vented through a grid of bronze nozzles beneath a freestanding gallery that once held choir stalls; each jet is fitted with a hinged copper flap whose periodic opening is governed by the expansion and contraction of a wax-filled bellows. Every flap therefore opens at its own languid interval, producing a soft, asynchronous ticking that constitutes the only rhythmic sound in the nave.

Below the gallery, the choir-stall niches have been retrofitted with rows of ribbed glass flasks. Inside each flask a single stalk of kelp grows toward a faintly luminous phosphor dot embedded in the flask’s stopper. The kelp is engineered to secrete an oily resin that collects beneath the stopper; once a flask is full, a weighted diaphragm at its base cracks open under hydrostatic pressure, releasing the resin as a slow, sheet-like plume. The plume travels three body-lengths before it breaks into individual globules that catch the low light and drift, each globule the size of a thumbprint and as transparent as gelatin. The globules eventually settle onto the coral lips of a recessed trench running the length of the nave; there, they feed a colony of pale, membrane-winged crustaceans that never leave the trench, their movements visible only as minute ripples against the silt.

Light reaches the floor in thin shafts through seams between the coral growths, each shaft bent by refractive shifts in the brackish thermocline that hovers four metres above the pavement. The shafts themselves drift a few centimetres every hour as the thermocline flexes. In these moving shafts, the only fish that tolerate the space—a transparent, ribbon-bodied species—hang vertically and rotate slowly, mouths open to strain the resin globules. Their eyes are a dull ruby; when a shaft of light passes through them they glow like tiny garnets before the shaft drifts on. No other vertebrate has been recorded here.

The temperature stays within one degree of twenty-eight Celsius, maintained by geothermal capillaries that rise from a seam beneath the former high altar. The capillaries are wrapped in sleeves of braided copper that conduct the heat outward in fine threads; where these threads contact the coral, the coral thickens into low domes, each dome housing a pocket of steam that gently vents through a pinhole nozzle. The steam forms transient clouds no larger than a hand, which rise two metres and then collapse into a drizzle of warm droplets—each droplet no bigger than a millet seed. When enough droplets accumulate on the upper edges of the pistons they slide, one by one, down the cylinder walls, leaving faint tracks through the bacterial bloom before rejoining the nave water.

The only visible movement on the architectural scale is the gradual migration of the entire coral mass: each decade the polyps abandon the lower courses and colonise the next higher ring of sandstone blocks. The vacated stone below is colonised by a dark-green encrusting alga whose metabolic rate is so low that its respiration registers only as a faint, steady draw on dissolved oxygen. This draw creates a minute but measurable gradient, a layer of water just above the algae that is one part per million poorer in oxygen than the layer above it. The gradient is stable for years at a time; only a patient instrument would detect its creep.

Above, the mirror surface occasionally receives a single, large raindrop from some higher cistern whose overflow leaks through a hairline crack. The drop hits, spreads into a transient lens, and vanishes, leaving behind a faint ripple that travels outward at walking pace and eventually dissolves against the coral walls. The ripple is the loudest transient event the nave experiences; once it passes, the water returns to its original density of suspended life and engineered motion, a cathedral whose congregation consists of resin, steam, and the slow, polishing current.
