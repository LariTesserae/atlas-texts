The land is not land.  
It is a single cell of living tissue the size of a derelict warehouse, somewhere beneath the skin of an incomprehensibly larger organism. The floor is a continuous plane of green-white cambium that flexes with every respiratory pulse; veins the width of wrist-thick ropes climb and descend like elevator shafts, glistening with condensation that beads, rolls, and evaporates in slow, deliberate cycles.  

Nothing here has been cut or measured. The surfaces grow exactly where they touch, meeting in seamless lips of cellulose. The air is warm and saturated, smelling of chlorophyll and distant fermentation, like the inside of a greenhouse left closed too long. Condensation forms on every upward-facing surface and releases in tiny, plosive drips that echo with a metallic clarity—an ordinary rain, caught, magnified, and repeated inside a resonant cavity. Each drip is answered by the slow hydraulic creak of xylem pressure equalizing across neighboring vessels, a dull bass note so constant it passes for silence until you leave and realize it has gone with you.

At intervals the cambium shivers. The tremor begins at the far wall—actually a membrane—and travels outward in an expanding ring, nudging the forest of fibers that rise like immense reeds. These fibers are not trunks; they are single elongated cells, translucent enough to reveal slow cytoplasmic streaming inside them. When the wave passes, each cell bows, then straightens, shedding a fine mist of droplets that linger and recombine before falling again. The mist is loud—every droplet splashes against the same taut surface, producing a rainstick roar that dies and rebuilds with each respiratory wave.  

Nothing else moves, because nothing else is needed. The only fauna is a single species of microscopic rotifer that lives, dies, and decomposes within the same droplet of sap, generations passing between one heartbeat of the larger host. Their presence registers as a faint, gritty sparkle when light shifts through the membrane overhead.  

Light arrives as if through layers of green glass—diffuse, steady, never harsh, filtered by the chlorophyll lattice two body-lengths above. It casts no shadows, only gradients of saturation, turning the colorless sap momentarily jade where it gathers in cups formed by surface tension.  

The world is young; none of its surfaces have weathered. Edges remain knife-sharp where cells have split to accommodate new growth. A fragment of cambium can still be peeled away in a single sheet, curling into a tube that seals itself again if left alone, already beginning the next cell division.  

Yet this youth contradicts the scale. The chamber is pocket-sized from without—barely twenty paces across—but inside it the distances behave differently. Walking straight feels like walking inward; the veins narrow and the fibers thicken, but the room never ends, only compresses around you until the very air seems to be moving in the opposite direction, carrying you back to the membrane you first entered.  

Each cycle of condensation, drip, tremor, and photosynthetic breath is its own weather system, isolated, self-contained. The space is almost empty of matter—only a few kilograms of biomass occupy the volume of a small auditorium—yet that matter is loud with living hydraulics, a single note repeated at changing volumes, a weather that never needs an atmosphere larger than itself.
