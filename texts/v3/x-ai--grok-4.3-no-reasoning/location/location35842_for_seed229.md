The plateau sits high enough that the horizon folds itself into a haze of muted violet, like the inside of a vast, half-collapsed lung. It is only perhaps a hundred paces from lip to lip in any direction—an island of stone afloat in thin air—yet the emptiness beyond its edges is not empty; it hums, throbs, sings. The stone is porous, black with iron and pale with salt, its surface rasped into shallow basins and crooked spines. Nothing glitters, for there is no free water to polish; the dryness is absolute, a mineral vacuum that siphons moisture the moment it is offered. Light falls in thick, dusty shafts from a low, cloud-muffled sun, enough to paint the stone the color of old wine, not enough to warm it through.  

Among the shallow basins, green things grow. They do not look like plants so much as slow motion—a living embroidery of fringed, velvet ropes that hang from the stone lips, coiling in the air as if stirred by unseen currents. Each strand is tipped with a tiny, lidless eye of iridescence; the eyes turn toward the shafts of dim light, then close when the shafts shift. Between them, clusters of fleshy trumpets rise straight from the rock, their throats lined with trembling cilia. Every few heartbeats the cilia snap in perfect unison, producing a single, soft report that multiplies across the whole plateau until the air itself seems to shiver.  

There is no rain, no dew, yet the green things drink. Minute, invisible crystals of salt exfoliate from the porous stone; the ropes sweep the air and gather them, dissolving the crystals into their velvet surfaces. The trumpets, in turn, exhale warm, faintly sweet vapor—the plateau’s only humidity—creating a soft lens of heavier air that clings a hand-span above the ground. Step through that lens and the temperature climbs; leave it and the air is again thin and cool.  

The loudness comes from movement. The wind that never arrives from any horizon nevertheless swirls across the plateau in tight, restless spirals. Each spiral lifts grit and crystal dust, carries it in a whirling column for a moment, then drops it somewhere else. The columns sing as they spin, a low, flute-note that fractures into a scatter of higher harmonics whenever one column passes through another. It is never still; the plateau is never still. The green ropes flex and the trumpets pulse; the dust spirals shift their paths by the second. Even the stone seems to breathe, its pores dilating when a spiral passes, closing when it departs.  

Fauna moves in the same restless grammar. Palm-sized gliders—six translucent wings arranged like the vanes of a seed—rise from the trumpets on columns of rising air, ride the dust spirals upward, then fold their wings and drop again, always landing on a different trumpet. They leave behind glistening threads of mucus that the ropes immediately harvest. Larger forms glide overhead: kite-bodied creatures the size of hawks, their undersides veined with the same iridescent eyes, their edges frayed into countless fine filaments. They descend only to the very lip of the plateau, never landing, never leaving, their shadows skimming the green embroidery like slow, deliberate hands.  

Scattered across the stone are remnants of earlier visitors. A single low wall of fitted basalt blocks, no higher than a knee, runs in an incomplete circle around one of the larger basins; inside it the ropes grow twice as thick, their eyes larger, the trumpets more frequent. Nearby lies a broken bowl of fired clay, its interior etched with the same spiral patterns the wind now draws in dust. The clay is cool to the touch, the glaze still faintly lustrous; whatever hand last held it has been gone long enough for the glaze to craze, not long enough for the dust to bury it. A second object—a length of braided fiber, half-unraveled—has been caught in the lip of a basin; the ropes have already begun to thread themselves through its strands, turning human craft back into green lattice.  

Nothing is loud in the usual sense; the plateau does not roar. Instead, every element—wind, wing, trumpet, rope, dust, breath—adds its own small report, and the reports arrive so quickly that the ear receives only a continuous, layered thrum. The sound rises and falls in waves that seem to follow the motion of the dust spirals themselves, as though the air is remembering each passage and replaying it a half-second late.  

Over time the plateau rearranges itself. A rope may detach, drift on a passing spiral, and re-anchor elsewhere; a trumpet may collapse inward, its cilia folding into a seed-like nodule that the next wind carries away; a new basin may open where a dust spiral lingers, the stone softening, subsiding, then hardening again. The basalt wall shifts by finger-widths each day, its blocks sliding along invisible grooves worn by generations of such movement. The plateau is never the same shape twice, yet the pattern of change is so established that it feels like a single, slow animal breathing inside its own skin.

In this place the only rule is that movement is metabolism: whatever does not move is harvested; whatever moves too long in one place is reshaped; the green things drink salt and motion, the creatures drink the green, the stone drinks the memory of both, and the wind carries the difference onward until it becomes the next configuration of the same unending exchange.
