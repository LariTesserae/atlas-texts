The Compact Eden — sometimes called the Drywell — is no bigger than a single steep alley stacked five stories high, yet the air here carries the same warmth that rises from ancient temple stones at midday. Overhead, the ceiling is a single slab of etched granite that has never known rain and never will; instead, daylight arrives as a slow, dusty amber seepage through a lattice of foot-wide cracks thirty meters above the floor. Each beam of light slants downward like a falling thread, warming the upper terraces before it is swallowed by the foliage.

What looks like “vegetation” is in fact a dense vertical forest of sky-roots—fleshy, ribbed trunks the color of dried turmeric that climb straight up from cracks in the stone and then branch outward in broad, overlapping shelves. The leaves are thick, waxy discs edged with minute glass spines; they catch the falling light and diffuse it so that even the lowest alley floor remains in perpetual twilight. Between the shelves, pale maroon orchids hang in trembling ropes whose nectar smells faintly of scorched sugar.

There is no free water anywhere. Moisture is carried inside hollow root nodes the size of wine casks; if one were cut, clear syrup would ooze out and be re-absorbed within minutes by the same root’s feeder hairs. The ground itself is a salt-and-granite cobbled pavement laid by forgotten hands, its joints filled not with mortar but with living fungal felt that swells when it senses weight and then contracts again, locking each stone in place. Every few minutes a warm updraft rolls along the pavement, carrying a dry, mineral tang and the soft percussion of thousands of tiny chitin plates.

The fauna is everywhere and almost touch-close. Thumb-sized gliders—six translucent wings stretched over finger-bones—drift between the sky-root shelves, their wingbeats producing a continuous metallic whisper. Below them, spindle-legged gleaners the color of old bone pick microscopic flakes of salt from between the cobbles; their legs click against the stone like knitting needles. Higher up, slow-moving herd beetles the size of roof tiles migrate along the topmost terraces, their carapaces ridged into solar collectors that glint like burnished copper whenever a stray beam of light touches them. None of these creatures require drinking water; each stores hydration in internal bladders and exhales only a faint mineral mist that the sky-roots immediately reclaim.

Because the alley is only four meters wide at its widest, every surface is in reach. Primitive clay-and-reed scaffolding zigzags between opposing walls, lashed together with strips of cured root-bark. On the scaffolds, artisans shape new irrigation bulbs from sun-baked salt-clay, then etch irrigation runes into them with obsidian flakes. The runes are not decorative; when warm air passes over the raised symbols it vibrates, producing a soft, continuous chord that underpins all other sound—the alley’s low, perpetual music.

Temperature stays within the narrow band that keeps the root syrup viscous and the fungal felt supple. When the upper lattice allows too much direct light, the herd beetles cluster together, forming living parasols whose overlapping carapaces cast rippling shadows across the lower levels; the gliders react by folding their wings and drifting lower, momentarily increasing the density of the space until every surface seems covered by a drifting veil of glassine wings. When the light fades, the beetles drift apart again, and the gliders ascend, restoring the layered traffic.

The alley has no exit in any direction; at each end the granite walls pinch inward until a single sky-root fills the remaining gap, its trunk fused to the stone like scar tissue. Yet the ecosystem continues its measured cycles within these invisible boundaries, sustained by the heat stored in the granite, the salt carried upward on warm updrafts, and the careful handiwork of the artisans who have never left the Drywell and have no need to.
