A high ridge of water.

Not a wave, not a tide, but a long, permanent suspension: a curved wall of ocean that hangs above the level where oceans should rest, held by nothing visible except the patience of its own density. From far below, its underside glows a faint turquoise; when you swim close, the color bleaches into milk-white. The surface that should be a horizon is now an inner ceiling, marked with slow-moving bands of lighter and darker water that drift like weather systems seen from beneath a cloud deck.

The ridge is high enough that the atmospheric pressure has dropped—your ears feel it as a soft inward tug—and yet the water is warm, almost bathwater. Occasional thin curtains of rain descend from the ceiling, falling upward for a few dozen body-lengths before dissolving into mist that hovers and is reabsorbed. Between these curtains the visibility is several hundred body-lengths, then it softens into a diffuse, jade haze.

Below the suspended ridge, the terrain is embryonic: broad, barely consolidated shelves of pale, porous stone that still leak faint streamers of dissolved mineral. The rock is the color of wet chalk and rings hollow when tapped. Nothing has yet etched channels into it; the first trickles of drainage are only now beginning to darken its surface in branching stains that will one day become canyons. Between these unfinished platforms drift suspended fields of kelp-like fronds whose holdfasts have not yet found purchase. The fronds themselves are weightless here; they rotate slowly in the faint circulatory currents, brushing one another with the sound of wet silk against wet silk.

On the upper surfaces of the unfinished stone, new settlements have begun. They are not built so much as grown and then tied down: long ropes of braided fiber have been lashed between knob-like protrusions of the raw rock and anchored with living weights—globular molluscs whose shells have been coaxed into squat domes and tunnels. The domes are linked by taut walkways of the same braided cord; each walkway carries a thin film of water that moves in slow pulses, driven by the rhythmic contraction of muscular rings spaced along the cord. Inside the domes, the air is held in delicate meniscus bubbles. Light is dim; it arrives already filtered by tens of meters of water above, arriving as a shifting, pearly dusk that makes every interior surface look varnished.

Fauna here moves at the speed of coral. Armored fish the size of small rafts drift in loose herds, their gill covers pulsing once every few minutes. Their eyes are small and recessed; they navigate chiefly by the faint pressure gradients that roll through the water like distant thunder. Smaller creatures—translucent slugs the length of an arm—creep along the fiber ropes, grazing on microscopic films of algae that grow only where the rope is shaded by the next dome above. The slugs leave behind faint iridescent trails that linger for days before dissolving.

Sound is almost entirely absent. The occasional soft report of a bubble detaching from a dome and rising to the ceiling is the loudest event; otherwise there is only the low, almost subsonic sigh of water negotiating its own density. Even the rain curtains arrive in silence until they strike an occupied dome, at which point the membrane vibrates like a drumskin underwater.

Over decades the embryonic stone will finish consolidating; over centuries the first true channels will appear. For now, the city and its scaffold exist in a state of perpetual near-completion, held between the slow upward creep of the ridge above and the slow downward settling of the unfinished stone below, while the inhabitants move through their corridors at the pace of their own breathing.
