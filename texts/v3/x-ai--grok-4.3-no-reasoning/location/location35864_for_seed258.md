The plateau is not so much *grown* as *held*. Every plane of the horizon is plant, and every plant is substrate; beneath your feet the turf is not soil with roots in it but a single, unbroken membrane of interwoven blades, six or seven hand-spans thick, breathing, warm, springy like kelp in a slack tide. The membrane spreads without fracture across an area larger than any city yet smaller than a country, curving only at its far lip where it folds over the edge of the cliff into mist.  

Because there is never change, the membrane does not thicken or thin; it is exactly as deep now as it was when its first cells met. Water seeps upward through microscopic channels that never widen, never clog. The water is warm—slightly above skin temperature—and arrives in droplets the size of millet grains that condense directly on the undersides of the blades. When the drops gather too large they slide down into the membrane and disappear again, their movement a single, constant whisper.  

Above the membrane the architecture rises: walls, vaults, hollow columns, and open ramps, all woven from the same living grass. No joint is visible; growth simply changes direction. A colonnade may twist into a ramp, the ramp forks into three fan-vaults; none of them have been cut or joined. They simply *are*. Light filters through the blades themselves, green-gold, refracted rather than blocked, so the space inside each chamber is never brighter or darker than the space outside.  

Because the world is young—still forming—there are abrupt absences. Here and there the membrane stops in clean, vertical faces three or four meters high. Beneath those faces you can see the unfinished matrix: pale, translucent, not yet vascular, like uncooked dough. No wind touches these scarps; nothing erodes them. They are the edge of the plateau’s present, waiting for the next increment of growth that will never arrive.  

The temperature is stable. The air is saturated but does not condense into fog; instead, thin films of water hang invisibly in the open vaults, distorting distance just enough that the next column seems farther than the last. Sound is constant: a single chord produced by the membrane’s breathing and the sliding droplets inside it. The pitch is low—C two octaves below middle C—and perfectly steady. The fauna are rare because every niche is already occupied by plant. Occasionally a transparent, shrimp-like organism the size of a fingernail drifts between the blades on the plateau’s underside, feeding on the condensate. Their passage makes no ripple in the constant sound.  

Nothing falls. Nothing rises. The light does not move across the sky because the sky itself is the living ceiling sixty meters overhead: a single, unbroken leaf turned toward a sun that never shifts. In this plateau, time and erosion have not yet found a grammar; only growth and equilibrium remain.
