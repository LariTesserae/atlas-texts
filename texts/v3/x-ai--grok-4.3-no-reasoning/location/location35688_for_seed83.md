Beneath the surface the city breathes through its own slow marrow. It is not carved from bedrock but poured, sheeted, riveted, and conduit-rutted until every surface is artifact. Walls are rivet-dimpled steel plates slathered with successive skins of once-white ceramic tile, their seams sealed by congealed grease that has darkened to amber. Floors alternate between poured ferrocrete, diamond-plate walkways, and long stretches of thick glass that look down into still black water three or four storeys below. The water is residual: the last thin membrane of an aquifer the builders tapped centuries ago and then forgot. It gleams faintly under the infrequent pulse of emergency bulbs—salt-scummed, still enough that reflections of the tilework hang upside-down like pale scripts.

The ceiling is no higher than a tall person could reach with an upstretched hand. Low ducts droop in clusters like mechanical stalactites, their insulation cracked into brittle flakes that snow, grain by grain, onto the shoulders of anyone passing beneath. Between the ducts run ribbons of copper piping, green with verdigris, each joint weeping a single slow bead of condensation that never quite falls; the droplets fatten, gleam, and are reabsorbed before they can complete their journey. That rhythm—build, release, reclaim—sets the only visible clock.

Heat is stored in the metal rather than the air. Every surface radiates the same temperature as a fevered palm. Fans the size of dinner plates, bolted into square niches, turn so languidly that their individual blades can be counted between each pulse. They stir a syrup-thick layer of humidity that never disperses; the moisture rises from the black water below, slides along the pipes, and condenses again on colder stretches of conduit. Occasionally, a relay somewhere deeper in the warren clicks; the fans pause, a draft of even hotter air sighs through a grille, and the cycle resumes.

Vegetation survives in opportunistic seams. Where a pipe has split and the leak runs down a vertical surface, a single species of rust-orange lichen grows. It forms coin-sized colonies that follow the rivulet exactly, as though the plant itself were liquid that had learned to cling. Nothing roots; nothing flowers. The lichen darkens and pales in millimetres-wide bands that record decades of humidity fluctuation like tree rings.

Light arrives only through thumb-sized red LEDs spaced every ten metres along the main corridor. They are powered by thermocouples that draw from the heat differential between the pipes and the water; when the system equalizes, the corridor goes dark for hours until the thermocouples rebuild their tiny charge. In those intervals the only illumination is the glass floor itself, which exhales a faint bioluminescent plankton bloom nourished by the same warmth. The glow is spectral, a bruised green that makes the metal look underwater even though it is not.

Fauna is present as absence first: no rats, no roaches, no crickets. The only inhabitants are slow armored things the size of thumbnails that live inside the seams between floor plates. Their carapaces are the same dull oxide color as the lichen; they graze on flaked insulation and leave minute trails of silver oxide behind them. Their movement registers as a soft, patient scraping that registers just above the threshold of silence.

Sound is therefore mostly a catalogue of things that almost happen: the subsonic thrum of water pressure fluctuating behind a bulkhead, the minute plink of a droplet finally losing its surface tension, the click of a fan blade slipping one cog further in its reduction gear. Once in a long while a deeper groan travels along the entire lattice of steel: thermal expansion flexing a joint half a kilometre away. The groan arrives so slowly that by the time it reaches an ear it is already part of the background hush again.

Time here is measured in corrosion stages. Paint grades from cream to mustard to iron black over decades; rivets develop concentric halos of rust that map the seasonal rise and fall of the water table far above. Maintenance drones still crawl the ceiling on magnetic treads, but their batteries have long since failed; they hang motionless, their sensor lenses filmed with condensate, becoming new stalactites in the perpetual dim. The only active machinery is the city’s residual circulatory system—the pipes themselves, the fans, the thermocouples—each locked into its own patient loop, each contributing one more faint degree to the equilibrium that never quite tips.

The rules are simple and absolute: nothing is allowed to be sudden. Temperature changes must propagate through metal rather than air; moisture must travel by surface tension or gravity alone; light must be generated from the temperature gradient or not at all. Anything faster than the pace of oxidation is considered trespass.
