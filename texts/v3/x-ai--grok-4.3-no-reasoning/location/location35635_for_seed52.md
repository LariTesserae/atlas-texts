The city sits where the land refuses to decide what it is yet.  A broad, low basin stretches under a permanently clouded sky, its floor cut by wandering ribbons of silver water—some only a hand’s width, some wide enough to row a skiff between the piers.  Between the streams, raised terraces of fine-grained basalt push up like fresh vertebrae; their edges remain knife-sharp, as though the bedrock itself rose yesterday and has not learned weathering yet.  Fog clings to the rock, condenses into droplets, slides down, and is gathered again by thin gutter-channels woven through every street.

Where the terraces flatten, an arrangement of towers and under-bridges knits them together.  The towers are not so much built as grown: lattices of smart obsidian and living glass climb thirty, forty stories, their walls continually re-slicing themselves into new load-bearing patterns.  Bridges hang in asymmetrical arcs between them, made of the same material and just as restless; every dawn a fresh filament may appear, or an old one dissolve into mist.  Walkways therefore shift—sometimes only by a finger-width, sometimes by an entire block—yet the city’s traffic moves as though the changes have already been mapped into memory.

Vegetation finds every crack.  Ferns as tall as a person unfurl from horizontal seams in the towers; their spores drift down in slow, glittering spirals and germinate in the wet gutters.  Between the ferns, clusters of tiny white lantern-flowers dangle on hair-fine stalks; at night they pulse once an hour, giving the streets their only reliable light.  Because the basalt terraces are still so young, the roots do not reach deep.  Instead, each plant drinks from the constant film of condensation that coats every surface.

Between all this greenery there is emptiness.  Wide plazas open where the terraces never quite met, leaving stretches of bare, dark stone crossed only by faint condensation trails.  In those plazas the air feels colder, though the temperature nowhere drops below a perpetual late-spring warmth.  People—when they appear—move singly or in pairs, footsteps dampened by pads of living moss they lay in front of themselves and pick up behind.  Their garments are thin, adaptive films that bead water away while exchanging heat with the skin; color shifts with each person’s mood or errand, a private Morse code visible only at close range.

Fauna is scarce and shy.  A species of translucent, six-winged midges patrols the lantern-flowers, their wingbeats too soft to register as sound.  Occasionally a larger form—something between lizard and kite—glides between towers on wings of stretched aerogel, hunting the midges.  These predators never land; they simply spiral upward until the fog swallows them.

The city’s systems talk to one another in a language of micro-friction.  Each bridge filament carries piezoelectric veins; every footfall or droplet strike is registered, folded into the larger pattern, and answered by a minute redistribution of mass somewhere else.  The sound of this conversation is almost none: a continuous low susurrus like an unfinished exhalation, occasionally punctuated by the soft, glassy click of two filaments deciding to merge.

Overhead, the clouds never break, yet the light that filters through them dims and brightens on a twenty-three-hour cycle, as though the sky itself were breathing.  When the cycle dips toward its darkest, the lantern-flowers pulse more frequently and the towers grow additional internal filaments to route stored photonic charge.  It is then, in that brief false midnight, that the few inhabitants step outside in greater numbers.  They walk to the edges of the terraces, lean over the still-sharp drops, and lower fine nets into the drifting fog.  What they collect is not water but memory: condensate that has already passed through every leaf and filament in the basin.  They carry the droplets home in narrow crystal vials, each one a palimpsest of the city’s single day.
