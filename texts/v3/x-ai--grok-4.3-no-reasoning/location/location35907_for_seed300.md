The Hollow of Weeping Glass

It is not a valley in the conventional sense, but a circular bowl that has been steadily filled by the slow exhalation of the ground. Every surface here remembers water, yet almost none of it stands in open pools; instead, moisture constantly condenses and re-evaporates in an endless, private tide. The basin is no more than two hundred paces across at its widest point—small enough that a single person standing at the rim can feel the opposite side as a tangible presence rather than a distant horizon.

The walls are made of dark, flinty stone threaded with veins of pale, translucent silica that the locals call weeping glass. Each fracture line glitters with microscopic droplets that swell, merge, and drip in a soft, rhythmic patter—quiet enough to be mistaken for silence until the ear adjusts. The silica veins are not static; they grow and recede over decades, leaving behind delicate, rippled textures that resemble frozen waves. At the very center of the bowl, a single industrial derrick—painted the color of old bone and streaked with rust—rises like the skeleton of an iron lily. Its pump-jack still moves, groaning once every seven seconds, though what it extracts is not oil but a continuous thread of vapor that immediately condenses into the surrounding air.

Vegetation is meager: tufts of iron-hard lichen cling to the silica veins, forming tiny forests that shift from gray-green to sulfur-yellow depending on how recently a droplet has passed. Between them, mats of black capillary moss drink directly from the weeping glass and exude a faint metallic scent when disturbed. No trees rise above ankle height. The ground itself is not soil but a compacted aggregate of crushed flint and silica dust, polished by generations of condensation into a mirror-smooth pavement that rings faintly underfoot.

The derrick is only the most obvious artifact. Scattered across the basin floor are half-buried relics of the same forgotten industry: gear housings split like seed pods, ceramic insulators cracked into star patterns, lengths of braided copper cable now braided instead with moss. Each object has settled into the landscape so thoroughly that it is impossible to say whether the ground grew up around them or they slowly sank. Where the silica veins intersect these relics, the stone has begun to incorporate the metal, creating hybrid textures—copper-green threads inside translucent stone, bolts whose threads have been replaced by mineral growth.

Fauna is scarce but not absent. Every dawn, a single pale salamander the size of a child’s hand emerges from a crack beneath the derrick’s base. It drinks from a droplet no larger than a pinhead, then retreats. The salamander’s skin is the same translucent silica as the veins; only the dark line of its digestive tract gives it away. At irregular intervals—sometimes days, sometimes weeks—a thin column of vapor rises from the pump’s exhaust stack and hangs for a moment like a question mark before dissolving. When this happens, the salamander pauses, lifts its head, and appears to listen.

The bowl is never truly still. The pump’s cycle creates a subsonic throb that travels through the flint pavement and into bone. Condensation forms faster on the north-facing silica veins than on the south; when the droplets finally fall, they strike the pavement at slightly different pitches depending on the mineral they have absorbed, producing an almost inaudible glockenspiel of micro-sounds. On still days the entire basin resonates at a single, low frequency that makes loose fragments of weeping glass quiver.

At the eastern rim, where the flint wall is lowest, a faint path of crushed silica leads outward—evidence of infrequent human passage. Whoever walks it leaves no lasting mark; within hours the path is re-polished by the same slow exhalation that shapes everything else. The basin accepts visitors but does not retain their presence. It continues its private cycle of weeping and polishing, vapor and condensation, pump-stroke and droplet-fall, as though the world outside has already agreed to forget it exists.
