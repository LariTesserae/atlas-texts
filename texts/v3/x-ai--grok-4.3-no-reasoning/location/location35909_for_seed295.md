A stair of interlocked basalt drums ascends from the lowland plains like a tide that never quite retreats. Each step is hand-dressed by root and frost, its edges blurred by successive generations of black-liver moss whose slow release of tannin keeps the rock perpetually damp even when rain has not fallen for weeks. Where water pools, it never evaporates; it instead beads inside the moss and slides downward by capillary creep, forming rivulets that trickle for days before sinking into the next step. The stair itself measures perhaps three thousand vertical feet, yet its tread widths vary from a carriage lane to the narrowness of an overgrown goat path, granting the impression of a single monumental causeway that nonetheless contains many smaller ones inside it.

Above this colossal ladder the mountain continues upward in an undulating meadow, its slope gentle enough that the forest canopy spreads without break. The trees—slender conifers whose resin smells faintly of heated brass—grow at intervals so short that their lowest limbs intertwine; needles, cones, and a snowfall of pale shed bark create a continuous duff layer that muffles footsteps to felt silence. Beneath that duff the soil never dries; its color is the bruised purple of perpetually waterlogged iron. It yields underfoot with a slow sponge inhalation, then closes again, leaving only the faint signature of displaced stems.

A translucent ceiling of leaf-litter and living needles filters the daylight into a perpetual penumbra. The light that does descend is the color of weak tea, diffused laterally until direction becomes meaningless. The resulting gloom is not quite night; it is rather the interior light of a vast shuttered hall whose clerestory windows are permanently sealed. Lichen on every trunk glows with a dull foxfire when rubbed; in places the glow survives untouched for hours, creating constellations of ghostly script across the slope. These bioluminescent veins shift in slow circadian drifts: what was a branching river of light at dusk will, by morning, have pulled itself into isolated islands no larger than a palm.

Between tree trunks, curtains of epiphytic mistletoe and hanging clubmoss descend like stage rigging. Each strand supports its own inverted watershed—tiny bromeliad cisterns collect condensation from every exhalation of warm air rising off the slope. A single droplet may take half a day to fall; many never fall at all, migrating instead along the velvet surface of the clubmoss toward the next waiting cup. Where two such plants touch, a narrow meniscus forms that trembles but does not break. In this way an entire horizontal aquifer exists in mid-air, inches above the ground yet wholly independent of it.

In the canopy the sound of the world accumulates. Millions of resin ducts contract and dilate with temperature change; each pulse releases a soft tick. Collective ticking aggregates into a single sustained hiss, the acoustic signature of an enormous kettle just below boil. Layered beneath it are the chewing of bark-boring larvae, each mandible fall audible as a distant grain of rice dropped onto wood; the papery unfurling of fern croziers responding to humidity; the minute squeak of water tension snapping when epiphyte cisterns overfill. All of these sounds remain constant yet phase-shift across hours, so that the listener perceives no discrete events, only a single low-pressure roar that swells and contracts like a lung behind felt.

Fauna moves within this matrix at densities that erase any concept of solitude. Herds of thumb-sized ungulates—antlered, six-legged—drift through the understory in schools, their synchronized footfalls creating the percussive undercurrent of the hiss. Larger browsers, their pelage the exact green-black of shadow needles, remain motionless for hours; only the sequential twitch of flank muscles as each stomach chamber activates gives them away. In the canopy, sheet webs span between branches twenty paces apart; their radial threads are spun from a protein that refracts the dim light into momentary violet halos whenever a current of warm air drifts past. The spiders themselves are nearly invisible, the size of sycamore seeds, yet their accumulated number warps every frond and limb downward by the weight of silk alone.

Nothing here is engineered by axe or kiln; the total built quality arises instead from the uninterrupted labor of successive generations of living tissue. Every surface is either growing or being digested; edges exist only as the momentary boundary where one organism’s expansion front meets another’s retreat. Over centuries this slow war has produced a topography whose contours are entirely organic—slopes adjust themselves by the volume of stored water in root lattices; ridges migrate laterally by a hand-span each decade as one flank prospers and the opposing flank collapses. The stair of basalt drums, though lithic at heart, participates in the same metabolism: its surface is continuously re-profiled by the acid excretions of the moss, whose patterns of retreat and advance record rainfall chemistry from seasons long ended.

At this elevation the air remains warm because the dense vegetation envelope captures and re-radiates heat absorbed at lower altitudes. Temperature gradients are therefore expressed vertically within centimeters rather than vertically across the slope; a palm held at knee height registers noticeably cooler than the same palm raised to shoulder height. The resulting micro-convection drives the perpetual internal drizzle without any need for external clouds.

Time registers here only as an extremely low-frequency undulation: the shedding of a single conifer needle is an event whose acoustic trace joins the hiss but does not alter it; the collapse of a single epiphyte cistern is a minute fluctuation that travels the aerial aquifer for days before equalizing. All processes are patient, all boundaries semi-permeable, all densities approaching saturation. The mountain does not wait; it is already complete.
