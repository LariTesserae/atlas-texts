Beneath a collapsed crystal floor in the flank of an old impact crater, a pocket of residual heat still moves.

The pocket is not a cave so much as a sealed capillary: a slow, pressurized blister of ancient subsurface melt kept liquid by its own trapped warmth. The ceiling is a single, curving plate of translucent olivine glass where bubbles have frozen mid-ascent, turning the layer above into a greenish lens that scatters light into slow-moving flecks. Every few hundred heartbeats a bubble detaches and rises; its departure leaves a momentary ripple that travels the entire ceiling and extinguishes for a moment the points of light it carries.

The floor is not stone but the same glass, now cooled enough to form a continuous sheet. Across it, faint hexagonal fractures catch the falling condensation. Water does not pool here; it condenses continuously onto every cool facet, then beads together until surface tension launches it in microscopic droplets that skip rather than fall—surface tension still greater than local gravity. When two droplets merge they become briefly luminous, light gathering inside the momentary lens before they split again or are drawn toward the distant, lower rim.

The air is thin, saturated, and almost without motion. Only the slow exhalation of the ceiling and the faint convection cells rising from the glass maintain any circulation. Because there is no dust and no vapor larger than a few molecules, sound has almost nothing to reflect from; the ear registers mainly the continuous, single-note pressure shift when a droplet accelerates across the sheet.

There are no organisms, yet the glass is not sterile. Thin films of mineral alteration—manganese oxides, trace nickel—form dendritic patterns that shift whenever a droplet crosses them. These patterns respond slowly to temperature; when a bubble releases overhead and cools one square hand’s-breadth of ceiling, the oxides beneath grow darker for minutes afterward, a living map of thermal memory.

Light arrives only from the single high fracture in the olivine lens. Sunlight or starlight, filtered and re-filtered, arrives already concentrated into a needle beam no wider than a finger. As the fracture slowly migrates with the planet’s rotation, the needle sweeps the chamber once per local day, tracing an ellipse across the glass floor. Where it passes, the surface briefly warms; where it has passed, a thin front of condensing droplets reforms in its wake, the miniature storm front crossing the pocket like the shadow of a minute weather system.

No entrance wider than a thread connects this place to the greater world, so its only scale is the reach of that needle of light and the time it takes a droplet to traverse the floor. Outside, the surface is already cold; here the glass remembers the impact’s heat and will continue remembering it for centuries, each quiet cycle of droplet, fracture, and beam keeping the pocket briefly, precisely alive.
