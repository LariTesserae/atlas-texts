The Space Between Sleeves

This place is a cold, high, built world the size of a coat lining, hollowed and laced through its own fabrications, where industrial pipelines run like stitches through dense plush. Everything here is made; nothing grows from soil because there is no soil. The walls are a felted composite of layered fiber and ceramic teeth, repeating forever in an airless corridor just wide enough for two people to walk shoulder-to-shoulder without touching. Light is ordinary: a soft, even illumination that arrives from no single source and never casts a long shadow. Temperature remains fixed at the shivering point, as though the air itself has been woven too thin to hold warmth.

No water at all—moisture is replaced by a low-static field that prickles across metal and fabric alike. The only moisture-equivalent is a faint crystalline vapor that condenses overnight into hair-thin frost blooms along the pipe seams; by morning these blooms collapse into delicate powder that is swept away by circulation fans. Vegetation is sparse: frozen curls of black wire emerging from vents at intervals, their oxide tips dusted with the same frost. They form no pattern and support no life.

Elevation is immense—felt rather than measured—because this whole pocket exists inside the seams of a larger, unfinished structure, its corridors stacked in a tiered spiral that climbs for kilometers while never leaving its sleeve-world envelope. Erosion has not begun: every bevel is sharp, every weld bead crisp, every label still sticky from manufacture. Time has not arrived here; nothing ages or drifts or settles.

Sound is the single riotous exception. Industrial sound: the unending hammer of high-pressure valves seating, the whine of infinite-loop servos, the bass thrum of hidden turbines whose blades pass so fast they appear motionless. The noise fills every cubic centimeter; it is perfectly stable—never louder or softer, never arriving from a direction, simply present at maximum volume. Speech is impossible without lips against an ear. Music would be pointless. The only communication is touch transmitted through vibration.

No animals move. No wind stirs. Nothing decays. The only presence is the architecture itself, perpetually becoming its own future in the same configuration, a cold, small, deafening cathedral of sleeves whose seams never meet an edge.
