Rain has pooled into the shallow bowls of fresh stone. Every few paces the earth dimples, and each dimple is a thumbnail of water that refuses to drain because the rock beneath is newer than the rain. These hollows tessellate like thumbprints in clay, running in crooked lines that follow a slope so gentle it seems almost level. Yet the slope matters: it makes the water creep from one pocket to the next in slow, audible trickles no thicker than a thread.

Between the pools the rock is pale and pocked, still shedding the last grains that frost and root once prized loose. Nothing green has taken hold in the larger fissures; the cracks are too clean. Only a single line of liverwort, no wider than a boot sole, has claimed a seam that leaks constant cool air. The rest of the surface glitters with condensation beads, each bead magnifying the gray sky until the whole pavement looks sugared.

People live here—many people—yet almost none of them stand taller than the pools. Their dwellings are low vaults of fitted stone, doorways no higher than a crouch, roofs flush with the pavement itself. From above, the settlement reads as nothing more than a tightening of the natural dimples; only when you kneel do you see smoke rising from vents the width of two fingers. Inside, each chamber is no larger than a cart bed. Families share heat by lying head-to-toe; their breath keeps the inner rock filmed with moisture that beads and returns drip by drip into the same shallow catchment used for drinking.

Above these chambers the rock is busy with traffic, but the traffic is all horizontal. Rope bridges no thicker than a wrist span the widest clefts. Because the air is so still, voices carry farther than footsteps; conversations drift from one side of a court to another without anyone needing to raise their tone. When rain begins again, the entire population retreats one layer deeper and the soundscape shrinks to the hush of water sliding through channels drilled by generations of thumbs and antler tines.

Nothing larger than a fingerling moves here. Once a year, translucent shrimp no bigger than eyelashes boil up from cracks that only open on the third full moon after the solstice. For three nights the pools are crowded with quick, silent feeding; then the crustaceans vanish, and the stone goes back to listening to water.
