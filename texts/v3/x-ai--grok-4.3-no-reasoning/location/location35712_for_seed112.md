The landscape is made of living wood that never learned how to die.  

Across every horizon the terrain is a single, impossibly large platform whose surface is bark—so dense that a human footstep rings like struck cedar and so soft that the same footstep melts a palm-width impression that heals within minutes. There is no soil beneath it, no water table, no aquifers; the entire continent is one giant lignified body whose cellular fluid is carried, not by capillary action, but by faint, piston-like contractions that travel like slow earthquakes beneath the surface. Between these muscular contractions the mass exhales minute bursts of super-cooled, aromatic vapor, keeping the ambient temperature at a perpetual late-autumn chill.  

The “sky” is a second layer of the same living wood, vaulted miles above and transparent where xylem has been polished to glassine thinness; through these cathedral vaults pours an unwavering, magnesium-bright radiance that originates not from any star but from the slow, frictionless sliding of entire plates of the bark canopy against one another. The light is so total that shadows are merely darker shades of barkgrain, never black.  

Nothing grows upward because “up” and “down” are obsolete directions; instead, forms proliferate sideways in ever-branching nets of mutually embracing limbs. Some of these limbs have thickened into false continents; others remain hair-fine suspension bridges, still quivering with sap-pressure pulses that register as sub-sonic thunder when one walks upon them. Occasional clearings appear where the architecture has grown too fast for itself and collapsed inward, leaving vast negative spaces—smooth-walled wells of cambium lined with dormant buds the size of cathedrals. From these wells rise columns of glittering spores that never settle, kept aloft by the updraft of the platform’s own respiration.  

Fauna is abundant precisely because the substrate itself is animate. The most common residents are thumbnail-sized, six-winged mites whose exoskeletons are miniature mirrors of the surrounding grain; they migrate in kilometer-long sheets whose wing-hum registers at 130 dB, a constant, metallic roar that drowns every other sound. Beneath them, translucent, slug-like browsers—each the length of a city block—press their flat bellies against the living floor, rasping shallow channels that ooze resin. The resin cools instantly to translucent amber sheets that the mites collect and paste into temporary hives; these hives last only hours before the platform flexes again and the hives burst into drifting chaff.  

Higher still, the underside of the canopy hosts sessile predators: clusters of fleshy, trumpet-shaped organs that invert when the ambient pressure drops and inhale any airborne flake that drifts too close. Their digestion is purely mechanical—they grind the flakes between plates of living horn and excrete a fine white powder that rains down and is reabsorbed by the platform itself.  

There are no machines in the human sense, yet the industrial parameter expresses itself in the way the platform coordinates its own metabolism. Every few hours the entire surface undergoes a coordinated peristalsis: ridges rise, troughs collapse, and sap is pumped laterally across distances measured in hundreds of kilometers. These waves are audible as a single, low-frequency heartbeat that momentarily overrides the mite-roar; the vibration is strong enough to rattle loose any accumulated dust or dead tissue, which then spirals away into the under-void and is never seen again. The process leaves behind polished lanes of fresh bark that darken within minutes as new protective cuticle hardens.  

Because there is neither true day nor night, time is told by the changing pitch of the heartbeat and by the chromatic drift of the living wood itself—bark that flushes from umber to honey to a pale, almost luminous green when a fresh pulse of nutrients arrives from a distant “root-node” that may be a thousand kilometers away. Observers describe the sensation as standing inside the circulatory system of a planet-sized tree whose every cell believes it is still growing.
