Here, the city is the sea.

It began as coral colonies that, over millions of slow seasons, learned to grow in squared-off terraces rather than chaotic reefs. Where the polyps died, their empty calcium skeletons were occupied by other, simpler lives—moss-like sponges and mat-forming sea cucumbers—that sealed the gaps into continuous masonry. The final walls are smooth as bone but colored by the generations of encrusting life that lived and died upon them: a spectrum of pinks, ochres, and bleached whites that fades only where silt has drifted in slow clouds across the lower districts.

The water is warm, thick with dissolved minerals, so every exhalation or paddle of a fin leaves a faint shimmer of precipitation. The temperature gradient is gentle—never cold enough to slow the corals, warm enough to keep the proteins of pelagic creatures fluid. Light never reaches these depths from the sky; instead, diffuse bioluminescence leaks from thousands of micro-organisms suspended in the water like the after-image of neon. It is enough to outline the edges of towers, but not enough to cast shadows.

Above the level of the roofs, the water turns a pale indigo, stirred by the perpetual descent of fine silt and the bodies of dead plankton. Below the level of the deepest foundations, darkness is absolute except for the slow pulse of geothermal vents that breathe rhythmic plumes of warmer, saltier water into the alleys.

The city plan follows the original coral skeleton: radial boulevards where once the main polyps fed outward, cross-streets where secondary branches hardened. Windows are simply the old oscula, now widened by erosion into oval ports. Doors are the same—rounded lips of stone left by whatever creature once needed quick ingress. The streets themselves are not empty; they are still the flues through which the currents that once fed the coral continue to flow. A constant pressure-gradient moves water at the pace of breath: in along the deeper thoroughfares, up and outward along the upper galleries, back down in slender spillways where centuries of upward growth have left channels narrower than a human hand.

Fauna fills every available surface and volume. Schools of glass eels slide through the vertical shafts of the tallest spires, their bodies lit from within by colonies of symbiotic bacteria. Armored mollusks cling to every ledge, their shells tessellated in fractal ridges that exactly match the original polyp pattern; when threatened, they withdraw and seal the opening with a translucent hood the color of old milk. Between the buildings, larger predators—ribbon-shaped fish whose fins have lengthened into undulating nets—patrol the open water that was once the market squares. Their hunts are almost silent; only the faintest vibration travels through the dense medium. When they strike, prey simply vanishes into the net, and the water momentarily clears for a body-length before the slow drift of silt closes the space again.

Vegetation is sparse by oceanic standards: thin, whip-like algae grow only on the sunward faces of the highest roofs, where a trace of surface light still reaches them. Their fronds move in perfect synchrony with the pressure-driven current, never touching one another. Between the buildings, nothing larger than single-celled mats survives.

The soundscape is a composite of micro-events: the click of a mollusk tightening its hood, the soft friction of an eel brushing stone, the bass thump of a predator’s net contracting. Occasionally a vent deeper in the foundation exhales a bubble of superheated water; the expanding sphere rises, cooling as it ascends, until it bursts against an upper cornice with a muted report that rolls outward like distant thunder before dissolving into the general quiet.

Nothing here is built in the human sense, yet the architecture is undeniably urban—dense, multi-layered, shaped by successive generations of life that each inherited the work of the last. The city continues because the same pressure gradients that first lifted the coral still move; because the same limestone chemistry that once precipitated exoskeletons now precipitates the living mortar that seals new polyps to old bone; because the darkness that would seem empty to an eye is, at these depths, simply the color of the only light that has ever mattered here.
