Across an immense shelf of granite the size of a modest county, the only consistent element is the constant cold. Each hundred meters of altitude gains a fresh layer of glass-clear ice that beads against stone in slow, thickening lenses. These lenses catch the dim and ever-present half-light, a thin gray wash that neither brightens nor dims and that casts no decisive shadows. The ice has been forming for an era; it no longer thickens or thaws, yet every flake that lands adheres with mineral stubbornness, so the shelf is etched in frozen runnels that map the remembered course of water rather than any present trickle.

Here and there, the granite tilts into shallow bowls. Inside each bowl a skin of hoarfrost sits like a membrane over a film of unfrozen, faintly saline water. These micro-ponds are the only moisture; they neither evaporate nor seep away. Vapor rises from their surfaces in threads so fine they remain visible only where the half-light refracts through them, forming minute halos above the ice. When the threads meet a pocket of colder air they condense again, returning the water to its bowl. The cycle never accelerates, never ceases; the bowls are reservoirs of paused rain.

Vegetation is reduced to solitary, rope-thick strands of black-green lichen that lace across the highest points of the granite domes. Each strand is anchored by a single holdfast no wider than a thumbnail, yet the holdfast has grown for centuries, prying microscopic flakes from the stone until it sits proud like a tiny pedestal. Between the domes the lichen disappears. Nothing else grows; the air is too cold, the exposure too total.

Far downslope, visible as a faint horizon line of paler stone, a solitary arch spans a cleft half a kilometer wide. The arch is natural, carved by an ancient wind that no longer blows; its undersurface is glazed with the same eternal ice, but the ice here forms inverted blades that point toward the cleft floor. When sound travels through the arch—sound arriving from nowhere in particular—it multiplies: the arch converts each low-frequency pulse into a choir of overtones that linger for minutes before the granite absorbs them again. The choir is the loudest thing present; it is constant, and it fills the emptiness without variation.

At irregular intervals the granite is interrupted by shallow depressions, each the diameter of a house foundation. Inside several of these depressions lie the fractured shells of long-abandoned stone shelters. The shelters have no roofs, no doors; only waist-high arcs of fitted blocks remain, the mortar between them turned to pale dust. The dust lies where it settled, unmoved by any breeze. One shelter still contains a single upright slab whose top edge has been worn into a shallow saddle by something that once rested there. Nothing rests there now. The slab and the dust are both the same temperature as the surrounding stone.

Nothing moves across the shelf at any scale the eye can register. Occasional filaments of vapor drift above the micro-ponds, rising and falling within the same few hand-spans of height. Far overhead, at the limit of visibility, pale motes of suspended ice crystals drift in slow horizontal orbits, never rising or falling. Their motion is not wind-driven; it is the visible trace of a larger atmospheric equilibrium that the entire plateau has entered and will never leave.

The sound of the arch continues. The vapor threads continue. The lichen holdfasts continue their microscopic labor. The ice lenses continue their slow accumulation of mass that adds nothing and subtracts nothing. Every element is at once arrested and absolute, a landscape whose every process has reached a condition in which change is no longer distinguishable from stillness.
