The creatures here are not individuals so much as self-sustaining knots in a single fluid exchange that has been going on for geologic ages. Each “animal” is a coherent current of specialized cells and symbionts flowing through membranes that are themselves part of the photosynthetic mat; what looks like a separate body is really a temporary condensation of resources that the lattice has decided to animate for a while. When a mountain-sized grazer “dies,” its substance does not rot or sink; the supporting leaf simply relaxes a few millimeters and lets the current of dissolved metabolites re-enter the common stream, so the next cycle of growth re-absorbs what was briefly set aside.

Inside each leviathan, the bioluminescent symbionts are not parasites but inherited organelles whose light patterns are the actual nervous system; the groans we hear are the mechanical by-product of slow, deliberate peristaltic pumps that circulate both nutrients and coded flashes of information. Younger symbiont lineages occasionally mutate into new pulse vocabularies; these novelties propagate as wavefronts through the herd, carrying news of slight shifts in dissolved ion ratios hundreds of kilometers away. Over centuries the dialects blend back into the common signal unless a patch of mat decides the new code is worth keeping, at which point the corresponding animals are guided by osmotic steering currents into regions where the mutation confers advantage.

The cleaning crustaceans are even less discrete. A single “individual” is an oscillating node in a colony that can reconfigure in minutes: when a cleaning task finishes, the node dissolves its chitin scaffolding, lets its cells drift on micro-currents, and re-condenses elsewhere as part of a different functional cluster. Their ultrasonic songs are pressure waves that also serve as a living census; by reading the returning echoes the mat knows exactly how much metabolic work is being performed in each clearing and can adjust osmotic turbine output accordingly. If the census reports too many nodes idle, surplus crustacean mass is reabsorbed into the leaf tissue as extra vascular scaffolding.

Nothing in this place is finished. The lattice is always slightly revising its porosity, the herds are always slightly revising their migration circuits, and every pressure gradient that looks stable is actually a standing wave whose nodes and antinodes are still negotiating with one another.
