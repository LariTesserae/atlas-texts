Between the xylem bridges and the suspended fluid there drifts a single, continuous species: the Stratum Eels. Each is a slow, radially symmetrical sac whose translucent walls are composed of the same stiffened lignin that forms the architecture itself, so the boundary between “creature” and “chamber” is visible only when the sac flexes. Their interiors are a sealed hydro-gel, patterned with microscopic chambers that exchange ions with the surrounding medium; the exchange releases faint warmth just sufficient to keep the gel from solidifying, yet never enough to thaw the deeper equilibrium. When one eel passes another, the two volumes slide across one another like adjacent soap films; neither pierces the other, and the contact leaves no scar.

The eels breathe by diffusion; they have no mouths, no lungs, only a lattice of fine tracheids that open and close in wavelets timed to the slow convection. That convection is itself generated by the eels. Their peristaltic pulses are not random; they are phase-locked to the architecture’s own micro-currents so that every contraction nudges a measured volume of fluid along the next xylem canal. Over millennia the pulses have become the convection: the animals maintain the temperature gradient that keeps the region from stagnating, yet they do so without any individual ever changing its station. Each eel is fixed in its tier, drifting only centimeters per century, an almost stationary organ in an organ-less body.

Sound propagates through the fluid as a standing wave whose nodes coincide with the thickest vertical trunks; the eels sense the nodes by the minute pressure changes across their own surfaces. When an eel begins to drift off-node it contracts more vigorously on the side away from the node, nudging itself back into acoustic alignment. Thus the architecture disciplines the inhabitants, and the inhabitants discipline the fluid, and the fluid disciplines the temperature, all without any organism ever altering its inherited form.

In the uppermost strata—once a canopy now compressed into a single, hundred-meter sheet—live thinner, sheet-like variants whose diameters can span an entire former clearing. They do not move at all; their contractions are isometrically contained, producing only sub-audible pressure that stiffens the sheet against the weight of the tiers above. From below they register as a faint, continuous shimmer in the acoustic field, like the ghost of wind in a forest that has forgotten wind.

Deeper still, the original root mass has been excavated into a lattice of octahedral chambers whose walls are a single cell thick yet incompressible. Within each octahedron lives a colony of microscopic analogues—essentially the same organism scaled down until the boundary between individual and tissue dissolves. They occupy the chambers completely, turning each void into a solid of living gel. Their micro-pulses are too rapid to be heard by larger forms, yet they sum into a carrier frequency that the Stratum Eels use as an orientation signal when, once every few thousand years, one individual must migrate between tiers.

Nothing here ages. The lignin-like polymer is self-repairing at the molecular level; any fissure is immediately infilled by new matrix secreted from the nearest living wall. The eels, likewise, lose no mass; their gel is recycled internally by continuous, imperceptible chemical loops. Reproduction does not occur because every volume that could be occupied already is. The only gradient left in the system is the infinitesimal one maintained by the eels’ own pulses—the same gradient that keeps the fluid from ever quite becoming still, and that therefore keeps the sound from ever quite becoming silence.
