The Lower Hollows do not yet have inhabitants in the ordinary sense; it is still being grown around the people who already live there.  

The first lives to arrive were the gypsum-crickets. They came with the earliest drip, pale thread-legged syllables whose bodies register light the way a mirror registers absence. Each night they skate across the film of new calcite that pearls on every chisel left on a bench, rasping the surface clean with mouthparts that never stop moving. Their stridulations are thin enough to be felt in the molars more than heard; when the braziers are banked they become the district’s only clock. In the dark they settle on the lantern-glass itself, invisible except as a moving meniscus of flame.

Below the crickets, colonies of calciphile bacteria have begun to tint certain drip-lines the faintest copper-green. The color is fragile; a mason brushing past with an elbow can erase a month’s work. The bacteria do not mind. They are content to wait for the next slow seep that will repaint the scar.

People arrived later. They came first as apprentices and stone-scrapers, then as families, and they have made a treaty with the stone rather than a home. In each barrel-roofed room the children have learned two postures: one for when the floor is new and the ceiling high, another for when both have moved. A three-legged stool is never set down without two flat stones ready beneath it; in the morning the stones must be exchanged for taller ones or the child will breakfast standing. Adults keep their tools on wall-hooks drilled at slightly different heights each season. The district smells continuously of cedar sap and wet limestone because no timber is allowed to age in place; every plank, prop, and ladder rung is rotated outward before its base can calcify.

When the single rift-light narrows at dusk, the braziers are stoked with moss-coal. The rising smoke follows the same cracks the water follows downward, writing the first black maps on the calcite curtains. These soot-maps are studied by the overseers each dawn; a new streak means either a new fracture or a new drip, and both must be braced or rerouted before the next work-cycle begins.

Deeper in, past the last occupied room, the masons have left the forest of straw stalactites untouched. They stand close enough together that a person must slip between them sideways; many are no thicker than a broom-handle and still translucent at the tips. At noon the rift-light threads between them in needles so fine they can be seen only when a drop falls through and fractures the beam into momentary rainbows. The straws are a promise and a warning: the water that built the district is still building it, and any object left too long will be folded into that continuing work.
