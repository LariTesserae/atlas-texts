Between the ceaselessly canting courses of glass-stone, a population of small, translucent orthoptera moves in the gutters. Their wing-covers are etched with the same capillary striations as the pavement, rendering them almost indistinguishable from the refracting surface until they choose to shift. Each jump or flight is no longer than the width of a finger, and the insects time the motion to coincide with the sub-audible resetting of surface tension, so that their landings register only as a pause in the cadence of clicks. Their digestive tracts are so transparent that a single bead of capillary moisture can be seen pulsing from mouth to abdomen, the water itself serving as both coolant and circulatory medium. When the city tilts, they rotate their body axes to remain aligned with the new gutter slope, their jointed legs providing infinitesimal adjustments that add micro-eddies to the moving film.

Lower still, inside the laminations, networks of syncytial filaments stretch between stone faces like slow musculature. Each filament is a single multinucleate cell, its membrane stiffened with microscopic plates of the same vitreous rock; the plates interlock like chainmail yet permit longitudinal contraction when the water tension between them changes. Contractions move in waves measured in hours: one end of a filament shortens, drawing the adjacent course of masonry inward by a hairline, while the opposite end lengthens, allowing the course above it to settle outward again. The process is silent, frictionless, and self-limiting; any excess force simply extrudes a droplet of water that immediately re-enters the capillary stone. Over decades these slow, reversible adjustments maintain the city’s tapered profile against the episodic micro-floods.

The succulents themselves shelter a hidden layer of mobile moisture: amoeboid vesicles, each a single membrane enclosing nothing but a volume of the same capillary water. They migrate across the succulent leaves in amoebic fashion, propelled by surface-tension gradients that shift whenever a leaf brightens or dims. When they reach a fissure in the pavement they descend into it, carrying salts drawn from the leaf and depositing them in the stone’s micropipes, thereby seeding the next generation of mineral ghosts that appear when the basin tilts. Their migrations are so small that the only visible sign is a momentary darkening of the translucent leaf tissue, a shadow sliding from margin to stem before the leaf recovers its even translucency.

The city, then, is not empty; it is a single, distributed metabolism whose participants are insects, filaments, and vesicles, each keyed to the language of water tension and each contributing, by their minute motions, to the perpetual recalibration of the streets.
