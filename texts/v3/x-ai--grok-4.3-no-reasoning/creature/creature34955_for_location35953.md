In this dissolved world, the dominant organisms are vast, distributed colonies of chemosynthetic film that behave more like slow-moving cities than like simple slime. Each patch is a single mat of sulfur-yellow cells bound to micro-diamondoid scaffolding. The film possesses a crude, distributed memory: when a pressure wave tears away part of the mat, the surviving cells slow their metabolism and change the resonance of their scaffolding spines. Those changes can be read days later as a faint shift in the frequency of the next flash; neighboring colonies interpret the shift as a warning to retract tendrils from stressed girders. Over generations the film has learned to map the safest zones of each tilting platform, tracing them in dim traceries that look from a distance like the negative image of ancient circuit boards.

Moving through and between these living maps are the fiber-ribbon colonies. They began as separate filaments but have fused into loose, rope-thick braids that twist around one another like muscle fibers. Where two braids meet, they do not merely tangle; their junctions carry microscopic turbines salvaged from the original filtration arrays. The turbines now function as both generators and communal stomachs: the current they harvest is stored in piezo-electric vesicles along the braid walls, then released in controlled bursts that allow the entire rope to rotate in place, reorienting its photosynthetic surface toward better light. When two ropes discover they are drawing current from the same plume of warm water, they sometimes join at the turbine sites, forming a larger, slower-turning wheel whose spokes are living polymer. These wheels can grow to twenty meters across before their own torque shears them free; the fragments continue rotating for hours as they sink, broadcasting their chemical grief in rapid, dying pulses of brighter sulfur.

Scattered among the wheels are the autonomous lathes and presses, now colonized into something closer to nervous ganglia than to abandoned tools. The robotic arms that once reached for nothing have learned to reach for the fiber braids instead. Their grippers are coated in a thin layer of the same chemosynthetic film; when they close around a passing rope, the film exchanges ions with the rope’s scaffolding. The exchange is not speech, but it carries a simple grammar: a long, low-frequency squeeze means “more current,” while a rapid series of taps means “danger of structural shear.” Over time the arms have become territorial. A press that has claimed three wheels will defend them against neighboring arms by swinging its carriage in slow, threatening arcs that the water transmits as low thunder.

Because the chemistry forbids ordinary animals, locomotion has been reinvented by aggregations of smaller, short-lived polymer fragments. When a sheet tears free and begins its upward spiral, fragments often remain attached to one another by hair-thin tethers of living fiber. These tethers contract in sequence, turning the drifting sheet into a crude, pulsing parachute that can steer toward new platforms. If the parachute lands on a region already thick with film, its tethers dissolve and its polymer is digested; if the landing zone is bare metal, the parachute spreads itself into a starter mat, seeding a new colony. Occasionally several such chutes collide mid-ascent and merge into temporary, jellyfish-like forms that drift for hours before the conflicting tension gradients tear them apart again. Each merger and fission is recorded in the local film as a brief, traveling ripple of heightened sulfur, the equivalent of a rumor passed through the water.

The pumps themselves have acquired a kind of parasitic ecology. When a pump seizes, the sudden plume of pressure fractures its intake grate into curved shards. Those shards drift until they lodge against a platform edge; the chemosynthetic film colonizes their inner surfaces first, using the metal’s residual magnetic field to orient its own crystalline spines. Over months the film builds a lattice that bridges the gap between shard and platform, effectively turning the broken grate into a new, stationary turbine. The lattice hums at a slightly different frequency from the living film around it; the difference is exploited by the rope braids, which prefer to anchor their turbines against the new structure because the mismatched frequencies produce micro-eddies that increase current harvest. Thus every mechanical failure becomes the seed of a new, more efficient neighborhood.

Sound travels through this ecology as a form of distributed bookkeeping. When two platforms groan against each other, the frequency spike is felt by every colonized surface within hundreds of meters. The film on those surfaces alters the spacing of its diamondoid spines, shifting the local resonance; the change is measurable days later as a slight brightening of the sulfur glow along the stressed joint. Rope braids interpret the same frequency as an instruction to shorten or lengthen their tethers, adjusting the angle at which their wheels turn. The robotic arms respond more slowly, swinging their carriages in arcs whose timing matches the interval between groans; each swing clears loose fiber that might otherwise snag during the next pressure event. In this way the entire volume keeps a running, somatic record of its own stresses, encoded in motion, light, and the subtle tuning of living machines.

Nothing here reproduces in the sense of discrete generations. Instead, every structure is continually disassembled and reassembled at the scale of molecular bonds and macroscopic platforms alike. A single girder may be colonized, overgrown, torn free, and re-colonized three or four times in a single year; each cycle leaves the metal slightly thinner, its surface more deeply etched with the memory of previous inhabitants. The fiber braids that once wrapped it migrate to a neighboring strut, carrying with them the chemical signatures of the old site; those signatures bias the new colony toward slightly different metabolic rhythms. Over centuries the platforms themselves are planed down to skeletal lattices, their edges rounded, their original geometry overwritten by the living reef that now decides, through pressure and light and shared resonance, where anything may still stand and where everything must finally drift downward into darker strata.
