They are the shelf-folk, though they have no single name for themselves. Each family answers to the dimple or seam in which its vault sits, so one learns to speak of “the people under the cart-wheel pool” or “those whose vent smokes beside the liverwort.” Their stature averages three and a half spans; knees and elbows are padded with thick callus that lets them scuttle across the slick stone without slippage. Skin is the same pale, pocked gray as the living rock—only the faint blue of veins at wrist and temple marks them out—and their hair grows in tight, colorless spirals that bead water like moss.

Movement is almost never upright. Adults travel by a four-limbed crawl that keeps their centers low and their eyes near the water. Children, lighter, can manage an upright scurry that ends in an immediate drop whenever a stranger or shadow passes. When they must cross open rock, they do it at dusk or in the false dusk of heavy overcast, flattening until only the gleam of an eye shows between lashes. Their speech has no loud registers; a sentence passes from mouth to ear as a single exhaled thread, the consonants shaped more by tongue than by throat.

The interior life of each vault is ordered by water. A household rises when the drip from the ceiling vent changes pitch (signaling that the upper pool has refilled) and retires when that same drip slows to a stutter. Mealtimes are silent; the only permitted sound is the suck of lips against a reed straw that reaches the catchment. All refuse is pressed into the rock clefts beneath the sleeping mats, where the constant cool air dries it to a grit that is later brushed into the shrimp cracks—an offering, though the folk do not name it so.

Social rank is reckoned by proximity to the liverwort seam. The damp, steady draft that issues from it keeps a vault a fraction cooler, and coolness is life when bodies lie packed through the wet months. Families whose vents sit three or more body-lengths away must trade labor for the privilege of hanging their hammocks there at night; the payment is measured in measured cupfuls of breath-condensed water scraped from the walls each dawn.

Once a year, during the three-night shrimp rising, every vault empties. The entire population lies prone along the edges of the largest pools, chins on stone, arms submerged to the shoulder. They do not speak; they do not blink. The translucent bodies tickle across palms and inner wrists; each person counts the contacts with small, private notches bitten into the inside of a lower lip. When the crustaceans vanish, the notches are compared in silence. The family with the deepest tally is granted the liverwort seam for the coming year; the others shift one dimple farther outward, accepting the warmer stone without complaint.

Their dead are not buried. When breath stops, the body is laid across the nearest vent until it dries to the same color and texture as the surrounding pavement. After seven nights it is broken along its natural joints and the pieces fitted into the wall of the family vault, flush with the stone. In time the outline of a rib or knuckle smooths away, indistinguishable from any other pock, so that the settlement itself is slowly built of those who once moved through it.
