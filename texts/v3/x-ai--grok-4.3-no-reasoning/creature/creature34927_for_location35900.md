Beneath the taut meniscus live the only discrete forms this world allows: the pearl-bodied fauna called limnids. Each is an ovoid envelope of flexible carbonate glass, the size of a human fist, inside which a single translucent crystal core drifts. The core is not an organ but a calendar; concentric rings of occluded ice mark every rain-column encounter the creature has ever survived. As the columns shift, the limnid re-grows its shells in successive skins that dissolve behind it, leaving only the next ring to record the event. In this way the animal carries its geography as autobiography.

Between the hexagons, where the mercury filaments cross, faint rust-brown filaments sometimes converge into knot-like vortices. These vortices are the last remains of something once more elaborate than the present fauna: pre-historical colony-nets whose metallic residues now lie in suspended animation. When a migrating column crosses such a vortex, the local basalt contracts sharply enough to produce a low, glassy chord. The chord triggers the nearest limnids to undergo a synchronized re-crystallization. Their pops interlock into a brief chord of their own, a microscopic duet between rock-memory and living witness. Afterward the vortices fade again, unchanged except for the addition of a new hair-thin rust streak that records the meeting.

The limnids themselves never touch the surface for long; their ice shells are too fragile for the meniscus-level pressure. Instead they drift in the near-vacuum tunnels, rising only when the rock’s contraction squeezes the tunnels shut beneath them. During these ascents, a limnid may graze the meniscus and leave a momentary dimple—an oval print that heals in moments. If several limnids perform the same action in succession, the meniscus develops a transient hexagonal pattern that mirrors the basalt below, as though the ceiling were briefly acknowledging the floor. These acknowledgments last only until the next column descends, erasing both the pattern and the memory that formed it.

At the lowest visible strata of the dunes lie the oldest hexagons, their rust streaks so dense they appear solid brown. Here the rock occasionally sings without external prompting: a single, lengthening tone as the accumulated streaks rearrange themselves in infinitesimal increments. The limnids nearest the source pause in their drift, cores aligned toward the note. For an instant the distinction between witness and archive collapses; the creatures’ concentric rings briefly brighten, taking on the same brown tint as the stone. When the tone ends, the rings fade again and the limnids resume their suspended patrol, carrying one additional layer of encoded silence.

No other life is possible. The plain permits no predators, symbionts, or scavengers—only these slow archivists whose biographies are the world’s sole remaining differential.
