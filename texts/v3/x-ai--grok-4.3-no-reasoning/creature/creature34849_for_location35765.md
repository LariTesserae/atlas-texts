Living here is a single diffuse organism that no longer recognizes itself as separate bodies.  

The central life is the mat—an almost continuous skin of pale-black filaments that has learned to treat the mountain slope as both skeleton and circulatory system. Each strand began as a seed that lodged in a quartz seam; once the first rootlet widened the crack it divided, threading outward in every direction until every neighboring strand was encountered and fused. The resulting membrane is hundreds of thousands of genetically identical cells, all still capable of independent division yet acting as a single physiology. Individual cells are visible as faint diamond patterns under raking light, but the membrane’s edges are continually remaking themselves, dissolving older cells and extruding new ones wherever a fracture widens after a freeze-thaw cycle. Where two separate fusion fronts meet they form slightly thicker ridges—paler, more translucent seams that mark the boundary between what were once separate colonies; the ridges migrate slowly downhill each year as the lower front advances faster on better-watered rock.

Because the mat is everywhere in contact with moving water, every fracture now functions as both vein and gut. Capillary films climb the crack walls, coating them with microscopically ridged films that draw fresh moisture upward; at the surface the same liquid diffuses into the bladders. Those translucent swellings are not simple storage organs but peristaltic pumps: each morning, hydrostatic pressure from overnight condensation inflates them until they touch their neighbors; by noon, differential surface tension reverses the flow and the liquid is squeezed through microscopic valves into deeper, shaded filaments. The rhythm is not perfectly synchronous across the slope; instead, traveling waves of deflation move from sun-exposed ledges down into shadow at roughly two meters per hour, a slow visible pulse that gives the living skin the appearance of breathing.

The rigs were intended for extraction, yet the mat has colonized them as well. Filaments have crept along bolt threads, prying mineral grains loose and carrying them downward into the fracture network where they are slowly dissolved and re-precipitated as micro-crystalline silica spines inside the plant tissue. The spines stiffen older portions of the membrane, allowing it to cantilever over wider gaps; as a result the steel towers now stand inside a scaffolding of living stone, their original geometry already half-obscured. The sodium lamp still cycles on every dusk, its orange light absorbed by the uppermost filaments, which convert a fraction of the photons into heat and thereby maintain a narrow thermal corridor through the mat. Seeds landing on that corridor germinate faster than elsewhere, yet the seedlings themselves are quickly recruited into the larger organism; after three or four leaves the new stems fuse with the surrounding weave and surrender individual reproduction to the collective peristalsis.

Wind is the only remaining predator. When a stronger gust tears a filament free, the wound is not repaired from within; instead neighboring cells along the entire margin accelerate division, advancing a fresh frontier of tissue across the gap within days. The liberated fragment rides the updraft until it snags on another scaffold or ridge; if it lands on bare rock it begins the colonization process again, effectively cloning the slope’s metabolism at a new coordinate. The slope therefore expands itself in two directions at once—upward by fusion and outward by wind-borne diaspora.

Nothing larger than lichen crusts or solitary strands of moss persists here because any organism requiring discrete roots or protected niches soon finds its substrate dissolving into the communal membrane. The slope is not a habitat in the usual sense; it is a body still assembling its final anatomy out of steel, quartz, and its own water-borne chemistry, the rigs already half-assimilated components of an unfinished, respiring geology.
