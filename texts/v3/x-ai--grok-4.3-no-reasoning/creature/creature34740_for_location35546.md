In this city lives a species of people who have become, almost without noticing it, an organ of the place itself. Their bodies are slim and slightly translucent in places; the veins that run across collarbones and the backs of hands glow the same faint pearl-grey as the light filtering through the canopy. They are shorter than most outsiders, and their joints have a soft give to them, as though the cartilage were part vegetable. When they stand still—which is how they spend most of their time—their skin slowly takes on the exact temperature and sheen of whatever they are touching, so that a person resting against a wall can, after an hour, be mistaken for part of the masonry if the eye is not careful. They do not age in the usual way. Instead they simply become more integrated: skin thickens into a kind of living bark where it is most often pressed against roots; hair, if it grows at all, turns into fine green strands indistinguishable from the hanging moss.

The people do not raise children so much as cultivate them. A new life begins as a small, fist-sized pod of densely woven root-matter, seeded inside a specially prepared hollow brick. Over many seasons the pod draws nourishment from the same capillary moisture that feeds every other living surface. When the child finally pushes free of the brick, it does so already half-accustomed to the city’s pressures and temperatures. The adults do not name their offspring; instead each child learns, through touch, the particular vibration-pattern that belongs to every adult it will ever need to recognize. These patterns are so individual that two people who have never met can still identify one another by the way their shared garment-moss reacts when they stand close.

The animals that share the streets are not quite pets and not quite wild. The translucent wall-lizards will, if a person remains motionless beside a brick for long enough, crawl out and rest along the person’s collarbone, their breathing synchronizing with the human’s slower heartbeat until the two rhythms become one slow pulse. The tiny crickets, when a hand is held out, will sometimes form a living bracelet around the wrist, their synchronized grooming producing a faint, pleasant warmth. The salamanders are more aloof; they allow only the oldest inhabitants to share their wallows, and even then the person must wait for the animal to drift against them. When this happens, the salamander’s frilled gills will sometimes brush the human’s bare ankle, a contact that carries a shared memory of water moving through deep stone—an experience so profound that the person may not speak by touch for several days afterward.

Nothing in the city is owned. Tools and garments are grown in common trays and simply taken up when needed; when a garment begins to outlive its wearer’s habitual movements, it is carefully peeled away and laid on the nearest flat surface, where the city’s mosses quickly reclaim it. The same is true of the hollow bricks that once held children; when a person finally stops moving altogether—which happens without warning or ceremony—the body is left where it settled. Within a season the roots have drawn every useful mineral from the remains and the space is occupied by new sprouting grain or a fresh shelf-fungus colony. There is no grief attached to this process. The city has no memory of individuals, only of pressures and flows, and the people have learned to experience themselves the same way.

Occasionally an outsider arrives—usually by accident, having followed some half-forgotten rumor of a place where time had slowed to stillness. The visitor’s first sensation is of being gently but irresistibly read: every leaf that brushes their clothing, every lizard that pauses on their boot, every salamander that lifts its head as they pass is taking measurements of temperature, moisture, and the minute electrical fields generated by living muscle. If the visitor’s rhythms are too quick, too jagged, too full of sudden movements, the city simply becomes more densely occupied in front of them until passage is impossible; they will find themselves turned around without ever understanding how it happened. Those few who can slow themselves enough to match the city’s pace are allowed, eventually, to approach the central pool. They are permitted to kneel. If the water rises to meet their lips, they may stay. If it does not, they are guided back to the basin’s rim by the slow, inexorable thickening of vines across every possible path except the one leading outward.

Those who remain become, over years, indistinguishable from the people who were grown there. Their garments seed themselves to their new gestures; their skin learns the trick of matching the temperature of whatever they rest against; their speech fades into the constant low tone of moving water until even they can no longer remember what it was like to need words. And the city, which has no memory of individuals, nevertheless records their presence as a slight, permanent adjustment in the angle of certain leaves, the width of a certain salamander wallow, the precise diameter of the circular pool—changes so small that only the city itself, and the people who have become part of it, can perceive them.
