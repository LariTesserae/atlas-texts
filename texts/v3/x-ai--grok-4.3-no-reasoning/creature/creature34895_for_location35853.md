The cleft is inhabited chiefly by frost, by the slow pulses of water that become ice each night and surrender again at midday, and by the small stony intelligences that live inside those cycles.

One of these intelligences has the approximate shape of a centipede, but only when the light is low; its segments are really thin plates of mica that have slipped out of the granite and arranged themselves into a translucent ribbon no thicker than a thumbnail. During the hours of full sun it lies flat against the warm inner face of a terrace wall, indistinguishable from the stone. When evening brings condensation it curls, each plate lifting a degree so that the whole creature forms a minute gutter; the droplets it collects are never drunk but merely passed from segment to segment until they reach the open air and fall as a measured, audible beat. The mica-centipede therefore does not eat so much as keep time.

Another resident is an almost invisible film of pale algae that coats the undersides of the pale vines. At night the film photosynthesizes by starlight alone, emitting a dull silver glow that fades by dawn; the resulting sugary film is harvested, grain by grain, by jumping spiders whose abdomens have become translucent sacks exactly the size of the algal patches. The spiders do not spin webs; instead they crouch where the vine crosses a drip line and leap once, twice, three times in succession, timing each leap to the arrival of a new droplet so that their trajectory itself becomes part of the soundscape.

A third presence is not an organism but the accumulating memory of the city’s former inhabitants, now resident only as a faint angularity in certain stones. When frost expands behind a block that once formed part of a threshold, the pressure reawakens the faint rectangular scar left by an ancient chisel; the scar widens by microns each winter, so that after another few centuries the block will shear away and the threshold will be gone. In that sense the memory is the only part of the city that is still deliberately building: it is constructing its own disappearance, block by block, at the same rate the mica-centipede measures water.

Together these three—mica timekeeper, starlit harvester, and the slow self-erasing threshold—constitute the only population the cleft can presently support. Everything else either leaves at the first hard freeze or becomes indistinguishable from the stone that is already forgetting it.
