The terraces are inhabited by an organism so diffuse that most observers fail to perceive it as a single entity. It is composed of three co-dependent strata that occupy the same volume and pass matter between them at rates so low that an entire human lifetime registers only as a single metabolic tick.

The lowest stratum is a lithospheric mycelium of living calcite whose rhizomorphic threads knit the basalt shelves into a continuous sheet. The mycelium does not grow outward; it thickens and thins in response to micro-gradients of pressure and temperature. Where a current strikes a shelf at an oblique angle, the threads align into parallel ridges whose spacing matches the wavelength of the pressure wave; where the current is laminar, the threads dissolve and re-form in broader plates. The mycelium therefore functions as a distributed sensorium: its shape at any moment encodes the history of flow across the plateau. In places where the escarpment stripes are darkest, the mycelium is thinnest, as if it has chosen to remember cold rather than to occupy it.

Above the mycelium, but still rooted in the same calcite, stand the enormous translucent blades. These are not individual plants; each blade is a single giant cell whose vacuole contains a dilute suspension of archaea that perform the actual photosynthesis. The cell membrane is studded with protein pores that open only when the local temperature rises above a narrow threshold; the archaea then fix dissolved bicarbonate into new layers of calcite on the inner face of the vacuole, lengthening the blade by a few micrometers per decade. Because the pores open and close in synchrony across entire meadows, the collective slow pulse of the blades is in fact a respiratory rhythm: the organism inhales carbonate-rich water and exhales the slightly warmer, carbonate-depleted water that the planetary circulation will carry eastward.

The third stratum drifts. It is a population of photoluminescent bacteria whose cells are each wrapped in a microscopic calcite shell secreted by the same archaea that live inside the blades. The shells are buoyant only within a precise temperature band; outside that band they sink or dissolve. Consequently the bacteria ride specific isotherms the way pelagic fish ride thermoclines. When a vent erupts, the sudden temperature front lifts an entire cohort of shells into a higher current; hours later, when the water cools, the shells settle again, raining living light onto whatever terrace lies beneath. The bacterial colonies therefore act as a mobile circulatory system: they transport trace organics from the meadows to the escarpment and return mineral-rich water to the blades, closing a loop whose period is measured in planetary years rather than heartbeats.

Because the organism is sessile at every scale, its “behavior” is expressed entirely as geometry. The orientation of calcite ridges on one shelf records yesterday’s current; the spacing of blades on the next shelf records the average vent frequency of the last millennium; the vertical distribution of luminous bacteria encodes the recent thermal history of the whole water column. An observer standing on any terrace is therefore surrounded by a three-dimensional text whose alphabet is the placement of minerals and the timing of light. Reading requires only patience; the organism has been writing itself for longer than the escarpment has existed, and it shows no sign of finishing.
