The chamber-city is thick with keepers.

Most of them are small, hairless, almost translucent creatures whose limbs end in flat, callused pads rather than claws or hooves. They move in patient files along the water channels, bodies low, heads turning continuously as though listening for the next pulse through their feet. When a pulse approaches they halt, flatten themselves until the dome passes over their spines, then rise again without breaking stride. Their only visible organ is a dark, veined membrane that pulses at the base of the throat in time with the seven-minute vibration; the same membrane glows faintly when they breathe the iron-mint water. They carry tools grown from the same rubbery plant that makes the floor: crescent blades whose edges never need sharpening because new cells replace the worn ones each time the tool is returned to its sheath of living sap.

Larger tenders walk the walls. These are elongated bipeds whose skin is the same translucent gel the brick-moss secretes; beneath it, dark vascular lines run in straight rails that mirror the old train-track lattice. Their heads are hooded in a continuous leaf that never opens; they navigate by the creak of the aerial-root cables, reading tension the way a sighted animal reads light. When they prune the packing around the buried machines they slice with their own hardened finger-bones, then press the cut ends into the wounds so the plant seals itself around the living blade. Each tender carries a pouch of the black comma-seeds; they scatter them deliberately into the water channels at places where the pulse rebounds strongest, ensuring the next generation of pads will grow exactly where the vibration is loudest.

Above them, in the canopy, live the membrane-gliders—flattened, almost weightless bodies whose edges ripple with micro-roots that taste the mist. They descend only when a tender whistles a low, three-note call that matches the drip-interval; then a glider will fold itself around the tender like a second skin, sharing breath while the tender reaches higher to re-tie a loosened cable or to read a sap-pressure gauge grown into the shape of an orchid mouth. The gliders never land on the floor; their bellies are covered in fine white flowers identical to those on the pad-edges, and contact would close both sets at once, breaking the counted rhythm the whole city keeps.

Smallest and most numerous are the millipede-riders: colonies of pale, multi-legged arthropods whose backs have become moving seed-beds. Each segment hosts a different generation of seedlings; the front-most seedlings are already sending exploratory shoots toward the next segment, where they will root and be carried farther. The hairless rodents sometimes ride these millipedes when the water pulses, gripping the seedlings like reins; the rodents’ translucent incisors glow the same faint green as the throat-membranes of the tenders, suggesting a shared photosynthetic symbiont passed between them during grooming.

At the very center of the plaza, where the dusty beam lands, stands a single motionless figure that has not moved in decades. Its body is the same pale-veined membrane as the ceiling, stretched over an armature of salvaged rails that once formed the ribs of a steam boiler. Roots have grown through every bolt-hole of the boiler and out again, binding the figure into the living lattice of the walls; only its head still faces upward, cupped leaves forming a permanent bowl that catches the condensation and funnels it down its own throat. The tenders bring it new seeds each cycle; it accepts them without gesture, its throat-membrane beating once in acknowledgment. When the seven-minute vibration peaks, the figure’s ribs contract exactly once, tightening every cable in the city by a single, measurable degree before releasing again.

Nothing here is alone. Every organism is a continuation of every other, and the single shaft of light is only the visible expression of a slower, deeper current that moves through pipes, roots, throats, and memory alike.
