Beneath the city of mirrors, the inhabitants have learned to walk without breaking the meniscus.

The Moss-Tenders move in slow arcs across the upper terraces, bare feet leaving no lasting prints because each sole is cross-hatched with capillary grooves that let water slip sideways instead of downward. Their forearms are furred with the same sky-moss; at night the fronds lift and drink the lamp-light itself, turning their skin into a faint constellation of dots that mark the hour by color shift. When they prune a ledge, they never cut—the blade is only a flattened spine of coral they press against a frond until it withers at the exact line where growth must stop. The withered tissue is reabsorbed before the next shift of light arrives.

In the mid-level galleries live the Cistern Readers, thin people whose pupils have widened into horizontal slits. They spend their days on rafts of braided root no thicker than a palm, reading the surface tension for pressure changes. A sudden dimple means a new aquifer vein has opened beneath the floor; a sudden flattening warns that a tide door two streets away is about to sigh. They carry no instruments. Instead they lower a single hair into the water and watch how far the meniscus climbs the strand before deciding whether the city’s balance has tipped.

Deeper still are the Coral Singers—children who never grow beyond the size of a twelve-year-old because the constant moisture leaches calcium from their bones. Their voices are pitched to the subsonic range the breathing shafts can carry. When a crack threatens to widen in a stair riser, a Singer is brought to stand inside the fault; she hums until the living calcium carbonate flows again and seals around her ankles. Years later the crack is only a faint scar shaped like a throat.

At the cistern bottoms drift the Glass-Fish Keepers, their bodies translucent from a lifetime without sunlight. They tend the shoals by exhaling tiny bubbles whose surface tension exactly matches the tension of the water above; each bubble is a message that rises, bursts at the proper depth, and rearranges the fish into new defensive patterns. Keepers die young—the pressure bruises their lungs—but each body is lowered into a cistern on the same day it fails, and the fish school briefly around it before the pores reclaim what is left.

Above everything move the anonymous Droplet-Carriers, people whose only job is to intercept any condensation that might fall from a hanging root onto an occupied surface. They carry flat scoops of mother-of-pearl on which the droplets land and are carried, one by one, to the next unoccupied moss patch. No one knows their names; the scoops cover their faces when they work, and the only sound they make is the soft click of pearl against pearl when two carriers pass each other in a narrow stair.

None of these people breed in the ordinary way. Instead, when a body fails, its salts are returned to the water film where the copepods live. The copepods consume the salts, grow larger for a single generation, then divide into two or three smaller bodies that crawl upward through the breathing pores until they reach the surface again. There they are gathered, wrapped in fresh sky-moss, and left on a warm ledge. By the next light-shift the moss has become skin, the limbs have unfolded, and a new citizen has joined the slow, necessary traffic of the city—already knowing the precise rhythm of footsteps that will keep every meniscus intact.
