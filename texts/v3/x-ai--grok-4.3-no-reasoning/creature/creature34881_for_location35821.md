What lives here is not a population, but the single, endlessly ramifying body of the city itself.

It is a continuous organism composed of countless semi-autonomous plant modules—leaves, stems, root-masses, spore clusters—linked by vascular networks that carry both water and informational signals. No individual cell or shoot ever reaches a fixed identity; every component exists in a state of perpetual becoming, growing toward or away from the demands of its neighbors. The entire mass behaves like a single colossal symplastic field: pressure changes in one basin are transmitted almost instantly to the outermost floating rafts, and a single enzymatic shift can alter the tensile strength of vines fifty meters away.

Coordinating the breathing cycle is a distributed lattice of contractile tubules that functions as a kind of vascular pacemaker. These tubules swell and collapse in staggered waves, ensuring that no single district inhales or exhales at exactly the same instant as its immediate surroundings; the resulting offset produces the low-frequency roar that fills the canopy. The same lattice also registers mechanical strain and chemical gradients, then adjusts growth rates accordingly—thickening a wall here, dissolving a floor there—so that the city’s internal geography continually rebalances itself without ever achieving stasis.

Because the system has no central nervous tissue, memory is stored in the configuration of its own architecture. A sudden contraction that occurred three cycles ago leaves a detectable curvature in a stem; that curvature biases the next growth spurt, which in turn influences water routing. Over many cycles these micro-adjustments accumulate into large-scale remodeling: what once was an open basin becomes a raised causeway, while a former avenue collapses into a shallow pool that the city then repurposes as a temporary nursery for floating pads. In this way the city’s “past” is literally its present shape.

Nothing inside the living mass can be called an inhabitant; each tendril or pad is simultaneously structure, organ, and participant. Yet there is something akin to volition at the scale of the whole: when light levels drop for more than two breath-cycles, the uppermost fronds redirect resources toward spore production, preparing to colonize new water surfaces. When a sector becomes overcrowded with its own detritus, the circulatory pulses intensify until the excess is flushed outward and the local density resets. These behaviors are not planned; they are the inevitable outcome of the same physical laws that keep water moving and leaves aloft.

At the ragged perimeter, where vegetation dissolves into open water, the distinction between city and not-city collapses entirely. Individual vines continue to advance, raft by raft, across the warm surface, while fragments of the city break away and drift outward, carrying the same circulatory pulse into new territory. In this sense the entity has no fixed body at all—only a moving frontier of self-invention that is, at any given moment, whatever shape the water and light allow it to assume.
