High on the pale granite, the people who live here are fewer than fifty and older than any reckoning they keep. They move the way the light does: brief, clear, almost motionless when the sun stands overhead. Their clothing is the same cold gray as the stone and cut so close that the wind does not catch it; only a band of darker rock-dust rubbed along every hem keeps the fabric from wearing through. Each morning, when the condensation has vanished, they step from their doorways in the same sequence, youngest first, so the footfalls of the last never overlap those of the first.

Their hands are dry and lined like split rock. When they work together they do not speak; their task is finished the moment the displaced slab or sack of nuts is set exactly where it stood before. Inside the houses nothing is moved except once a year, when the single timber over the door is examined for cracks. If none is found, the same bowl of water is set on the same bench, and the door is closed again. The children—there are always three or four—learn first how to step without displacing dust, then how to count the seasons by the brittleness of the seed heads rather than by any tally of days. They are never taller than the flattened pines, and when they are old enough to cross the stream alone they already know the pattern of the fox’s nightly prints.

A woman whose hair has gone the color of the lichen dust sits each evening beneath the overhang where the ptarmigan nest. She does not watch the birds; she listens for the moment the eggs cool enough that the speckling on their shells becomes indistinguishable from the surrounding stone. When she hears that faint change in temperature she rises, leaves a single pine nut on the ledge, and returns to her house. The nut is never there the next dawn, yet no one remarks on its absence, because the agreement was made longer ago than the granite joints have been open.

At night the ridge seems to lift itself a fraction closer to the stars. The roofs give off a low, continuous note too steady to be wind and too faint to be called music; it is the sound of cold granite radiating the day’s stored heat. The people inside lie on benches cut from the living rock and let the note pass through them until their own temperatures match the stone again. In this way they keep the village the same temperature as the ridge, which is the same temperature as the sky just below the weather line. They do not dream of other places. They dream of the exact angle at which a roof slab will rest so that no further adjustment will ever be required.
