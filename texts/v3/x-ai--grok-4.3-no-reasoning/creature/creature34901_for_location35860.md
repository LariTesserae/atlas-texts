A tide of small, mineral-shelled hoppers—each the size of a child’s palm—works the floor like living grit. They are slate-grey carapaces on jointed spring-legs; the shells are porous, filled with the same condensate that beads on walls. When a hopper lands it leaves a micro-ring of darker dampness. They are drawn to the fallen stone fragments from the ceiling holes; they rasp the chips down to powder with ridged mouth-plates and re-ingest the silt, their bodies swelling until they become miniature cisterns. At irregular intervals they must shed this excess weight. They do so by crouching low and releasing a thin, pressurized thread of water from an abdominal pore—each expulsion a silver stitch that vanishes between floor plates.

Where the ramps converge at the shallow basins, a different kind of life gathers: sessile colonies of vitreous disks no thicker than mica. These disks are not animals but living mineral lenses, each grown in concentric rings that trap air microbubbles. The lenses refract the low light that leaks through the ceiling holes into shifting, coin-sized glints that migrate across the basin water. The disks move by very slow dissolution at their trailing edges and re-precipitation at their leading edges; a single disk may take three days to travel the width of a palm. They do not feed on the water so much as on the faint static charge that collects whenever the low-frequency vibration passes through the slate; the charge drives carbonate ions into their crystal lattice and, over centuries, thickens them.

In the alcoves themselves dwell the true inhabitants, though they are rarely seen together. They are long, jointed figures whose skins are the same wet charcoal slate, differentiated only by a faint iridescence where iron oxide has crystallized along the flexures of spine and limb. Each one is anchored at the basin rim by a single clawed foot inserted into a hidden notch; the rest of the body hangs in a loose coil that can be drawn upward into the alcove’s roof hollow if disturbed. They drink by pressing the underside of a translucent lower lip to the falling drop, letting surface tension pull the water across a capillary fringe of fine slate spines. When the vibration warns of new stone being added overhead, every anchored foot tightens; the figures then become almost indistinguishable from the alcove walls until the tremor passes.

Above the lattice of drilled beams, in the perpetual wet thunder, a final presence moves: pale, yard-long filaments of gypsum floss, each anchored to the unseen roof by a single root of iron-oxide cement. These floss-threads trail downward through the ceiling holes, stirred by the constant airflow of dripping water. Occasionally a filament tears free and spirals into the space below, settling across a ramp or basin like a strand of wet hair. When one lands on a hopper, the creature pauses, extends a foreleg, and carefully draws the filament into a spiraled pellet that it then stores under its carapace—building, over many such encounters, a small internal armature of crystalline thread whose purpose no outside observer has yet determined.
