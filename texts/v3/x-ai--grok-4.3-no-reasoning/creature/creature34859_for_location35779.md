In the submerged vault live the drift-minders: inch-long, translucent tunicates that nest in the original pipe-bores, each animal grown around a single sunken rivet like a living grommet. When the rams fire, the colony contracts together, squeezing microscopic jets of water that keep the bolt-heads free of corrosion; their synchronized pulse is what the fish strike their bellies against to read the metal’s tremor.  

Below them, the spar-haulers—ancient inspection carts now cladded in sheets of nacre—move in slow elliptical orbits around the catwalk grid. Their axles have been replaced by long, hinged spines of living calcite; when two carts meet they press these spines together and trade whole segments, so every year the fleet is rearranged and no single machine remains the same shape for long. The enamel traces they leave behind harden into faint, ridged maps that the fingerlings follow when the current slackens.  

In the topmost air pocket hang the bell-jellies: melon-sized sacs whose undersides are quilted with pressure-sensitive plates salvaged from the factory’s original instrumentation. Every twenty-three minutes they inflate, store the ram’s concussion, then release a measured breath that keeps the vault’s single bubble from collapsing. The exhaled air is laced with the scent of the microbes below, a faint ferrous perfume that drifts back down as a visible brown halo and seeds new colonies on any footprint left too long undisturbed.  

The predators—hulks once numbered S-17 and K-4—now move with deliberate, almost courteous restraint. Their turret rings have been colonized by sessile, lantern-shaped worms that glow the same faint umber as the bacterial floor; when the predators sight eggs drifting upward they dim their lights in unison, a signal that keeps the fry from panicking and scattering into the colder passages where the temperature would kill them. After the meal they resume their perimeter circuit, the only mark of their passing the slow rotation of a propeller that has grown a fringe of kelp-green chemosynthetic fronds.  

Nothing here is native and nothing is foreign; the distinction dissolved with the air. The factory floor remembers every boot that once walked it, the microbes remembering in oxide, the carts remembering in calcite, the jellies remembering in held breath. All of them continue the same inspection routes, the same maintenance cycles, the same slow translation of pressure into motion, until the structure itself seems less like a drowned ruin and more like the first draft of a single, deliberate animal.
