A place without fixed tenants, the Fracture Plateau nonetheless supports a community of processes that are nearly alive.  
What lives here is the slow chemistry of the rock itself: salts that precipitate in veins until whole slabs are laced with hair-thin needles of borate; those needles, when they finally split, release fresh crystalline faces that immediately begin to effloresce again—an endless loop of birth, growth, and re-dissolution measured in seasons instead of heartbeats.

Embedded in the same lattices are mineral yeasts—microscopic halophilic films that drift in the fog veils, settling wherever a droplet lasts longer than a few minutes. They metabolize the lithium and boron leached from the crust, secreting a glue that binds one layer of bloom to the next. Seen from a tripod’s notch, the plateau’s white crust is revealed as a palimpsest of billions of such films, each generation adding another micron of opacity so the light that passes through thickens imperceptibly with time.

Between the rosettes and the salt, tiny translucent arthropods no bigger than sand grains patrol the fissures. Their exoskeletons are themselves grown from the plateau’s borates; they rasp the succulent leaves for condensed moisture and in turn deposit metallic feces that darken the leaf-edges, marking each plant with a record of visits. When the violet light-band sweeps over them, their shells luminesce in short Morse bursts—patterns that older generations appear to have learned and newer ones repeat, though whether this is communication or simple crystal resonance is impossible to say.

Beneath the visible surface, the grinding strata maintain their own fauna of stress-shadow bacteria, nourished by the piezoelectric voltages that flicker across any newly opened crack. The bacteria migrate with the stress fields, riding the electrical gradients from plate edge to plate edge, leaving behind trails of reduced minerals that later become the next generation of succulent crevices.

Above everything else, the acoustic lattice itself becomes host: sheets of crystalline dust that are lifted and dropped by the wind form temporary colloidal membranes, vibrating like drumheads. Certain frequencies that coincide with the infrasonic moan produce standing nodes where water vapor momentarily coalesces; microbes from the mineral-yeast films colonize these nodes, metabolizing the vapor before it can fall as frost. Each such coalescence lasts only minutes, yet the cycle repeats so regularly that the plateau supports an aerial ecology measured in square kilometers of dust, rather than in soil or leaf.

Nothing here is finished; every inhabitant is simultaneously architecture and inhabitant. The standing-stone tripods, the ropes, the rosettes, the films, the stress-shadows, the dust-membranes—all are still sliding into the shape that the next widening fissure will redefine.
