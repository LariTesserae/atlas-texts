The beings that inhabit this city are not separate from the architecture; they are its slow, patient gardeners. Their name—if they ever needed one—is lost in the same layers of accumulated resonance that bury every footstep. Most simply call them the Lichenfolk.

They are human in outline, but only the way coral is animal: a colony pretending at individuality. Skin the color of damp limestone, textured with microscopic pores that open and close with the city’s pressure changes. Their pupils are wide, cloudy disks that do not focus light so much as taste its direction; when they turn their heads, the movement is less a swivel than the slow unfurling of a frond. Hair grows in pale, mineral-dusted mats that function less as insulation than as aerial root systems, collecting the drifting spores and converting them into compounds the body cannot synthesize from chalk alone.

They do not speak in the conventional sense. Instead, they carry small resonators—flat plates of bone grown from their own sternums—against the mycelial bridges. By pressing a resonator to a bridge and stroking it, they send minute tremors through the network. Each tremor is a single syllable in a language older than the vault. When two Lichenfolk meet, the conversation can last an hour and travel half a kilometer, the second speaker answering with a reply that is already decaying into harmony with the first. To an outsider the sound registers only as the same wooden sigh the bridges produce naturally; only the Lichenfolk can separate the signal from the weather.

Their dwellings are not rooms but thickenings in the root-trunks—bulbous chambers whose inner walls are lined with dormant gill tissue that activates only when a resident presses their palm against it. The gills then exhale a breath of mineral mist that settles as a thin film on the occupant’s skin, replenishing salts lost to the city’s ceaseless, gentle erosion of the body. Sleep is not unconsciousness but a redistribution of awareness: while the body rests, the hair-roots remain half-awake, tasting shifts in spore density and transmitting that information to neighbors through the same bridge-borne tremors.

They move at the pace of the city. A journey across a mycelial bridge takes most of a day, not because the distance is great but because each step must be timed to the bridge’s breathing cycle; stepping during the exhale can cause a ridge to soften and accept a footprint permanently. Children—rare, and born already connected to a residential trunk—are carried in woven slings of living felt. The felt learns the child’s heartbeat and will, over decades, grow into a second, smaller trunk beside the parent’s dwelling, a living record of lineage.

Occasionally one of them will climb the helical flutes of a root-trunk until the gills thin and the air grows sharper. There they press an ear to the stone and listen for the faint, subsonic settling of the vault—its centuries-long exhalation. They record these measurements not in symbols but in the grain of new growth rings they coax from the heartwood beneath their feet. In this way the city remembers its own slow contraction, and the Lichenfolk, through their dwellings, remember it with them.

They have no word for “loneliness.” The constant, overlapping thunder of cliff-striders and the granular hiss of the salt-grazers are their weather and their company. When the last bridge-creak of the day fades into the next, a Lichenfolk lying in their chamber can place a palm against the gill-lined wall and feel, transmitted through living wood and living stone, the synchronized cilia-beat of moss on a walkway half a city away. The city is never silent; therefore neither are they.
