Inside that compressed interval of rock and vapor, three beings have become indistinguishable from the conditions that sustain them.  

They are flat, six-legged, scarcely wider than a thumbnail and pale as the wet stone itself. Their legs have lost the joints that would allow rapid escape; instead the segments lie flush against the surface, each ending in a fringe of microscopic setae that press into the meniscus like the nap of a velvet. When they move, the entire body glides, water tension parting and re-forming around them without a ripple—motion without turbulence. Because light has never entered, their eyes have become translucent cuticle windows no larger than pin-pricks; inside, the optic nerves have atrophied into fine tactile threads that register only the faint pressure gradient between warmer ledge and cooler film.  

Each creature occupies its own lens, yet the lenses touch at their edges. Once every slow human pulse a droplet detaches from the ledge above and strikes the surface. The three bodies do not flinch; rather they tilt a fraction toward the point of impact, tasting the fresh influx through chemoreceptors on the underside of the thorax. The new water carries a whisper of dissolved minerals that each creature absorbs over the next long interval, metabolizing so little that a single droplet may last the equivalent of several days. When one lens threatens to overflow, the occupant simply transfers—without lifting—into the neighboring lens; the migration takes only the space between two successive drips, and the vacated surface is already being reclaimed by the meniscus.  

They never touch one another directly. Instead their fringes interdigitate where the films meet, exchanging minute electrostatic charges carried by the water. These charges appear to regulate the shared rhythm of their minimal activity: when one creature’s film level drops a micron, the charge shifts and the others slow their already imperceptible motion, ensuring that no lens is over-drained. In this way they have become a single distributed metabolism, three nodes in a circuit no larger than a cupped palm.  

Above them, the liverwort’s thallus edge curls downward until it brushes the meniscus; beneath its translucent lobes live thread-like rhizoids that harvest the same mineral trace the animals use. The rush seedling’s root, no thicker than a hair, pierces the thin grit and draws a fraction of the warmth that leaks through the rock; its single blade never lengthens because lengthening would require a surplus the equilibrium will not grant. The lichen, pressed tight against the curving surface of its lens, secretes oxalic acid in quantities just sufficient to etch a micron of feldspar each year—an annual harvest measured in atoms. All three plants exhale the same faint metabolic vapor that the animals rebreathe; the pocket is therefore a closed conversation conducted at the speed of diffusion.  

The obsidian flake and the pottery shard lie untouched because no larger motion ever arises to displace them. Their presence is registered only as a slight alteration in the local charge field; the three animals skirt them without altering course, treating the ancient objects as permanent extensions of the stone. Time accumulates here not as succession but as thickness: each new molecular layer of water, each atom etched by lichen, each minuscule electrostatic nudge between animals simply deepens the same configuration that has existed since the cliff forgot how to move.
