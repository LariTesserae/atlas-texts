In the Drywell the very air seems to have a memory. Old sunlight that once fell on open stone is still held inside the granite ribs that close the place like a living vault, and each slow breath of warmer draft is a faint reheating of that buried noon. Because nothing ever evaporates away, every exhaled molecule is caught again and offered back to the roots; life here is nothing more than a careful handing of heat from wall to root to beetle to wall again, the same pocket of warmth passed around an unbroken circle.

The artisans who work on the scaffolds are not so much inhabitants as slow-moving extensions of the fungal felt. Their skin has taken on the same pale, veined translucence; at the joints it flakes into miniature shelves that catch and redirect the amber seepage, turning their bodies into additional filters for the light. They rarely speak; sound is kept for the runes that sing. When they do speak, the words are pressed into the clay of a new irrigation bulb before the clay hardens, so the syllables themselves become part of the chord that rises from every surface.

Among the herd beetles there are individuals whose backs have been worked into living altars. Tiny clay basins are fired directly onto their carapaces while the shell is still soft and growing; each basin fills with a single droplet of root syrup and is sealed again by the beetle’s own lacquer. The artisans collect these droplets the way orchardists collect resin, not because they need the liquid, but because the act of touching the beetle’s back and the beetle’s slow, consenting shiver renews the contract between craft and creature.

The gliders do not roost. Instead they follow the temperature gradient in endless spirals, their wing membranes flattening against the warmest current until the membranes become almost invisible. In those moments the only evidence of their presence is the faint metallic whisper that thickens or thins as the temperature changes. The whisper is the sound of six translucent wings adjusting their angle by fractions of a degree, a microscopic negotiation between body and rising heat that has been going on longer than any single glider’s life.

At the far pinch where the sky-root fuses with stone there is a single place where the fungal felt has grown thick enough to record weight. Every artisan who has ever walked the alley leaves a momentary footprint; the felt swells, then slowly relaxes, yet the memory of pressure lingers in a faint discoloration that can be read like a palimpsest. No two footfalls are identical; the pattern of lighter and darker patches tells who passed when, how long they lingered, and whether they carried a bulb or an obsidian flake. The discoloration never fades, but it never deepens either; it simply continues, a living census of everyone who has ever needed to be here and nowhere else.
