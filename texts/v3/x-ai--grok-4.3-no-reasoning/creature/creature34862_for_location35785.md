Within the basin’s humid cathedral nothing walks or flies that is larger than a fingertip, yet the place is crowded.

The walls themselves are beginning to grow a living skin. On every quartz streak that still gleams like new metal, lime-green algae has taken hold in threads fine as silk, catching each descending droplet before it can regain momentum. The mat thickens into a velvety felt that glows at night with the faint green of buried fireflies; spores drift upward in the constant mist, alight on warmer patches, and settle again, so the living film climbs the rock in slow tides measured by weeks. Where the algae meets a jet of warmer water, it erupts into velvet blisters that rupture after a day or two, releasing fresh spores like green pollen.

Clinging to the algae—sometimes within its very strands—are the glass salamanders. Their skins are so clear that the eye must search for the thread-thin shadow of a spine before the creature resolves. They spend most of their time motionless inside the carbonate scale that sleeves the power lines, bodies flattened against the pale mineral like veins of milk. When one does move, its legs appear to stroke rather than stride, each step advancing it no farther than the width of a scale crystal. The eggs they lay resemble minute pearls pressed into shallow depressions; after a fortnight the pearls split, and the hatchlings, already finger-length, wriggle out wearing the translucent livery of their parents.

A different order of life occupies the carved stone channels. Here, where water runs deliberately rather than seeps, tiny red worms no thicker than a hair knot themselves into living sieves. Their bodies pulse in synchronized waves that keep fine grit suspended; when enough grit gathers they loosen, let the current carry the load away, then re-weave downstream. The worms never surface; light is anathema to them, so each channel hosts a separate, hidden population whose only evidence above water is the absence of clogging sediment around the turbine intakes.

Over the pooled sediment on the pier, something stranger still has taken residence: a floating colony of minute, transparent discs—each the size of a lentil and veined with silver—held together by surface tension alone. They rise and fall with the subtlest change in humidity, sometimes stacking into low domes, sometimes flattening into a single iridescent film. If a falling quartz grain strikes their membrane they contract, the impact absorbed and dissipated before the grain can sink through to the sediment below. The colony is renewed nightly when condensation drips from the tarp; each droplet carries dissolved ions that the discs incorporate into their own delicate lattice before the next sunrise.

Even the turbines possess tenants. Inside every housing, where lubricant mist mixes with rock-warmed air, a pale yellow mold grows in radial fans along the spinning axle. The mold’s fruiting bodies are timed to the vibration of the shaft; every thirteenth revolution a filament straightens, catches an updraft of warmer air, and releases spores that swirl for a moment like sparks before settling again on cooler metal. The mold’s color deepens when the turbine’s pitch rises, so each machine displays its own faint halo of ripeness according to the note it contributes to the basin’s chord.

Above all, the basin’s atmosphere itself behaves like an organism. Exhaled breath lingers in discrete, opalescent parcels that drift until they touch a living surface; on contact with algae or mold they collapse and the moisture is absorbed. In this way the air continually feeds the green skin and the silver discs, while the heat rising from the turbines keeps the parcels buoyant enough to circulate rather than settle. Over days the pattern of drifting breath maps the hidden thermals inside the bowl, a shifting cartography visible only as the slow ghosting of condensation on eyelashes and lips.

In this way the basin is less an emptiness than a single, slow metabolism: water, stone, electricity, and vapor exchanging substance with one another through the agency of algae, worms, salamanders, mold, and the floating discs, all turning together to the same low, deliberate music.
