The plateau is inhabited by a single, continent-wide organism whose separate “individuals” are no more independent than the cells in a lichen. Each columnar succulent is only a photosynthetic lobe of a shared subterranean mycelial mat whose hyphae thread through the rock at the depth where the dew condenses each night; the distance between trunks is the distance at which the mat can still exchange nutrients without allowing one lobe to starve another. When a new seedling attempts to root inside the exclusion zone, the mat does not merely exude surfactant; it diverts its own vascular flow, starving the intruder by withholding water it has already lifted from the sink-hole pools two hundred meters below. The apparent competition is therefore an immune response of the single body.

The six-legged grazers are mobile immune cells of the same organism. Their scraping mouth-parts are specialized mycelial organs grafted onto motile, six-limbed housings; each grazer carries a fragment of the mat in its gut, and the condensation film they skate upon is the mat’s own extracellular matrix extruded through pores in their feet. When two herds meet at the spacing limit, they are not negotiating territory; they are exchanging genetic packets of the shared mat so that every local population remains a single genetic individual. The pause lasts exactly long enough for the exchange, then the herds resume their single-file procession, re-knitting the mat as they go.

The translucent gliders are seasonal spores. At intervals determined by the slow electrolytic cycle of the columnar lobes, certain hyphae swell with buoyant lipids and rise through the sink-hole shafts as larval gliders. Once aloft they feed on the aerosolized salts vented by the lobes; their papery membranes are living electrodes that close the circuit of the plateau’s carbon fixation, returning electrons to the mat through corona discharge each time they tack. When the gliders finally tire, they do not land; they fold their vanes, precipitate as mineralized husks, and drop into whichever sink-hole pool lies beneath them, seeding the next generation of the mat’s aquatic organs.

The pools themselves are not separate seas but lymph nodes. The eyeless worms are digestive tubules of the mat; their castings are pellets of concentrated rare-earth elements the mat scavenges from the strata to serve as cofactors for its electrolysis. Each pool’s micro-atmosphere is a controlled culture medium whose humidity halo is maintained by the mat’s own exhalation, preventing cross-contamination between genetic libraries housed in neighboring nodes. When the dripping sounds differ from pool to pool, they are the mat humming different metabolic tunes to different communities of its own symbiotic bacteria.

The living varnish is the mat’s skin. Its rust, verdigris, sulfur, and violet are not separate lichen species but sequential oxidation states of the same extracellular polymers, switched daily as the mat adjusts its redox balance to the convective flip of the air layers. The geometric salt rings around each trunk are the visible signature of this daily breath; each ring is a printed page in the mat’s ledger, and the entire plateau is a single, continuously updated manuscript whose text is chemistry rather than words.

Thus the plateau is not a landscape dotted with life. It is one organism the size of a subcontinent, the individual plants, animals, and pools no more separate than the lobes, leukocytes, and lymph nodes of any other body. The spacing, the silence, and the absence are its anatomy: the precise distances required for its tissues to remain one coherent, self-regulating metabolism operating on the timescale of geological epochs.
