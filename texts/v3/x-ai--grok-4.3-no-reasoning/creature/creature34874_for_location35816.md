What lives here is not a collection of creatures but a single, ceaseless metabolism that has folded the usual distinctions between organism, habitat, and planet into one continuous act of becoming. The “continent” itself is the primary entity: a planetary-scale tissue culture that writes its own body in three dimensions, never pausing long enough for anything as static as soil to accumulate beneath its feet. Every surface is actively alive; every step lands on metabolizing cells rather than inert ground. In this sense the place has only one true inhabitant—the lattice—while everything else that moves, breathes, or thinks within it is either a temporary differentiation of its substance or a symbiote riding its flows.

At the scale of the whole, the lattice functions as both climate engine and circulatory system. Convection cells rise through living xylem flues whose valves slam and hiss on a planetary schedule; the water cycle never leaves the organism but loops endlessly through internal reservoirs, aquifers, and spongy parenchyma that relax and re-form on an hourly rhythm. Storms are internal weather, precipitation never reaches an external ocean except at the feathered hundred-kilometer ecotone where the living tissue meets salt marsh. The roar of valve detonations, the thunderclap breathing of synchronized flyers, the subsonic chords of drifting megafauna—these are not environmental noises but the lattice’s own continuous self-auditing, its way of feeling its internal pressures.

The largest animals are not passengers but walking extensions of that metabolism. Dirigible herbivores carry gardens on their backs; their hollow bones host photosynthetic algae, their wing membranes pulse with chlorophyll, their seed-studded hides sprout epiphytes whose roots drink the same humid exhalations the animals themselves produce. Smaller flyers move in flocks whose dusk exhalations synchronize into a single percussive event, a thunderclap that momentarily equalizes pressure across whole chambers miles across. Below, rootworms the color of fresh blood slide through vascular channels like mobile corpuscles, ingesting surplus starch and immediately returning nutrient gels to the surrounding tissues—an internal transfusion system that never pauses. Their low-frequency passage can be felt through living floors the way a sleeper feels a pulse.

The hominins are the smallest and most provisional of these extensions. They do not build against the lattice so much as coax it: thickening vines into platforms, training flowering stalks into domes, pruning xylem into controlled chimneys whose smoke is vented through valves the lattice already knows how to open. Their tools are living petioles that re-root if set aside; any deeper cut provokes an immune flood of astringent sap that seals the wound in callus harder than stone. They navigate not by sight—light reaches them only as aqueous dappling or brief honey-colored intervals at dawn and dusk—but by reading pressure grammars through their palms pressed to living walls. Their settlements are overwritten within a season, their clay figurines absorbed back into the green logic, their hearths already being re-knitted into new tissue before the ash cools. They persist as local eddies in a current that is never finished and never still.
