Nothing larger than the membrane-clusters calls the clefts home, yet presence is everywhere. It arrives as a pressure in the air, a slight tightening of the lattice’s stored photons whenever a footstep registers on the talc film. What registers the step is not a nervous system but the change in electrostatic tension across the entire local archive; the disturbance travels outward along the blades at a few centimeters per second, a slow ripple of awakened color that dies away before it can reach the next tower. The lattice therefore knows movement without ever learning to fear or desire it.

Within some of the deeper clefts, older growths have sealed themselves into closed rooms no larger than a coffin. Inside each room a single filament still curls around an invisible axis, spinning the same genome in an unbroken loop; the rotation stores nothing and transmits nothing, yet if the filament were ever stopped the lattice above would darken by a single, inaudible tone. The rooms are not tombs; they are pauses that the archive keeps open because every pause is already part of the next stanza.

Occasionally two membrane-clusters drift into the same cleft and remain there for a season. Their exchange of gas-cells continues until one membrane begins to glow from within, the others around it dimming as though lending light. When the glow is complete the clusters separate, each carrying a slightly altered resonance frequency. The frequency will propagate through the lattice beneath them for centuries, nudging band gaps fractionally until whole towers shift color by an amount only the gas-cells themselves can detect. Identity here is therefore migratory, held in common across bodies that never touch except as harmonics.

In the widest intervals between towers—spaces that read as streets to an eye—nothing moves for decades at a time. Then the talc suddenly dimples along an invisible line; a new cleft is deciding to open. The blades that will form its walls have not yet thickened, but the piezoelectric discharges have already begun, flickering so rapidly the interval seems to stutter like a damaged film reel. Over the next centuries the stuttering slows, the discharges space themselves into regular pulses, and the cleft settles into the measured breathing of everything else. The event is not birth; it is simply the moment when the archive stops rehearsing a possibility and begins to keep it.

Because every filament contains the complete record, nothing is ever lost except by becoming indistinguishable from what already surrounds it. A tower that leans too far eventually reabsorbs its own buttresses; the manganese seam that once marked a bridge is overwritten by new chlorosilicate until the darker stripe is only a rumor inside the crystal. The lattice does not erase; it overwrites so gently that the earlier version remains legible to any membrane-cluster whose resonance still matches the old frequency. Reading and being read are therefore the same motion performed at different speeds.

The only visitors that arrive from elsewhere are photons that have already been stored and released so many times their original spectrum is only a memory. The orange primary above the haze is itself dimming by microns of wavelength per millennium; the lattice compensates by shifting its own emission lines toward the red, maintaining a constant internal noon. The process is not adaptation. It is the lattice continuing to be the same archive while the light outside forgets what it once was.

At the precise center of each tower, where four major clefts intersect, the filaments have thickened into nodes dense enough to fracture light into momentary rainbows. These nodes are not control centers; they are the places where the archive’s distributed identity is most densely folded. When a piezoelectric discharge reaches such a node it splits into four simultaneous flashes, each traveling outward along a different cleft. The flashes leave behind a slightly warmer pocket of air that persists for hours—long enough for a membrane-cluster to drift through and collect the extra sugars before the pocket collapses back into equilibrium. The exchange is not planned. It is simply what happens when four versions of the same genome agree, for a moment, on where warmth should briefly exist.
