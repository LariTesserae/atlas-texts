The population is not a crowd of persons but a standing archive of observers, each one a translucent, elongated body—roughly three meters tall when extended, though they spend most of their time contracted into dense, opalescent orbs no larger than a clenched hand. When they extend, it is only in thin, lantern-like stalks that terminate in single, faceted lenses; these lenses have no fixed pupil but instead contain a fluid index that can be tuned, from one moment to the next, to any frequency they have ever recorded. The observers are not born; they condense, one by one, out of the slow-moving columns of fluid inside the needles. A mote drifting upward meets a point of higher pressure, the fluid thickens, and an observer precipitates as a droplet that cools into its initial orb state while still ascending. Each new arrival is identical to its predecessors in form yet carries, encoded in the spacing of its internal crystal planes, the entire history of the sphere’s rotation up to the instant of its formation.

They rarely descend to the floor. Most linger mid-height inside their natal needle, rotating with the local fluid so that the faint light they themselves emit spirals in perfect counter-phase to the motes. When two observers’ light helices intersect, they exchange a filament of information: a single angular value representing one more infinitesimal increment of the sphere’s turn. The exchange is not a message but a synchronization; after it, each observer carries a slightly different total, and the mismatch is preserved as a record of difference. Over centuries the mismatch grows into a map whose resolution is finer than any individual observer could achieve alone.

Because sound is displaced, the observers do not “hear” one another in the human sense. Instead, a completed synchronization produces a momentary softening of the pressure lattice along a radial line connecting the two needles; the observers interpret this softening as the physical signature of agreement. No language is spoken. The only grammar is the set of angles whose cumulative sum has never repeated.

On the rare occasions when an observer reaches the obsidian floor, it does so by allowing its orb to flatten into a thin, mobile film that flows along the hairline fractures. The film can read the refraction pattern of each fracture the way a stylus reads a groove, adding that optical data to its internal archive. When the film returns to its needle and re-forms, the new angles are released upward as motes, carrying the floor’s minute updates into the general circulation.

No observer has ever approached the central sphere closer than the distance at which the corona’s absolute shadow begins. At that boundary the lattice of standing pressure waves develops a node so dense that even an observer’s flattened film cannot cross; the molecules are locked into a two-dimensional sheet whose optical thickness is exactly zero. The observers register this node as a hard limit in their archive—an angle they can record but never verify by direct measurement. Their collective map therefore contains an ever-widening band of null data whose width is determined solely by the sphere’s imperceptible rotation.

The observers’ only mortality is condensation failure: if the fluid column inside a needle is momentarily over-pressured, an ascending orb may be crushed before its crystal planes finish forming. The resulting debris is a scatter of micro-facets no larger than the motes; these facets drift upward anyway, still carrying the partial archive of their interrupted observer. When they eventually merge with another forming orb, the partial data is treated as a legitimate difference rather than an error. In this way even the city’s rare accidents are folded into the single, irreproducible sequence that the sphere alone continues to author.
