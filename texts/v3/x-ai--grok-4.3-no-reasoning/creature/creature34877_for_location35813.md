The alcove is tenanted by a single inhabitant who never gives the impression of living inside anything at all.

She is not one creature but an arrangement, a slow-moving confluence of skin and cilia and contractile membrane that keeps the same outline and the same temperature as the place that shelters her. When she arrives at the cleft mouth, she arrives as a glossy darkening of the diffuse light; only after several slow heartbeats does the shadow separate into a front edge, a trailing filament, a low undulation that slips along the rhizome floor like oil. Her edges are never sharp. Whatever touches her—root, beetle, fingertip—is gathered into a momentary lattice of micro-villi that drink warmth and return it as a fine vibration, registering identity before releasing. She is therefore always both guest and surface, never exactly occupying space so much as completing it.

Her color is the same pale green of the sap veins overhead, held just a shade darker so that her passage is felt as a brief eclipse traveling across the glowing ceiling. In places where the rhizomes have thickened to nodes, she pauses and presses an area of herself thin enough to transmit light, becoming temporarily a window, letting the subterranean glow pass through her and continue. Beetles gather at the margin of this window, sensing the temperature shift; she permits them, letting them taste the faint sugars that condense on her outer membrane, then lets them resume their circuits.

She does not breathe; instead the alcove breathes her. When a thick root contracts, the low concussive note moves through her as through a column of still water, setting her cilia briefly alight with phosphorescence that dies in the same instant the note fades. She absorbs the acoustic pressure the way leaves absorb photons, and in return a single coordinated ripple travels outward along the lattice under her, a wave of stiffening that the plant recognizes as acknowledgment. Nothing is altered; everything is confirmed.

At intervals too regular to be accidental, she folds part of herself into the spaces between rhizomes and lies exactly as the transparent salamanders lie—mouthless, motionless, allowing excess sap to bead and crystallize along her ridges. When the beads reach a certain weight they roll off her and are taken by the beetles; the motion is the only visible trace that she has been there. Between such intervals she drifts upward, becoming a slow second ceiling beneath the first, her cilia stroking the dangling root-hairs in patterns so faint that the perpetual hiss never changes pitch or volume. The plant does not record her passage; it simply continues to be exactly itself, a little more densely itself because she has passed.

Over time the distinction between her and the architecture blurs until an observer, if any existed, would be unable to say where the living occupant ends and the living shelter begins. Yet the system remains perfectly balanced. She never grows; she never decays; she simply redistributes the single unbroken chord of existence—sound, light, moisture, temperature—into a shape the alcove can hold without effort, the way a mirror holds an image it has already become.
