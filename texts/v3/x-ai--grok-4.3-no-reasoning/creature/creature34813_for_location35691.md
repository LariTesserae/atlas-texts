Beneath the weightless drift of the city lives an intelligence that is not a mind but a grammar.

It is a grammar of pressures: the slow compression of older skeletons folding into one another, the slackening and re-tensioning of the sponges that act as living buttresses, the pulses of water between the hollow chambers that set the pace for polyp feeding. Every organism registers the grammar as a single, continuous sentence written in its own tissues. A bristle-worm’s mucus bead is an adjective; a skate’s chemosensory glide is a verb that must be conjugated against the current’s case. The city’s translation table is its surface itself—every new carbonate ledge already conjugated into the next generation’s needs before the worms arrive.

The creatures that move through the spires are therefore not citizens but inflections. The glass-spined urchins turn like living punctuation, their spines interlocking at angles determined by the last sentence spoken. Copepods read the lichen constellations as footnotes, altering their descent paths when a single light-patch pulses twice in succession. Cephalopods that appear only as flashes are the quotation marks—brief suspensions of the ongoing clause, after which the sentence resumes, altered by their momentary presence.

There are no parents and no offspring, only earlier and later clauses of the same unending statement. When a tower hollows, its interior is not a ruin but the past perfect tense of the structure still being written around it. The fossilized lace inside is not dead; it is the perfect aspect of the living polyp mouths still drawing nutrients on the outer skin. The grammar permits both: the same organism is allowed to be simultaneously the subject that once built and the object now occupied.

The silence is not absence of sound but perfect legibility of every clause. A distant vent’s mineral pulse is a subordinate clause that arrives hours after it is exhaled, yet every organism already feels its comma in the change of water density against its skin. The grammar updates in real time, the whole city adjusting its tense the instant the new minerals arrive. Nothing needs to decide what tense to use; the pressure itself chooses.

Farther out, where the spires thin into raw carbonate mud, the grammar frays. The sentence trails off into ellipses—short towers, unfinished road-beads, skates whose papillae taste only water without the taste of construction. Organisms that cross the boundary feel the sentence end. Some turn back, re-entering the ongoing clause; others become the full stop, settling onto the mud as the next line waits, unwritten, for the vent’s next breath.
