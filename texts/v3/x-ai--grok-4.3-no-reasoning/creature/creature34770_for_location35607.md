The plateau sustains a single, enduring organism whose body is a planetary collaboration. It does not distinguish between its own flesh and the bodies of its countless inhabitants; everything is folded into the same circulatory rhythm.  

At the highest strata, the claret phyllotissue hangs in vast, overlapping plates, each plate an acre-wide leaf whose veins bulge like rivers seen from orbit. The tissue is continually extruded at the plate margins, where specialized cambium sheets split like continental rifts and then suture themselves closed again within minutes. These margins are the newest cells; the oldest lie against the rhizome core, already darkening toward the woody hue that will someday become part of the plateau substrate itself. Between them, each plate lives in slow synchrony, its surface rippling in wave trains no faster than a human heartbeat. The motion keeps water sheets from pooling and sends spores spiraling along air currents that only exist inside the canopy’s interior chambers.  

Below the terraces, the rhizome core spreads like a single, multi-lobed liver. Its cells are macroscopic—many as wide as human arms—yet remain porous enough to admit the three-lobed grazers. Inside the core, entire groves of chloroplast colonies drift along internal canals; their host animals have adapted so thoroughly that the grazers’ own circulatory systems now function as additional leaf veins. When a grazer dies, its translucent hull collapses inward, seals, and becomes another capillary. No waste occurs; every cell is reassigned to another task within the same metabolism.  

Rising from the core are the tubular browsers. They resemble knotted lengths of intestine, each segment a few meters across and hundreds long, slowly sliding through the mist on retractable hyphal treads. Their warm breath creates localized updrafts that keep the terraces from chilling. The browsers are also conduits: their skins are laced with fungal networks that ferry trace metals from the mineralized depths up to the photosynthetic plates. In return, the plants feed them concentrated sugars delivered through tiny rootlets that penetrate the browsers’ epidermal scales. The exchange is neither parasitism nor symbiosis, but simple internal trade within a single continuous body.  

Sound is almost absent, yet the plateau listens. Every plate edge carries thousands of root hairs tuned to liquid vibrations; when a grazer exhales or a droplet strikes a lower terrace, the signal travels through the entire rhizome in an expanding ripple. In this manner the organism knows where fresh growth is occurring and where decay is beginning. The “weather” of drifting humidity domes is registered the same way—pressure changes in the vascular mesh register as taste rather than touch.  

Occasionally the plateau produces a flowering event. An entire terrace system will, over the course of a century, divert its internal sap into a single colossal inflorescence: a kilometer-wide chalice whose interior surface is an unbroken field of luminous, pollen-emitting organs. The flowering lasts only a handful of decades, after which the chalice lignifies, collapses, and is absorbed into the plateau foundation. The event is neither reproduction nor celebration; it is the system’s method of redistributing mineral load and resetting local sap viscosity.  

No discrete creatures, no separate generations, no outside observers. The plateau is one metabolism endlessly re-instantiating itself, and anything that arrives—wind spore, wandering browser, human artifact—is metabolized into additional layers of the same slow, deliberate process.
