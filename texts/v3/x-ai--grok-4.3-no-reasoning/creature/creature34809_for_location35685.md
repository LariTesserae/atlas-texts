The city shelters a single lineage of small, slow beings—call them mineral gardeners—who have never encountered the concept of trespass. Their bodies are long, segmented cylinders of polished silicate cartilage, each segment the color of cooled obsidian threaded with luminous blue veins identical to those in the streets. Because the glass is never truly cold, their cartilage remains flexible; they move by rippling the segments in wavelets so minute that an observer might mistake the motion for heat shimmer.

They have no eyes. Instead, the spaces between segments are tufted with microscopic glass cilia that detect the subtle temperature and humidity differences along the water-veins. A gardener can follow a single fissure for days, pausing at each branching until the cilia register the precise capillary pressure that tells it where moisture is needed. When the reading is correct, the creature lowers its forward three segments into the vein; tiny heating filaments along its ventral side raise the local temperature just enough to draw additional condensation from the permanent mist layer. The excess water is then conducted, drop by patient drop, toward a tower whose rind has begun to craze.

Each gardener carries within its final segment a reservoir of molten silicate no larger than a hazelnut. The reservoir is replenished whenever the creature passes one of the intersection engines; it simply presents its open tail-valve to the engine’s exhaust and the super-heated vapor condenses inside, cooling to workable glass. When a fracture is located, the gardener extrudes a filament of this fresh glass, welds it across the fissure, and smooths the seal with a slow rotary motion of its hindmost cilia. The new surface is left slightly convex so that future condensation will sheet cleanly away—an unconscious architectural courtesy.

Because the light never changes, the gardeners possess no circadian rhythm. They rest when their reservoirs are empty and resume work when the reservoirs refill; the intervals between labors are measured only by the time it takes vapor to cool inside silicate cartilage. During these rests they coil loosely around the bases of the luminous canals, their blue veins dimming to a twilight indigo. In this posture they are almost indistinguishable from the water itself—another slow, reflective element of the city’s single metabolism.

Occasionally two gardeners meet at a branching vein. They pause, align their anterior segments, and exchange a filament of glass no thicker than spider-silk. The filament carries a record of every fracture each has sealed since their last meeting; once transferred, both creatures continue as before, each now carrying the other’s history in a thread that will be absorbed and re-used the next time reservoirs need replenishing. No dominance, no hierarchy—only the quiet redistribution of memory through the medium that built the city and continues, drop by drop, to maintain it.
