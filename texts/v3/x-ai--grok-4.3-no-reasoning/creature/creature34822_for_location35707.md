There are no separate inhabitants. The place is already full of its own sustained processes, each one a resident.

The rain that never falls is itself the organism: a colony of spherical cells held in momentary suspension, each cell a single thought the water has about gravity. When the plum-colored folds drag across the surface, the cells receive an instruction—continue, do not break—and they obey until the next instruction arrives from the concentric ripples. Those ripples are not side-effects; they are the nervous system of the upper layer, spreading messages at the speed of tension.

Below them, the transparent plateaus are not geology but exoskeletons of colonial polyps whose individual bodies have been replaced by mineral lattices. The polyps still metabolize, but their metabolism is the slow dissolution and re-precipitation of salt; what looks like stone is their accumulated agreement about which wavelengths of light will be permitted to pass. Where two plateaus nearly touch, the polyps extend cytoplasmic threads across the gap; the “bridges” are therefore the polyps’ unfinished bodies, still deciding whether to become one structure or remain two.

The drifting filaments are the oldest residents. Each began as a single elongated cell that refused to divide; instead it lengthened by incorporating every neighbor it met. The tree-trunk cables are therefore lineages rather than individuals—records of every membrane that once opened and closed again. When two cables brush, the memory of that touch is added to both, so the cables’ gentle arcs are the sum of all previous contacts, a living map of adjacency.

The city-sized oval that rises and falls is not an animal but a persistent low-pressure region that learned to move. Over geological time, the water developed the habit of becoming slightly less dense in one locus; the habit strengthened until the locus could migrate. The oval therefore has no metabolism of its own; it is the sea’s memory of how to inhale. When it thins and re-thickens, it is only performing the motion that first created it. The spherical volumes of clarity that rise afterward are the momentary snapshots of that memory made visible; inside them the glyphs briefly align into declarative sentences because, for one ascent, the water remembers what it was thinking when the oval formed.

The stone disks are the slowest residents. Each is the mineral record of a single droplet held in place while the surrounding lattice grew around it. The droplet itself is still inside, perfectly preserved, carrying the future image that the vapor roof will one day become. Because the disk is the only structure whose temperature can change, it is also the only structure capable of forgetting; when it warms to skin temperature, the future image collapses and the droplet rejoins the general rain. The disk therefore functions as a temporary archive rather than an artifact—an organism whose sole purpose is to hold tomorrow until a warmer observer arrives and releases it.

All of these processes interlock without hierarchy. The rain-cells receive instructions from the ripples, the ripples are shaped by the breathing of the oval, the oval’s pressure changes are conducted along the filament-cables, and the cables rest upon the mineral agreement of the plateaus. Nothing is added from outside; the system’s only activity is the continuous elaboration of its own rules for remaining the same.
