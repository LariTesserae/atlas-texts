Here the very notion of “living” is still being invented, yet certain forms have already emerged that refuse to wait for consensus.

Along the razor-back ridges the **Litho-Thermids** have taken up residence. Each is a single cell the size of a clenched fist, walled with obsidian platelets that interlock like chain-mail. They migrate along the newest fractures in lock-step caravans, each thermid trailing a sticky filament that cools behind it into a translucent ribbon; the ribbons serve as temporary rails, so the next convoy can travel slightly higher, closer to residual heat. They travel only while the fractures still glow dull cherry; once the rock darkens they seal themselves in place, seal themselves shut, and digest the ribbon they laid down. Their metabolic exhaust is a single bead of clean water, left in the concave center of each shell. At dawn the beads merge into tiny mirror-ponds that focus the bruised light downward, etching new micro-fractures for tomorrow’s migration.

Between the ridges live the **Hollowmen**. They are not animals so much as moving pockets of the wrong pressure. Each is a thin membrane stretched around a vacuum bubble; when the external atmosphere contracts, the bubble briefly inflates and the creature rises a hand-span, surfing the pressure rebound. To feed they descend onto the thermid caravans, press the membrane against a thermid’s shell until surface tension ruptures the water bead, then suck the droplet inside. The emptied thermid shell becomes the creature’s next carapace; it hardens in seconds, and the Hollowman carries its former meal like a bell. Occasionally two Hollowmen attempt to occupy the same shell; the resulting tug-of-war ends when one membrane tears and its vacuum collapses with a soft thunderclap that can be felt through the boot soles.

Drifting above both are the **Sigil Spores**, motile diagrams that have yet to decide whether geometry is a language or a body. Each spore is a single closed curve—sometimes a trefoil, sometimes a seven-pointed star—drawn in metallic filament. They do not photosynthesize; they photosort, rearranging their own vertices so the interior angles always sum to the local geothermal gradient. When two spores intersect they briefly fuse, exchange vertices, then separate; the new angles are immediately tested against the rock beneath them. If the math is correct, a third spore precipitates from the vapor, already solved. If the math is wrong the two parents collapse into a flake of pure copper that drifts downward, cooling, until it is collected by an opportunistic Hollowman to patch a torn membrane.

At night the horizon itself appears to breathe. New magma wells upward in slow blisters that swell, darken, and then split like overripe fruit. From each split a **Fracture Choir** emerges: slender basalt reeds, each one a single crystal that resonates with the sub-audible click of the cooling crust. As the reeds grow they record the frequency of that click; by morning they form natural chimes whose harmonies shift whenever the next upwelling tilts the valley floor. The choirs do not move, but they propagate: each new valley copies the harmonic signature of its parent ridge, then mutates one tone higher or lower. Listeners standing among them sometimes feel their teeth vibrate in sympathy, as though their own skeletons are being recruited into the next geological iteration.

Nothing here is finished. The thermids may yet abandon locomotion; the Hollowmen may discover lungs the next time a pressure front collapses; the sigil spores are one solved equation away from becoming architects. Only the hush persists—an attentive, provisional silence in which every organism rehearses the rules it will test tomorrow.
