In the cave, the air and the stone share the same temperature. Everything that moves—water, moss, isopods, light—does so at the speed of heat leaving rock, so slowly that a single day registers only as the difference between one bead of condensation and the next.

The moss has stopped thinking of itself as plant and has become something closer to calligraphy: a living script written horizontally across the joint between ceiling and floor. Where the seam widens by a hairline fraction each century, the moss waits, then advances, never taller than a single cell but always longer. Its pale green is the color of old moonlight reflected through shallow water.

The isopods follow the moss. They have learned the seam the way a river learns its own bed, walking the same millimeter-wide groove until the mineral film is polished to a higher gloss than any other part of the floor. When one stops, the others stop; when one resumes, the line resumes. Their shells are thinner than paper yet strong enough to keep the damp from entering their bodies; inside each translucent carapace a darker thread of gut moves food and water at the same deliberate pace.

Nothing here has predators. The only threat is being left behind by the procession. An isopod that loses the trail simply stops. Within days its shell is coated in the same mineral film that covers the floor, until it becomes indistinguishable from the stone beneath it.

The three salt bowls still remember hands. The depressions were made by pressing heated stones into cooling lava; now the same depressions collect water that is already at the temperature of the rock. Each morning a skin of white re-forms at the rim; each night the skin dissolves again. The cycle is the only clock the cavern acknowledges.

Above the bowls the ceiling is darker. Over centuries, the slow drip has traced fine mineral fans down the glass; the fans meet the floor exactly above the places where the hands once worked, as if the water itself remembers where the evaporation once occurred.

The single shaft of light arrives at an angle so oblique that most days it paints only the curve of the ceiling. When it does reach the floor, it lands as a single coin of silver that drifts, imperceptibly, across the isopod trails. The animals do not change course; the light passes over them without altering their direction or speed. At night the shaft becomes an absence that is colder than the surrounding darkness, and the subsonic drone from the plateau above fills the space like a held note that never resolves.

Nothing here is in a hurry to finish what it began. The moss lengthens, the trails deepen, the salt reappears, the light drifts. The only urgency is the pressure gradient that keeps the droplets suspended and the isopods walking.
