The people who persist here are not quite a population and not quite a maintenance crew. They move through the vertical gutters like slow sediment—thin-shouldered figures in waxed canvas and pitted brass buckles, their coats stiff from condensed salts. Their faces are difficult to read; most carry the same faint bloom of mineral dust that softens every surface, so that individuality is more a matter of gait or the particular tilt of a lantern than of visible feature.

They are born in the cavities behind the oldest façades, where the limestone has retreated just far enough to leave dry alcoves the size of freight pallets. Childhood is measured by how many handholds a child can reach before the next seasonal film of condensation re-frosts the climbing routes; by the time they can traverse a single sluice-duct without a safety line, they are expected to begin logging the rate at which the rust migrates along the western rails. Adolescence is simply the moment when their chalked notations begin to be trusted by others.

Daily labour is a choreography of small, repeated resistances: shifting a beam a finger’s width to keep a turbine in alignment, swapping one corroded coupling for its identical twin before the drip can work its way inside the housing. When a bearing finally creeps its single tooth, the ritual replacement is attended by every available adult; they lower the new ring on ropes braided from salt-tolerant root fibre and listen, heads cocked, for the quarter-tone shift that tells them the machine has accepted the correction.

Conversation, when it occurs, is almost tactile. A raised palm against a railing means *the condenser has vented early*; three short taps with a ferrule on the same rail means *the upper aquifer is running clearer today*. Lantern colours change according to an unspoken spectrum: sulphur-yellow for *leave this channel*, oxide-red for *watch your step on the next span*, and a bruised violet that simply indicates presence—someone else is also awake and moving through the same quarter-hour of vapour.

They do not keep calendars. Instead, they track the slow recession of a particular cornice shadow across the limestone floor; when it reaches a certain iron staple, they know a generation has turned. Funerals are conducted by sliding the wrapped body into a side-channel whose flow has already been measured and found sufficient to carry it beyond the inhabited levels. There is no marker; the absence of that particular set of footsteps is considered record enough.

Occasionally a child will ask what lies below the lowest ducts. The answer is always the same quiet gesture: a hand held flat, palm down, then slowly rotated until the fingers point at the stone itself. The city, they imply, does not end; it merely continues downward at the same unhurried rate at which everything else is being unmade.
