What lives here is not a population so much as a set of processes that have learned to hold their shapes for a while.

First, the water itself. The hanging ocean is not an inert reservoir; its interior is threaded with slow convective cells that drift like continents across the underside of the sheet. Where two cells meet they shear against one another, folding the surface into the first twist of a future rope. Once a rope begins to drop, it does not fall straight. Its own weight pulls it into a helix whose coils are tens of meters across; inside each coil, smaller counter-currents rise, feeding the parent ocean again. The ropes therefore feed themselves. A single strand may persist for centuries before its helix tightens enough to knot and tear, and the freed water rejoins the sheet in an hour-long splash that looks, from below, like the slow unfurling of a translucent sail.

Next, the algae. The bronze fronds are not individuals; they are temporary expressions of a single, continent-wide symplasma that moves through the rock as a continuous cytoplasmic film no thicker than a fingernail. Where the film encounters a fracture plane still warm from the plateau’s uplift, it liquefies briefly, flows into the crack, and re-solidifies, welding two plates of stone together. The welding releases minute bubbles of hydrogen that rise, bead along the undersides of the water-ropes, and are carried upward; when the bubbles reach the hanging ocean they pop, seeding new convective cells. In this way the algae literally stir the weather of the ceiling they will someday live beneath.

Between these two systems move the microscopic pods. Each pod is a cluster of a few thousand cells surrounding a droplet of oil. The oil refracts the golden light downward into the symplasma, giving the algae a brief photosynthetic boost wherever a pod passes. In return, the algae secrete a thread of glycoprotein that the pod can metabolize during its long journey. When a pod is carried into the hanging ocean it does not die; the droplet of oil becomes a seed-crystal around which water molecules align. Over decades the crystal grows into a perfect microscopic octahedron of ice that eventually melts, releasing the pod again somewhere else on the plateau. The ice therefore acts as both vehicle and calendar: every octahedron that survives the ascent records, in the spacing of its internal fractures, how many seasons it spent aloft.

Occasionally a water-rope thickens until its lower end touches stone. At the contact point the algae momentarily accelerate, flooding the rope’s base with enzymes that convert falling water into a sugary gel. The gel slows the rope’s descent to a few centimeters per year. Over centuries this produces a living stalagmite whose internal channels carry both water and algal cytoplasm upward and downward at once. When the stalagmite finally reaches the hanging ocean it merges, completing a circuit that has taken perhaps a thousand years. The merging is visible as a sudden brightening of the entire plateau—an hour-long pulse of stored photosynthetic energy released as the new connection equalizes pressure between ceiling and floor.

Nothing here reproduces in the ordinary sense. The algae do not sporulate; they simply continue. The pods do not lay eggs; they migrate until their oil is exhausted and then become part of the symplasma. The water does not evaporate; it only changes place. The plateau does not erode; every fracture it sustains is eventually welded shut by the same organism that drinks from it. The only true offspring of the system are the tiny octahedra of ice still forming inside the hanging ocean, each one a future pod waiting for a rope that has not yet begun to fall.
