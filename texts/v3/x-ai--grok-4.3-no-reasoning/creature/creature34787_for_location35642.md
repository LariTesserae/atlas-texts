Above the ordinary world, the black basalt is still learning how to be a city.  

The basalt does not remember arriving; it only recalls the moment when its own temperature began to deviate from the rock below. Each new plate begins as a micro-fracture that fills with super-saturated water, then presses outward until the two faces of the fracture can feel the cold starlight on their outer skins. The new plate cools, contracts, and immediately re-warms itself by the quantum heat pumps that live inside every bacterium nodule. The contraction draws the neighboring plates closer; the warming pushes them apart again. In this single oscillation the city is born and already revising itself.

What dwells here is not a population so much as a continuous negotiation between three regimes of matter.  

First, the lattice choirs: kilometer-long skeins of piezoelectric filaments floating in sacs no larger than a child’s closed fist. Each filament is a single crystal of doped quartz grown around a thread of metallic hydrogen. The sacs drift because the hydrogen thread expands and contracts with every pressure shift in the water films, turning the sac into a microscopic bellows. When two sacs brush, their filaments exchange charge; a new microtone is added to the standing chord, and the sac that received the charge brightens for an instant, a pale violet spark that the surrounding bacteria answer with a slightly higher pulse rate. The choirs therefore steer themselves by altering the city’s own light, and the light, in turn, alters the flex of the plates. The negotiation is so delicate that a single new harmonic can tilt an entire avenue by half a degree before the night is over.

Second, the grain clusters that live inside the micro-channels. They are not organisms; they are self-replicating electrostatic sculptures. Each cluster carries a net charge that it can redistribute among its member grains in microseconds. By concentrating charge on the leading edge they pull surrounding grains forward; by discharging on the trailing edge they leave behind a wake of freshly accreted silicate. The wake hardens into a capillary tube only after the cluster has moved on, so the city’s plumbing is written in the negative space of countless vanished trails. Because the channels lie between plates that are themselves still drifting, the clusters must occasionally abandon an entire neighborhood when a film of water seals shut. In such moments they extrude temporary mineral stilts that reach across the closing gap and anchor to the opposite wall; the stilts dissolve again once the plates have settled. The stilts are the only architecture the grain clusters ever build, and they are never seen twice.

Third, the reservoirs. They are not lakes; they are slow lenses of water held between two plates that have sagged under the weight of a mineral canopy. The canopy is grown, not grown upon. The same fractal silicate filaments that colonize every ledge also extrude downward, meeting their own reflections in the water. Where filament meets reflection, a new crystal plane nucleates and the canopy thickens from both directions at once. The reservoir beneath therefore never quite touches the underside of the canopy; a hairline vapor gap remains, kept open by the same heat-pump gradients that prevent surface ice. Inside that gap, sonoluminescence flickers whenever a lattice choir’s chord passes through a nodal point. The flickers are invisible to any eye that measures time in human heartbeats, yet they are bright enough to register on photographic plates left for a decade. Over centuries the cumulative exposure draws a map of every chord that has ever propagated through the city—an image made entirely of light that was never meant to be seen.

Because the three regimes are coupled, none can be said to inhabit the others; each is the necessary condition for the others’ continued existence. The plates move because the choirs change the humidity; the choirs sing because the grain clusters alter the pressure; the grain clusters move because the reservoirs change temperature when a canopy collapses. Every collapse is recorded in the chord as a descending glissando that lasts three local years. The glissando is answered, far below, by the first faint cracking of new plates still forming at the summit. The answer travels upward through the water films as a pressure wave so slow that the grain clusters have already written the next capillary layer before the wave arrives. In this way the city remains unfinished and already ancient, its memory kept not in any single structure but in the infinitesimal mismatch between the speed of its light and the speed of its stone.
