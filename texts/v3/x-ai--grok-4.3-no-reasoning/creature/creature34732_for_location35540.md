Above the uppermost platform, where chalk lines have long since surrendered any claim to permanence, the fracture continues into a realm of suspended glass. Here, fine filaments of the living crystal—less mineral than symbiotic—have extruded from the limestone in slow, branching fans. They catch the half-dawn and hold it like water in cupped palms. Small disks of condensed cloud orbit these filaments, rotating in patterns too deliberate to be weather alone. Within each disk moves a single silver filament that flashes once every three rotations; the flash coincides with the faint exhalation from the algae miles below, as though the entire city inhales through the fracture and exhales through the glass.  

Nothing with a name lives up here. Instead, translucent sheets of what might be either membrane or fog fold and unfold along the glass branches. When fully extended they form temporary chambers large enough for a human to stand inside; when collapsed they resemble nothing more than wrinkles in the light. A climber who lingers finds that breath condenses on the inner surface of these chambers in shapes that resemble the next level of scaffolding the city has not yet built. Touch the membrane and the shapes rearrange themselves, sometimes matching existing ledges, sometimes suggesting corridors whose angles have not yet appeared in the rock below.  

The chambers drift upward at the rate of one hand-span per lunar cycle. When two of them brush, their silver filaments braid for an instant, then separate; the braid leaves a tiny knot of denser glass that continues its own slow rotation. Every knot eventually detaches and falls, spiraling for days before lodging in a girder far below. Maintenance crews collect these nodules and grind them into the phosphor nutrient that keeps the algae luminous; the algae, in turn, pulse brighter for several weeks afterward, as though remembering the height from which the glass descended.  

Occasionally a chamber will complete its climb and vanish into the cloud ceiling. No sound marks its departure—only a momentary brightening of the filaments, like a held breath released. Those who watch from the uppermost scaffold say the light lingers in their eyes for hours afterward, carrying with it a faint impression of stairwells that continue upward at angles no geometry yet records.
