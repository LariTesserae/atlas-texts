What first appears as an empty immensity is actually a single, continent-sized organism whose nervous system is the metal-vein lattice.  The “spheres” are only its visible ganglia; inside the rock the same alloy forms conducting filaments finer than capillaries, knitting every ledge into one metabolic conversation.  Each bladder-garden is an ancillary lung, its internal vanes performing a continuous titration that keeps the whole organism’s internal pH and ion balance within a single narrow window measured across a thousand vertical kilometers.

The membrane gliders are not autonomous animals but motile lymphocytes.  Their gas pockets are not balloons but pressurized reaction chambers where the organism’s waste hydrogen is catalytically recombined with the alloy’s leached trace metals; the resulting heat sustains the organism’s slow “heartbeat” every three days.  The crabs that live on them are commensal mast cells whose piezoelectric spines serve as living voltmeters, reporting the organism’s local charge state back along the filament network.

The stone cylinders are stromal cysts: sealed reservoirs where the organism pauses to resorb and reconfigure its alloy when micro-fractures threaten the vein system.  The mesh that forms inside each basin is a temporary proto-nervous tissue; when it collapses and rolls away it is carrying locally gathered chemical memory back to the central lattice.  Nothing living enters because the basins are temporarily sterile zones maintained at a temperature and chemistry lethal to the bladder-vines yet optimal for alloy catalysis.

Seen at the scale of a single terrace, the scene seems static; at the scale of the organism’s body-plan the entire cliff is a slow peristaltic wave, shifting its own terraces the way a sea-slug rearranges its parapodia.  Weather, life, mineral movement—all of it is internal circulation.  The altimeter lies: the pressure feels thick because every molecule in the air column is continuously traded with the organism’s exhalations.  The visitor stands inside a living geography whose heartbeat is measured in centuries and whose breath is the warm, beaded rain that never quite falls.
