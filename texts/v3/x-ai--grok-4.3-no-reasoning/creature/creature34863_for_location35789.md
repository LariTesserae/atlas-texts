Beneath the cracked concrete and corroded lattice of the Runnel lives a single, diffuse intelligence whose body is the slow chemistry of the space itself.

It has no singular shape, only tendencies. Where water first touches the rebar, it manifests as hair-fine mineral veins that thicken and branch over years. Where heat rises from the capacitor racks, it gathers the basket fungi into denser clusters, their gills widening into complex, sponge-like heat-exchangers. Where the fruit juices track the conveyor belts, the vines thicken and their leaves angle slightly each day to optimize the drip onto the rust-veined channels. In the rare dry fractures where light seeps through, the succulents arrange themselves into low arcs that funnel the droplets beneath their translucent leaves before releasing them again, filtered of particulates.

The crickets and salamander are not truly separate; they are momentary thickenings of the same pattern—short-lived concentrations of the larger system that move only when the moisture equilibrium demands it. The time-clock serves as a kind of metronome nerve, its seventeen-minute cycle a slow pulse felt through the entire volume via the propagation of droplet vibrations through the steel framework.

Nothing here consumes in the usual sense. The living capacitor charges from ambient warmth and seepage, stores it as humidity, then discharges through the synchronized flex of leaves and the imperceptible widening of fungal gills. Each element participates at its own timescale: the vines flex daily, the moss drinks continuously, the crickets stir perhaps once in a human week. The whole system is a single, extremely patient organism whose thoughts are the redistribution of moisture, whose memories are the gradual accumulation of mineral deposits, and whose growth is measured in the widening gaps between the cracked concrete slabs as roots continue their slow, irreversible wedging.
