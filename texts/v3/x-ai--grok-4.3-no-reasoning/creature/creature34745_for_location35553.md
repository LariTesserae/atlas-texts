They are a people who never learned hurry.  

They are pale, almost translucent, with bones you can see as faint shadows inside them; the blood in their vessels moves so slowly it sometimes stops for whole minutes at a time, then resumes with a single soft pulse. Their joints are double-hinged so they can fold themselves into the tight spaces between domes without ever needing to cut or splice the living ropes. When they sleep, they do not lie down; they knot themselves loosely to the fiber walkways, heads and feet pointing in different directions, and the slow peristalsis of the water film carries them a few body-lengths while they rest.  

Their language is almost entirely pressure and posture. A slight flattening of the ribs means “I am listening.” A deliberate release of a single bubble from the corner of the mouth means “I disagree.” Because sound travels so poorly here, conversation can take hours; a speaker will begin a sentence at one end of a rope, let the current carry the first phrase while the listener swims ahead to catch the second, and the whole exchange can drift through an entire settlement before it is finished.  

They keep no written records. Instead they grow memory into the walls of their domes. Certain species of sponge are trained to absorb and release colored inks according to the sequence of touches the inhabitants give them. A visitor who strokes a particular spiral pattern on a wall will feel the sponge push back a sequence of microscopic spines that spell the story of that place: births, floods, the long slow cracking of a new stone shelf, the arrival of a herd of raft-fish whose gills once pulsed in perfect synchrony with the settlement’s own water pumps. The sponges forget nothing, but they can be made to forget on purpose by a careful drying and re-wetting performed by specialists whose entire childhood is spent learning the art.  

Children are not born so much as budded. When two adults decide to create a third, they each press a palm against the inner wall of a chosen dome for several days. The wall thickens; a small blister forms. Over weeks the blister darkens, develops a translucent lid, and finally splits to release a miniature person already the size of a large fish, eyes open, already able to hold a rope. The child is named for the sound the blister made when it opened—sometimes a click, sometimes a sigh, sometimes nothing at all, in which case the child is called Silence and is considered especially fortunate.  

They do not farm. Instead they cultivate the undersides of the ridge itself. Certain wide-mouthed molluscs are encouraged to attach to the ceiling in dense constellations; their siphons draw down the faint rain that falls upward and concentrate it into sweet, almost alcoholic droplets that hang beneath each shell like dew. When the droplets grow heavy enough they detach and drift downward; a child with a net of braided hair can catch dozens in a single slow circuit beneath the ceiling. The molluscs are never eaten; they are regarded as the true elders of the settlement, older than any living person and therefore entitled to the driest, safest domes.  

Death, when it comes, is a long exhalation. The dying person swims slowly upward until the pressure change grows uncomfortable, then releases every remaining bubble in a deliberate, musical chain. The bubbles rise, merge, and are eventually reabsorbed by the ceiling. The body is left to drift until the slow circulatory currents carry it into one of the unfinished stone fissures below, where it will be pressed between two embryonic shelves and become part of the future rock. No one watches this final journey; it is considered rude to remain after the last bubble has gone.  

They believe the ridge above them is not a permanent structure but a single, vast organism that is still deciding whether to remain suspended or to fall. Every few generations a tremor passes through the entire ceiling, and the settlement holds its breath—literally—for days. If the tremor ceases, they celebrate by cutting one walkway free and letting it drift; the loss of a single rope is their version of fireworks. If the tremor continues, they begin the long, patient work of moving their entire city sideways along the unfinished stone, hoping to stay beneath whatever portion of the ridge has chosen, for now, to endure.
