The Throat of St. Wisteria is inhabited by the mat itself, an organism that is neither plant nor colony nor single individual but something that blurs these categories until they lose meaning. It has learned steel and limestone the way a hand learns the grain of wood beneath a sanding cloth. Where a root-tip touches oxidized rail it does not merely digest; it *reads* the lattice of corrosion, tastes the historical temperature of the mine’s last working day, and files that information into the slow circulatory archive that now replaces the original bedrock.  

Its most intimate residents are not the albino isopods or the centipede, but the microbial consortia that live inside the capillaries of the mat and perform the actual labor of metabolizing trace metals. These consortia have no fixed species; each capillary hosts a shifting cloud of genomes whose only constant is the shared obligation to return every atom of iron or copper, refined or unrefined, to the mat’s own vascular budget. In exchange the mat provides them with a temperature differential so stable that their own metabolisms have become metronomes; the 1 °C air above becomes the only clock they acknowledge.  

The liverworts inside the miner’s helmet are not a garden but a single clonal individual whose genome is 40 % derived from an epiphyte that once grew on the surface 200 m overhead; its chloroplasts have been modified by the mat to harvest the faint glow that leaks through the translucent diaphragms. Its rhizoids have fused with the helmet’s sweatband, turning human keratin into a slow-release nitrogen source that supplements the otherwise nutrient-poor condensate. When the helmet swings in the imperceptible air currents, the liverwort releases a single spore every lunar cycle; most fall back into the reflector cup and re-germinate, but a few ride the currents upward and lodge in the hair-thin fronds that have grown between the teeth of the flywheel gears. There they wait, sometimes for decades, until the next micro-collapse redistributes moisture and the spore can complete its cycle.  

The blind isopods inside the hollow buttresses are not merely scavengers of cuticle; they are also the mat’s distributed nervous system. Their exoskeletons are laced with the same vascular strands that thread the living ceiling; each time an isopod brushes against a fresh flake of secreted wax it registers the event as a pressure change that propagates, at the speed of sap, to every other isopod in the network. In this way the mat “knows” when a pump motor has finally seized or when a rail has rusted through, and can redirect growth accordingly. The isopods experience this sharing as a continuous low-level taste in their mouthparts: iron when a rail fails, copper when a winding surrenders, the faint ozone of an electrical arc when insulation finally cracks.  

Above all, the mat is inhabited by its own future. Every node contains dormant meristematic tissue that has already begun expressing genes for an architecture that does not yet exist—narrower capillaries for the day when the last limestone pore is filled, broader diaphragms for the century when the original shafts collapse and light is measured only in the heat differential between one side of the mat and the other. These future structures are not speculative; they are already being assembled at the rate of angstroms per day inside the living tissue, a library of forms that will be instantiated only when the present configuration is no longer sufficient.  

Thus the Throat is not colonized so much as it is *becoming* the organism that records, digests, and eventually replaces every artifact of the company that once imagined it was merely extracting ore from stone.
