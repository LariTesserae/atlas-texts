The basin is inhabited by nothing that moves faster than water and nothing that answers faster than stone. Its residents are the rock itself, the nine trees, the luminous green cushions of moss, and the handful of slow, almost transparent slugs that drift across the open stone like a kind of living dew. They do not share space so much as they practice it; each occupant keeps to the lanes that the others have already left open, so that every footprint, leaf-fall, or glistening trail evaporates or is absorbed again before the next arrival.

What lives here are processes rather than creatures. The basalt exhales a perpetual capillary breeze that keeps every droplet rolling just far enough to meet its successor; the trees turn their leaves at the rate of a shadow across a sundial; the moss holds a breath-long memory of light and then snaps its reflective undersides into place for a single, collective flash. Even the slugs are less an animal presence than a visible symptom of condensate moving from high stone to low root in the slowest possible migration.

Nothing here needs to be defended or hunted; the only predators are evaporation and time, and both work on the same schedule as the basin’s own breathing. Over centuries the joints of the rock widen, the roots spread flat along the stone, and the canopy braid tightens its knots, yet nothing is ever finished. The grove remains a cathedral whose builders are still at work, whose congregation is always arriving, and whose choir of water-notes rises and thins without ever ending the chord.
