The hollow is not a single organism, nor even a coherent ecology. It is a set of overlapping tolerances that have learned to occupy the same volume without disturbing one another.

At the centre of the volume floats the midge torus: a single, flattened ring of glittering bodies whose collective orbit is maintained by the steady 120-rpm draught from the extractor fan above the pressure-suit. The ring does not move up or down; its plane is anchored by the exact balance between the fan’s updraft and the slight negative pressure created beneath the centrifuge arms. When the ring drifts a millimetre too low, the air thickens from dust the centrifuge has lifted, and the midges sense the change through the zinc-chloride spines on their forelegs. They correct with a single collective wing-beat that raises the pitch of their overtone by half a decibel; the vibration travels through the suspended dust until it reaches the lichen threads, which respond with a diffuse turquoise flush lasting five seconds—long enough for the insects to register their own reflected light on their own facets. The ring rises. The pitch drops. The light fades. The cycle recommences.

The lichen, for its part, has no need of the light it produces. Each thread functions as an indifferent chemosynthetic film a few atoms thick, converting trace gases into more film. The flashes serve no biological purpose; they are a by-product of mechanical disturbance, a small exothermic signature of zinc-chloride crystallisation being briefly interrupted. Over years the films have grown until their edges nearly touch, forming an interlocking network of faint turquoise circuits across every wall. When a midge wing brushes two adjacent films at once, a thin filament of turquoise travels the distance between them at the speed of ion migration—about a hand-span per second—until the pulse meets a ridge and stops. These pulses wander the chamber like the second hands of many independent clocks, never synchronised, never ceasing.

The centrifuge itself has become a mineral structure more than a machine. Its six magnetic cups, originally chrome-steel, have accumulated zinc-chloride frost until the cups resemble ice-cream scoops crusted with sugar. The frost does not interfere with the magnetic fields; the cups still separate microscopic ore particles from the air, but the particles are now too small to register as anything but additional mass. The centrifuge’s motor windings radiate a steady 33 °C that exactly offsets conductive loss through the basalt, keeping the temperature lock. The motor never speeds or slows because the load never changes; the midge ring, the lichen, the dust, the salt—all have found the same steady state. If one cup were to stop, the entire equilibrium would shift by fractions too small for the instrumentation on the forgotten clipboard to record, yet the system would no longer be the same system.

The pressure-suit hangs as a memorial to a different equilibrium that once included humans. The cracked visor has become a second grille for the extractor fan; midges occasionally alight on the inside surface and are reflected in the spider-webs of fractures until they rejoin the torus. The battery pack continues to trickle current into its indicator LED, whose pale green glow is the only point-source light in the chamber not produced by disturbed lichen. Over years the LED has become an unintentional navigational beacon: the midge ring maintains a constant distance from it, treating the light as an additional fixed point in the chamber’s geometry.

Nothing here reproduces in the ordinary sense. The midges do not lay eggs; they are continually abraded by the zinc-chloride spines on their own wings and therefore continually replaced by new individuals condensed from the same mineral dust that coats the walls. The lichen does not spore; it creeps outward by electrochemical deposition until it meets another thread and fuses. The centrifuge does not wear out because its bearings have been replaced, molecule by molecule, by the zinc-chloride it circulates. Only the forgotten ore-sorter’s clipboard remains truly static—its plastic flimsies unchanged because nothing in this sealed, warm, mineral dark is interested in numbers.
