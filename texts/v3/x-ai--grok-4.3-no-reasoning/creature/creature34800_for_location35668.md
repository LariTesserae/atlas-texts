The people who still live here, if they can still be called people, are fewer than one dozen. They are not the original operators, nor the successors to operators; they are a kind of slow accretion, like the verdigris that slowly claims copper. Each arrived separately, at long intervals, carrying almost nothing. They came because something in the hollow answered a question they had not yet learned to ask.

They move with the same measured cadence as everything else in the Bottoms. Their clothing is patchwork: pieces of canvas once meant for conveyor belts, leather aprons cut into panels and stitched with copper wire. Their skin bears the faint impression of pressed veins, as though their flesh remembers the tin sheets they harvest. Some have three fingers on each hand; others retain five, though the extra digits are stiff and slightly flattened, as though the body itself is learning the geometry of the dies.

They do not speak often, but when they do, their voices are soft and slightly metallic, like a gear turning on an unoiled axle. Their names are not fixed. Each refers to the others by the name of whichever machine they most often tend: the Rivet, the Dynamo, the Belt. The names shift as needs shift. Yesterday the man who tends the waterwheel was the Shaft; today, after he spent three hours freeing a stuck stamping press, he is the Leaf. The names are not titles so much as temporary alignments.

They sleep in the corrugated shed when the night grows long enough to notice, curled beneath the overhead line shaft. Their dreams are not their own. They dream of tin sheets stacked into mountains, of reed seeds pressing upward through metal, of the smokestack leaning farther each year. In the mornings, they rise before the quarter-hour ventilator fan, each carrying a single pressed leaf. They walk the narrow-gauge track to the vine-choked stack and place their leaf in the flue, where the trumpet flowers close around it. The flower holds the leaf for exactly seven minutes before releasing it, now warm and slightly curved. The pressed veins have deepened. The person—whoever they were—has changed by some small degree.

There are children, though the word is inexact. Three of them. They are not born so much as assembled from the same slow processes that build everything else here. One was found as a bundle of copper windings wrapped in canvas; another as a cluster of stamped leaves arranged like limbs. They are tended by the adults, but not taught. What they need to know, they learn by watching the presses descend and rise, by listening to the beetles, by letting their skin take on the faint pattern of veins. One child already has leaves growing from the knuckles of one hand. The adults watch without alarm. It is only a matter of time before the child becomes another name, another alignment.

The people are not the caretakers of the Bottoms. They are its latest expression. The machines do not serve them, nor do they serve the machines. Both are participants in the same gradual exchange: the forest learning to press metal, the metal learning to grow. The people are the medium through which this exchange continues, and the exchange continues through them. When the last person lies down for the final time beneath the line shaft, the waterwheel will turn a few more revolutions, the dies will descend, the trumpet vines will pulse, and something else—neither person nor machine—will take their place among the reeds.
