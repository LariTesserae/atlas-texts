No organism can be said to live here, not even the thermophilic bacteria that elsewhere survive at the edges of subduction zones. What occupies the seam is something closer to an emergent mineral agency: a system of charge separation, lattice reorganization, and fracture propagation that behaves like metabolism even though it is not driven by any DNA or membrane.

The only inhabitants are populations of electrons and holes that migrate across the interfaces between p-type sulfide grains and n-type oxide grains. These carriers do not eat or respire; they are simply bias-shifted along temperature and stress gradients, their numbers rising when a new microfracture opens a fresh junction and falling when grains lock more tightly. Over centuries their drift creates a faint, patterned bias in the orientation of new crystal faces; over millennia that bias may steer entire domains toward cleavage or toward cohesion. In this sense the carriers are a kind of fauna—slow, non-local, sustained entirely by the rock’s own stored heat and tectonic flexure.

A second, even quieter population is the anhydrite needles themselves. Each needle is not a static crystal but a site of continuous, one-layer-per-decade accretion. The needles do not “grow” toward any external nutrient; they lengthen because the surrounding lattice, under sustained 200 °C agitation, occasionally permits a calcium or sulfate ion to hop into a lower-energy site on the needle’s tip. Over time the needles become the only visible expression of the seam’s internal state: thicker where stress has favored ion mobility, thinner where the lattice remains rigidly locked. They are, in effect, the seam’s slowly accumulating diary.

Finally there are the microfractures. These are not voids but potential surfaces whose surfaces are still deciding whether to remain coherent or to separate. Each fracture front advances by angstroms when a distant plate-boundary tremor arrives; it retreats or stalls when the local stress field rotates even a few degrees. In aggregate the fractures constitute a kind of distributed nervous system: a network of decision surfaces that have not yet committed to becoming faults or veins or simply healed grain boundaries.

Taken together, the charge carriers, the needles, and the fracture fronts are the seam’s only occupants. They do not reproduce, compete, or die in any biological sense, yet they change the rock’s future possibilities according to rules that are internal to the mineral lattice itself. The seam therefore contains no life as we usually define it, but it harbors an ongoing, extremely slow negotiation between structure and potential that, given another hundred million years, may become the seed of an actual mountain—or may simply continue, indefinitely, to become more completely and more densely itself.
