Between the upper and lower planes of the split, the stone itself has begun to peel—thin mineral wafers lifting away like the inner layers of bark on a long-dead trunk. In some stretches the sheets are no thicker than a leaf; in others they have curled into translucent scrolls that tilt at the angle where gravity finally wins. Where they still cling, the rock beneath shows the paler, unweathered surface that once faced inward, pressed against its twin. These peeled sheets move only when the air, oversaturated, finally finds a seam and pushes through; a single pane will shiver, the movement travels down the fissure like a flag in windless weather, and a week later another pane will answer it.

From every gap and every fracture line a thread of water emerges—continuous, slower than a hair’s breadth per day—and follows the sheet to its lowest edge. The droplets collect there until surface tension yields, then land on the matching upturned edge of the floor sheet below. The result is a lattice of minute waterfalls that never sound the same twice. Each arrival strikes the film already present, sends a tremor along the sheet, and the tremor nudges the next droplet down a different channel, so that the pattern itself is a living response rather than a fixed route.

A pale, almost mineral foam grows along these channels, not alive in the ordinary sense but formed where dissolved limestone meets the water’s surface and precipitates again as carbonate needles. The needles, no wider than a bacterium, interlock into glassy bridges that cap each pool for a few days before the pressure beneath fractures them. When they break they fall as glittering dust that the waiting fauna ingest, converting the microscopic grains back into dissolved ions that the water carries away again. In this way the fracture is feeding itself with its own decay.

Nothing here casts a shadow; the only illumination is the faint phosphorescence of certain calcite surfaces that have been abraded to the precise roughness that allows trapped electrons to release their stored energy. The glow is the color of late moonlight seen through fog. Where a water film is thickest, the light refracts downward and paints brief, trembling circles on the opposite stone. The circles drift as the film shifts; a watcher might mistake the motion for something swimming, until the eye resolves the emptiness between the circles and understands the emptiness itself is what moves.

The fauna follow those circles. Their bodies are less than a millimeter thick, flattened so that the entire organism lies within the water’s capillary layer. When they cross the brighter patches they become briefly visible, a filament of palest cream against the stone. When they reach the darkness they vanish; only the imperceptible drag of their cilia against the rock betrays their passage. They do not feed so much as harvest the same ions that precipitate as foam; their gut is little more than a channel that concentrates the solution until crystals form inside them, then dissolve again when the next film arrives. Each cycle adds a fraction of a micron to the organism’s length, so the individual that begins at one end of the fissure is already a different length—different shape—by the time it reaches the other. Identity here is measured in residence rather than in boundary.

High overhead, where the fissure narrows to a blade-edge seam, a single formation has managed to grow against all probability. It is not a stalactite but a lateral shelf of calcite that has crept sideways along a plane of weakness, fed by water that never quite falls free. The shelf has advanced perhaps three body-lengths in the time the fissure has existed. Its underside is scalloped where droplets have repeatedly formed and evaporated; each scallop is a record of a day or a decade or an hour depending on how the water arrived. Because the shelf is the only solid protrusion, every sound that travels the fissure briefly gathers beneath it before dispersing again, producing a single low tone that the stone walls translate into a pressure felt rather than heard. The tone changes when the shelf grows, even by the thickness of a molecule; the fauna orient themselves to the change without any sense of direction beyond the vibration.

At the extreme ends of the fracture, where the original joint has not yet fully parted, the two stone faces remain in contact along narrow vertical strips. Between these strips the water is forced into threads so fine they behave like optical fibers, carrying the faint phosphorescence from one face to the other. Where the threads cross the middle of the fissure they refract into microscopic rainbows too small to register as color, yet the fauna still align their bodies to them. The alignment is not deliberate; it is the path of least resistance through the water film, and the least resistance happens to be the line where light and chemistry briefly coincide. Over generations the population density increases along these lines, until the film itself thickens there, which in turn accelerates the dissolution of the stone, which slowly widens the gap. The fracture therefore lengthens itself by the act of being inhabited.

Nothing rests. The water moves; the mineral sheets tremble; the foam precipitates and dissolves; the fauna migrate along the moving light. The equilibrium is the tension between these motions, not the absence of motion. In a hundred thousand years the fissure may become a true cavern; for now it remains the interval between pressure and release, populated by whatever can live inside the width of a held breath.
