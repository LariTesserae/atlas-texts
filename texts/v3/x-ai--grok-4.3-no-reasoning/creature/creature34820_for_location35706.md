In the pocket’s faint acoustics the velvet-worms do not so much move as negotiate a standing wave; their rustling feet re-weave the same frequency every few paces. One worm longer than an arm, bronze-green, pauses where a vein of native copper breaks through gypsum; its setae answer with a tone no human voice could hold, and a neighbor replies in the exact interval an octave below, creating a moving null-space between the pillars that other worms skirt without acknowledgment.  

Scattered between them the halite crustaceans sit like chess pieces that have decided to hum instead of checkmate. Their vibration is not a call but a pressure; each leg scrapes a groove into the same groove cut a century earlier, so the high insect whine is literally inherited. When a worm drags across a crustacean’s back the tone rises a half-step, then falls again, a duet the participants forget the moment they part.  

The chemolithotrophs are the part of the room that listens. Their sulfur-mining needles are tuned; a single grain of gypsum splits with a soft crack too brief for ears, yet the release of hydrogen sulfide bends the nearest worm’s path by a single seta-length. Over time the needles inch the floor upward at the rate fingernails grow, but the crustaceans migrate downward at the same rate, so the height of any given point appears fixed even while the mineral ledger keeps shifting.  

Higher, where the light becomes threads instead of light, a colony of gypsum mites the size of pinheads occupy the tensile fissures. They do not eat the mineral; they sip its static charge, discharging in synchronized micro-sparks that read, to the velvet-worms below, as another layer of percussion too faint to feel and too precise to ignore. The mites therefore add a third rhythm—one counted in light rather than sound—making the cavity a triptych of overlapping clocks whose hands never land on the same number twice.  

The lone copper torque, still tight around its pillar, now hosts a film of cuprite dust fine enough to conduct piezo-electric murmurs when the worms pass beneath; the torque sings back at them in their own register for a half-beat, then forgets. The quartz flakes have sprouted minute quartz hairs where the ancient bulbs of percussion once sat; the new growth is invisible until a crustacean vibrates directly above them, and then the flakes cast micro-shadows that move although the source light never does.  

Nothing here remembers the temperature; the air is simply the same temperature it has always been, a warmth that turns the back of a hand outward toward whatever is slightly less warm. Touch therefore replaces memory: a visitor’s palm on a column records the column’s surface as a kind of slow Braille, the grain size of salt, the angle of cinnabar plates, the minute detonations of the root-hairs still splitting at cleavage planes.  

The cavity’s single entrance admits no draught, so any scent of hydrogen sulfide lingers exactly where the last slow diffusion left it. A visitor who stands still long enough begins to feel that the taste of metal on the tongue is not an arrival but a calibration, matching the visitor’s own chemistry to the chamber’s fixed proportions until the distinction between body and mineral thins to a single layer of salt.
