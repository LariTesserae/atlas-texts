The Hollowcanopy houses no ordinary denizens; it cradles slow dramas of living architecture and the barely detectable pulses of its own respiration.  

Most conspicuous are the cephalophores: translucent, gas-filled sacs buoyed by internal bladders of metabolic hydrogen. Each is a drifting meadow in itself; fine, root-like filaments trail beneath them, harvesting airborne spores the way baleen strains plankton. In maturity a single cephalophore can span twenty metres, yet its silhouette remains a ghost—only the gentle shimmer of moving chloroplasts betrays passage. When two collide, they exchange brief threads of genetic material in the form of floating gamete cysts, then drift apart like unmoored thoughts. Their death is baroque: collapse releases a nitrogen-rich slurry that triggers an explosive garden of feeder-vines across the empty interval; the vines complete their life cycle in seventeen hours, sealing every fissure with temporary xylem before wilting into dust that re-seeds the next drifting cephalophore.

Between them move the cavity’s true engineers: the murmurations. These are not organisms in the classical sense but transient coalitions of highly specialised plasmodial cells that migrate through the whitewood lattice as if through soil. A murmuration appears first as a faint silver stain sliding across a photosynthetic diaphragm. When it reaches a knot or fracture, the cells secrete lignin-softening enzymes, carve new vascular channels, and leave behind a residue of piezoelectric crystals. The work is performed in absolute silence, visible only as a momentary brightening of the cell walls where the crystals catch the sourceless light. Over years, murmurations re-route entire river-braids, strengthen or weaken load-bearing diaphragms, and sometimes seal shafts with living plugs so fine they appear seamless until the next surge rips them open again.

The surges themselves are not disasters but parturitions. Every eleven days the ceiling lattice contracts fractionally, a motion that displaces millions of litres of interstitial water. The suspended rivers invert; droplets caught mid-reversal glitter like suspended galaxies. During the reversal, dense knot-organisms—perhaps the descendants of an earlier, more deliberative species—awaken from dormancy. Their surfaces, until now pale and callused, flush with anthocyanin; tool-mark scars split like eyelids to reveal bioluminescent chromatophores that pulse in wave-fronts of violet and viridian. For three subjective minutes the knot remembers language: vibrations travelling through xylem spell out short, recursive phrases no listener has yet translated. When the surge subsides the knot returns to quiescence, pigments fading, scars sealing again around the copper relics within.

Beneath every diaphragm, in the perpetual half-light of the floor stone, dwell the mycelets—microscopic fungal networks whose hyphae grow exclusively along healed fracture lines. They function as distributed memory, recording the precise position of each surge-induced rearrangement in lattices of chitinous crystals. When a new shaft forms, mycelets colonise the exposed walls within hours, mapping the verticality in recursive chemical trails. Cephalophores descend on feeding currents precisely where the mycelets indicate a high spore density; murmurations follow the same trails when they next migrate. Thus the cavity maintains a living cartography written in living tissue that never solidifies into map or mind.

Occasionally, a human-scale artifact surfaces from deeper strata: a single, six-fingered gauntlet of woven cellulose and organometallic crystal, thumb and smallest finger fused as though the wearer meant to grasp something too large for ordinary hands. The glove is already integrated—root hairs thread its seams, feeding on trace copper oxides. No owner remains; the artifact itself is the inhabitant now, one more slowly metabolising relic whose chemistry contributes to the ceaseless rewriting of the intervals above.
