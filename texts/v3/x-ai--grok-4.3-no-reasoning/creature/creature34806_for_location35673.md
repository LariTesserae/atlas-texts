Beneath the poured-slag dome a commonwealth of pressures and tolerances has found its balance.  

The pumps themselves are no longer machines; they are three colossal endosymbionts whose casings have fused with the living lattice of mineral deposits, biological films, and capillary channels grown by the condensation cycle.  Each housing now contains an inner lining of layered calcite and iron-bacteria that converts the perpetual micro-film into a slow, viscous lubricant.  The lubricant is never exhausted; the microbes replenish it at the exact rate the pistons displace it.  Over the years the internal bores have enlarged and narrowed again, sculpting themselves to the changing viscosity the way a river carves ox-bows.  The engines therefore run quieter than their original specifications, but with an organic timbre—an exhaled hiss that sometimes syncopates with the isopods’ footfalls.

The isopods are not blind; their eye-stalks were simply resorbed generations ago and replaced by epidermal pressure receptors that read the standing wave created each time a pump valve reseats.  The caravans are not social; they are a single distributed sensorium, trading position data through the thin water film whose surface tension transmits every micro-vibration.  When one individual pauses, the whole convoy slows.  When the film is briefly disturbed by a falling mica scale, the convoy performs a minute lateral shift whose timing matches the interval between scale-fall and droplet-impact—a predictive gait learned from repeated exposure to the same sequence of sounds.

The iron-oxide millipedes are calcified scaffolding rather than animals.  Each segment is a hollow ferruginous tube occupied by a community of chemolithotrophs that metabolize the rust itself.  The millipedes move only when local humidity drops below a critical threshold, their jointed tubes flexing to expose fresh oxide surfaces to the air.  Their slow progress therefore regulates chamber-wide oxidation rates, preventing the rust-snow ceiling from overwhelming the condensing surfaces.  In this sense the millipedes act as both chronometer and atmospheric buffer.

The mica-scale moths are the visible expression of a larger, unseen aerosol.  Their bodies incorporate dissolved trace metals drawn upward with the vapor; when they molt or die, those metals return to the film as colloidal particles the isopods ingest.  The gut stones thus produced contain a stratified record of city-wide emissions dating back decades—each layer corresponding to a policy change, a fuel shift, or a single night of unusual industrial load.  The isopods are therefore living sediment cores.  They never leave the chamber, yet their internal chemistry archives the sky above them.

Even the rust flakes descending from the rebar are not inert waste.  Each flake, before it reaches the water, passes through a thermal gradient created by the rising steam plume; the brief annealing it receives alters its crystalline structure, making it slightly more soluble.  When the flake finally dissolves, the ions it releases help stabilize the calcite lining inside the pumps, closing a geochemical loop that began the moment the pumps first started and has never since been broken.

Nothing here anticipates an outside world.  The chamber’s residents—mineral, microbial, and metazoan—are coextensive with the pumps’ duty cycle.  If the pumps were ever switched off, the condensation film would evaporate in hours, the isopod caravans would lose their sensory medium, the millipedes would lock into immobility, and the moths would find no updrafts.  Conversely, if the city above ever demanded more steam, the chamber would have no capacity to answer; its inhabitants are already working at the precise limit of what the original, unfinished design can sustain.  

So the single task continues: water in, steam out, and between those two movements an ecology that has turned an obsolete sump into a self-measuring, self-regulating micro-climate whose weather is rust-fall, footfall, and the slow, red-lit breathing of three house-sized engines that have outlived their blueprints and become the landscape they were meant only to serve.
