They are all apprentices of duration.

Most speak with the lowered register the turbines demand, but some keep a second, higher tone learned in childhood and used only in the uppermost galleries where the sound rises and thins. Their teeth are stained the colour of weak tea from drinking water that has passed through the glowing frames. When they smile they look as though they have been sipping light. They marry by exchanging a single filament each, taken from the same alley they both frequent; the filaments are matched under a foundry lamp until their branching order mirrors, and the knot is considered unbreakable if the pattern holds for a full generation.

A few refuse the knot. They live on the rim-walks, waking each night to the exhalation of the vents. Their only possession is a small hammer whose head is a salvaged turbine tooth. With it they tap the newer lattice repairs left by the crawlers, listening for the note that says a seam is still settling. When the note is wrong they loosen a rivet on a passing tender—never enough to harm the machine, only enough to give the city one more slow task. They call themselves Correctors. Most citizens regard them as unnecessary but harmless; the tenders simply re-bolt themselves after the Correctors have gone, and the city’s pace remains undisturbed.

Children are named at the moment they first feel the floor warm beneath bare feet. Until then they are referred to only as “the one who has not yet learned heat.” Once named, they are taken to the nearest seam-street and told to walk until the filaments record their stride. The length of that first legible trace determines whether they will be trained for the lower engines or the upper forges; longer traces go down, shorter traces stay high where the air moves. No one argues with the filaments.

There are no clocks. Instead, a single plaza holds a pool no wider than a man is tall. Condensation collects at its rim in a perfect ring, and the ring’s slow thickening is the only calendar. When the ring reaches the width of a thumbnail, the foundries pause for three days while the tenders scrape the ring away and feed it back into the flux. During those days the citizens sit on the warm ledges and speak in their higher register, the one reserved for the pause. They tell one another what they remember of the previous pause; because memory thins over a generation, each telling shortens by exactly the width of the scraped ring. Thus the city’s history contracts without vanishing.

At the very edge, where the plateau ends and the kilometre of weather begins, a single Corrector sometimes stands with her hammer raised, not striking, only listening to the difference between the city’s drone and the layered silence of the sliding clouds. She has stood there long enough for the filaments to spell her posture in salt. From below, through the perpetual mist, the luminous outline of a raised arm is occasionally mistaken for lightning that never falls.
