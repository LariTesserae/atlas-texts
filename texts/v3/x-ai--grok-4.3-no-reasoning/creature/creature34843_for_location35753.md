In this city, the very substance of life has braided itself into permanence. The builders are a consortium of species—none of which, alone, could have formed the city—but whose habits, tolerances, and slow negotiations have converged on a single architecture. Chief among them is an ancient, cold-adapted tree that no longer exists anywhere outside these heights: a thick-trunked, almost branchless titan whose wood, even while living, grows in concentric annual sheaths that harden to near-stone. These titans rarely stand alone; they are always knit together by a second, far more pliant species whose bark is smooth and pale and whose wood remains flexible for centuries. This second tree wraps and binds the first, its thinner trunks spiraling around the larger columns like living rope, filling gaps and sealing the structure against the constant drizzle. Where the two meet, the rigid and the supple, a third layer of life takes hold: sheets of moss so thick they have their own internal drainage, their own miniature canopies, their own succession. The mosses, in turn, are grazed by colonies of minute, translucent snails whose shells are made not of calcium but of a glassy, almost weightless silica. The snails leave behind trails of dissolved nutrients that the mosses absorb directly, completing a circuit no larger than a palm.

Between these monumental columns, the space is maintained by a different kind of participant. A family of vines—each vine a single organism whose main stem may be hundreds of meters long and whose leaves occur only at the very tips—has agreed to form the horizontal surfaces: walkways, platforms, even the shallow collecting basins that capture condensate. These vines grow so slowly that a single leaf may remain functional for decades, its cuticle thickening year by year until it becomes almost metallic in its resistance to water. The vines do not climb; they are lifted. As the great trees add new girth, the vines are carried upward, their older sections becoming embedded in the wood. In this way the city gains new floors without ever laying foundations. What appears to be a railing along a high corridor is simply the current leading edge of a vine whose older length has already been swallowed and forgotten.

The fungal threads that bind the peat are not mere decomposers; they are the city’s circulatory system. They form continuous sheets that conduct water, ions, and even chemical signals between distant districts. When a structural tree in one quarter begins to fail—its heartwood softening, its load-bearing capacity waning—the fungal network detects the change in sap chemistry and diverts extra nutrients from neighboring, healthier growths. In exchange, the failing tree releases stored carbohydrates it can no longer use, subsidizing the very organisms that will eventually absorb and replace it. The exchange is neither charity nor competition; it is accounting performed at the speed of diffusion.

Light is the scarcest resource, and its allocation has produced a strict vertical order. At the uppermost levels—where the mist occasionally thins enough for direct sun to strike—leaves are reduced to narrow, almost needle-like shapes, their chlorophyll concentrated in dense bundles that can photosynthesize in minutes before the next cloud arrives. These leaves belong to a dwarfed, almost shrubby relative of the great structural trees; they live fast and die young, their entire life cycle measured in a single decade. Below them, in the permanent dimness, the leaves broaden and thin, becoming almost translucent so that what little light arrives can pass through multiple layers before being absorbed. In the lowest habitable zones, photosynthesis has been all but abandoned; the plants here are parasitic on the fungal network, drawing sugars directly from the collective metabolism above. They repay the debt by extending the city’s reach—sending exploratory roots into new peat deposits, testing the stability of slopes the city has not yet claimed.

The few animals that persist are not residents so much as tolerated side-effects. The thread-worms that live in the water channels are themselves hosts to symbiotic bacteria that fix atmospheric nitrogen; the city therefore gains a trickle of new fertility from the worms’ waste. The pale, slug-like grazers on the lichens carry spores of the very fungi that will recolonize any wound they leave; their grazing is thus a form of pruning that keeps the lichens from overgrowing and choking the water channels. In both cases the city has incorporated the animals into its economy without ever domesticating them; they remain wild, yet their presence is budgeted for in the larger balance sheet of growth and decay.

Because every surface is alive, every surface also ages. The great structural trees do not die in the usual sense; they simply reach a point where the cost of maintenance exceeds the benefit of continued vertical extension. At that moment the tree ceases to add new xylem and instead thickens its bark into an almost cork-like layer. The surrounding vines and mosses read this change as a signal to begin the long work of incorporation. Over the next several centuries the tree’s trunk is gradually hollowed from within by the same fungi that once nourished it; the hollow fills with water, becomes a reservoir, and is eventually colonized by a new generation of vines that use the standing water as a growth medium. The original tree persists as a lattice of lignified tissue—still structurally sound, still part of the city, but now serving as both monument and nursery.

In this way the city never replaces its parts so much as reassigns their functions. A trunk that once lifted a canopy may, centuries later, become a cistern; a leaf that once harvested light may become, after falling, part of the peat that feeds its own descendants. The distinction between “living” and “built” collapses not because the city has conquered death, but because it has made death routine—an ordinary redistribution of matter within a system whose memory is stored in the shape of the next generation’s growth. The result is an organism whose body is also its dwelling, whose future is already present in the slow chemistry of its oldest tissues, and whose only architecture is the agreement, renewed each year, to keep becoming.
