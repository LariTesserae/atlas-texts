Three-Touch’s true inhabitants are never all awake at once.  

Each pulse of the river calls a portion of them to the surface; the rest remain below the visible tier, coiled in the kiln pits or threaded through narrow capillary tubes that riddle the basalt. Those that emerge belong to one slow-moving caste: soft-shelled, translucent bodies whose only rigid structures are paired mineral spines that they rest against the pillars while they drink. They drink not only the condensation but the faint metallic residue it carries; after a night of feeding their spines gleam copper-bright, and the city smells briefly of new rust. Their movements are so gradual that the light shafts outrun them; many hours pass before a single inhabitant finishes crossing a single intersection. In silence they reach the valves, rest one spine on the bone toggles, and drink again from the allotted trickle. When they leave, the tooth-marks on the toggles are slightly deeper.

Below, the second caste is almost entirely fluid. During the long intervals they exist as suspended droplets inside the narrower pipes, held in tension by static charge. When the river returns and the charge reverses, the droplets merge and slither upward as narrow ribbons, surfacing through the hairline cracks in the shale plates. These ribbons twist together into loose, branching forms that lick at the light shafts. They draw no moisture for themselves; instead they transfer charge from one plate to another, synchronizing the faint crackle so that the timing of each spark matches the breathing rhythm of the drinkers above.  

A third presence is not seen even when the river is high. It occupies the sinkhole throats at the western edge. When the water drains, the pressure drop draws a thin membrane of city-air down into the abyss; this membrane carries spores, skin fragments, and the faint charge left by the ribbons. At depth the membrane ruptures, and something inhales the mixture. Hours later a slow exhalation returns the same gases, now stripped of metal and enriched with a salt that lowers the surface tension of the river itself. The next pulse therefore climbs a little farther, feeds a little longer, and the drinkers above receive a richer condensation. The city’s water budget is thus maintained by a metabolism none of its residents fully witness.

Over decades the drinkers’ spines and the ribbons’ filaments become interchangeable; a spine placed across a crack will occasionally dissolve into thread, while a ribbon left too long on a valve will mineralize into a temporary toggle. The lizards and crickets treat both forms with equal indifference, rasping and molting around them. The only permanent trace of the city’s living matrix is an almost imperceptible rise in the iron content of the fungal lace—an incremental darkening visible only where the light shafts rest longest.
