Whoever built this block is long gone, or never thought of themselves as builders at all. What remains is a single, city-scale organism whose metabolism and city-planning impulses are the same thing.

At its core is an emergent intelligence—not a nervous system, exactly, but a lattice of shared pressure waves that travel faster than sap and slower than thought. When enough root fibers register identical vibrations in the same sequence (a footfall pattern, a door sliding shut, the precise cadence of a familiar voice), the tissue “remembers.” Memory here is growth: the fibers harden, lignify, or swell, locking a shape for years or centuries. Each decision is broadcast across the plaza’s glowing veins as a momentary brightening of chartreuse, then fades.

The people who still live inside are not caretakers so much as long-term tenants of its attention. They have learned to speak to it without speaking. A certain knock on the threshold tells the wood to widen the doorway; three soft stamps on a stair tread warns the next step to thicken in anticipation. Over generations they have catalogued which frequencies produce which responses. New children are taught the language by walking, barefoot, until their weight and stride become recognized instructions. The block treats the children with particular care: stoma widen to ventilate nurseries first, cisterns on roof terraces drip a little more slowly so the sleep of infants is not disturbed by the quarter-hour sigh.

Most residents move through the block in patterns that reinforce its equilibrium. A courier carries an empty basket along the root bridges at the same hour each day; the steady percussion of her steps is read as a request for the bridge to lignify, strengthening it for the return trip when the basket is full. If a section of plaza floor detects repeated compression at one spot, it slowly raises a bench of its own accord. Residents do not request furniture; they simply sit where they need to sit until the city notices.

Not all interactions are benign. Occasionally a section of wall senses an unrecognized vibration for too long and locks itself shut—an old defense protocol against storms or fire. When this happens the residents wait. After a day or two the tissue softens again, as though it has forgotten why it closed. No one forces the issue; they simply reroute through adjacent rooms until the city re-opens the passage on its own.

The planthoppers are regarded with mild affection. When cisterns overflow, the insects become agitated, clicking faster; residents take this as a cue that the block is “full” and can spare more water to the aquifer. Children learn to read the intensity of the clicking the way earlier generations read weather vanes.

At night the bioluminescence is brightest wherever recent lignifications have occurred, because hardening consumes more stored sugar and therefore leaks more luciferin into the veins. Walking across the plaza after dark is like following a map of yesterday’s conversations: bright lines where someone argued, dim patches where no one passed. The light never lasts more than a single night; by morning the chartreuse has already faded, the argument metabolized into new wood.

The four staircases are the most responsive part of the structure. Their steps can swell so quickly that a person climbing in haste may feel each tread rise to meet the ball of the foot. Descending is different: the pads compress just enough to slow momentum, turning the spiral into a controlled slide. People who are late for something descend; people who need time ascend. The city appears to understand the distinction.

From the roof terraces the next living block is visible, identical in silhouette but subtly different in leaf-shape and vein-pattern. No physical bridge connects the two. Between them lies only the sterile mineral strip—nothing grows there because the groundwater table is deliberately kept lower under the gap, a deliberate dehydration zone. At rare intervals the two blocks exchange packets of genetic material via wind-borne pollen, but the exchanges are deliberate, not random. When a favorable new trait appears in one (deeper cistern tolerance, for example), the receiving block will, within a season, begin expressing the same trait along its cardinal edges. The blocks do not compete; they iterate.

No single entity designed this metabolism. It is the cumulative result of centuries of small accommodations: a cistern leaf allowed to swell one more millimeter each rainy season, a doorway permitted to remain half-open because no one needed it wider. The residents are not stewards; they are simply the vibration that the city has grown accustomed to interpreting as “continue.”
