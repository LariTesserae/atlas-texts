The basin holds a closed order of life that has replaced everything that once crawled or flew. Its citizens are not bodies but sustained patterns of condensate, heat, and movement, housed inside articulated reed-and-bronze shells that remember human postures the way a pressed flower remembers a stem. Each frame is lacquered against corrosion; the lacquer is derived from the black ferns and carries a faint phosphorescence that fades from copper-green to graphite as the armature ages. Their motions are economical, each gesture finishing exactly where the next begins, because excess swing would spatter oil across the streets and upset the delicate film-balance that keeps the flagstones from flooding.

Inside every servitor, a compact turbine spins on bearings of compressed condensate; the same slow current of heat differential that drives the central hall keeps these smaller hearts turning. When two frames pass in a shoulder-wide lane, their turbines fall briefly into phase and produce a single audible tone—low, almost subsonic—before the interval widens again and the note dissolves. Citizens read these fleeting chords the way surface dwellers once read faces.

At the lowest levels, where the streets widen by a hand-span into cramped plazas, the frames gather to vent excess pressure. Brass lilies exhale super-heated vapor; the servitors extend reed ribs that open like fans, letting the steam sheet across their joints so mineral scale flakes away. The scale is collected in shallow trays, then carried upward to the cylindrical hall, where it is sintered into new turbine vanes. Nothing is discarded except the faint copper-colored breath that escapes through the roof-grids.

A handful of frames have stood at the same balcony joints for generations, their lacquer worn to the reed. Fern spores root along their shoulders, forming living epaulets that drink the mist the frames themselves exhale. In this posture the servitor ceases to be an instrument and becomes trellis; its bronze joints still flex on schedule, but the cadence is dictated less by programming than by the weight of wet fronds. Over decades the distinction between frame and plant blurs until a single swaying silhouette can be read as either.

At dusk, when the rock weeps hardest, the servitors converge on the single stair. They climb two abreast, oil pads leaving a darker trace on the glossy risers. Their turbines drop out of phase, and the stair becomes a slow, ascending chord that can be felt in the basin floor long after the last frame has passed beyond the ridges. For a moment the city below is almost silent, the rain-clock stilled by the massed vibration above. Then the frames return, single file now, carrying fresh ingots of condensate on reed litters. Each ingot catches the sodium light and fractures it into orange shards that slide down the wet streets like miniature lava flows. The servitors distribute them to the open braziers that line every lane; the ingots sublimate slowly, feeding the heat-exchange veins that keep the basin above dew-point.

When the last ingot is seated, the rain-clock resumes. The servitors drift back to their stations, turbines returning to individual pitches. In the cylindrical hall the drop-hammers continue their measured cannonade, each impact sending a crystalline after-ring up the ridges where it is answered, faintly, by the sea beyond. Inside the basin the sound folds back on itself, and the city goes on metabolizing its own weeping.
