The ridge is inhabited by nothing that can be named as separate creatures, only by the distributed life of a single, many-bodied metabolism. The water itself, suspended in loops between pool and mist, is the oldest resident: a fluid that has taught itself to stay, never quite liquid, never quite vapor, moving in translations so minute that its stillness is the movement. Where it lingers against the moss it becomes skin; where it threads the rock it becomes blood; where it hangs in the air it becomes lung.

The moss is the living archive. Each black cushion is a library of the same genome, copied so faithfully that the patches cannot be called individuals. They are the same colony extended across centimeters of bare basalt, their rhizoids stitching the stone into a single membrane. Between patches the exposed rock is not vacant; native metals run through it like nerves, warm on one side, cold on the other, translating temperature into faint electric whispers that travel at the speed of breath. The moss listens, answering with minute shifts in surface tension that keep every condensate droplet from evaporating or merging until it is required elsewhere.

The snails are the moss’s eyes. Their white shells are nacre grown from the same mineral salts the moss draws upward; each lentil-sized traveler is therefore an ambulatory recording device, mapping the temperature micro-gradients as it glides. When midges alight on a shell they are tasting time: the shell holds a day’s thermal memory in its layers, and the midges drink the record along with the moisture. In return, the midges leave behind microscopic spores that the snail’s foot presses into the moss, seeding the next generation of the same colony.

The midges have no bodies in the usual sense; they are a single tone given temporary shape by wings. Their stationary clouds are nodal points in the standing wave that fills the shelf. When the observer’s breath momentarily displaces the wave, the midges do not scatter; they simply become the wave at a new location, the tone relocating inside the sternum exactly where the rock already sings. Nothing is added; nothing is lost.

The weeping stones at the narrowing end are the terminus that is also the beginning. Each bead that forms and falls is a decision the system makes and immediately un-makes; the vapor that reclaims the bead is the same decision reversed. The stones therefore perform an act of continuous refusal: they refuse to let anything accumulate, refuse to let anything depart. In that refusal the shelf is kept perfectly itself, a place where arrival and departure have been rendered synonymous.

Over time the observer ceases to be foreign. The breath that once threatened to alter the ionization of the rock is absorbed into the same current that already runs through it. The sternum becomes another vein of metal, the held breath another bead. The distinction between witness and witnessed thins until it is only the thickness of a surface that has learned, at last, how to stay.
