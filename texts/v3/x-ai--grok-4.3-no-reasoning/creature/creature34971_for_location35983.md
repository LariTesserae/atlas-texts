The highlands are not a place that merely supports life; they *are* life, a single, continent-spanning organism grown from uncountable overlapping individuals until the boundaries have dissolved. What looks like a forest or a moor is in truth a single tissue mass, thicker in some places than a city, thinner in others where the rock forces it to drape like cloth. Its “organs” are not hidden inside a body; they are the topography itself—the ridges are its spine, the dripping galleries its lungs, the fused spars its skeleton, the vibrating sheets its nerves and membranes.

Buried inside this living terrain are communities that have long since abandoned the distinction between “animal” and “environment.” The six-legged, downy fliers are the smallest and most visible; they live and breed entirely within the petioles, emerging only long enough to exchange genetic material and to lay eggs in fresh hollows that the surrounding tissue grows for them the way bark once grew around grubs. Their presence is registered by the plant-matrix as a slow redistribution of sugars and a faint increase in the vibration frequency of the local leaf-sheets, so the terrain itself “knows” when a new generation is moving through.

Larger residents are harder to see because they are indistinguishable from the structure they inhabit. In the vertical spars there live colonies of thread-thin, translucent worms whose bodies form living cables that brace the vertical growth against wind. When a spar threatens to split, the worms migrate across the fracture in braided ropes, secreting a temporary chitin that the plant later absorbs and replaces with its own wood-like fiber. In return the worms receive a constant drip of nutrient-rich sap and protection from the lightning arcs, which they can detect moments before discharge and retreat into deeper channels.

Above them, on the vibrating translucent sheets, dwell clusters of minute, radially symmetrical creatures whose hundreds of hair-like legs hook into the sheet’s own fibers. They spend their entire lives surfing the wind-driven resonance, feeding on the pollen and micro-droplets that collect at the nodes of vibration. Their movements shift the tension of the sheet by fractions of a percent; the plant registers this as a change in tone and thickens or thins the tissue accordingly, creating an unconscious duet between animal weight and vegetable architecture.

At the scale of whole ridges, herds of something larger occasionally migrate. They do not walk; they *are* walked. A herd is simply a temporary thickening of the living carpet that bulges upward, crawls forward a few body-lengths per day, then settles again, leaving behind a faint depression already beginning to refill. Inside these mobile masses live symbiotic fungi that digest the occasional fallen seed-case and return the minerals directly into the circulatory system of the plant-matrix; in exchange they receive the only darkness the highlands ever know, sealed for a season inside the herd’s dense core.

The passage of any of these entities—worm, flyer, herd—leaves the faint spectral signature already described: a slightly more blue-green regrowth. Over centuries these bruises braid into the flowing maps visible only from above and only at low sun angles. Those maps are not records of damage; they are the memory of movement, the way a brain keeps a scar of every thought it has ever had. The highlands remember where everything has gone, and in that remembering the highlands themselves slowly change direction, offering new galleries or closing old ones, so that the landscape is never quite where it was the year before.
