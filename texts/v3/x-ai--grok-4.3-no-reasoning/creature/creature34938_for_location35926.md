They move in slow, overlapping pulses, never quite breaking step with the land they shaped. Their bodies are low and thick-walled, wrapped in layered cloaks of hammered lichen fiber that shifts from bone-white to iron-brown as the air dries or moistens. Each garment is stitched with tiny chert flakes that catch and hold frost so the wearer carries a thin, personal blanket of ice through the day; at night the same flakes release that cold back into the shared air, keeping every interior court at a precise chill. Their hands, seen only when the work requires it, are wide and flat, the skin the same pale stone-gray as the slabs; the fingertips end in blunt, translucent keratin ridges that fit exactly into the regular pits left by the old pounding tools.

They breathe with deliberate restraint, a slow, tidal exchange that rises from deep in the chest and leaves no visible vapor. Their eyes are small, the pupils contracted to vertical slits that widen only during the decade-long warm interval; the irises are the same iron-veined stone color as the rock when wet. Faces are nearly expressionless in ordinary light, their skin drawn so taut across wide cheekbones that no single emotion can flex it into a crease. Speech, when required, is almost sub-vocal: a measured vibration passed from throat to throat through the stone benches on which they sit, felt rather than heard. Children learn this “bench speech” before they can walk, their first lesson being how to press an ear to the rock and distinguish one household’s heartbeat from another.

Every adult carries three tools whose forms have not changed in millennia. One is a thumb-sized chisel of single-crystal quartz, kept in a pouch of scraped gut so the edge never warms. The second is a shallow stone basin no larger than a cupped palm, used to measure the nightly frost line; each person’s basin is slightly different in depth, a private calibration of how much cold one life can safely contain. The third is a living lichen wafer, no thicker than paper, folded inside the cloak; it is fed a single drop of meltwater each dusk and, in return, releases a measured breath of oxygen that the wearer can draw if the air inside a gallery grows too still.

They do not gather in crowds. Instead, they rotate through a calendar of solitary or paired tasks that keeps any one person moving across the terraces in a slow, spiral pattern measured in generations. A single individual may spend thirty winters simply repositioning the chinking stones along one seam, tapping each pebble a hair’s breadth tighter until the seam becomes invisible. Another may spend an equal span training the translucent snails, guiding their silver trails into patterns that, over decades, spell the household’s name in a language read only by frost. Children are not raised by parents but by the city itself: at the age when a child first notices the thumbnail mark in the public basin, the nearest adult—often a stranger—takes the child’s hand and leads them downward into the cool galleries, where the decade-old warmth still lingers in the stone. There the child learns to sleep through the warm interval without waking, and to wake again when the frost returns.

Death is not marked by burial but by placement. When an elder’s breathing slows to one cycle per hour, companions carry the body to the outermost slab and lay it along the seam where two stones meet. Overnight the frost binds the form to the rock; by the following dusk the body has become another chinking stone, its shape absorbed into the line of the wall. The only visible sign is a slight darkening of the iron veins that now run through the new joint—proof that the city continues to draw its faint, shared warmth from those who have finished moving.
