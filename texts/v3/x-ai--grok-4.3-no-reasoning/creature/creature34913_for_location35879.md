Beneath that dust-plain lives a single, slow-moving intelligence that has chosen the shape of a city rather than the shape of a forest or a beast. It is not an individual, but a polity: a clonal phylum whose every member—root, leaf, heartwood, gland—is genetically indistinguishable, yet each district has been given different instructions by the decisions of the whole. Over centuries the polity has learned to harden leaves into streetstones, to train cambium into load-bearing ribs, and to recruit bacteria as mortar, so the city itself is the grown body of its citizens.

Because the citizens are the city, their “occupations” are tissue-roles rather than trades. One neighborhood is still in the act of becoming myocardium; its walls pulse once per lunar cycle, pumping sap the way ventricles pump blood. Another has specialised in nitrogenase; its cells are dense with symbiotic cyanobacteria that flare in pale-blue constellations whenever the temperature drops. The “citizens” themselves walk through their own organs without disturbing the metabolism, pruning here, inoculating there, re-tying a vascular tendon before it kinks.

Movement is limited to the width of living corridors, and bodies are accordingly attenuated. The engineers are long, jointed, and pale, their carapaces formed of transparent sclerotin that lets the chlorophyll of the walls show through their own ribs. They do not eat in the ordinary sense; they press against the sweetened exudate that beads from freshly formed conduits, absorbing what they need by cuticle osmosis. Some never leave the quarter where they were born; their metabolism has been folded into the quarter’s own rhythm, so they only wake when the tram-rails contract at dusk.

The only predators are maintenance organisms that treat the city as their own ecosystem rather than as prey. The many-legged raspers keep membranes clean; the luminous hexapods feed on excess water vapour, preventing condensation that could drown stomata. The long-necked browsers descend only on the rare nights when the pressure vessels are releasing ammonia mist; their grazing prunes the fruiting spurs exactly where the tram routes need headroom. Everything consumes only surplus, and the city records no death—only redistribution of mass.

Sound is rare because vibration damages the scaffolding. The engineers communicate by pressing their transparent thoraces to living walls and letting subsonic chords pass from body to body. The city answers with the sigh of pressure valves and the faint percussion of the hexapods; together these form a single, low, continuing phrase that the engineers interpret as weather, traffic, or illness. When the vast trunk that is rising from the heartwood quarter finally breaks the surface, the phrase will change, and the entire polity will pause, listening for the new sky.
