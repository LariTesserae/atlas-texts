Beneath the resin-veined coral, inside each cylindrical wall of the pistons, there live blind, polychaete worms whose segmented bodies are no thicker than pencil leads. They enter the metal pores as juveniles and grow in lockstep with the cylinder wall itself; by the time they reach a metre in length they are embedded like living O-rings. Each worm draws a microscopic current of its own through the pores, expelling minute strands of mucus that catch and re-release stray silt grains, keeping the scrubbers from clogging. Their own egg-cases look like frost flowers stamped against the inner metal; the larvae drift upward with each ventilation pulse, some settling on higher flanges, others carried out through the transept rent, never returning.

Between the stalled pews, a single, crab-sized hermit has taken up residence inside an old chlorine cartridge. Its carapace is the same copper-green as the cartridge shell; it has added, over years, a fringe of discarded kelp resin flakes to the rim, turning the cylinder into a lopsided, shingled turret. When it feeds, it lowers its smaller claw through a slot at the base, snatching resin globules as they settle. The larger claw is never seen; rumor in the small circle of visiting biologists holds that it has fused permanently into the cartridge rim and now serves only as an anchor.

Suspended just below the fluctuating mirror, a colony of salps has learned to ride the clockwise gyre. Each salp is no bigger than a lentil and transparent except for a single, ruby-coloured vacuole that stores dissolved copper ions. During the three-day bacterial bloom the vacuoles fluoresce briefly, producing tiny moving constellations that chase one another around the nave at chest height. When the bloom dims, the salps sink a metre, absorb fresh ions from the steam drizzle, and rise again. The entire colony amounts to less than a gram of living tissue, yet it traces the same slow orbit that the silt motes follow, an ephemeral living ring inside an architectural one.

At the very bottom of the trench, beneath the membrane-winged crustaceans, the coral has grown downward as well as upward, forming a series of inverted vaults whose ceilings are lined with clusters of gas-filled vesicles. Each vesicle is a millimetre across and contains, instead of steam, a single archaeon whose metabolism produces minute hydrogen bubbles. When a vesicle reaches critical pressure, it tears free and rises—never bursting—until it reaches the copper-threaded domes around the altar. There it is captured, its hydrogen absorbed, its empty membrane folded and recycled by yet another resident: a biofilm that behaves like living parchment, flattening itself across the pinhole nozzles to modulate steam flow. The membrane, when stretched, shows a faint grain that matches the helical polish of the coral above, as though the two organisms have begun to exchange patterns across the intervening metres of water.

No name exists for the whole of this assembly. It is not a single organism, nor a simple symbiont, nor even a clear case of ecological succession. It is an industrial ruin that has been metabolised—stone into coral, copper into claw, wax into clockwork—until the categories of artifact and biota have dissolved into a single, slow-moving equilibrium.
