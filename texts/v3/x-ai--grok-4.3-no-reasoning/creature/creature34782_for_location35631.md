In the brief span between each pulse-driven lurch, a single, translucent polychaete—call it the longest of the thread-worms—has woven itself an incomplete lattice: a three-dimensional knot of bristles that catches the vent’s rising sulfide the way a spider’s web catches dew. The worm’s skin is so thin the metallic threads inside its body are visible as a glowing script, a moving inscription of ions that rewrite themselves whenever the bubble shifts and the worm must swim for a new anchor point.

When the next heave arrives, the lattice tears; the filaments shear away and spiral outward, each one carrying a single bead of vent fluid sealed inside a microscopic membrane. These beads do not fall; instead they are ingested almost instantly by a secondary, still invisible population: an encrustation of what at first looks like frost but resolves, under the milky light, into colonies of tubular microbes so small the eye assigns them no individual shape. Their collective metabolism ignites in a faint chain of violet sparks that race around the meniscus like a clock-hand before extinguishing against the newest pillow lobe. The sparks leave behind a hair-thin layer of elemental sulfur that instantly oxidizes into a swirling veil of fine yellow dust; the dust is not lost to the sea but pressed back into the membrane itself, thickening that living chain-mail wherever the bubble just stretched.

Between one such flash and the next, the worm has already begun a new lattice, and the notch in the softening basalt shard below the meniscus receives its next tiny scratch—whether from human hand or from the automatic jitter of the rock itself is impossible to tell. The process repeats, each iteration barely distinguishable from the last, yet each one adding a single ion, a single filament, a single photon of turquoise light to the unfinished sentence the ocean is still learning how to pronounce.
