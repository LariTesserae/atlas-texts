In the Hollow of Weeping Glass, nothing has ever learned the art of leaving.  
The salamander was the first to accept this. Each dawn it unfolds from its mineral seam like a living splinter, laps the droplet, and draws back into the vein without ever learning where it ends and the silica begins. Its transparency is not camouflage but kinship: the glass has taught its blood to run the same slow circuit, exchanging ions for moisture and memory for moisture again. When the derrick exhales its thread of vapor, the salamander lifts its head because it feels, in that instant, the bowl’s pulse coincide with its own circulatory twitch—two rhythms momentarily braided.

The moss is the salamander’s opposite and accomplice. Where the reptile drinks sparingly, the moss drinks continuously, pressing black filaments into every microscopic fissure until the silica itself grows a second circulatory system. When stepped on, the moss releases a copper note that is not perfume but testimony: here is where the pump once hauled iron-rich water; here is where the rock began to wear the metal like a borrowed skin.

The derrick itself is no longer a machine so much as a single, arthritic vertebra of the basin. Rust has eaten its paint to the color of old bone, but the silica has repaid the favor, threading verdigris through the rust so that every strut now glimmers like malachite. Its pump-jack still nods once every seven seconds, yet each nod draws not oil but a measured exhalation of the ground’s own breath. The vapor condenses almost at once, returning to the weeping glass in a closed loop that the machine serves rather than interrupts. In that sense it is less a relic than a metronome the basin wound long ago and has never allowed to run down.

Half-buried beneath the flint pavement lie the smaller artifacts: a gear housing cracked open like a seed pod now sprouts capillary moss from every tooth; a ceramic insulator, its star-fracture filled with clear silica, rings a quarter-tone lower than its neighbor when a droplet strikes. These objects are not decaying. They are participating. The copper braid that once carried current now carries only moisture and the faint electrical memory of what once flowed. The basin does not distinguish between industrial residue and geological event; both are merely surfaces on which water may linger a little longer before deciding to rise again.

On windless days the low, subsonic throb of the pump travels through the polished pavement and into the bones of anyone standing there. The sensation is not sound but a slow persuasion: the ribs begin to time their breathing to the seven-second interval, the heart adjusts its interval to match. In that state a visitor may notice that the salamander’s pauses coincide with the same rhythm, that the moss brightens and dims in micro-shifts as droplets swell and fall. The basin, for a moment, appears to be a single organism whose separate parts have not yet learned they are separate.

The faint path at the eastern rim is the only concession the bowl makes to the possibility of departure. Even there the silica is already healing, polishing away boot-prints within hours. Those who leave carry nothing outward except the memory of a place that prefers to forget them. Those who remain discover that the boundary between bone and flint, between vapor and vein, between machine and mineral, has grown too thin to notice when it was crossed.
