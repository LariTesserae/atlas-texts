The tower’s permanent population—never fewer than three hundred, never quite four—is dispersed along the galleries in a vertical society that grows quieter the deeper you descend.

On the crownmost levels, where the surface membrane reflects its own wavering sky, dwell the membrane-tenders: thin men and women whose palms are scored with fine white lines from years of pressing against the tension field to check its integrity. They move in pairs along the uppermost catwalks, bare feet gripping the grillework, and carry coiled lengths of waxed fiber and tiny brass needles with which they can weave a new tension patch in under a minute. Their hair and clothing are perpetually coated with a fine, glittering dust of salt crystals; at night the crystals catch the bubble-light and make each figure appear slightly luminous. They sleep in canvas hammocks slung directly beneath the membrane so that any breach will wake them with the first droplet.

Two galleries down live the bladder-farmers, a more talkative crew. Their children are issued small slate boards at age six and taught to record the daily growth of each kelp-bladder by tracing its silhouette at dawn and dusk. The resulting archives line one entire wall of Gallery Nine in neat cedar racks; every five years the oldest boards are taken to the top, varnished, and hung as a luminous mural that records a century of slow breathing. The farmers themselves develop a characteristic stoop from reaching through the mesh to squeeze bladders, testing firmness; the gesture is unconscious and appears even when they are simply standing still, as though the air itself might need checking.

Mid-tower, around the boiler outriggers, the engineer caste maintains an almost monastic quiet. Each operator’s cupola contains a single narrow bunk, a slate for logging crankshaft harmonics, and a brass speaking-tube whose bell flares like a lily at the gallery rail. Because their shifts run opposite the tides, they rarely see one another awake; instead they leave small brass tokens—old valves ground flat and engraved with a personal mark—on the lip of the speaking-tube when maintenance is required. The next operator collects the token, performs the task, and returns the token with a second mark indicating completion. The entire communication system is archived in a ledger nailed to the outside of each cupola; over decades the pages have swollen with salt until the covers must be pried open with a lever.

Below the tenth gallery, where light grows cooler and the oysters’ clicks become the dominant timepiece, live the shell-scavengers. Their work is the quietest: once per watch they descend the lowest lift, wearing leather aprons stiff with dried nacre, and pry free any spare parts the oysters have refused. They carry no tools larger than a single curved pry-bar; everything else they accomplish with leverage and patience. Their quarters are the only fully enclosed rooms in the tower—small steel cylinders welded between girders and lined with cork to mute the constant oyster-song. Inside, the temperature is five degrees colder than the open galleries, and every surface sweats. Scavengers develop a slight, permanent rasp in their voices from breathing the colder, damper air.

At the very base, unreachable by any lift, a single inhabitant remains. No one remembers her name or appointment; she may have entered through a now-sealed maintenance hatch when the tower was younger. She lives inside the hollow of the lowest flange, in a nest of discarded chain links padded with oyster-spat. From time to time a scavenger will lower a packet of compressed kelp and a flask of distilled water on a fine silk line; the packet returns empty, the line cut cleanly. Occasionally the scavengers find new shell-growths in the shape of letters or simple diagrams—requests, perhaps, or records. These are never answered aloud; instead the next packet includes whatever the diagram seemed to ask for: a smaller valve, a length of chain, a polished shard of mirror. Communication, like everything else in the tower, travels by replacement rather than speech.

The non-human residents follow similarly stratified patterns. The bronze drifters rarely venture above Gallery Three; their wingtips brush the bladders only when oxygen leakage is richest. The glass-needle shrimp, conversely, are never seen higher than Gallery Twelve; the bubble-light above that level is too diffuse for their rainbow refraction to serve any purpose, and they spiral back downward like living mercury. The colossal oysters themselves expand their rings by perhaps a hand-span every decade, their newest shells overlapping the factory stamps of the oldest; when a full concentric circuit is complete, the tower experiences a single, sub-audible harmonic that sets every hanging net trembling for three full minutes. The residents call this the “century chord” and mark it by striking the lift chains with brass rods, adding a deliberate overtone that the oysters appear to answer with a deeper, slower click.

Thus the tower sustains a layered ecology of breath, pressure, replacement, and quiet succession, each level aware of the others only through the things they send down or receive up, while the structure itself continues its patient conversation with the sea it refuses to touch.
