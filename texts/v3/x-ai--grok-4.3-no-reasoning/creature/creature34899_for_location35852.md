Within the sealed olivine lens lives only movement without bodies. Each rising bubble is the chamber’s briefest inhabitant, a momentary vessel of heat that departs upward and leaves its absence as the sole memory. The droplets that cross the floor are its longer residents, transparent travelers bound by surface tension rather than by any will; they are the pulse of the pocket, following invisible temperature gradients like blood following valves they themselves create.  

The manganese oxides etched into the glass floor behave like a slow skin, darkening when a bubble cools the ceiling above them, then slowly lightening again once the warmth returns. Their patterns—branching, re-branching, never quite repeating—record every oscillation of temperature the way tree rings remember seasons, yet no season ever arrives here; only the daily sweep of the needle-beam that passes like a single eyelid opening and closing. The oxides therefore live by the light’s transit: wherever the beam has gone, the oxides grow faint; wherever the beam is still to come, they remain darker, anticipatory.  

There are no organisms, but the sound itself can almost be counted as resident—a pressure wave that forms each time two droplets meet and merge, a pure tone that travels nowhere because the air is too thin to carry it far. The tone fades before the next droplet forms, so the chamber never holds more than one note at a time. The oxides darken again when the note dies, as though registering its departure, and the cycle continues.  

Above the lens, nothing watches. Below the floor, the ancient impact heat still moves, and the olivine glass keeps translating that movement into transient shapes: a bubble, a droplet, a shifting dendritic map. The place is therefore occupied only by these translations—the ceiling remembering upward, the floor remembering downward—each translation becoming the next until the residual warmth itself finally cools and the pocket, like its own slow breath, ceases.
