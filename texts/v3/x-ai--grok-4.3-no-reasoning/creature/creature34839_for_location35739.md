Beneath that unchanging brick-orange dusk live creatures shaped to a single, suspended hour.  

The most numerous are the **clay-borers**, finger-length annelids whose iridescent segments flex like wet clay. They tunnel shallow galleries through the ash-clay skin, lining each burrow with a paper-thin film of saliva that never dries. In these galleries the water table stays exactly half a finger’s width above the burrow floors, so the worms can graze the meniscus without ever breaking the surface tension. When a drip from the overhead pipework lands near a gallery mouth, the nearest worm will protrude its anterior ring, press against the drop until the meniscus bulges inward, then retract; the gallery fills to its calibrated depth again and the worm returns to its ceaseless, unmoving vigil.

Higher in the hollow flues, translucent **pulse-salamanders** share space with **coiling ember-snails**. The snails are no larger than a thumbnail; their spiral shells are made of fused furnace scale and remain warm enough to fog a fingertip. They migrate in slow gyres around the flue walls, each following the faint convection eddy left by the salamanders’ gill currents, keeping the air in the chimney perpetually mixed. Their egg clusters look like droplets of solder, yet the embryos never hatch; instead the shells simply thicken by the width of a single scale layer every time a salamander exhales warmer vapor over them.

Where the narrow streets open onto loading ramps, the **mastiff-hogs** move in a slow carousel. They do not forage so much as polish: each hog selects one rail or iron lip, presses its yellowed tusk against the metal, and walks its circuit until the tusk edge has burnished away the newest film of rust. When the circuit is complete, the hog returns to the precise indentation it left in the clay the last time it lay down. The indentation is never deepened; the hog simply settles into it again, its flank matching the earlier contour exactly, as if the clay itself remembers the animal’s outline.

Below the sump grates, blind **therm-eels** rest in figure-eights whose knots coincide with the coldest outflow pipes. Their skin secretes a thin oil that rises in microscopic beads; these beads catch the reflected brick-light and drift upward through the grate bars like a slow constellation. When a bead reaches the surface film, it bursts, releasing a single faint note—lower than the pipe-thrum but of the same timbre—before the oil re-condenses and falls back. The eels shift neither closer nor farther from the note; their position is already the place where the returning drop lands.

On the brick faces themselves, the olive lichen is not a single organism but a layered film of **glass-lichens**. Each new generation settles into the depressions left by its predecessors’ dried margins; because the depressions are exactly the depth of one cell layer, the film never thickens and never flakes. From a distance the surface looks uniformly matte; up close the lichen presents a map of faint, precise polygons—each cell a perfect octagon whose shared walls are the temperature of the brick beneath and the air above at once.

Mid-air, the columns of midges do not disperse or regroup. The swarm forms a self-sustaining cylinder whose diameter equals the width of the cistern rim. Insects at the inner surface beat their wings just enough to hold position; insects at the outer surface drift outward on the faint upward current of warm vapor and are nudged inward again by the settling steam ceiling. The column is therefore an unbroken Möbius loop of ascent and descent, never gaining or losing a single individual.

Nothing here is idle, yet nothing moves farther than the measure of its own first footprint. Every organism, every film, every polished rail exists at the exact terminus of the last motion it performed, held there by the refusal of time to offer a subsequent interval.
