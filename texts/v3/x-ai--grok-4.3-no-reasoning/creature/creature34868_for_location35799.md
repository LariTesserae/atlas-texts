The inhabitants have no name for themselves either, because they too are phrases within that sentence rather than separate speakers.

They are grown, not born—each new citizen a slow extrusion of living fiber extruded from the city itself. When the drone dips to a particular microtone, the walls begin to bud a row of translucent, finger-thick filaments that elongate inward until they meet one another and fuse. Over decades these filaments braid, hollow, and finally inflate into a translucent, pear-shaped sac. Inside, a single transparent seed forms, then divides, then reforms. After several centuries the sac withers and splits along one seam, releasing a motionless infant whose skin is the same pale, tensile tissue as the chamber walls. The child is already rooted; umbilical threads trail from its back and are immediately grafted into the nearest web, where they begin to take sap. Within hours the infant’s eyes open—pure black, lidless, with pupils that are merely the absence of fiber. They will never blink.

These people do not walk; they migrate along tension gradients, pulling themselves hand-over-hand across the mesh with movements so gradual that an observer might mistake the motion for the trembling of the cables themselves. A single transit across the chamber can occupy an entire lifetime; when elders reach the far wall they simply merge with it, their limbs flattening into reinforcing ribs, their sensory fibers dissolving into the parent tissue until only faint, branching scars remain.

Communication occurs entirely through minute changes in the tension of the web beneath their feet. A tremor passed from one foot to another can travel the diameter of the city in three generations, carrying the entire civic memory along its route. Because information moves at botanical speed, history is experienced as topography: the oldest stories are the tightest cables, vibrating at the highest subsonic pitch; newer memories are slack, lower loops that sag until later generations climb upon them. When a story is forgotten, its cable loosens until it finally drops away, the memory vanishing into the central darkness.

They have no art except the city itself. Every alteration—every splice, every re-tensioning—is both utilitarian and aesthetic, the difference irrelevant when everything proceeds at the speed of lignification. The only ritual is the seasonal re-tuning of the chamber walls, performed when the nutrient pulse finally arrives. Entire districts loosen their grip on older cables, allowing the wave of pressurized gel to pass; the city sings a single, enormous chord that lasts several human lifetimes and can be felt in the bones as warmth. During this interval births are especially numerous, as if the increased pressure encourages extrusion.

Death, when it comes, is indistinguishable from structural failure. A citizen whose fiber can no longer hold tension simply thins, desiccates, and finally parts. The strands remain where they fall, becoming additional footpaths for later generations. In this way the population never declines; it merely redistributes its mass into the architecture, the dead becoming the very medium through which the living continue their imperceptible migration.
