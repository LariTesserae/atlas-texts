The feldspar bowl is not a city so much as a single, breathing organism that has folded an entire ecology into the width of a man’s arms.  

Each lithops and barrel succulent is a distinct citizen whose shape and schedule dictate the rhythms of the whole. The smallest lithops—disks the colour of dried blood—form the dense under-mesh, their translucent leaf-windows tilted toward the hidden vents so the 47 °C glow is diffused into a continuous red twilight. Their roots, only a millimetre long, are woven into a living net that catches every grain of dust the wind slips over the rim; in this way the floor is continuously repaired by the plants that rest upon it.  

Above them, the dwarf barrels act as columns and condensers. Their spines are capillary flutes; at dusk they cool a hair’s breadth faster than the stone, pulling moisture from the trapped air until droplets race downward into the leaf-rosettes below. The barrels also store the communal heat: each thick green globe is a thermal battery, releasing warmth after the vents have dimmed, so the temperature inside never drops enough to stall the nightly germination pulses.  

Sound belongs to the seed-pods. The largest barrels carry the deepest drums—elongated bladders that thrum at roughly two-second intervals, a bass ostinato felt as a slow throb in bone and tooth. Smaller lithops add higher clicks, almost insectile, tuned fractions apart so that the whole chamber resonates in a single, ever-shifting chord. When the pressure vents open, the sudden steam softens these tones for a moment, giving the space a human sigh before the pods reseal and the chord resumes.  

No creature larger than the succulents themselves has ever entered; the low lintel of superheated air above the rim scorches wings and fur alike. The only “fauna” are microscopic—thread-like nematodes coiled inside the spines, feeding on the resin-sweat and, in return, vectoring the minute salts that keep the feldspar slick. Their movement adds a whisper-fine friction that the pods incorporate into the music, turning the nematodes into part of the percussion section.  

There is no waste and therefore no rot. Every shed spine, every flake of dried resin is reabsorbed by the capillary web; salt crystals left by the collapsing steam blisters are lifted by the same spines and redistributed to the rim, thickening the only architecture the garden will ever need. The bowl therefore grows—not upward or outward, but denser, more resonant, the chord more perfectly in tune with itself.  

In this state of permanent resonant noon, time is measured not in hours but in the interval between one pod-pop and the next. Any visitor who crouches within the ring long enough begins to feel their own pulse aligning with that interval, as though the miniature desert is gently retuning the listener to its own metabolism.
