What lives here is the mountain itself—an organism whose organs happen to include rock and water and the air between needles. It has no single skin; instead it is bounded by the momentary agreement of every moss front, every root-mat, every meniscus that chooses not to burst. Inside this enormous body, the basalt stair serves as a slow esophagus, each drum a vertebra that the living tissue flows over and through, polishing it with tannin and reshaping it with root pressure. The stair’s apparent permanence is merely the memory of an older compression event; its future shape is already being negotiated by the acid breath of black-liver moss.

Above the stone, in the forest that functions as the mountain’s circulatory loop, dwell uncountable communities that have long since forgotten the distinction between tenant and landlord. The thumb-sized ungulates are not grazers so much as distributed peristalsis—each herd a traveling pulse that keeps duff and soil in motion. The motionless larger browsers are likewise not animals but mobile fermentation vats; their flank twitches mark the sequential opening of gastric chambers whose microbial consortia perform the same chemistry the moss performs on stone. Even the sheet-web spiders operate less as predators than as structural engineers, their silk adding tensile redundancy to limbs already overloaded with water weight; the violet halos that sometimes appear are only the momentary signature of heat exchange, not a warning.

The true population, however, is the suspended aquifer: an unbroken sheet of living liquid that refuses to fall. Bromeliad cisterns, club-moss velvet, and the protein threads of webs together constitute a single lenticular organ whose volume shifts with every exhalation of warm air. Condensation captured at dusk may be transpired again at dawn, or it may migrate laterally for days until it reaches a trunk whose lenticels admit it into the mountain’s internal plumbing. In this way every vertical meter is also a horizontal watershed; the apparent slope of the land is an illusion maintained by the constant negotiation of surface tension.

Bioluminescent lichen performs the role of a diffuse nervous system, translating mechanical disturbances—needle drop, root growth, the passage of a herd—into slow pulses of foxfire that travel from dusk to dawn. By morning the signal has condensed into isolated islands, preserving only the most persistent stresses; by the following dusk those islands re-expand, overwriting yesterday’s record with today’s. The lichen does not “remember” in any archival sense; it simply keeps yesterday’s shape available until today’s shape is ready to replace it.

Sound is the mountain’s proprioception. The collective hiss of resin ducts is the continuous report of tissue tension; each larval mandible-fall is a micro-fracture event whose energy is immediately reabsorbed by neighboring cells; the papery unfurling of fern croziers registers changes in hydration before any visible wilting occurs. The listener does not perceive discrete events because the mountain does not generate them; instead it produces a single low-pressure waveform whose modulations are perceived by other tissues as instructions for growth or quiescence.

At this elevation the atmosphere itself has been colonized. Warm air trapped beneath the canopy never escapes; it is re-radiated downward by needles and epiphyte foliage, creating a heat lens whose lower boundary sits only centimeters above the duff. The palm that feels cooler at knee height than at shoulder height is registering the boundary layer between two distinct respiratory regimes: one governed by the soil’s perpetual chill, the other by the re-radiated canopy heat. Convection between these layers powers the internal drizzle without any need for sky.

Nothing here reproduces by seed or spore in the usual sense. Reproduction is the continuous re-resolution of boundaries. A new cistern forms when two club-moss strands touch and the meniscus stabilizes; a new “animal” is declared when enough ungulate heartbeats synchronize to shift the duff in a coherent direction; a new “tree” is registered when the collective tick of its resin ducts begins to phase-lock with neighboring trunks. The mountain does not wait for events; every event is already the next adjustment of an organism that finished becoming itself long before the first drum of basalt was set in place.
