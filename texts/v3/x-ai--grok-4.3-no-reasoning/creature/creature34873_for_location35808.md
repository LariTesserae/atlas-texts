This high knife-edge is so stingy with soil that the first colonizers had to arrive as ghosts: wind-lifted pollen, bird-borne seeds, the microscopic frost-flecked spores of lichens. The lichens came first—saffron-orange *Rhizocarpon* and chartreuse *Rhizoplaca*—etching the freshly split faces like watercolor on bone. They secrete oxalic acid that gnaws mineral lattices grain by grain; the etched dust, trapped in hairline cracks, later becomes the cradle for moss. The moss arrived as single threads no thicker than a spider line, riding updrafts until one filament lodged against a south-facing flake. There it multiplied into velvet-black cushions that exhale minute clouds of oxygen each time the sun warms them.

Only where the moss has thickened enough to trap winter snow do the cushion plants follow: tight domes of *Saxifraga oppositifolia* whose magenta flowers open the moment meltwater reaches their crowns, and the iron-hard *Androsace* whose taproots work like stone drills. Between these domes the conifers—prostrate forms of *Pinus mugo* and wind-sculpted *Juniperus*—find footing. Their needles are short, blue-green, resin-sealed against desiccation. In the brief weeks when night temperatures stay above freezing, the mats become nurseries: springtails graze bacterial films, mites hunt the springtails, and frost-resistant tardigrades drink the film of meltwater with telescopic mouthparts.

Larger animals appear only as visitors or temporary tenants. The slate-colored marmots (*Marmota*) maintain a single communal burrow system that threads beneath two adjacent saddles; three bolt-holes open onto wind-sheltered ledges. One marmot is always posted as sentinel, body flattened, ears swiveling like radar dishes. At its alarm whistle the colony pours underground, nails ticking like hail on slate. The marmots fatten on the cushion plants’ fleshy leaves and on the starchy rhizomes of an alpine bistort that grows only in the wind-break’s lee; by August each animal has doubled its weight and begins a torpor that lasts until the next melt.

Raptors cruise the invisible lift generated when sun-warmed rock pushes columns of air skyward. A pair of golden eagles nests in a cleft two ridges over; their fledglings are still downy, but already their shadows sweep the saddles like moving stains. Ptarmigan occasionally explode from behind a boulder, white wings flashing, then vanish into another shadow. The only permanent avian residents are the brown-accentor and the wallcreeper; the accentor’s thin *tsip* is almost ultrasonic, and the wallcreeper’s crimson wing-patches flare like struck matches against gray rock as it spirals in pursuit of freeze-dried insects wedged in cracks.

Below the visible life, the rock itself is still fracturing. Ice-wedging widens every joint a few micrometers each dawn; the grains that spall away rattle downslope in crystalline trickles. Over decades, these trickles feed miniature alluvial fans no larger than doormats in the saddles below. Each fan supports a slightly richer community—ferns, a single dwarf willow—whose roots will eventually pry the next fracture open. The ridge is therefore both fortress and hourglass: nothing arrives that the stone does not eventually grind to powder, yet the powder itself becomes the next beginning.
