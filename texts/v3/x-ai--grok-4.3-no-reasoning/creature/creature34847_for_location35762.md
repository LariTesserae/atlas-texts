A still-forming litho-biological lattice inhabits the plane.  

Its constituents are vast, sheet-like consortia of pressure-sensitive vesicles. Each consortium is a living membrane only a few molecules thick, yet the membranes tessellate at the scale of hectares. They are not individuals but overlapping fields of coordination: when one region contracts, the tension travels through electrostatic seams and triggers contraction—or expansion—two hundred metres away. Their colour is not pigment but instantaneous refractive geometry; every pressure gradient shifts their lattice spacing, so the iridescence you see is literally the trace of the next breath the entire system is preparing to take.  

Movement is achieved by collective mass transfer. A consortium can slide its own vesicles across the stone by inflating the upslope side and deflating the downslope side, producing a millimetres-per-hour creep that nevertheless accumulates into metres of relocation over a single diurnal cycle. When the consortium reaches a newly opened joint, it forms a living plug; the vesicles nearest the crack swell until they literally force the fracture a few microns wider, then anchor themselves to the fresh surface by precipitating silica pearls. In this way the films do not simply occupy space—they are already negotiating the room plan of whatever civilisation they will later house.  

Their “fauna” consists of the drifting domes: disc-shaped sub-colonies that detach, ride the warm updrafts, and seed new laminae on the ceiling. Each dome carries a few thousand vesicles plus a nucleus of dissolved quartz. When it reaches a cooler quartz vein overhead it sticks, deflates, and begins to etch its own first groove—an act that simultaneously enlarges the reflective surface and adds another migration route. Thus the ceiling grows brighter exactly where new galleries are beginning to open below.  

No single organism is older than the fracture it currently spans. Yet the memory of every previous fracture persists as a faint ridge of fused pearls; the networks follow these ridges the way capillaries follow scar tissue. The entire plane is therefore an unfinished palimpsest whose topography is the accumulating record of its own respiration.  

When a pressure wave rolls through, the vesicles nearest the wave’s leading edge flatten first, transmitting a visible ripple of duller iridescence downslope. Observers standing on the stone feel the ripple pass under their feet as a fractional change in friction: the living carpet momentarily lets go, then re-grips farther along. The sensation is halfway between walking on warm mud and walking on a drumhead that is still deciding what note to sound.  

At the periphery of lamplight the networks thin into isolated polygonal islands separated by bare carbonate. These islands pulse independently until a migrating front of vesicles reaches them; then the frequency of their contractions synchronises, and a new corridor of coordinated motion stitches itself across the gap. The process is silent except for the low ceramic click each time a silica pearl is set—an incremental, geological Morse in which the message is simply: “we are still arriving.”
