A thin, pale carpet of life spreads across the basin floor like the nap of a very old velvet, each fiber grown in place rather than moved there. The dominant cushions—call them vela—form shallow domes ten or twelve centimeters across, their broad leaves arranged in overlapping rosettes that tilt ever so slightly toward the next nearest neighbor. Where two domes meet they do not quite touch; a millimeter-wide channel of bare clay remains, just wide enough for the night-migrating slugs to pass without bruising the living edges. Each rosette holds its leaves at an angle that changes with humidity: when the air grows wetter the leaves relax outward, increasing the surface exposed to condensing droplets; when the mist thins they fold inward again, protecting the stomata from the brief, dry wind that sometimes slips under the cloud cap.

Between the vela rise the scale-stalks, tall as a forearm, each one a stack of translucent, parchment-like plates hinged at their bases. The plates are not rigid; they swell and shrink by fractions of a millimeter as they absorb and release moisture, so the whole stalk seems to breathe in slow motion. At the tip of each stalk sits a single, hair-thin filament that remains permanently wet; this filament acts as a wick, drawing condensed water down into the central axis and then outward into the mat of roots below. The roots themselves are not roots in the usual sense but a living felt, pale orange when seen from beneath, that rides the upper surface of the clay lenses. Every few years a lens shrinks or swells enough to crack the felt; the crack is immediately colonized by new growth, so the mat is a palimpsest of healed seams.

The translucent slugs—call them veiltails—move in loose, amoeboid herds. Each individual is no longer than a grain of rice, its body a clear gel threaded with fine, branching vessels that carry a pale yellow fluid. When they graze they leave behind a tracery of emptied condensation films; these films re-form within minutes, but the silver lines remain visible because the surface tension of the new film differs slightly from the old. The slugs themselves are nearly invisible except when they cross a reflective leaf-underside; then their bodies flash for an instant like brief lenses.

Below the mat the thumb-sized isopods—call them shard-borers—maintain an architecture of galleries that shifts with each generation. Their tunnels are not random; each isopod follows chemical gradients left by the previous occupant, reinforcing walls where the clay is weakest and abandoning galleries where the mineral signature has become too saline. Over decades the result is a three-dimensional mosaic of permeability: some layers conduct vapor upward in tight, efficient columns, others hold it longer, releasing it only when surface pressure drops. The shard-borers’ castings accumulate at gallery junctions, forming millimeter-high terraces that further slow the movement of water and create micro-habitats for even smaller organisms—thread-like fungi that specialize in metabolizing the isopods’ nitrogen-rich waste.

Above the entire basin the midges—call them veil-flies—exist as a single, distributed organism whose individual bodies are interchangeable. At any moment some fraction of the cloud is descending to drink from the wet filaments of the scale-stalks; another fraction is ascending on the rising columns of vapor, carrying spores and bacteria between distant cushions. Their motion registers as a continuous scintillation, brightest where the light gradient is steepest, near the edges of the basin where the cloud cap occasionally thins.

The clay lenses themselves are not passive reservoirs. Each lens carries a faint electrical potential generated by the daily cycle of wetting and drying; when vapor columns rise, the potential briefly spikes, and the shard-borers orient their galleries along the resulting field lines. Over centuries this feedback has sorted the sediment into patterns that look almost deliberate: concentric rings of finer clay around each lens, radial spokes of coarser grains between them. The vela cushions follow these rings, their growth arcs aligning with the electrical contours, so the basin floor becomes a map of its own invisible metabolism.

Nothing in the basin is loud, but nothing is silent. The scale-stalks produce a constant, minute creaking as their plates adjust; the slugs leave a soft, wet friction as they glide; the midges’ wings create a subliminal hiss that rises and falls with the vapor. Beneath it all the clay lenses sigh—single, low-frequency releases of pressure that travel through the sediment like the slow pulse of a very large, very old heart. The sound is felt more than heard, a periodic loosening of the entire floor that momentarily lifts the living mat a fraction of a millimeter before it settles again.

At the very center of the basin, where the stone trough is deepest, the felt mat is thinnest. Here the shard-borers have excavated a single, perfectly circular chamber whose walls are lined with a living membrane of specialized vela tissue. The membrane is not photosynthetic; it exists only to sense the electrical field of the lens below and to modulate the flow of vapor into the chamber. When the field strengthens, the membrane contracts; when it weakens, it relaxes. The result is a slow, rhythmic exchange between the deepest water and the surface air—an exchange so measured that a human observer might watch for a lifetime without noticing any change at all.

The basin holds its weather the way a lung holds breath: not by containing it, but by letting it move through in patterns too slow to see, too consistent to escape. Everything that lives here has learned to inhabit that movement, to become part of the same slow respiration that turns stone into vapor and vapor back into the living carpet that covers the floor.
