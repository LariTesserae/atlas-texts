In this sealed hollow there is a community that no longer moves forward or backward; it simply maintains. What lives here has stopped thinking in generations and begun thinking in terms of equilibrium, in the slow redistribution of mist, light, and pressure.

There are the substrate-binders: colonies of filament-thin mosses whose holdfasts have fused into a single sheet beneath the root-felt, no longer individual plants but a continuous skin whose outer cells are photosynthetic and whose inner cells trade oxygen for nitrogen in a closed loop. They taste the mist as it falls, release a molecule or two when it is too rich, draw a molecule back when it thins, never letting the balance slip more than a single percent. Their work is audible as an almost imperceptible background tick, like the cooling of warm glass.

Farther up, on the vertical wood-walls that have long since lost the distinction between xylem and bark, dwell the leaf-sculptors: flat, branching colonies of liverworts that have rearranged themselves into millimeter-thick tiles, each tile precisely the shape of the curve it coats. Their edges interlock like jigsaw pieces; when the entire wall inhales or exhales, the tiles flex together without seam or gap. At dusk—though dusk never quite arrives—they darken one shade and lighten again when the silver light returns, maintaining the surface temperature of the wood at a constant few degrees above ambient.

Drifting in the upper third of the mist are the veil-spinners: arachnids no larger than eyelashes whose webs are not traps but atmospheric condensers. Each thread is strung with a repeating sequence of microscopic water-traps—bulb-shaped nodules that swell and contract in a rhythm measured in heartbeats per hour. When a droplet forms inside a nodule, the spider tastes it, decides whether the dissolved minerals are within tolerance, and either drinks or releases the droplet back into the circulation. Their webs form a loose, three-dimensional net that fills the hollow like an inverted thunderhead; the combined movement of ten thousand tiny valves produces the soft page-turning sound visitors mistake for the xylem turbines, though it is only the last audible residue of their collective decision-making.

Occupying the depressions in the floor are the glass-eels: translucent, jawless vertebrates whose skins are permeable to both water and light. They lie curved within each basin, gills folded shut, bodies rising and falling with the level of the liquid as though breathing with it. Their blood is clear; only a faint bronze shimmer marks the path of oxygen from skin to spine. They do not feed; instead they exchange trace ions with the water, maintaining its precise salinity so that the root-felt beneath continues to accept moisture at the correct rate. If the salinity drifts by more than a tenth of a percent, the nearest eel will twitch—once—and the basin’s chemistry resets.

On the ragged upper rim, where foliage still touches open sky, live the edge-keepers: slow, lichen-like symbioses of fungus and alga that have formed a living gasket between the hollow and whatever weather still exists beyond. They tolerate rain when it arrives, reject hail by secreting a temporary hydrophobic film, and, during rare winds, tighten their hyphal mesh so the skylight contracts by a finger’s width. Their presence guarantees that the silver light inside never yellows or reddens; it remains the same cool tone whether the season outside is summer or mid-winter.

Nothing here is born in the usual sense. When a salamander’s drifting slows to absolute stillness, neighboring individuals secrete a thin envelope of gel around it; within weeks the gel hardens into a transparent sarcophagus that the root-felt quietly absorbs, returning every atom to the mist. When a veil-spinner’s thread breaks, the neighboring spiders splice a new filament into place within hours; the newcomer does not replace the lost individual but continues its exact tension and droplet rhythm. In this way the hollow replaces loss without introducing novelty.

The collective effect is a population that is numerically stable yet biologically ancient: individuals are exchanged, but the functions they perform have not varied in so long that the memory of variation itself has been dissolved. The only evidence of time is the incremental thickening of the xylem turbines, each growth-ring now indistinguishable from the next, adding width without altering speed or pitch. The sound therefore deepens by microns per century, a change no ear can register but one the root-felt senses as a gradual increase in pressure, which it compensates for by releasing one additional molecule of water into each basin per decade.

Thus the inhabitants persist not by resisting change but by rendering change imperceptible, until the entire hollow reads, from inside or out, as a single finished thing whose only project is to remain exactly as it is.
