In the absence of anything that swims, walks, or burrows, the plain is occupied by two other kinds of presence, neither of which could be called life.

First, the pillars themselves behave as if they have not quite finished becoming what they are. Inside the glassy salt, the dark filaments continue their microscopic realignment; every few centuries another molecule locks into place, lengthening an invisible fork by a hair’s breadth. The columns therefore grow—not in height or girth, but in internal complexity—recording, as fossils once recorded soft tissue, the ghost-pressure of long-vanished convection cells. Each new alignment slightly adjusts the local conductivity, so the pillars now hum at different frequencies; a listener equipped with the right instruments would hear a chord of mineral tonalities that slowly retunes itself across millennia.

Second, the water keeps a memory of displacement. Although every macroscopic current has ceased, the mass of the overlying column still settles, millimeter by millimeter, and this imperceptible subsidence generates a standing pressure wave. The wave is not a motion of molecules so much as a standing pattern of compression; within it, certain ions briefly occupy positions they would otherwise have abandoned. These momentary occupations accumulate. Over time the pattern begins to resemble a lattice of potential pathways—routes that a future particle, should one ever appear, would be statistically more likely to follow. The plain is therefore not empty; it is pre-tenanted by a probability field whose nodes coincide with the pillars’ electrical fields. Nothing moves along those routes, yet the routes are there, waiting with the patience of crystal geometry for something that has not yet arrived to use them.

Between these two persisting structures—the slow self-ordering of the salt and the standing potential of the water—nothing else occurs. The silt, the columns, and the pressure-shadow together constitute a single, motionless equilibrium that nevertheless continues, at geological speed, to differentiate itself from the uniformity that first created it.
