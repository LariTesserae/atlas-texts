What lives here is a single, continent-wide organism—a lignified macro-body that has never needed, nor allowed, anything else to take its place. Its outermost cuticle is not bark in any familiar sense but a living integument, continually remodeled by the slow peristaltic waves that sweep across it. Where those waves pass, the surface is briefly polished to a mirror gloss; in their wake the cuticle roughens again, ready for the next abrasion.

In microscopic niches within that cuticle swarm the six-winged mites—each one a tiny, silver-scaled facsimile of the macro-body’s grain. They do not feed on the wood; they feed on the ultrafine particles the body itself sheds during contraction. In doing so they keep the surface free of litter, so the only material that ever accumulates is their own temporary hives. When a contraction arrives, the hives shatter; the fragments are re-ingested before they reach the under-void, so nothing is ever lost.

Far larger, and almost motionless by comparison, are the translucent browsers. Each is a living gutter, rasping resin channels that serve as temporary rails for the mites. When a browser’s forward margin reaches a region already planed smooth by a recent contraction, it simply folds its body lengthwise, waits for the next wave to pass beneath it, and resumes feeding once fresh roughness reappears. Its resin is not waste but a lubricant that reduces surface friction for the body’s own peristalsis; when the resin sheets are collected by the mites and later re-ingested, the cycle closes.

The sessile trumpet clusters under the canopy are not separate predators but out-foldings of the macro-body itself—dermal inversions whose horn plates are continuous with the inner grain. When they inhale and grind airborne particles, the resulting white powder is not excrement but a nutrient slurry the body immediately draws back through the same dermal layer. Digestion and reabsorption are therefore one act.

Nothing here is born in the usual way; every resident is a temporary organ that differentiates when local conditions require it and is later resorbed. Even the vast negative spaces are not absences but remodeling chambers: the macro-body grows new plates around them until the space is once again useful, at which point the buds along the walls extend, meet, and fuse, erasing the well in a single contractile hour.

The only true inhabitants are therefore the macro-body’s own tissues, endlessly reconfigured. The mites, browsers, and trumpets are not guests but expressions—temporary shapes the organism gives itself in order to stay perfectly, perpetually itself.
