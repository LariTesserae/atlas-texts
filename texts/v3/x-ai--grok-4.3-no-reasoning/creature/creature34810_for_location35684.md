A world that thinks in sheets.

Its body is one continuous skin—veins woven out of leaves, membranes as flexible as cartilage and as taut as sailcloth. Every pool, every path, every living bridge is part of the same slow-moving organism. What we call “plants” are only the surface expression of a single, respiring volume whose interior is both architecture and climate. Beneath the translucent tissue run tubes the width of a wrist; every few hours they contract with the patient rhythm of a gut, pushing water from the upper catch-basins to the lower ones. The air inside these tunnels is warmer than the void by just enough degrees to keep the water from freezing twice.

The ribbon-gliders are not animals in the usual sense; they are motile organs of the same body. Each translucent wing bears parallel ridges that lock, when pressed against a membrane, into the vein pattern like a key into its wards. Their landings are fertilizations: a brief chemical handshake, a transfer of molecules that the plant-membrane registers as orders to grow a new drip-lip or to thicken a load-bearing ridge. After the glider peels away again, the membrane flushes emerald for half a minute, sending a pulse down the entire chamber like a memory of impact.

The thread-worms and the skating arthropods belong to the parasite guild—organisms that have learned to live inside living architecture without tearing it. The worms follow condensate gradients the way salmon follow currents; the arthropods’ crackling legs are vibrational triggers that tell distant sheets when to tense or relax, an inadvertent maintenance crew. When one cluster accelerates, the tremor races outward in concentric ripples; minutes later another cluster answers from a distant wall, keeping the sheet surfaces in slight, ceaseless motion so that micro-fractures never accumulate.

Human-scale traces are absorbed rather than removed. The obsidian blades now lie within green, fibrous sarcophagi; the wooden hafts have been replaced cell-by-cell with the same tissue that grows the drip-pools. The pale-stone post, set into a cleft before the canopy arrived, now wears a collar of living membrane. At intervals the collar tightens, lifts, settles again—an almost imperceptible peristalsis that grinds the stone thinner by microns each year. The concentric carvings are being overwritten: where a shallow ring once meant a human tally, there grows instead a raised vein that registers sap pressure. In a few more centuries only the axis of the post will remain, a mineral spine inside the organism’s own slow digestion.

Light in the upper reaches is the colour of sun through glacier ice. Deeper, the bioluminescent seams flare every quarter-hour in a travelling wave that moves from the newest cleft to the oldest sealed wound, marking safe corridors for the gliders and for the worms. Between pulses the dark is perfect. Sound, however, never stops; every droplet impact, every glider strike, every ice-filament snap is conducted along living tethers until the whole volume sings at a frequency just below human hearing. It sounds like blood moving under a membrane—close, oceanic, constant.

The world is still forming. Sharp stone ridges continue to rise, slicing new vents through the canopy; the plant responds with clear sap that hardens first to gel, then to amber, then to new leaf. Until those wounds close, cold air pours downward in threads, seeding new vortices that lift fragments of older tissue into the upper light. Each fragment, caught on a membrane, roots within hours. The organism grows by eating its own weather.
