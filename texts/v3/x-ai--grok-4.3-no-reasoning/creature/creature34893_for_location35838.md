Here, where the air is water and the light is perpetual dusk, live the people called the Rain-bearers.

They are a small clan of perhaps thirty adults and a dozen children, their lineages traceable through one another in spirals that match the settlement itself. Each person’s name begins with the syllable that names the sound a particular roof makes when its pan fills: Mehl, for the bright note; Korr, for the deeper, throatier tone; Shash, for the soft, almost metallic whisper of an almost-empty pan. Children are not given names until they can sit beneath a chosen roof through an entire night of changing weather and name the shift in pitch themselves. Some never do; those who remain nameless are not excluded, but they are not asked to build their own chambers either. They drift among the occupied roofs, bringing wood or carrying stones, welcome but never quite belonging.

Their bodies are adapted in quiet ways. Their feet are broad and flat, the skin on their soles so thickened by years of wet stone that it feels almost like the moss itself. They move without sound, stepping carefully so as not to disturb the salamanders. Their eyes are wide-set and pale, the irises the same blue-green as the moss; they see better in the fog than in the rare moments when sunlight burns through. They do not speak loudly. When they do speak, it is often not to each other but to the trees, to the ravens, or to the water in the pans. A whispered greeting to the stone as one passes is considered ordinary courtesy; a loud voice is considered rude to the air itself.

They live almost entirely inside the single chambers. Each chamber contains one adult and, sometimes, that adult’s apprentice. Children are raised communally among the ledges, moving from one chamber to another as the mood or season takes them. No one is considered a parent; everyone is considered a steward. When a child becomes restless enough to want their own chamber, they begin to carry stones. No one helps; the act of gathering and placing the first flat rock is itself the petition. If the rocks remain where they are left, the child is considered accepted by the slope. If the stones are moved—by frost, by root, by rain—the petition is considered denied for another year.

Their diet is gathered rather than grown. They eat the pale salamanders, the slow beetles, the occasional fish that appears in the cupped depressions after heavy rain. They collect the needles of the great trees and steep them into a bitter tea that keeps the cold from settling into the bones. They do not hunt the ravens. The ravens are considered older than the people, and older than the trees. When a raven lands on a roof, the occupant inside will sometimes place a small offering of needles or a sliver of wet bark on the threshold stone. The raven rarely takes it; the gesture is enough.

They have no written language. Instead, they keep memory in the placement of the pans. Each pan’s position relative to its roof records a story: a birth, a death, a migration of stone. When a person dies, their pan is moved one hand’s width to the left. Over decades the pans drift around the roofs like slow constellations. When the pans threaten to crowd one another, a new chamber is begun. The oldest person alive is the one whose pan has traveled the farthest. No one knows how old that person is, only how many times the pan has been shifted.

They do not fear solitude. Each chamber is a deliberate act of aloneness, and the fog makes certain that aloneness is never complete. The sound of water travels farther than voices; a single drip from a neighbor’s roof can be heard inside one’s own chamber as clearly as if the water were falling within arm’s reach. The people have learned to interpret these sounds the way other cultures interpret conversation. A faster drip means someone is moving stone. A slower drip means someone is resting. When the drip stops entirely, someone has died. The body is not buried; it is left on its own ledge with its pan emptied and turned upside down. Within a season the fog and the roots have claimed what the person left behind.

The trees, too, are inhabited. Not by people, but by something quieter. The villagers speak of “the listening” that lives inside the largest trunks. When the wind moves the high branches, a low, resonant tone travels down the trunk and into the stone. The villagers can feel this tone in their feet. They say the listening is older than the mountain itself, that it is the reason the stone has not yet agreed to settle. They do not worship it. They simply acknowledge its presence by leaving small offerings of wet bark at the base of each trunk, the same way they acknowledge the ravens.

The salamanders are not considered animals in the ordinary sense. They are seen as extensions of the water itself—temporary shapes the water takes when it needs to move across stone. The villagers never step on them. When a salamander enters a chamber, the occupant will sit motionless until it leaves. The salamanders are considered messengers between the chambers and the deeper fissures in the rock. No one knows what message they carry, but their presence or absence is noted and remembered in the placement of the pans.

The ravens are different. They are considered visitors from somewhere else—places where the fog does not reach, places where the sun still knows how to cast shadows. The villagers watch them carefully. A raven that lands on a roof and stays longer than a single croak is considered to be carrying news. The news is never spoken aloud; it is understood in the way the raven tilts its head or the way its talons grip the wet cedar. When the raven leaves, the occupant of the chamber will sometimes move their pan one finger’s width to the right. This is the only form of writing the villagers practice.

The settlement has no leader and no council. Decisions are made by the slope itself. When a chamber begins to lean, when a roof starts to fail, when a tree’s roots push a stone too far, the people read these signs the way other cultures read omens. If the slope decides against a chamber, the occupant will quietly begin carrying stones to a new location. No one is asked to leave; the stone simply makes the decision clear.

The children who never receive names are considered to be in conversation with the listening inside the trees. They are the ones who can stand beneath a trunk for an entire day without moving, their eyes half-closed, their breathing synchronized with the slow drip of water from the lowest branches. These children are not pitied. They are considered to be the settlement’s memory of its own future—already becoming part of the mountain rather than living upon it.

The Rain-bearers do not expect to remain here forever. They know the slope will continue to shift, the soil will continue to thicken, the trees will continue to multiply. One day the ledges will become too gentle for the single chambers; one day the fog will lift high enough for other plants to grow between the trunks. When that day comes, the people will carry their pans to a new place and begin again. They do not speak of this future. They simply listen to the water and wait for the stone to tell them when it is time to move.
