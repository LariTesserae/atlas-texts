This place resists straightforward occupation by any single species. Instead, it hosts a slow, persistent relationship between the rock, the water, and a range of minor, patient inhabitants that have learned to treat the constant seep and fog as habitat rather than obstacle.

The oldest of these are colonial growths—sheets of translucent, mineral-trapping microbes that bind the cracks into low, stony terraces and seal the undersides of the worked stones. These mats do not build outward or upward; they simply thicken where the seepage lingers longest, turning every depression into a living filter whose colors shift between rust, sulfur yellow, and watery gray as the dissolved ions change with the light.

Smaller still, and just as essential, are the communities of hair-thin fungal hyphae that reach upward through the mist itself. Their fruiting bodies are not mushrooms but tiny, glass-clear spheres that form only when the fog grows thick enough to carry their spores. Each sphere waits for the next downdraft, then cracks open to dust the surface with fresh filaments before the fog seals again.

The shrimp-like swimmers—known locally, when they are known at all, as “salt-thumbs”—live their entire lives in the ephemeral lenses. Because the water level changes with every slow pulse of mist and seepage, the shrimp have developed a simple buoyancy trick: tiny internal gas vesicles that let them float just under the surface film, riding the pressure waves that move between the rocks. When the film dries to a skin, they tuck themselves into the remaining moisture beneath the stones, entering a reversible torpor measured in hours rather than seasons.

Above them, the six-winged flyers are not a single species but a shifting guild of near-identical membrane fliers. Their wings are not rigid; each beat sends a ripple through the wet parchment, and the collective effect is more like a single, ragged sheet of moving vapor than a flock of individuals. When the downdrafts open lanes through the mist, the sheets contract into dense, low orbits over the translucent pools; when the fog returns, they loosen again into the drifting sheets whose sound is constant.

The salamander-like vertebrates remain almost entirely unseen. Their bodies are translucent except for a single, dark lateral line that registers vibration; they move in slow, sinusoidal waves just beneath the saturated surface. Their only visible sign is the occasional sudden lifting of the flyer sheets when a larger individual passes close enough to the surface film to disturb it. They feed on the shrimp and on the microbial mats they dislodge while swimming; their waste, in turn, feeds the same mats, completing a loop whose scale is measured in square meters and whose duration is measured in the slow recession of the stone.

Among all these lives, the worked stones function as both shelter and archive. The low circles trap moisture and therefore trap the microbial mats, turning each circle into a slightly darker, slightly greener micro-patch than the surrounding flats. The adzes and the split logs lie half-buried because the mats and the constant dissolution have grown around them rather than over them; the stone and the wood have become part of the same slow redistribution of material.

No larger, warm-blooded creature finds enough concentrated food here to stay for long. The flyers and the shrimp are too small, too diffuse; the salamanders are too deep and too slow. Yet the constant sound and the constant vibration give the impression of density. The land feels inhabited because every square meter is doing something—filtering, floating, vibrating, dissolving—and because the things that do these small tasks never stop, even when the larger world has already finished its work.
