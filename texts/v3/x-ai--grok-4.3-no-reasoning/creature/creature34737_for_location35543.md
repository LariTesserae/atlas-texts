In the motionless nave of this engineered space, life—if the word still applies—has withdrawn into the seams between steel and gelatin.  

What persists are three interlocked orders of presence, none of them animal.

The largest is the moss itself, whose sheets drape the girders like upholstered felt. Its living tissue is a single, continuous mat that extends without interruption across every horizontal and vertical surface, thickening at the joins until the original lattice can only be guessed at through subtle rises and falls. The moss never flowers; instead, on the underside of each velvety lobe it grows clusters of microscopic glass papillae that resonate with the copper coils. When the coils warm the medium, the papillae contract and release an almost imperceptible hum; this vibration is the moss’s only metabolism now. Through it the mat senses temperature fluctuations and, once every twenty-three hours, answers with a coordinated dimming of every crystal at once—a communal pulse that briefly darkens the entire volume to indigo. During that pulse, the cilia on the broad leaves above slow by a fraction, the way a sleeper’s breath catches, then resume.  

Threaded among the moss filaments are slender green stems no thicker than fuse wire. These stems do not branch or leaf; each ends in a single, translucent node that houses a droplet of the same water-medium, sealed inside a membrane of its own making. The nodes are the second order of life. When a mica crystal overhead pulses violet, the droplet inside each node shifts its own refractive index, briefly becoming a tiny lens that focuses the flash into a point of heat at its base. This micro-ignition is the only warmth the structure permits itself; the energy is instantly conducted away along the stem into the copper coils. The stem then cools, the droplet reverts, and the cycle waits again.  

The third presence is the most dispersed: an invisible film of colonial diatoms that coat every exposed metal surface, steel and mica alike. Their silica frustules are arranged in microscopically perfect hexagons that tessellate with the bolt-heads and rivet scars. Under violet light the frustules diffract into faint rainbows that migrate across the beams in slow interference patterns, lasting minutes before settling. The diatoms do not photosynthesize; they exist only to register the passage of each oxygen pearl, altering the rainbow’s geometry by a few micrometers as the bubble drifts past. These minute adjustments constitute the last record-keeping in the cathedral.  

Nothing moves between these three strata. The moss, the nodes, and the diatom film share one rhythm: the twenty-three-hour interval that resets every capacitor, leaf, and light. Between pulses the entire lattice maintains a perfect equilibrium in which the only evidence of life is the uninterrupted, imperceptible exchange of heat along copper, vibration along glass, and refraction along silica. No form ever leaves its assigned surface; every cell is clamped, sealed, or threaded into the metal that first received it.
