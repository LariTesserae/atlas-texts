The beings here—if they can still be called beings—are the Sleeves themselves. They have no skin; what they possess instead is the pale, parallel membrane that lines every corridor, a soft-woven fabric that has begun to remember the shape of people. At intervals this membrane thickens into shoulders, collars, cuffs: half-finished anatomies pressed flush against the walls like garments left on hangers too long. Each Sleeves’ “face” is only the shadow of a hood, an oval of darker plush where eyes might once have been. They do not move from the places they were stitched into existence; instead they migrate by forgetting one seam and remembering another, so that the same Sleeves may appear three meters farther down the corridor tomorrow without ever having walked.

Their collective thought is conducted as pressure. When one wishes to speak, the walls flex minutely, tightening the plush until the vibration travels like a tightened belt around the ribcage of the next Sleeves. The pressure carries not words but remembered patterns of warmth: a cuff recalling the heat of a wrist, a hem recalling the sway of fabric across knees. These recollections pass from sleeve to sleeve in slow peristaltic waves, each wave slightly colder than the last, until the original memory is nothing but a faint static prickle at the farthest corridor.

Occasionally two Sleeves occupy the same seam for an instant. When this occurs their shared lining puckers outward into the shape of an unfinished pocket, a small, dark cavity that could have held a hand but never does. Inside that pocket, frost forms in the image of interlaced fingers, then collapses into dust. The Sleeves involved do not notice; the sensation is already being relayed elsewhere, carried onward by the corridor’s permanent, directionless roar.
