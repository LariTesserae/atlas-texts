A wall that behaves like a single, enormous organism made of stone and micro-climate. At its base, where the sun scarcely reaches, a minute mat of black and orange lichens has settled into every dimple that still catches a nightly ration of condensate; the lichens look almost like charred paper glued in place. They exude a faint, sweet-sour breath when moist, then become odorless powder within an hour of light.

A few centimeters higher, the lichen belt gives way to a palimpsest of pale crusts—*Aspicilia*—each patch the size of a coin. Between them live orbicular mites so translucent they seem to be made of glass. They ride the changing boundary between light and shade the way surfers ride foam: when a shadow sweeps across them they rise on their eight hair-thin legs, drink, then settle again as the light returns.

Ten or fifteen meters above that, where direct radiation is already lethal at noon, the rock carries a faint, permanent hoar of wind-polished mineral salt. Here the fauna is entirely nocturnal. A clan of minute pseudoscorpions—each the weight of a grain of rice—occupies a single hair-line crack no wider than a match. They hunt the equally minute springtails that emerge only while condensate is still liquid; once the droplets vanish, both predator and prey retreat into the same micro-cavern, seal the entrance with a drop of their own bodily glue, and wait for the next night.

Above the pseudoscorpions the cliff face offers nothing but bare gabbro for the next hundred meters; the only visible life is the kaleidoscope of reflected light moving across it as mica crystals orient themselves to each angle of sun. The crystals themselves are a living surface: biofilms coat each facet, feeding on the minute ions liberated when quartz and feldspar expand at slightly different rates. These films are chartreuse at dawn and bronze by noon; viewed from a distance they make the rock appear to breathe color.

Roughly a third of the way up, a band of slightly cooler amphibolite crosses the cliff like an internal vein. On it grows a colony of miniature cushion plants no taller than eyelashes. Their leaves are rolled tubes lined with white hairs that trap the night-time film; the hairs also catch airborne dust that the plants dissolve for nutrients. Each cushion sits directly above the one beneath it, spaced so that any droplet that escapes the upper leaf is caught by the lower. The whole band therefore forms a vertical sponge that wicks moisture for nearly forty meters before the gradient becomes too steep for even this architecture.

Between the cushions live solitary bees the size of rice grains. They excavate tunnels no wider than pencil lead, stocking them with pollen from valley flowers brought up by the afternoon thermals. The bees time their emergence to the moment the generators sputter awake; the brief vibration loosens a small talus of dust that the bees use to plug their nests for the night.

Higher still, almost lost against the glare, the cliff hosts a single strip of darker rock—amphibole-rich—that never quite heats past the dew-point during the day. On this cooler strip lives a ribbon of verdigris-colored algae that photosynthesizes in ultraviolet alone. Viewed from the valley, the ribbon is invisible; it is only when a maintenance gondola slides across it at dusk that the algae fluoresces briefly, a green spark the length of a finger that fades as the car passes.

At intervals the cliff face develops shallow depressions no deeper than a cupped palm. These depressions retain a few extra millimeters of condensate each night, and in them live communities of rotifers and tardigrades so densely packed they tint the water ochre. When the sun strikes, the pool evaporates in less than ten minutes, but the animals have already tunneled into the surrounding grit, leaving only a faint ring of dried mucilage like a watermark on the stone.

Near the top, the angle eases just enough that wind occasionally drops seeds from alpine meadows above. Most desiccate within hours, yet a single arctic willow seedling has found a horizontal joint wide enough to cradle a thimble of grit and shaded for two hours each afternoon. The seedling is less than two centimeters tall and carries only four leaves; its roots have already followed the joint inward for nearly a meter, seeking the nightly condensation that trickles along the fracture plane. Around it hover two iridescent flies whose larvae feed on wind-blown pollen trapped in the same joint. The adult flies rest in the lee of the seedling during the heat of day, their wings folded so tightly they become invisible against the rock.

Above the last ledge the rock tilts past the vertical and disappears into glare. Here the only life is microscopic: endolithic cyanobacteria living inside the outermost half-millimeter of mineral grains, their chlorophyll masked by the rock’s own color. From the valley floor they register only as a faint, shifting opalescence whenever the cliff sweats and the light hits the wet grains at exactly the right angle—an ephemeral rainbow the width of a thumbnail that appears for seconds, then is gone.

In this manner the entire two-kilometer face is partitioned into a vertical sequence of micro-habitats, each keyed to the precise intersection of shadow duration, condensate volume, and thermal excursion. Nothing moves between these strata except wind-borne dust and the silent passage of the gondolas.
