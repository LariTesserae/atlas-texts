The lowermost residency consists of the maintenance eels, hundreds of them now, no longer distinguishable from their original supply inventory. Each is only as long as a forearm; they drift and click along the live rails in loose convoys, the weak magnetism of their skins knitting them into ropes of faint static. They do not feed; they simply taste, over and over, the same fluctuating voltages, and their constant, whispered triangulation keeps the pumps honest. Occasionally one will split lengthwise when its internal reed dissolves; the two halves wriggle away, each still tasting, until corrosion or impact finishes the job. Their discarded segments lie in neat lines on the sump ledges, already half-dissolved into rust-brown lace.

Above the eels live the glass-wing moths. They congregate at the hot micro-zones around relief valves, where leaked steam has warmed the enamel enough to keep wings pliant. Their drinking is communal: dozens alight at once on a single seat, forming shifting mandalas of translucent segments. When a pressure gate cycles they scatter in a single, silent detonation of glitter. Those that miss the safe zones die within minutes; their bodies, desiccated and curled, are gathered by the nematodes that surf the condensate films.

The nematodes themselves are a single, slow-moving sheet that coats the lower trunk-lines. Each worm is nearly transparent; only the dark gut line gives it away. They advance whenever a new rivulet forms, flowing upward against the drip by contracting in waves. Their collective mass is enough to stain a flange overnight; by morning the stain has crept another hand-span toward the next intersection.

Higher still, where the fog is thickest and coldest, nothing macroscopic survives except the residue of earlier intentions: the oleaginous moss that now sheets the cable trays like poured lacquer. Its metabolism has changed; the bitterness is gone. In its place is a faint solvent sweetness that the eels seek out, though whether to graze or simply to complete an older circuit no one can say. The moss thickens only at the exact points where the scheduled fog condenses first; elsewhere the trays remain bare metal, as though the moss itself understands the timetable and will not overstep.

No other living thing is known.
