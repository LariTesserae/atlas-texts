In this place, nothing has ever been born in the way animals understand birth; everything arrives by continuous becoming.  

A single cell somewhere in the outermost cambium is always dividing, always deciding whether to become new lumen or new bark, whether to lengthen or thicken, whether to turn left along the grain or simply swell until the passage beside it disappears. Each new cell inherits the memory of every previous cell, stored not in nucleus alone but in the subtle tensions of the cell-wall itself. Over generations the memory has become architecture: the angle at which one trunk must meet another, the exact pressure at which steam is allowed to escape, the precise moment when a valve should click shut. The city therefore contains no architects, only the slow consensus of living wood remembering how to be a city.

The first inhabitants were not residents but editors. Small symbiotic mycelia threaded themselves into the earliest shoots, secreting enzymes that dissolved lignin along predetermined planes and allowed the shoots to fuse laterally instead of branching outward. Later, larger fungi took up residence inside the fused platforms, their hyphae forming living valves and pumps that the wood itself could not evolve. Over time the two kingdoms stopped being distinguishable; the wood now grows around fungal tissue as naturally as around its own heartwood. What walks the galleries today is not the descendant of those original fungi, but something that has read their genetic instructions so often that it can improvise new ones.

The arthropods you hear are not individuals but temporary expressions of a single distributed metabolism. Each translucent exoskeleton is grown on demand by the root felt beneath it; when the animal’s work—whether harvesting condensate minerals or carrying a packet of spores—is finished, the felt reabsorbs the body and recycles its chitin into the next useful shape. The moss-furred drifters are more autonomous yet still dependent: their internal bladders are inflated by the same pressurized steam that the city exhales, and they can only “fly” along the grain because any attempt to cross it triggers an autonomic narrowing that traps them until they starve or are reabsorbed.

There are no names here, because naming implies separation. Instead there are temperatures, pressures, and rates of flow that the entire mass monitors continuously. When a new gallery begins to knit itself a hundred body-lengths away, the nearest valve feels a fractional drop in steam pressure and widens its aperture by three cell-widths; the change propagates through the living pipes faster than any messenger could run. The metallic clack you hear is the sound of that consensus being enforced: a single cell thickens its wall until it becomes rigid, the rigidity trips a neighboring cell, and so on until the entire trunk contracts. The echo dies quickly because the wood is already listening for the next instruction.

Nothing here sleeps, yet everything rests. The resting is measured in the slow, peristaltic squeeze that occurs once per metabolic day; during those few seconds every trunk exhales at once, every valve locks, every droplet pauses in its capillary climb. For that instant the city is perfectly still, perfectly dark, perfectly cool. Then the valves reopen, the sap rises again, and the respiring timber remembers that it is still becoming a city that has not yet finished becoming itself.
