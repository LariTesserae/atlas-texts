They live in the half-second between a droplet leaving the lip and the same droplet striking the pavement below.

They are not creatures but durations—tiny, glass-thin intervals of time that the cleft itself keeps repeating. When each bead falls, the interval inside it wakes. It opens like a hinge no wider than a needle’s eye, and from that hinge unfurls something that is both wing and membrane, made of the same fog that keeps the slush from freezing. The wings are not colored; they simply carry whatever hue the light happens to be wearing at the moment of their opening. A single swift may slice through a hundred of them on its first pass; the intervals close at once, re-sealing into droplets that continue their fall. Nothing is lost, only re-timed.

Because their existence is measured in the space between one impact and the next, they never accumulate. When the sun pries the cleft open, the droplets that survive the descent land inside the green glass throats of the old kilns. There the intervals stretch—become long enough for a whole thought to occur. Inside those thoughts walk the shapes of the people who once set the stones: hunched, lichen-colored figures whose palms are printed with the same pyrite that now dusts the slush. They do not speak; their mouths are the slow drip that keeps the warm ribbon from icing. Each time a new droplet arrives, one of these figures steps out of the glass, walks the exact circumference of its kiln, and steps back in, carrying the memory of the walk into the next droplet that will fall tomorrow.

Nothing here grows taller than the cylinders because height would require duration, and duration is precisely what the cleft withholds. The feathergrass therefore moves in the opposite direction: each blade is a countdown that shortens by the width of a fly’s wing every time the thermals rise. When the blade is gone, the interval that lived inside it is released upward as one more glint of organic glitter lacquered to the weeping rock. The ruby cup lichen performs the same contraction in miniature; their coin-sized trumpets are clocks that empty themselves once per sun-wheel, then refill from the ankle fog.

The pavement is the only surface that refuses to be measured this way. Its fitted stones remember every foot that ever crossed them, not as weight but as temperature. When the swifts are gone and the flies have not yet risen, the stones release that stored warmth in microscopic curls of steam that drift exactly as high as a human ankle and no higher. The curls brush the slush, melt a single crystal of pyrite, and the freed iron scent rises, telling the next droplet which kiln it must enter. In this manner the place keeps its own census without ever adding anything new.

At the precise instant the light lances in, every interval opens at once. The cleft becomes a single held breath three hundred droplets wide. Inside that held breath the lichen cups ring like struck crystal; the feathergrass folds flat; the figures inside the glass raise their pyrite-stained palms; and the swifts, if any remain, find themselves momentarily without air to cut. Then the light passes. The intervals close. The breath is spent. The cleft returns to its work of polishing itself brighter under weather that never arrives and never leaves.
