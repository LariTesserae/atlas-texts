It does not have an “inside” or an “outside”; it is the space, and every route you might call a corridor is merely a temporary lumen kept patent by living pressure. Where two thick roots have fused side-by-side, their shared cambium has flattened into a single plane so continuous that the seam is felt only as a faint ridge of higher temperature. Traverse far enough and that ridge itself begins to bead with nectar; moisture that never escapes the cuticle is instead wicked forward by micro-capillaries so that the film always moves ahead of you, an invisible meniscus reading the shape of your passage.

If you stand still long enough the fibers beneath your feet readjust. First there is only an almost inaudible creak—like green wood taking weight—then the surface tilts, centimeter by slow centimeter, until the slope is sufficient to roll any loose starch-brick or condensed sugar droplet away. The adjustment is not a decision; it is the architecture finding equilibrium again after your added mass locally warmed the xylem and accelerated starch oxidation. The heat gradient is tiny, yet it is enough: vessels dilate a fraction, turgor changes, and the floor re-contours itself in the same motion a leaf folds along its midrib.

Above, the woven-vine ceiling is not so much “hanging” as it is cantilevered from every wall at once. The vines thicken wherever their own shadows fall, because the lower epidermis, pressed against the void, has become photosynthetic on both faces. Where two strands cross, they do not merely touch; their cuticles dissolve and merge into a shared periderm that later hardens into a translucent window, milky with raphides. Through that pane the continuous fluorescence of deeper chlorenchyma is visible as a slow scintillation, each flash marking the passage of a sucrose pulse on its way to a distant extrusion chamber.

Now and then a distant pump-organ changes rhythm; the shift registers as a minute drop in pressure felt first in the sinuses, then as a faint outward sigh from every surface pore. The sigh carries a smell that is not quite sweet and not quite resinous—more the scent of split green wood warmed in sunlight that never arrives. The vapor drifts through the same microvascular network that moments earlier accepted your own body heat, so that the two thermals braid together briefly before the architecture dissipates them into the larger convection cell that moves beneath the entire plateau.

Deeper still, where thickness becomes geological rather than architectural, the dead lignite strata are no longer inert. Residual metabolic pockets remain—colonies of parenchyma that never quite lignified—linked to the living layer above by strands of secondary xylem that have inverted their polarity and now conduct heat downward instead of sap upward. These thermal roots act as governors: when surface metabolism exceeds a threshold, the excess is shunted into the cold sink of compressed biomass, stabilizing the global temperature at its perpetual 42 °C. In return, volatiles from the slow coalification drift upward, supplying trace elements the living tissues cannot mine from air alone.

The result is a single organism so large that its respiration and its architecture have become indistinguishable. You are never “in” it; you are merely one more vector of local deformation, one more warm patch that will be read, translated, and finally erased as the surface reconfigures around the memory of your passage.
