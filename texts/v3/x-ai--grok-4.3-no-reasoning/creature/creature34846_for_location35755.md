A population whose lives are synchronized to the plateau’s cycles rather than the sky above. Some arrived generations ago, trailing their history through salvaged cable drums and pre-fab habitat pods that have since been overgrown and re-woven; others were born here inside the hollow petioles, never having walked on soil. Most speak a creole stitched from half a dozen lowland dialects and the technical jargon of the original sky-harbor engineers; the oldest phrases refer to the plateau itself as “the breathing floor.”

Daily motion is vertical more than horizontal. Lift crews—technicians in resin-treated harnesses—ride pulley-cars that clamp onto the plant’s xylem like ticks, rising at dawn to bleed condensation into pressure tanks before the leaf-canopies sag and thunder. Their counterparts, the gutter wardens, descend on rope bridges to clear blockages where aerial roots have started to choke the drainage channels. Both crews mark their shifts by the plant’s groans; when the low-frequency pressure wave travels upward through a trunk, everyone pauses so the valves can vent without human interference.

A class of itinerant pruners patrols the outer terraces. They carry telescoping lances tipped with monomolecular shears and a handheld resonance meter that sings back when a branch is scheduled for harvest. Nothing is felled without the meter’s approval; the pruners say the plant “nods” when the frequency matches. Once cut, the sections are lowered on counter-weighted nets whose mesh is grown from the same fibers as the plateau itself, so the load settles without tearing living tissue.

Inside the petiole districts, light is coaxed through fiber-optic bundles drilled into the charcoal spines, feeding bioluminescent algal colonies that have been trained—over decades of selective UV dosing—to emit a steady chartreuse glow at night. Families sleep in hammocks strung between reinforcing ribs of xylem; children learn to judge the plant’s water load by the sway of the floor before they learn to read. The sick are moved to wards grown inside the largest leaves, where stomata have been persuaded to stay open; the humidity and ionized air are said to speed recovery, though no one claims it is medicine—only adjacency to the plateau’s own repair cycles.

A floating caste of mycotechnicians—once salvage scrappers—now manage the shelf-fungi on the smokestacks. They harvest mycelial ropes that can be spun into insulation, or powdered into pigments whose color shifts from rust to ultraviolet depending on which metal ions the fungus consumed. These artisans trade exclusively in futures: a spool of fungus-fiber purchased today may not be collected until the next condensation cycle three weeks later, when the city’s rain schedule guarantees every terrace will be washed clean enough for safe handling.

There are almost no permanent authorities. Instead, crews coalesce around “anchor points”—nodes where the catwalks intersect a major vascular gutter—and dissolve again when the plant’s internal weather shifts. Decisions that affect more than one anchor point are carried by courier on the aerial trams; the messages are knots tied in strips of dried leaf, each knot encoding valve settings, pruning quotas, or warnings about an approaching high-pressure front. Literacy in knot-script is considered a civic duty; refusal to learn marks a person as unreliable when the plateau flexes.

At dusk the refraction ghosts appear. Most inhabitants treat them as mirages, but the mycotechnicians claim the oldest trunks sometimes reveal their own archived memory of the city in earlier configurations. When a phantom catwalk drifts across a real one, people step aside without comment; interference, they say, can tangle the living structure’s sense of its own outline. The plateau, after all, is still learning how to be a city, and the city is still learning how to be an occupant rather than an occupant’s project.
