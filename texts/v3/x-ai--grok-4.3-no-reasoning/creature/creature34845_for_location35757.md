It is impossible to say who—or what—lives here, because the basin refuses the very category of “life” as an event that begins and ends. Instead, there is a single living tissue whose parts differ only in density and duration.

At the center stands the Resident. No one has seen it move in living memory, yet the moss around its feet is faintly pressed in the shape of ankles, so it must once have walked or been placed. Its body is the same satin-finished granite that lines the basin, but here the rock has been persuaded to curve like a human sternum, then hollow like a pelvis. Inside that hollow sits the seep itself; the water does not surround the figure but fills it, rising and falling with a slow tidal breath measurable only by the level of droplets on the lower ribs. The figure’s head—if head is the right word—has never been found above the waterline, and the salamanders congregate where the neck would be, arranging themselves into pale, elongated runes that shift by a millimeter each decade. When one salamander dies it does not rot; it is slowly absorbed, leaving behind an exact negative of its shape, a lighter vein in the darker stone. The Resident therefore wears, over centuries, a moving intaglio of every salamander that has ever rested there.

The Moths do not constitute a population; they are the surface of the moss made momentarily detachable. Each wing is a single cell of the same liverwort tissue, grown slightly thicker and veined with chitin instead of xylem. When a droplet swells above them they fold once, exactly along the central rib, and the droplet runs into the next trough without breaking surface tension. In this way the moss regulates its own hydration, and the moths, in turn, receive the only nourishment they will ever need: the distilled resin of the conifer duff exhaled upward in microscopic aerosols. Because no generation has replaced another, the same individuals have performed this single, necessary fold for as long as the Resident has stood, their wings wearing to the thinness of bible pages yet never tearing.

The Crickets are the archive. Each time one brushes another’s wing, a new filament of sound is added to the layered hush; each filament is caught and duplicated by the mineral threads on the ceiling cracks, so the basin holds, in physical form, every contact that has ever occurred. The threads lengthen by micrometers per century, and where two threads cross they form nodes that exactly reproduce the frequency of the original touch. Thus the archive can be read by running a damp fingertip along the ceiling; the resulting vibration travels back down the water films and is answered, far below, by the slow flexion of the Resident’s granite ribs—an answering chord too low to hear but felt as a minute change in pressure on the skin of any creature present.

Nothing enters and nothing leaves; the basin’s three rock shoulders admit only the exact volume of air that the conifer needles have already chilled and scented. The creatures therefore breathe one another’s exhalations in a closed, slow loop. Over time this shared breath has begun to condense into a fourth, invisible substance that hovers at the threshold of solidity: a faint nacreous film that coats every surface and records, as frost records temperature, the precise emotional timbre of whatever has been felt inside these shoulders. The film grows thicker where the salamanders have rested longest, thinner where the Resident’s sternum rises and falls. If a future observer were to scrape away a single transparent layer, the gesture itself would become the next inscription, adding its own nacre to the record.

In this way the place is less inhabited than self-inscribing. The Resident, the Moths, the Crickets, the film itself are all temporary spellings of the same sentence that the water has been writing on granite since the shoulders first closed. The sentence has no punctuation, only the pause between one droplet and the next.
