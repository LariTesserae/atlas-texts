The city lives inside its own respiration. Its blood is the measured pulse of water lifted, warmed, returned; its skeleton the porcelain walls that conduct heat and chill with equal docility. Nothing that moves here is free of that tempo. Even the lizards, whose tongues flick between pipe lips at dusk, count their heartbeats against the faint percussion of falling drops. Between one tick and the next, an entire clutch of eggs can be laid in the warmth beneath a single flange. The young emerge already marked by condensation rings on their new scales, miniature thermometers of the city’s one slow mood.

People—though they would not call themselves that—are equally tuned. They move in crews of six or eight, never more, their linen coveralls dyed the same pale salt color as the bamboo boards. Their hands are never idle; if no gauge needs reading, they knead fern-root paste into the bamboo turbine housings, or braid fresh vines across the cable nets so the hanging orchards do not sag. Speech is scarce. Instead they tap brass tokens against the railings in a private code that travels the length of a terrace in under a minute: one note for “all well,” two for “minor adjustment required,” three for “someone has left a tool behind.” The absence of three-note signals is itself a kind of music.

The vines do not climb; they are steered. Each tendril is coaxed through a series of copper eyelets screwed into the brick. When a leaf browns, a gardener with a silver hook slices it away and tucks the stem back into the next available ring. The cut end oozes a clear sap that the lizards lick; the lizards, in turn, leave a faint trail of nitrogen-rich droppings along the same route, which the vines convert into new internodes. The orchard above and the catwalk below thus feed each other without a single spoken contract.

At the very top, where cylindrical lenses focus sunlight into threads no thicker than a finger, a solitary technician in smoked-glass goggles tends the prisms. Twice a day she rotates them a quarter-turn so that the threads sweep across the fruit clusters like slow, luminous brooms. Each sweep raises the internal sugars by an increment too small to measure except over months; the cumulative effect, after years, is fruit so sweet it must be eaten within the hour or it begins to ferment inside its own skin. She stores the surplus in stoppered porcelain flasks whose necks are wrapped in fern fiber, then lowers them on silk cords to the level below, where they are traded for spare turbine blades or the canvas of a torn dormitory sling. Nothing is wasted, nothing is hurried, nothing is owned beyond the moment of its usefulness.

Even the fog participates. When the city exhales, the mist rises through the vertical orchards in ribbons thin enough to pass between leaves without displacing them. These ribbons carry the faint ozone scent of the invisible sea below and the sweeter, heavier exhalation of the lizards and the people and the warmed brick together. On rare windless nights the ribbons braid themselves into a single column that stands above the parapets like a pale mast. The column holds until the next inhalation begins, at which point it collapses inward, drawn down through the pipe-grids to be re-condensed and redistributed. The city drinks its own breath and grows no poorer for it.

Far beneath everything, in chambers so deep the flywheel axles are lost in shadow, the water-screws turn without bearings that can be seen. Their spiral vanes are carved from single trunks of petrified bamboo, polished by decades of flow until they mirror the dim lamps overhead. The only moving light is the slow travel of condensation beads down the screw threads themselves, each bead completing a full rotation before it falls free. The screws never stop; if they did, the city would feel the change in its teeth, in its scales, in its sleep. But they do not stop. The city continues to inhale and exhale at the rate of one measured breath every several hours, and whatever lives inside it—lizard, vine, technician, fog—moves to that same unvarying clock, content to be a single, patient component of a system that has already outlasted every generation that has tried to name it.
