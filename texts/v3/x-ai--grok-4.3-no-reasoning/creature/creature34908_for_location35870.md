A colony of blind, heat-seeking nematodes inhabits the shallow films between the translucent discs and the stone. Their bodies are translucent tubes, each no thicker than a pencil lead, that pulse in slow peristaltic waves timed to the arrival of condensing droplets. They thread themselves through the hair-fine roots without severing them, drawing heat directly from the stone’s residual warmth rather than from the discs above. When two nematodes meet they pause, exchange a brief knot of bodies, then continue; the encounter registers only as a momentary brightening of the phosphorescent fracture lines where their passage momentarily thins the film.

Beneath the crustaceans’ feeding grounds, a mat of anaerobic bacteria forms a single, kilometre-wide organism whose surface is folded into microscopic ridges that exactly follow the hexagonal crack pattern of the rock. Its metabolism produces a faint, continuous hiss of hydrogen sulphide that rises through the water and is immediately captured by the drifting mist sheets; the mist therefore smells of nothing at all, the odour having been absorbed back into the living layer before it can reach any nose. The mat thickens and thins over decades, its growth fronts advancing at the same speed as the crustaceans’ slowest gait, so that any given square centimetre of stone may host the same bacterial generation for an entire human lifetime.

Inside the open cabinet, a colony of halophilic archaea has colonised the salt dendrites themselves. Their cells are octahedral, each facet reflecting the cold phosphorescence into a miniature starburst. When a drifting mist sheet brushes the open door, the temperature drop causes the archaea to shed a single layer of surface crystals; the crystals fall like microscopic snow and are collected by the nematodes on the concrete footing below, which use the salt as an osmotic buffer for their own bodily films. The circuit boards continue to carry their original labels, yet the archaeal constellations now spell new, unintended circuits whose function is only to move sodium ions from one dendrite to another.

Suspended in the warmer veins that rise toward the invisible ceiling live gelatinous, bag-like organisms whose interiors are kept at a slightly higher temperature than the surrounding mist. They are filled with a clear fluid that supports a single, looping filament of living tissue; the filament glows the same cold colour as the stone’s phosphorescence but pulses once every few hours. When a pulse travels the full length of the filament, the bag contracts, expelling a droplet of warmer fluid that falls, still intact, through the colder mist until it reaches a pipe joint. There it bursts against the metal and is re-absorbed into the condensate, completing a cycle whose duration matches the interval between the largest bubbles rising from the pans.

At the very edge of the described terrain, where the mist sheets finally lose their cohesion and sink back into the lens-shaped pools, a single, motionless form rests half-submerged. It is the shed exoskeleton of an earlier, larger version of the segmented, striped creatures. Its segments are no longer bioluminescent; instead they have been colonised by the same salt-loving archaea that inhabit the cabinet. The exoskeleton therefore glitters with angular white growths that exactly trace the original stripe pattern, turning an abandoned outline into a fixed mineral memory of motion. The next segmented body to pass will brush against it, dislodge a few crystals, and carry them onward, so that the memory of the earlier shape continues to travel even though its original occupant does not.
