What persists here is not life, exactly, but its aftermath: a system that once moved and now only remembers motion.

Embedded in the layered discs of the cylinders are fragments of what might have been protective carapaces—each disc a compressed lattice of translucent plates once articulated by living hinges. The spiral gaps were once passages through which fine chemosensory filaments emerged and withdrew. Those filaments are gone; what remain are mineral replicas so faithful that, under certain angles of the diffused light, the ghost of a branching pattern can still be read. Nothing now moves along those paths, yet the alignment of the replicas suggests they were once synchronized, rising and falling in microscopic pulses timed to the daily migration of the water table.

The nematodes are not descendants but survivors of a later stage: the first organisms to re-colonize the mineral surfaces after the original builders withdrew or failed. Their glass-fiber sheaths are not bodies but scaffolds, secreted by transient colonies that deposit silica as they move. Each colony lasts only long enough to etch a single migration trail before desiccating; the next colony follows the same trail because the etch marks channel the rising moisture more efficiently than untouched silt. Over generations the trails have become self-reinforcing ruts no wider than a hair, invisible except where a condensation bead lingers slightly longer above them.

Nothing here feeds in the conventional sense. The warmth that seeps upward carries trace volatiles released by slow dissolution of the iron-rich substrate; the nematodes draw those volatiles through their sheaths and convert them into the next layer of silica. The exchange is so slow that a single sheath may persist for centuries, gradually thickening until the colony inside can no longer migrate and the trail is abandoned. New colonies then begin beside the old, inheriting the warmth but not the exact chemistry, so the mineral signature of each generation shifts by fractions too small to measure except across the full span of the valley’s recorded stains.

The three cylinders themselves are not ruins but stabilized states. Their unmortared stones were once held in tension by living tissue that has long since mineralized into the same pale discs that now fill them. The central voids were never empty; they were occupied by contractile columns whose expansion and contraction once drove the entire spiral lattice in a breathing motion synchronized with the daily appearance and disappearance of the condensation beads. When that motion ceased, the columns hardened in place, preserving their final posture as negative space. The valley therefore contains not absence but a record of the last coordinated inhalation, held open because the surrounding equilibrium will not allow it to collapse.

Nothing enters or leaves. The only exchange is the perpetual redistribution of the same water molecules through the same pores, the same mineral ions precipitating and re-dissolving along the same hairline fissures. Even the rare visitor’s footfalls are already anticipated: the capillary reset that erases prints is simply the valley returning to the configuration it occupied an instant before, an equilibrium so precise that the disturbance registers as already corrected before it can propagate.

In this sense the place is not empty. It is full of the completed version of itself.
