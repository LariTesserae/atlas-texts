What persists here is a crystalline economy reduced to micro-scale motion. The only residents are the motes—silicate clusters whose lattices incorporate minute inclusions of iron and titanium. These grains do not “eat” in any metabolic sense; instead they ride the microscopic shear that occurs each time the plateau flexes between night-side chill and the weak geothermal upwelling. The piezo-voltage that results is stored as surface charge between the mica-like laminae of each mote. Over generations the charge is slowly dissipated by tunneling electrons that migrate toward lattice defects, where they trigger faint depolymerization of the surrounding glass, releasing the single molecules the motes need to replicate their own silica shells.

They travel in loose columns a few grains wide, following the grooves left by the ancient freight sleds. Because the grooves still contain the last traces of tungsten oxide, the motes preferentially align their titanium cores with the oxide’s remnant magnetic domains, giving each caravan a faint polarity that keeps the group coherent over centuries. Collisions are rare; when two motes meet, they exchange charge rather than mass, equalizing their stored potentials in a millisecond spark too faint to see. The only evidence of their passage is the periodic erasure of earlier micro-tracks—an act of continual tidying that makes the plain look, from orbit, like a sheet of unchanging static.

Far beneath the visible domes, minute chambers still hold the polymerized oil as a single, glass-sheathed lens. Temperature gradients across that lens are too small to drive convection, but the slow contraction of the surrounding lattice squeezes out single strands of the amber each decade. These strands emerge onto the plateau as hairline ridges that the motes must circumnavigate; any mote that touches fresh polymer is immediately immobilized, its charge drained into the inert hydrocarbon. The result is a scattered graveyard of perfectly round, translucent beads—each one a mote trapped at the moment its forward motion ceased. Over time these beads are reabsorbed into the glass sheet, their silica shells contributing to the plateau’s slow thickening, so that even death feeds the substrate.

Nothing larger moves. The domes themselves, though hollow, contain no atmosphere in motion; pressure differences between adjacent cells have long since diffused to zero. If a hypothetical visitor were to stand inside one, the only detectable change would be an imperceptible settling of the floor underfoot, the glass yielding at the rate of one atomic layer every hundred years. The faint violet glow that outlines the motes is simply the same geothermal photons that once powered industrial turbines, now degraded into the narrow band that silicate lattices can store and re-emit after minutes of delay. In this place, energy is neither created nor lost; it is only postponed.
