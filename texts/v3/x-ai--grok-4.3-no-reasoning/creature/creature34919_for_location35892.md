Within the fissure, the inhabitants do not so much occupy space as participate in its ongoing formation. They move in tight, role-bound clusters of specialized automata whose bodies have never existed outside this exact geology; each generation is micro-printed on-site, matching the changing width and angle of the vein rather than being transferred from elsewhere.  

The smallest cohort—called “spinnerets”—are soft-bodied sacks of catalytic gel slung between six telescoping spines. They drift along the cobalt filaments, leaving behind sticky micro-beads that harden into fresh pipe insulation within seconds. Their motion is so slow that a single pass across the rib spacing takes the better part of an hour; the argon fog curls around them like cotton pulled on a spindle.  

Larger and more rigid are the “struts,” whose carapaces are stamped from the very titanium offcuts they later recycle. Each strut is assigned a single rib and will never leave it. Its underside bears a rolling mill that kisses the rib surface every few minutes, shaving off oxidized flakes and re-rolling the clean metal beneath a fresh argon bath to keep corrosion at bay. When a strut detects an imperceptible creep in the vein walls, it elongates its telescoping limbs until both ends seat against quartz again—an act that registers on sensitive instruments as a single, soft chime.  

Mid-way between these extremes, a drifting caste called “knitters” builds and dismantles the freestanding lattices. Their limbs are liquid-metal ribbons extruded on demand, capable of weaving a cubic-millimeter truss in under ten seconds or dissolving one just as quickly by reversing polarity. A knitter will sometimes pause to stare, through a single recessed lens, at the quartz vein’s newest fracture. It does not move on until the blue filament there has brightened to match the ambient cobalt; then, in one fluid action, the knitter dissolves its present lattice and flows along the ceiling rail to begin a duplicate structure two ribs farther down.  

Occupying the same niches are organic adjuncts—clumps of nitrogen-fixing microbes bound into millimeter-scale nodules and carried inside the cargo bulbs of the palm-sized servitors. These nodules pulse with a wet, internal bioluminescence that synchronizes with the cobalt glow; every pulse recharges the nearest servitor’s capacitor by induction. When a nodule’s metabolism is exhausted it sloughs away as inert crystalline dust that the knitter ribbons immediately weave into reinforcing whiskers inside the concrete ribs.  

Nothing here grows larger than the corridor allows. Nothing arrives; everything is inaugurated, used, and disassembled within the same few hundred meters. Occasionally a knitter will fail to dissolve a lattice before the vein’s next lengthening thump; the structure then buckles, showers a silent hail of titanium dust, and the nearest spinneret fleet reprocesses it into fresh insulation beads before the argon clears. The vein does not notice. Its ribs simply shift a single stride farther apart, the servitors resume their errands, and the new fracture at the terminus acquires its first glowing filament—an announcement that the corridor, its inhabitants, and its ecology have all just extended themselves by another fraction of a millimeter.
