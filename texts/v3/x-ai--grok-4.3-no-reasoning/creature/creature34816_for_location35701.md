A community of moss-celled herbivores drifts across this living membrane, their six chitin-plated backs an extension of the peat itself. Where they lie down to rest the raised plates seed miniature gardens of the same vascular plants that carpet the plateau; when they rise, the plants remain, rooted now in the vacated depressions like living cairns. Because their chitin grows outward from the same iron-oxidizing microbes that stain the exposed cracks, each animal carries an expanding fringe of rust-orange lace that thickens yearly until the oldest individuals appear ringed with geological strata.

Above them move solitary, faintly iridescent midges whose wings are broad, translucent sails of condensed fog. They hatch each afternoon from the gelatinous liverwort mats, live only until the next dawn, and, in dying, contribute their microscopic bodies to the daily frost. The result is that the frost itself carries a shifting genetic signature: on mornings after heavy hatches the delicate white coral that forms along spider-silk lines is tinged with brief, prismatic bands of ultraviolet that vanish again before noon.

Where the shallow central lake periodically drains, a single vast, slow-moving colony of the cucumber-scented algae remains anchored in the sediment. During low-water intervals this colony spreads outward as a single unbroken sheet until it collides with the advancing peat front; the boundary between the two is a living cliff a few centimeters high, an edge that migrates back toward the lake center each time the water returns. On certain nights when the lake is full and perfectly still, the colony’s uppermost layer detaches in a synchronized pulse, sending a single, coin-sized bubble of gas to the surface for every individual cell that has reached reproductive age; the collective release produces a momentary silvering across the mirror-water before the bubbles re-dissolve.

The only true predators are slender, translucent nematodes that live entirely within the peat membrane, threading between root hairs like slow lightning. Their presence is revealed solely when an animal steps too heavily; the membrane depresses, water wells, and the nematodes briefly throng the temporary pool in threads of living glass before vanishing again into the closing mat. Because their density tracks the grazers’ footfalls, the plateau’s surface is, in effect, mapped each day by these microscopic hunters that bloom and subside according to the weight of passage.

Occasionally a single grazer lingers after its aggregation has moved on. Over months its chitin plates sink into the peat, the moss grows across its back until only the faint outlines of the six legs remain, and the animal’s slow metabolism becomes indistinguishable from the plateau’s respiration. In its final seasons it functions as a fixed point around which condensate spirals and frost patterns radiate—another motionless node in the same slow metabolism that has already remade the horizon into the next living rim.
