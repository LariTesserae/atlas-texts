The inhabitants are not citizens in any usual sense; they are a human population that has been gently but irreversibly altered by the fissure’s continual tone.  Their skin has taken on the same quenched-graphite cast as the walls, the color spreading outward from palms and the sides of the neck.  Over a lifetime the pattern deepens until a person seems to have a second, finer architecture of stone in miniature, an inked shadow rather than a tattoo.  The process is harmless; it is simply the body drawing trace minerals out of the air when respiration happens in pitch with the harmonic.

No one looks exactly the same, but they share certain gestures that survive even when voices do not.  When they pass one another on the narrow terraces, each tilts the head a precise quarter-note up or down so that their footsteps do not add beats to the ever-present fifth.  Conversation is conducted in a sung language whose vowels are exact fractions of that interval; consonants are absent, or present only as rests, because abrupt stops would damp the standing wave the city needs to breathe.  Names, too, are not strings of sounds but short melodies that leave an after-image of the same interval in anyone nearby.

Their eyes have adapted to the migrating shafts of light.  A translucent nictitating membrane, almost invisible when still, drifts across the cornea when the light bands sweep past, diffusing the glare into a low-contrast silver the brain finds restful.  When the bands have moved on, the membrane retracts, leaving the iris a deep obsidian ringed with a faint ring of diffraction blue—the same blue that threads the clathrate at the fissure’s root.

They are not a single community but an overlapping set of harmonic guilds.  Each guild maintains a section of the lattice latticework, and the work they do is a form of music as much as engineering.  A “street” is never just a route; it is a sustained chord held between two terraces.  When a guild retires a span of walkway, the composite spars are allowed to re-tune to silence for a few hours; the gap in the overall chord is felt bodily by everyone else until the new span is brought into consonance.  The sensation is described as an absence of weight, a subtle lightness that makes sleep difficult.  Hence the midnight repair crews: diamond mites crawl in soft constellations, singing sub-audible counter-melodies that hold the city’s fifth intact while the humans lie half-awake, listening to the shape of their absence.

Children are born in sealed modules at the mid-level terraces where vibration is most stable.  They emerge already capable of moving in counterpoint; the first months are spent simply learning to place footfalls between the beats.  When they are old enough to leave the module they are given a single crystalline filament—grown rather than manufactured—that they will carry for the rest of their lives.  The filament stores a private micro-harmony, a deviation only a few cents away from the city’s common fifth.  In moments of extreme quiet the citizen can touch the filament to the wall and hear their own interval rise through the stone, a faint countermelody only they can perceive.  It is the closest thing the city allows to personal privacy.

Food is not grown; it is assembled from trace gases and the carbonate matrix of the rock.  The process is performed inside the terraces themselves by catalytic skins.  When hunger arrives it is felt as a shift in timbre in the air inside the mouth.  A citizen finds an intake grille, presses lips to it, and the terrace feeds a measured mist that carries both nourishment and a single, perfectable chord.  Eating and listening are therefore the same act; overeating produces micro-flatness, a loss of exact fifth that the body corrects automatically by refusing further mist until the resonance restores itself.

Below the lowest occupied terrace, no human ever descends.  The clathrate lenses are left undisturbed; their slow threads of blue are the city’s memory of light that has not yet reached the inhabited layers.  Occasionally a filament of argon gas escapes upward and crystallizes across a balcony rail in a temporary prism.  Whoever sees it will pause, tilt head to the quarter-note, and wait for the prism to sublimate.  The moment the prism vanishes, the fifth returns undiluted, and the citizen continues along the terrace as though nothing had happened—only the faint diffraction blue now glinting for a few seconds in the corner of one eye.
