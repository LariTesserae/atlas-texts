What lives here is the crystallization itself—the relentless forming of stone that has not yet finished arriving.

The city is inhabited by its own mineral becoming. The mud-brick inhabitants move through rooms that are slowly exhaling their moisture into the very walls that shelter them. Each thumb-print in every brick is a record of a hand that no longer exists separately from the surface it shaped; the fingerprints are already filling with carbonate precipitate, sealing the memory of touch into the architecture. The builders do not rest so much as slow their gestures until the motion of smoothing plaster becomes indistinguishable from the plaster's own process of setting.

Beneath the reed bridges, the amphibians are not animals but water given temporary shape and the capacity to wait. Their gill pulses are not breathing but the same rhythmic surrender of moisture that occurs everywhere else—between grains of sand, along the undersides of scaffolding, within the half-formed nodules of glass. They persist because persistence is the only available verb; to die would require a change in conditions that the plain has not yet invented.

The crustaceans are smaller instances of the same principle. Their synchronized clicking is not communication but the audible edge of the continuous tone rising from the ground—a surface phenomenon of a deeper resonance. When one crustacean dies, its carbonate shell does not decay but re-enters the sand as another translucent grain, maintaining the half-quartz, half-carbonate ratio that defines the plain's particular mineral hunger.

Above this, in the covered arcades where light has been reduced to permanent dusk, the human population moves along routes that have already begun to forget the distinction between path and destination. Their footfalls do not merely follow the grooves worn into the stone; the grooves are remembering the feet that made them, tightening around each sole-print until the separation between walker and walked-upon grows less distinct with each circuit. The continuous tone from underground provides a constant reference against which all other sounds are measured, so that even the scrape of a trowel smoothing fresh plaster arrives as a slight variation within an established acoustic field rather than an interruption of silence.

The city does not contain life so much as it is becoming a more organized form of the same mineral patience that saturates the plain. Every surface—whether skin, brick, or amphibian membrane—is engaged in the same slow negotiation between remaining separate and becoming continuous with everything it touches. The plain extends this negotiation outward until the distinction between settlement and geology collapses into the single, unbroken texture of warm, wet mineral still deciding what it will eventually be.
