Beneath Greenmantle, the city-that-was-swamp maintains a vast, slow cohabitation.  

Its lowest stratum is still pure wetland: black water two metres deep, threaded by cypress knees that have grown fat and horizontal, meeting one another like the knuckles of clasped hands. Above them rise the first generations of tupelo, their trunks flattened into load-bearing plates; every twenty years their outer bark is planed away so fresh mortar can be buttered directly onto sapwood. Between these plates the water does not stagnate; it moves, millimetre by millimetre, following a gradient no one plotted but the trees themselves.  

Upward from this hidden marsh, the structure thickens into a single living mezzanine. Here, the pilings have grown into rooms. A man may step through a doorway whose jamb is the cleft between two boles that have pressed together for a century; the floor he walks upon is the fused upper surface of their roots, still ridged like the plates of a tortoise shell. In the corners, where the angle is too sharp for human use, the swamp has left small wells; lily pads float there, each leaf large enough to serve as a low table if any resident were small enough or patient enough to sit at it.  

Light arrives as amber resin, five storeys overhead. The panes are cloudy with age and with the constant exhalation of the plants beneath; each dawn the resin warms and releases a scent of camphor and citrus that drifts downward like invisible snowfall. In that dim syrup of illumination, leaves turn without haste, following an arc measured not in hours but in seasons. A single ficus that began life in an iron planter on an upper boulevard now fills four vaulted cells; its newest branch has lifted a loose roof-tile and grown through it, so that rain sometimes enters the corridor as filtered green water, already half-digested by the leaf.  

Movement here is measured by the interval between one graft and the next. Work crews consist of arborists who speak in the same registers as the sap-conductors they splice; a single spoken word can be mistaken for the click of a vessel under pressure, and the workers wait until the sound resolves before continuing. When they raise a section of floor, they do so on bladders of tanned kelp that inflate over three days; once the new pilings are seated, the bladders are punctured and their fluid is pumped back into the marsh, carrying any splinters or iron filings with it. Nothing is wasted; even rust becomes food for iron-fixing bacteria that line the undersides of the leaves.  

The inhabitants are not residents in the ordinary sense. A handful of municipal foresters live in scaffold-niches grafted into the oldest trunks, their hammocks slung between branch-ends that have been trained to grow parallel. They mark their shifts by the pitch of the dripping stalactites; when the interval between drops lengthens by a fraction of a second, the barometric pressure has dropped enough to warrant an inspection tour. Children—there are few—are raised in the same alcoves, their first language the low, arrhythmic ticking of pressurized sap. They learn to walk on the ridged root-floors before they learn to speak; their earliest steps are already part of the metabolism of the place.  

Animals exist mostly as rumor. A pale salamander, blind and nearly translucent, is said to travel the condensation pipes once each decade, following routes opened only when two adjacent stomata iris shut in the same lunar cycle. Bats enter through the roof gardens far above, but lose their bearings in the half-light and must be guided upward again by low-frequency fans whose infrasound mimics the dusk chorus of the swamp that once was.  

Nothing is sudden. A dropped wrench does not ring; it is caught by a mat of liverworts whose rhizoids have already begun to incorporate the iron into their cell walls before the next shift arrives. When a new conduit must be laid, the workers do not dig; they request a particular branch to swell, and in three years’ time a hollow forms exactly where the pipe is needed. The pipe is then inserted gently, as one might slide a letter under a door whose hinges still sleep.  

Above it all, the surface streets continue their ordinary traffic, unaware that every taxi tire rolling over resin-sealed cobbles is also rolling over the newest leaf of a canopy that has not finished deciding what shape a city ought to be.
