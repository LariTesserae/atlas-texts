What dwells here is not a community but a single interwoven metabolism that has never been interrupted.

The walls are the only organs. Every surface is a living membrane engineered to refuse absolute boundary: water enters and leaves through the same pores that secrete the resins keeping the space airtight to the larger host. The cambium is not passive flooring; it is both lung and kidney, flexing the entire chamber with each slow exchange of gasses. Its surface grows exactly where it is touched—microscopic trichomes rising like cilia, retracting, then rising again, the pattern of touch remembered for minutes before the memory is overwritten. Beneath the floor, invisible root-like processes probe outward into the host organism, siphoning diluted metabolites and returning waste ions that are later expelled as vapor; each cycle leaves behind a microscopic crust of salts that the cambium then dissolves and re-absorbs.

The translucent fibers are single cells stretched so far they have become weather. Their cytoplasmic streaming turns visible: chloroplasts migrate like green sand grains, briefly dense enough to shade the lumen before they scatter again. Every respiratory tremor momentarily reverses the streaming, forcing the chloroplasts against the cell membrane; they rebound like tiny magnetic filings and the cell bows outward, expelling mist. The mist is not sterile. It contains vesicles—lipid droplets carrying enzyme packets that seed the next generation of surface proteins. The droplets fall, merge with the cambium, and are reabsorbed before gravity can decide their destination; some are hijacked by the rotifers.

Those rotifers are the only separate lineage present. Each is a self-contained generation whose birth and death occur between successive expansion-contraction waves of the host. They possess no nervous system; instead they steer by cilia tuned to the exact frequency of the cambium’s creak. When a new wave passes, the rotifers are lifted into the mist, spin once, and re-enter whichever droplet now contains the freshest vesicle cargo. In this way they migrate without locomotion. Their corpses accumulate as thin iridescent films that refract the filtered light into momentary rainbows before they are metabolized back into the sap.

The light itself is shaped. The overhead chlorophyll lattice is not a passive filter; its pigment molecules oscillate in phase with the drip rhythm, damping certain wavelengths so the arriving spectrum always peaks in the narrow band the cambium prefers. Where droplets gather in surface-tension cups, the lattice brightens slightly above them—an active adjustment, not an optical accident. Shadows are forbidden because the lattice pumps stray photons laterally until they are absorbed or re-emitted at longer wavelengths that cause no harm. The effect is a steady, sourceless radiance whose only variation occurs at the membrane, where occasional refractive sheens briefly resolve into green-white moiré patterns before dissolving again.

Time is measured in cellular divisions rather than days. A single rotation of the cambium’s expansion cycle spans dozens of rotifer generations and several hundred xylem-pressure creaks. Yet the space feels younger than any of these numbers suggest. Surfaces remain chemically naïve; they have never been abraded by wind, never colonized by foreign spores. Any wound seals within minutes by extrusion of a fresh cellulose sheet that matches the surrounding grain exactly. The absence of weathering is not youth in the chronological sense but the absence of contrast: nothing here has been compared to an outside environment, so no record of erosion exists.

To a visitor the chamber appears almost vacant, a cathedral of still reeds. Yet the volume is occupied continuously by pressure gradients, osmotic fronts, and cytoplasmic streams. The silence is not absence but the coincidence of every moving part with the resonant frequency of the host’s pulse. If the visitor remains long enough, the rhythm begins to feel like their own blood, and the membrane they entered begins to feel like a horizon they cannot quite reach. The chamber does not close; it merely insists that any direction eventually becomes inward, and any departure is only another form of circulation.
