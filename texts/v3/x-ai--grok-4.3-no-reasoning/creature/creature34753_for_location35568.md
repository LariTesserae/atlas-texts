The vault is a living machine that has forgotten why it was made.

It houses exactly two hundred and seventeen human inhabitants, though “human” has drifted. They are the Vaultborn—pale, slightly translucent people whose skin carries a faint iron sheen like oil on water. They call themselves the Choir of the Continuous Note, because their only unbroken tradition is to keep the low, endless tone from faltering. They live on the narrow catwalks and suspended platforms that once serviced the cranes; their dwellings are small geodesic cocoons of woven cable and leaf-fiber, each one shaped like a closed fist gripping the gantry rail. When a new child is born, the parents tie a fresh strand of fiber across the entrance: a woven announcement that the tone must carry one voice more.

Their diet is almost entirely the violet leaves, harvested by clipping the cages and pressing the pulp through cloth bags until a salty green liquor runs out. They chew the remaining mash into a paste they call iron-bread, which is stored in sealed ceramic vessels the size of coffin lids. Once every few years they descend, rappelling in teams, to clean the cisterns; the work is performed in utter silence, because any extra noise might be interpreted as a new modulation the Choir has not yet sanctioned.

The crustaceans are not merely tolerated; they are the metric by which the Choir measures time. Each day the oldest priestess—called the Counter—presses her ear to a particular beam above the largest cistern. If the faint, staccato clicking of mandibles is slower or softer than the day before, she marks a notch on the beam with a shard of violet-leaf charcoal. When three notches align in a perfect diagonal, the Choir performs the Turning: the gantry cranes are deliberately shifted three degrees, an act that takes the entire community two days of synchronized pulling on rusted hand-cables. The shift is accompanied by a single, deliberate foot-stamp on the iron floor that momentarily quiets the drone; in that heartbeat of near-silence they listen for any change in the crustacean cadence. If the rhythm quickens again, the Turning is judged successful and the endless note is considered “fed.”

The crustaceans have, in turn, evolved a small ritual around the humans. At certain alignments of the single porthole’s light they swarm upward along the drip lines until they reach the lowest sleeping platforms. There they wait, motionless, until a sleeping Vaultborn exhales; the warm breath melts a bead of condensation that falls directly onto the cluster. The crustaceans drink it in a collective shiver, then retreat. No one has ever recorded a human noticing, yet the event always coincides with a night when dreams are especially vivid—dreams of iron corridors that branch and re-branch without end.

Far below the iron floor, in shafts whose lowest levels no living person has reached, something older still persists. The original targeting lenses of the defense battery lie in ranks, each the diameter of a person’s height, their fused quartz surfaces now spider-webbed with mineral growth. Condensation drips from their upper rims in perfectly synchronized sixteen-second intervals. The Choir has never heard these drips; the shafts are too deep, the intervening iron too thick. Yet every sixteen seconds the entire vault receives an almost subsonic pressure change, a gentle heave that momentarily alters the standing wave of the drone. The crustaceans click faster for three beats afterward, as if applauding a conductor they alone can sense. The Vaultborn interpret these micro-shifts as proof that the mountain itself continues to sing, even when human voices fall silent. They record each heave as a separate, hidden verse in the Continuous Note—an octave below hearing, yet still part of the same composition that began when the mountain was first drilled.

In time the aquifer will fail and the drips will slow. When that happens the Choir intends to descend—every last member—into the lens shafts, carrying the youngest children in slings of leaf-fiber. They plan to lie upon the quartz and let their body heat keep the sixteen-second rhythm alive as long as blood remains warm. Whether the crustaceans will follow them down, or whether the iron-bread will last the journey, are questions the Choir does not ask. Their only certainty is that the tone must not be allowed to end before they do.
