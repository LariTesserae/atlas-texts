In the half-lit concavity live the people who once had another name and now answer only to the pattern underfoot. They are shorter on average than the surface folk, their spines subtly curved from a lifetime of reading the herringbone with the soles of their feet. Their skin carries a permanent ochre film, the same pigment that settles in the brick channels; each generation therefore inherits the same faint stain, so the living and the newly dead are distinguished mainly by warmth.

Children appear first as small, lamp-colored moths that fold themselves into the wall-handprints at dusk. When they emerge again they are given a thumb-sized isopod to carry on their forearms; the creature’s slow circuits teach them the precise intervals at which each nodule will contract. By the time they can name the three-beat rhythm they are already expected to pour the spent lamp-fat into the lowest vein, their baskets balanced on hipbones already bearing the faint herringbone bruise that never quite fades.

Adults move in matched pairs whose footfalls cancel rather than double the thunder transmitted through the brick. One plants, one lifts; one grinds, one sifts. Their speech is not softer than the surrounding percussion, only shorter; most exchanges last no longer than the interval between two nodule releases. When they need longer words they press palm against palm and let the vibration travel through bone, a private telegraph learned before language.

Elders no longer carry baskets. Instead they tend the condensation nets, sliding along the catenary lines on rope seats worn to the polish of river stone. Their hands are mapped with the same overlapping prints that cover the walls; as they work, the prints on skin and clay align until it becomes difficult to tell which surface receives the water and which dispenses it.

The dead remain in the lowest concavity where the fat-vein disappears into a vertical cleft no wider than a wrist. Their bodies are laid directly onto the brick so that the warmth can finish what breath began. Over years the herringbone records their outlines in a darker, slightly slicker red; the next cohort of children learns to read those stains as the biography of the village itself.

Nothing here reproduces quickly. The pale isopods gestate for three full temperature cycles; the salamander-crickets lay clutches the size of a lamp bowl once per generation of children. Human births are announced by the sudden brightening of a single lamp when its wick is doubled—an extravagance permitted only for the arrival of new feet to learn the floor.

The village therefore accumulates rather than replaces. Each new handprint overlaps an earlier one without erasing it; each new footfall settles into a depression already mapped by ancestors. The only true migration is the slow outward creep of the clay ceiling as successive layers of handprints thicken it, pushing the wooden columns minutely farther apart until, centuries hence, a new ring of trunks will have to be carried down in baskets to hold the next memory of soil.
