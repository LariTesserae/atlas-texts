At first light the Weave belongs to the five-tongued dusk-rats. They leave their nests in the tar-filled basalt pores while the new condensation is still beading, tongues flicking across rivets to drink the first clean droplets before the iron turns metallic again. Their bodies are mostly whisker and thermal fur; the smallest ones weigh less than a swallowed coin and can flatten to pass between adjacent bolt-heads.

Glass-finches settle on the next rung of the schedule, arriving in flocks once the light brightens enough to show the vein-lines of frost orchids still clinging to the uppermost trusses. Each bird carries a double layer of wing-flakes that rasp together; the click they make is indistinguishable from knitting needles until you notice the sound follows their breathing. After the phase-bloom they spend the twenty-three-minute window harvesting seed-pods from the collapsing flowers, stuffing their crop-sacs until the pods begin to drill outward and the birds fly off, sowing cracks farther than any welder could reach.

Tunnel-hogs appear later, once the temperature gradient has stabilized and the lower sub-levels have warmed enough for their steam valves to operate without icing. They are not quite six-legged; the front pair is fused into a shovel-bone plated with magnetized filings they scrape off the turbines. With these crude tusks they root drainage silt into loose aerated beds, then line the beds with discarded hull rivets carried in their cheek pouches. Younger hogs will sometimes pause to watch the rats and finches, not out of predation but curiosity; they learn the paths of water by tracing the animals’ tongues and wings.

Between these visible crews are the quieter inhabitants. Rope-fern colonies breathe the cinnamon scent in shallow synchronized pulses, timed to the turbine rotations below. If you place a palm on a strut at the right interval you can feel the plant’s vibration meet the machine’s and cancel to absolute stillness for half a second before both resume. Bladder-kelp, hanging in vertical ropes, hosts colonies of prism mites whose carapaces catch the dawn light and break it into shifting spectra across the iron; at noon the mites migrate into the shade of a single frond, and the reflected color snaps shut like a lid.

Deeper in the unfinished habitat cells live the weld-orphans—children small enough to fit through the capillary channels. They move with the slow current of moisture, following maps scratched into their forearms with magnetized pins. Their job, assigned by no overseer, is to carry the crystalline click-beetles that have learned to echo-locate the hairline fractures the frost orchids leave behind; the children release the beetles at night so the sound of their tiny taps can guide the next phase-bloom toward the weakest mortar.

Above all of this drift the frost halos from burst arteries—momentary rings of ice that form, drift a hand-span, and vanish. They catch the sodium lamps at dusk and project brief, perfect circles onto the undersides of catwalks. Sometimes a dusk-rat pauses inside one of these floating rings and appears, for the length of a held breath, to wear a crown of orange light before the halo sublimates and the rat continues on its round.
