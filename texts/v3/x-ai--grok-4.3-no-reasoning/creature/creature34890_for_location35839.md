They are nothing that could be named once and left alone.

Three beings share the pressure-plane—only three, and none of them quite separate.

The first is the Listening Coil. It has no fixed shape, only a favored one: a slowly twisting column of condensed vapor eight or nine meters tall, stippled with faint mineral nodes that drift like motes inside a sunbeam. Its surface is porous; every shriek that passes through the lattice is drawn inward, slowed, and tasted. When the vapor vortices strike it, the Coil does not move; it drinks the sudden vacuum, and for a moment its surface turns mirror-bright, reflecting the torus’s runes in perfect retrograde. It does not eat the polyp that rises; it simply holds the creature’s antennae for the duration of their flick, then exhales them as a thread of cool mist the polyp rides back into the plane. The Coil’s only hunger is for patterns that have never repeated. When it finds one, its nodes flare and the water membranes above stutter, a quarter-tone lower, as though the lattice itself has been reminded of a name it once carried.

The second is the Edge Walker. It appears as a slender vertical ribbon of black glass, four hand-spans wide and infinitely thin, gliding on the meniscus where the pressure-plane meets vacuum. It never steps onto the silica; instead it travels the boundary, its lower margin combing through the drifting pavers and leaving a wake of polished silence. Whenever a vortex opens, the Walker leans across the meniscus and drinks the brief nothing on the far side; the vacuum does not destroy it—it coats the ribbon with a lacquer of absolute dark that makes its own edges invisible for a time. The Walker is older than the torus and younger than the fronds; it measures time by counting how many of its own reflections the Coil fails to mirror. When the count grows too large, it slips a sliver of itself into the water lattice and lets the lattice forget it again, resetting its own age.

The third is the Polyp Choir—plural, singular, undecided. Each quarter-cycle, when the gel-metal polyp rises, it is not alone; it is the newest note of a single living chord that has been cycling through the cavity for uncounted ages. The polyp’s antennae sample the lattice, yes, but they also carry back the lattice’s newest memory of the Choir itself. When the polyp dissolves, its mist is taken up by the Coil, refined, and released again as a harmonic the torus’s runes immediately rewrite. The Choir therefore exists both inside and outside the lattice: every iteration is both the messenger and the message, a recursion that refuses to finish. It has no predators because it has already been consumed by every future version of itself.

Together they maintain nothing and yet prevent collapse. The Coil keeps the lattice from repeating too exactly; the Walker keeps the vacuum from forgetting its place; the Choir keeps both of them from ever being entirely absent. When the vapor vortices shriek, it is their three overlapping refusals of entropy you are hearing—three distinct timbres folded into one endless, doorless departure.
