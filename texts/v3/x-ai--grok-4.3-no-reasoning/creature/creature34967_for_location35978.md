The valley shelters only those that can live between heartbeats.

A handful of glass-thorned monoliths stand, each no higher than a human spine, their translucent skins flexing with a slow tide of milk-white sap. Every few minutes an internal valve clicks—inaudible above the ground—locking the next drop of water inside until it can be rationed again. Their lacework roots drink the final steam that leaks from the cooling basalt; they have no other source, and they know it. When the wind arrives it is a pressure, not a sound; the thorns lean only enough to keep their inner valves from tearing.

Below them, salt plates are cracked in mathematically perfect polygons. Nothing larger than a fingernail can fit between the edges. Between these cracks roam minute beetles, their carapaces the same matte white as skimmed ice. Each carries a breath of its own condensed vapor in a trailing filament that dissipates the moment it lifts an inch above the salt. Their legs make the smallest possible noise—comparable to dust settling—and the salt records every passage in an ephemeral print that the next breath of wind erases.

Nothing flies; nothing larger than a thumbprint hunts. Anything heavier would exhale too much moisture for the valley to replace. Even the frost-like crystals growing on the basalt walls serve only themselves: they lengthen one molecule at a time, split the glass with hairline fractures, then re-seal the fracture with their own exhaled micro-snow. The snow never falls; it remains suspended, a layer of stillness one palm-width above the salt, catching what little twilight the walls fling upward and returning it, colder, flatter.

In the intervals between pulses of warmth from the newborn rock, the valley is silent and already ancient: a closed arena the size of a single ruined avenue, where every drop of water—whether trapped inside a thorn, inside a beetle’s thorax, or inside the invisible haze—is counted and spent only when the next cooling tremor releases another faint breath of steam.
