The living surface is a single, slow-motion metabolism the size of an inland sea. Its outermost membrane is not a simple skin but a living pavement: sheets of velvety, black-green hyphae half a meter deep, their mycelial strands functioning simultaneously as rebar, capillary plumbing, and data bus. Every upward thrust of fresh tectonite is met within minutes by a creeping tide of this velvet, sealing fissures before new snow can settle; within days the same filaments have woven themselves into the scoria, converting metallic sulfide veins into nutrient pathways and tapping the faint thermoelectric gradient between sun-warmed surface and still-warm interior.

Rising through this fungal mat are the pillar-trunks—five to fifteen meters tall, glassy-grey, their bark a mosaic of overlapping quartz plates grown like scale armor. Each plate is both a photovoltaic cell and a piezoelectric resonator; temperature swings and direct UV stress cause them to expand and contract, releasing low-frequency tones that propagate through the mass of the trunk like a struck tuning fork. The pillars stand shoulder to shoulder in uneven groves, yet they are not individual organisms; microscopic hyphal threads link them into a single radial network, allowing surplus hydrogen generated at one site to migrate toward another whose bladders are running low.

The “herd organisms” that roam between these groves are more geological than zoological. Each is a colony-organism whose outer carapace consists of interlocked plates of calcium-magnesium carbonate and native copper; as the mass drifts forward at centimeters per hour, the plates rasp against one another, smoothing themselves and shedding a continuous dust of calcitic grit that the glass-moss immediately incorporates into its own refractive lattice. Inside the carapace, gelatinous predator-nodules drift like amoebae in slow convection currents, enveloping pockets of fungal sporocarps or injured megaphyte tissue. Their digestion is exothermic; the heat they release keeps thin films of liquid water alive at the base of the shell, lubricating the colony’s crawl across ground that is otherwise frozen solid.

Above, the air itself is part of the metabolism. Katabatic pulses, loaded with suspended ore dust, are not merely weather; they are the circulatory breath of the continent. When these winds eddy against the windward faces of the pillar-trunks, they trigger the release of micro-spores from the velvet-fungi; each spore is encased in a metallic-protein shell that resonates with the same infrasonic frequency the pillars emit. The spores ride the wind until they lodge in fresh scoria fractures, where the sudden jolt of meltwater triggers germination. In this way the fungal mat reseeds itself even as the rock itself is still cooling from its most recent tectonic extrusion.

The temporary melt-ponds that appear after a reservoir burst function less as lakes than as staged reaction vessels. For the brief window of their existence—perhaps thirty to forty years—the water becomes supersaturated with dissolved metallic ions and dissolved methane. A violet-red scum of extremophile algae colonizes the surface within weeks; the algae fix nitrogen and excrete complex organic ligands that chelate the metals, concentrating them into nodules the size of human fists. When the pond finally evaporates, the nodules are left scattered across a cracked plain of dried algal film; within a century the drifting carapace herds will converge on the plain, drawn by the faint thermoelectric signature of the nodules, and ingest them as raw material for new carapace plates. Thus a single century-scale flood event becomes, centuries later, an incremental thickening of the living causeway beneath the next generation of herd organisms.

Acoustically, the entire system hums on the edge of human perception. The constant, low-grade tectonic tremor—roughly one micrometer every three hours—sets every pillar-trunk vibrating at its own resonant frequency; because the trunks vary in height and mineral density, a standing wave field slowly migrates across a grove, producing a moving chord that an observer would perceive as a drifting, almost sub-audible organ note. Superimposed on this are millions of microscopic “ticks”: each new cell extruded by the glass-moss releases a tiny elastic snap; each time a carapace plate abrades another by a single crystal width, a click registers. Over a human lifetime these clicks sum into an almost inaudible increase in background noise, registering to instruments as a slow rise in the floor of the soundscape—proof that, even while appearing frozen, the continent is still metabolizing, still manufacturing, still listening to its own geological pulse.
