In the Swell the only creatures that move of their own accord are the motile monitoring cells—microscopic crews that emerge by the billions from hidden meristematic bunkers beneath every ridge. Each cell is a flattened sphere, its cytoplasm packed with chlorophyll lenses and piezoelectric organelles that let it “see” micro-fractures in leaf membranes the way a bat sees a moth. When damage is found the cells lock together into temporary flagellar ropes, swim through the phloem until they reach the injury, then fuse into a living bandage that is reabsorbed once the tissue has healed. They never compete; their single genetic program is to be replaced after twenty-four hours, so the Swell remains a place without ancestry or offspring—only maintenance.

Larger beings exist, yet they do not roam. Every ten thousand square kilometers or so the leaf surface thickens into a spindle-shaped vault the size of an inland sea. Inside, motionless botanists—human technicians whose circulatory systems have been grafted to the vascular mesh—float in suspended hydrogel. Their eyelids are transparent screens; their dreams are data visualizations of nutrient gradients across whole latitudes. They wake only when an anomaly exceeds the drones’ tolerance. The awakening is chemical: a slow flood of ethylene through their personal xylem tendrils sharpens perception for seventeen minutes, after which the gardeners return to coma. Their bodies are already half-leaf, and their exhaled CO₂ is the Swell’s most carefully measured harvest.

Above the gardens, beneath the luminous ceiling, hangs the choir: kilometer-long ribbons of aerohyaline tissue that vibrate at the precise frequency of shifting atmospheric aldehydes. Each ribbon is one enormous cell, its vacuole tuned like an organ pipe. When a gardener’s seventeen-minute session ends, the ribbon above that meridian contracts once, producing a single sub-sonic chord that the monitoring cells interpret as an all-clear. The sound travels through the solid scaffolding of the world and returns weeks later, Doppler-softened by the planet’s own rotation.

Occasionally, near the structural stanchions where xylem fibers are thickest, the curvature of space becomes so pronounced that light folds back upon itself. In those lenses a visitor can glimpse, for an instant, a miniature duplicate of the Swell—identical emerald horizon curled inside its own horizon—before the optical chloroplast computer corrects the view and the duplicate vanishes. Some technicians swear the nested image is not a reflection but a memory, a geometric echo from an earlier calibration pass, preserved in the bent metrics of the photosynthetic crystal. They never linger there; vertigo in the Swell is not merely dizziness, but the sudden intuition that the entire engineered world is one enormous cell remembering itself.
