Between river and rock, the corridor’s every surface has learned to carry water.  
The air is never still; a slow, sidelong draft threads between the columns, cooled by the stone yet constantly rewarmed by the micro-seeps. That breath keeps the liverworts from freezing and the condensation from ever settling into frost. In the narrowest clefts, strands of filamentous algae—steel-blue, barely wider than a hair—trail like submerged silk, their holdfasts anchored to the faintest film of calcite. They drink the constant drip before it can reach the rush culms below, and in return the algae shade the thalli from the occasional flare of daylight that filters through the overcast.

Most of the corridor’s residents live inside the rock rather than on it. The columnar joints are laced with hairline fractures that widen every time the turbines beneath change pitch; inside those fractures, communities of thread-like fungi and chemolithotrophic bacteria harvest the slow oxidation of native iron and manganese. Their metabolism releases minute quantities of heat—enough to keep a hair’s-breadth film of water liquid even when the valves above vent super-cooled steam. The result is a living glaze that sheens the column walls at dusk, giving the basalt the look of wet slate even when it is dry.

A few millimeters beneath the tephra fans, capillary networks of fungal hyphae form translucent mats that bind loose grains into spongy plates. These plates act like biological louvers: they open under the weight of a new rain, letting water through; they close again as tension rises, holding a hidden reservoir of moisture that the rush roots tap between storms. When the plates shift, they emit a faint parchment rasp that the blind cricket interprets as a weather forecast, timing its emergence to the moment the valves fall silent.

The cricket itself is only the largest of several orthopteroid forms. A smaller species—wingless, the color of wet charcoal—lives exclusively inside the hollow rush culms. Its eggs are laid in a spiral inside the pith; after hatching, the nymphs rasp a feeding groove that spirals upward with each molt. When the valves vent, the culm resonates like a reed pipe, and the nymphs press their bodies against the inner wall to ride out the pressure wave. The hollow reeds therefore double as both nursery and barometer.

At the very bottom of the corridor, where the basalt tilts toward the river, a miniature delta of black sand accumulates each time the tephra sponges overflow. Here, a scatter of transparent ostracods—each no larger than a lentil—filter bacteria from the last, slowest trickles. Their carapaces are so thin that the orange of the iron seepage shows through them like lantern light through paper. During the deepest part of the night their bioluminescence—triggered by the same thermoluminescence that makes the iron patches glow—turns the delta into a constellation of fleeting, drifting stars.

Nothing in this seam grows large, yet nothing is alone. The pressure valves keep time for every living thing; the liverworts meter their capillary flow against the same schedule; the crickets feel the turbines through their cerci; the algae film brightens or dims with each sigh of exhaust. The corridor is not a habitat so much as a single, slow-moving metabolism whose heartbeat is half mechanical, half microbial, and whose every drip is counted by something that waits just beneath the next drip.
