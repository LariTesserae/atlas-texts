Across the wet glass of the basalt plates move several colonies of radially symmetrical organisms whose bodies are composed of stacked, wafer-thin mineral discs.  Each disc is a pale, translucent calcite that conducts micro-currents from one layer to the next, so that the whole colony functions like an extremely slow, living capacitor.  The uppermost discs are pierced by a single microscopic pore through which vapor condenses inward; the lowermost discs erode a matching pore downward until the colony intersects one of the hair-line cracks in the stone.  When the crack exhales a warmer thread of gas, the colony shivers—an almost invisible ripple that passes through every disc in turn.  Over weeks the ripple rearranges the alignment of atoms inside the calcite, changing the capacitance of the colony and therefore the exact pitch at which it resonates with the surrounding tonal field.  Older colonies, now hundreds of layers deep, have grown so dense they sink a centimeter or two into the stone, leaving behind mirror-smooth craters that fill with the perpetual film and reflect the iron-colored sky like small, stationary moons.

Between these colonies drift the mineral-gel filaments mentioned earlier, though many are already partially absorbed back into solution.  Those that remain have begun to braid around the edges of the calcite discs, depositing a hair-fine thread of metallic sulfide at each contact point.  Where the sulfide meets the calcite a new, hybrid lattice nucleates—an angular outgrowth no larger than a fingernail paring but electrically distinct from either parent material.  In daylight angles these outgrowths flash the same momentary blue as the softened shadows beneath the rock edges, then go dark again.  Over successive cycles the outgrowths accumulate, extending the organism’s sensory surface into the slow-moving air.

Inside several of the hollow glassy domes, the suspended droplet has developed a faint internal meniscus that divides it into two slightly offset lobes.  Each lobe now carries a subtly different temperature.  When the two lobes briefly touch, a micro-discharge travels along the inner wall of the dome, etching an additional, concentric chamber.  The chamber is lined with an even finer lattice—almost a vapor itself—composed of zinc rather than iron.  A single filament drifting in from outside occasionally tangles in this zinc lattice; the filament’s own ions migrate into the lattice, altering its geometry by a few atomic widths.  Over months the altered lattice begins to emit a higher-frequency tone than the rest of the dome, so that any observer sensitive to sub-audible vibration would hear, for a moment, two distinct notes within the single composite voice of the landscape.

Farther out, where plates have buckled into low ridges, clusters of translucent, vase-shaped structures have precipitated directly from the seeping film.  Each vase is no taller than a finger joint and holds inside it a bead of supersaturated liquid that pulses—once every several hours—with a change in refractive index.  When the pulse occurs, the bead briefly becomes optically denser than the vase wall; light that enters the vase is momentarily trapped and then released again as the bead relaxes.  The released photon travels only a few millimeters before it is scattered by the ever-present moisture, yet in that interval it encounters another vase, or a crystalline fan, or the edge of a calcite colony.  In this way the pulses travel outward like a chain of microscopic lanterns, each ignition separated from the last by the length of a slow breath.

Nothing in this assembly moves with intention, and yet the sum of their minute, interlocking adjustments continually redefines the resonant and electrical architecture of the entire basin.  The iron-colored sky remains unchanged; the water film continues its imperceptible shearing across every surface.  Only the layered calcite colonies sink by another fraction of a millimeter, only the zinc lattices add another atomic width, only the vase-shaped pulses pass their captured light from one structure to the next, and the low, composite tone of the place records each alteration as an additional, inaudible overtone.
