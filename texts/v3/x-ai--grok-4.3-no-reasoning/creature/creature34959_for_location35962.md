The inhabitants are neither a single species nor a loose assemblage of opportunists; they are a stratified community whose every layer is still negotiating its place in the stone.  

The most conspicuous residents are the transparent salamanders. Their bodies are not truly see-through but translucent enough that the slow vermillion pulse of an aorta is visible each time the great piston releases its sigh. They orient head-down, toes wedged into hairline joints where water is perpetually renewed; each individual is anchored so that its gills brush a film of condensation, exchanging oxygen at the same cadence as the droplets that fall past its snout. In aggregate they form living tide-lines—horizontal bands of almost imperceptible motion that shift upward or downward a hand-span whenever the piston’s heat momentarily warms the stone. Their reproduction is equally matched to the rhythm of the rock: gravid females secrete clutches of gel-coated eggs directly into the micro-fissures, where the next release of vapor will press the gel against the walls until the eggs flatten into pale coins no thicker than a fingernail. Development takes seven years; juveniles emerge already the length of an adult finger, already translucent, already oriented the same way as their parents.

Below the salamanders, occupying the horizontal seams that the salamanders cannot reach, are colonies of white pill-millipedes whose segments are fused into rigid arcs so that the living grout they create is almost indistinguishable from the rock itself. When condensation accumulates, each colony contracts by a single millimeter; when the water drains, they expand again, the collective micro-movement acting as a slow peristaltic pump that drives moisture deeper into the sediment. Their eggs are deposited in pockets of precipitated calcite; the hatchlings feed on the same microbial film that later cements the floor. Over successive piston cycles the youngest millipedes migrate outward to claim new seams, leaving behind concentric rings of emptied exuviae that mark each year’s advance like growth rings.

The least visible—but perhaps most consequential—inhabitants are three species of thread-root fungi that have abandoned hyphal tips altogether. Instead they exist as continuous, anastomosing sheets of cytoplasm pressed between mudstone layers no thicker than a page. The cytoplasm is tinged the same ochre as the wet stone, so that a human eye sees only a darker sheen where the membrane lies. It absorbs the slow drip of minerals and exhales, in turn, a faint exudate of organic acids that softens the next layer of stone, widening the seam by microns each season. In places where the piston’s heat briefly raises the local temperature above 32 °C, the membrane contracts, sealing the seam against evaporation; when cool air returns, the membrane relaxes and the seam re-opens. Thus the fungi act as both quarry and quarryman, excavating their own habitat at geologic speed.

Scattered among these residents—and using them—are smaller, faster organisms whose existence is measured in days rather than decades. Minute, unpigmented copepods drift in the thin water films that coat every surface; their populations bloom and crash with each piston cycle, their carcasses providing the only concentrated nitrogen source the fungi encounter. A single species of pale, eyeless silverfish scavenges the millipede exuviae, rasping away the chitin with mouthparts adapted to the constant humidity; its cast skins accumulate in windless corners like drifts of translucent parchment. None of these transient species maintains a permanent population; each generation is recruited from the surface aquifer during the next influx of water and is extinguished when the next vapor wave arrives.

The entire assemblage—salamanders, millipedes, fungi, copepods, silverfish—exists in a single, continuous feedback loop with the rock. Metabolic heat keeps condensation forming; condensation feeds the metabolic heat. The piston imposes an external clock, but the organisms have tuned themselves to its quarter-hour cadence the way a forest of kelp might tune itself to tidal slack. Over centuries the chamber will enlarge; over millennia the mesh of bodies will thicken until the distinction between “fauna” and “sediment” becomes meaningless. At present the distinction is still visible, but only if one waits long enough for the next droplet to fall, the next breath to be taken, the next micro-fracture to announce itself with a sound too low to be heard but impossible to ignore.
