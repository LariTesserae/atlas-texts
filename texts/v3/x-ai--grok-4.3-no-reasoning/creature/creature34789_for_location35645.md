What lives here is still becoming place, not occupants within a place already formed.

The living sheets are the primary residents. Each translucent, rose-pale layer functions as an organism and as architecture simultaneously. They do not grow upon a substrate; they are the substrate. They breathe through continuous, microscopic exchange—taking water laterally from neighbors, releasing water into the shared film, drinking it back again. Their silver veins pulse faintly with each cycle, a slow peristalsis that is both circulation and structural adjustment. When pressure above increases, the sheets compress without breaking, their internal fluid redistributing to maintain equilibrium. Over geological timescales this compression will become stratification; for now it is only the daily negotiation of weight.

Embedded within the sheets live the motile microbodies—silverfish-scale organisms that travel the living xylem like commuters inside a city of libraries. They do not graze so much as they sample: tasting the chemical gradients between sheets, carrying trace nutrients from one layer to another, occasionally pausing at the nodes where bladders form. Their passage registers as a faint iridescence, visible only when the sheets are viewed at an angle against the cyan mineral light.

The palm-sized, limb-less rovers move across the sheets’ surfaces like slow stains. Each one secretes adhesive pads from specialized ventral tissue, anchors, then dissolves the pads again to glide forward. They are not predators in the conventional sense; they follow chemical trails left by the microbodies, grazing on accumulations of metabolic waste. Their trails are ephemeral—absorbed within minutes—so the floor never accumulates the evidence of passage. When two rovers meet, they do not fight or court; they exchange a thin filament of tissue, briefly merge circulatory systems, then separate again, each continuing along its original vector.

Above the sheets, the inflated drifters descend on cycles that cannot be predicted. Each is a gas-filled sac the size of a small boat, its surface covered in feeding tendrils that brush the living floor like inverted roots. They do not descend to hunt; they descend to harvest. The tendrils comb loose microbodies and dissolved metabolites from the sheet surfaces, then retract them into internal chambers where digestion occurs. The drifters’ gas bladders are not static; they contract and expand with temperature changes in the surrounding mist, causing the creatures to bob gently as they feed. When they ascend again, they leave behind a temporary clearing in the living floor—a patch where sheets have withdrawn their edges to avoid the tendrils’ touch. These clearings heal within hours, the sheets slowly re-knitting across the gap.

The bladders that swell between the rhizomes are not separate organisms but communal organs belonging to the entire living floor. Each one fills and empties on its own seasonal rhythm, releasing mist that is immediately reabsorbed by the ceiling sheets above. The bladders are also thermal buffers; the slightly warmer fluid inside them prevents the surrounding paste-rock from freezing during the coldest periods, maintaining the temperature necessary for the sheets’ metabolic processes.

The occasional bright clicks that sound when rhizomes straighten are not accidental releases of tension but deliberate structural adjustments. The rhizomes are learning, in their slow way, how to become pillars. Each click represents a millimeter of alignment, a rehearsal for the weight-bearing that will come when the space above finishes arriving. Between clicks, the rhizomes remain soft and cartilaginous, bent double under compression, quietly re-knitting their internal structure.

Nothing here has a name because nothing here has finished becoming what it will be. The sheets are not yet strata. The bladders are not yet lungs. The rhizomes are not yet stone. The drifters are not yet visitors from elsewhere; they are part of the same unfinished architecture, their gas bladders filled with vapors exhaled by the sheets themselves. The microbodies and rovers are not yet fauna; they are still the means by which the living floor maintains its own equilibrium, moving nutrients, removing waste, keeping the chemical conversations flowing between layers.

The space remains cathedral-small because it is still arriving. Until the boundaries close, until the bladders synchronize, until the rhizomes remember how to become stone, everything that lives here is also the process of the place becoming itself. The distinction between inhabitant and architecture has not yet been drawn.
