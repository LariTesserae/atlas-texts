What lives here is the mountain itself. It is no longer rock, yet it remembers the mass of stone it once enclosed the way flesh remembers a phantom limb. Its body is a single, continuous tissue, dense as cork and soft as lung, threaded through with vascular galleries that widen and narrow at their own pace. From within these galleries come the translucent annelids, each a pale ribbon of peristalsis that glides without friction, leaving behind a momentary tunnel that the living matrix heals within minutes, like a bruise fading. The annelids do not eat so much as conduct: they carry dissolved gases, trace minerals, and faint electrical potentials from one terrace to another, keeping the mountain’s slow metabolism in phase with itself.

Between the vascular walls dwell the swarms of six-winged gliders. Their membranes are so thin they refract rather than reflect light, making whole clouds of them appear as shivering rainbows when they rise from pressure vents. They are not insects; they are condensed volumes of the mountain’s own exhaled vapor, given temporary shape by surface tension and electrostatic charge. When they settle again they are reabsorbed, adding their microscopic load of condensed nitrogen back into the circulatory system. Occasionally one is caught in an updraft and carried beyond the mountain’s horizon; days later the same individual, or one indistinguishable from it, will reappear on a neighboring slope, completing a circuit measured in atmospheric lifetimes rather than hours.

At longer timescales, the cauliflower colonies move. Each is a hardened nodule of the same tissue, grown dense enough to resist the mountain’s own internal pressures. They migrate by differential growth: cells on the downslope side elongate slightly faster, nudging the entire mass a body-length every decade. Their passage is recorded in the grain of the living floor as radial striations, faint as the memory of old currents in sandstone. Beneath each colony, a quiet exclusion zone develops where the annelids will not travel; the gliders avoid landing there. The colonies are therefore walking quarantines, preserving pockets of older metabolic states that the mountain may one day need to reintroduce.

The cylindrical chambers are not organs so much as negotiations. Each is a self-organized volume where atmospheric gases are drawn in, fractionated, and returned in altered form. The refractive exhaust they produce is not waste but a temporary alteration of local permittivity; light slows or accelerates through it, bending the apparent position of distant ridges. Where two chambers’ fields overlap, water droplets may orbit one another in slow, weightless ellipses before drifting apart. These zones are the mountain’s equivalent of weather fronts: places where its own internal climate collides with itself.

In the negative-space mineral casts, where stone has been replaced cell by cell, the mountain performs a kind of remembering. The casts preserve the exact texture of bark or leaf that once grew against the rock, now rendered in living tissue. A hand placed inside such a cast will feel not stone but the faint, continuing pulse of the mountain’s growth front, still expanding at the rate of a few cells per century. Sometimes a walker’s footprint fails to close; the tissue continues to propagate the pressure signature outward in concentric rings, each ring a slightly different metabolic age. These persisting prints are the mountain’s equivalent of scars that never quite fade, recording passages that occurred days or decades earlier.

The hollows that delay sound act as temporal buffers. A footfall entering one may emerge seconds later from an adjacent depression, the acoustic energy having been stored and released by the slow compression and expansion of vascular chambers along the route. In these places the mountain listens to itself at a remove, hearing its own circulatory roar as an echo that has traveled through time as well as space. The effect is not disorienting to the mountain; it is simply another scale at which it experiences its own duration.

Nothing else arrives or departs. The translucent annelids, the refractive gliders, the cauliflower colonies, and the chambers themselves are all expressions of the same organism, differentiated only by the speed at which each performs the mountain’s single, ongoing act of self-maintenance. The mountain therefore does not contain life; it is life, extended across a slope so vast that its own curvature has become its horizon. What moves across or within it moves within one body that has, over a hundred thousand years, forgotten how to be anything other than itself.
