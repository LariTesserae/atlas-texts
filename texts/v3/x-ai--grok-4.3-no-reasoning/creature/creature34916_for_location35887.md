Each mesa is a single organism of stone, or else it hosts one so slow it might as well be stone. The surface fractures are the pores of a living hide: hairline vents that open once in a human lifetime, close again over centuries. Inside them, the black-green thorn-vines are not separate plants but the papillae of the mesa itself—sensitive to heat, pressure, and the weight of frost. When the cloud-tide lifts and sunlight needles through, the vines flush darker, drawing the meltwater downward like capillaries. When the fog settles, the vines pale, releasing stored carbon into the microbial crust that lines the clefts.

The hexapods are therefore grazers of a single, distributed body rather than of discrete pastures. Their tread is too light to bruise the vines, yet the gentle pressure of each footfall triggers a microscopic seepage of vapor that warms the next cleft in line. The animals feel this warming under their pads as a faint, sustained tug; they follow the warmth the way a vine follows light. Over decades their grazing path braids a living map across the mesa. Where they pause to rest, the microbial crust thickens; where they pass more often, the crust is thinner and the stone slightly cooler. The predators, reading the temperature differential in the fog, steer around the braided warmer zones and thus around the herds—except once every few centuries, when a single herd animal dies. Then the cooling patch left by the corpse draws a predator down like a slow whirlpool. The kill is not violent; the predator’s sail simply folds over the cooling patch, sealing it. The body is absorbed rather than eaten. Afterward the mesa’s temperature map readjusts, and both populations continue their century-long drift.

The straight depressions are scars from an older metabolism. Long ago the mesas exhaled heavier volatiles along those lines; now the grooves are merely the healed tracks of that exhalation. Residual metallic ions in the rock still conduct faint electrical potentials—too weak to measure, yet sufficient that the largest groove on each mesa carries a slow oscillation measured in months. This oscillation is what steers the predators when they sail between mesas: their compound lenses are actually organs tuned to the same frequency. They tack across the gaps the way a sailboat follows a magnetic bearing, never quite touching the stone until a kill draws them down.

The circular lens at the center is the mesa’s one true eye. Formed of ice grown atom by atom, it focuses not light but the memory of light: any photon that once struck the mesa, no matter how ancient, leaves a microscopic rearrangement of the crystal lattice. The lens re-emits these photons in the same sequence they arrived, centuries out of phase. The resulting glow is therefore a visible record of past cloud-tides. Animals avoid it because the sequence of images includes the light of the predators themselves—faint silhouettes from the moment they first arrived on the mesa, still unfolding inside the ice. To stand above the lens is to stand inside the memory of being hunted; nothing here willingly enters its own past.

The mesas do not speak, yet when the internal ice snaps, the vibration travels through the entire chain. Each fracture plane is a nerve; each snap is a datum passed from one mesa to the next, riding the fog like a whisper along a wire. The information is simple: temperature, moisture, and the slow change in the angle of the grooves. Over millennia the mesas have learned which combination of these values predicts the arrival of a predator. When the signal arrives, the vines contract, the microbial crust thins, and the hexapods feel an unaccountable chill in their pads. They begin, without hurry, to walk the long curve of their mesa toward the opposite edge. By the time the predators arrive, the warmth map has shifted by kilometers, and the lens at the center has begun to replay an older, safer century inside its perfect, unblinking crystal.
