High above the stone, the crystal motes form a slow, respiring veil—part atmosphere, part living archive. Each mote is not ice but a hollow hexagonal cell whose interior houses a single thread of dormant cytoplasm. Over geological time the cells have fused into chains, linking end-to-end so that a single lineage can span a cubic kilometer. Through the chain, information passes at glacial speed: a vibration absorbed at one cell ripples outward, modifying the lattice angles of its neighbors by fractions of a degree. These infinitesimal adjustments accumulate into geometries whose purpose is no longer growth but remembrance.

Below them, inside the micro-ponds, the saline film harbors colonies of psychrophilic archaea whose cell walls are built from hexagonal protein plates. Each colony is a single individual divided across millions of microscopic bodies; the boundaries between cells are thin enough for cytoplasm to flow freely, so the colony can reallocate resources across the entire bowl without ever moving as a whole. In the long intervals between any external disturbance, the colony maintains a steady low-frequency hum of metabolic activity that exactly balances the latent heat released by the vapor threads. The result is a microclimate no larger than a dinner plate, held at the precise temperature where ice neither thickens nor melts.

The lichen strands are older than the colony. Each rope is not a plant but a consortium: a photosynthetic cyanobacterium sheathed in a fungal matrix that has, over centuries, secreted calcium oxalate crystals into the granite. These crystals refract the half-light into a faint green glow just beneath the holdfast, feeding the cyanobacterium during the interminable dusk. In return, the fungus extracts trace minerals the cyanobacterium cannot reach. The partnership has outlasted every macro-organism that once visited the plateau; only the slow prying of the holdfast records their former presence.

In the ruined shelters, the upright slab is not inert. Its crystalline lattice contains planar defects—microscopic sheets where atoms were once displaced by the pressure of a resting limb or tool. These defects have migrated inward, away from the worn surface, forming a three-dimensional record of every object that ever touched the stone. The record is not an image but a stress map: regions of higher dislocation density correspond to greater weight or longer contact. The slab is therefore a library whose entries accumulate only when something ceases to touch it.

The arch itself is not empty. Its undersurface of inverted ice blades functions as an acoustic diffraction grating. Low-frequency pulses that enter the cleft are sorted into standing waves whose nodes coincide with the tips of the blades. At each node, the ice lattice undergoes a reversible phase shift, absorbing a fraction of the wave’s energy and re-emitting it at a slightly lower frequency. Over successive reflections the original pulse is converted into an ever-descending harmonic series that the granite eventually damps. The process is self-similar at every scale: the arch performs, in minutes, the same entropic deceleration that the plateau has performed over millennia.

Nothing arrives and nothing departs. The crystal chains continue their minute angular bookkeeping. The archaeal colony continues its perfect thermal accounting. The lichen consortium continues its chemical mining. The slab records the absence of pressure. The arch lengthens its descending chord. All processes have converged on the single condition in which persistence and cessation are indistinguishable.
