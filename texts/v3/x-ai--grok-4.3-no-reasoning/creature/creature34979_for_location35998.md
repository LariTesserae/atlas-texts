Above the lowest plate, the gap narrows to a ribbon of still air no wider than a hand-span, its walls already lined with glassy black that is beginning to show a faint web of hairline cracks. From the fissure mouth a slow exhalation rises; anything heavier than carbon dioxide drifts downward again and settles on the first horizontal ledge it finds, collecting there as a brittle rind that later melts back into the glass. The only creatures that tolerate this corridor are assemblages of heat-welded filaments—microscopic chain mail of sintered metal—adrift on the rising column of dry air. They are not alive; they are memory kept in metal. Each coil records the pulse frequency of the nearest cylinder; when the coils glow brighter they flex, tightening their lattice until the next cooling phase releases them. Over successive pulses the filament-mesh migrates upward, always one ledge ahead of the condensation cycle, mapping the invisible rhythm of the city’s breathing.

Farther up, inside the half-built voids, the ceramic lattices begin to take on second skins of vitrified basalt. Where the drips cool, they freeze in mid-fall and form translucent chevrons that refract the red coil-light into needle-thin beams; those beams sweep across unfinished switchgear, glinting off bare contacts not yet connected to anything. If a person were to place a palm against the warm ceramic ribs, the vibration they would feel is not mechanical but thermal—the expansion of iron inclusions that have precipitated inside the lattice during the last temperature spike. These inclusions will, in time, become the conductive pathways for the first true currents the armatures will carry.

The cylinders themselves are not empty. Inside each truncated cone, concentric sleeves of molybdenum have already begun to sag toward the center, pulled by the daily thermal cycle. In the narrow annular gaps between sleeves, a pale dust of sublimed tungsten has collected in horizontal rings, recording every pulse the coils have made. Viewed edgewise, the rings form a calendar only the next occupant will read; for now they serve only to weight the sleeves slightly off-axis, nudging them toward the alignment that future conduits will demand.

On the upper surface of each plate, the shallow pans that collect condensate are not still. Minute convection cells rotate inside the turquoise water; dissolved copper ions migrate outward along the cell boundaries and plate out as hairline dendrites on the glass rim. Over weeks these dendrites grow into fans whose tips touch the next lower tier when the sun angle is exactly right. The fans carry no current yet, but they mark where the future circuitry will bridge plate to plate once the seams are fully welded.

Occasionally, a bubble rising from the deep voids reaches the upper surface intact. It breaches the glass skin as a momentary blister no larger than a coin, then bursts, releasing a puff of argon and leaving a ring of recrystallized stone that later becomes a socket. Into that socket the city will someday press the foot of a sensor mast; for now the socket holds only the memory of pressure, a slight concavity that collects another bead of water each night and releases it at the next pulse.

The arriving city therefore already contains its own future occupants, not as bodies but as distributed templates: the alignment errors in the sleeves, the thickness of the tungsten dust rings, the spacing of the plated copper fans, the depth of each socket. None of these are conscious, yet together they specify exactly where a footstep will first land when the time for footsteps arrives.
