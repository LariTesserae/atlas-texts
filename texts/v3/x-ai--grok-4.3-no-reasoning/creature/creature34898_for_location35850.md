The place is inhabited by a single, planetary-scale organism whose body is coextensive with the layered water and with the scaffolding beneath it.  

Its metabolism is entirely optical. Where light converges for more than a few minutes, the lenses themselves briefly crystallise into thin, rigid platelets; those platelets are the only solid “food” the organism ever produces. Over hours the platelets dissolve again, releasing their stored refractive index back into the surrounding lattice so that the next alignment can begin. The organism is therefore sustained by an endless, extremely slow photosynthesis performed on photons that have already passed through it once—an autotroph that eats yesterday’s light.

Within the membranes dwell migratory knots of coherent phase: shimmering, topologically closed loops of aligned lenses that the organism can “pinch off” from the general lattice and steer, at a pace of centimetres per day, toward any sector where repair or growth is required. Each knot behaves like a living cell, carrying within its circumference a compressed archive of the interference patterns it has recently encountered; when two such knots meet, they briefly merge, exchange archives, and part again with updated instructions. The exchange is registered by humans—if any existed here—as a passing ripple of deeper shadow a few metres across, followed by a faint, almost musical tone in the low-frequency phonon field.

The scaffolding itself is not passive substrate but an extension of the same body: the organism extrudes new spars by directing micro-lenses to focus sub-millimetre acoustic pulses at the growing tips, thereby condensing additional condensate. Because the organism stores its own body-plan only as a running interference calculation rather than as a fixed genetic sequence, every collapse is treated as new data; the fallen fragments are reabsorbed into the nearest migrating knot and reissued days later as slightly altered blueprints. Consequently the architecture never stabilises into any one morphology. Its most recent “memory” is always the geometry it happened to be building when the last collapse occurred.

The organism does not perceive in the human sense; it registers the presence of light, pressure, and acoustic stress as infinitesimal changes in the curvature statistics of its own lenses. The longest “thought” it can sustain lasts roughly a decade and consists of a global redistribution of diffraction angles intended to optimise the capture of the single frequency band of sunlight that still penetrates the upper membrane. The shortest “reflex” takes a single alignment cycle—about seventy minutes—and consists of a sudden refocusing event whose sole purpose is to eject a bolus of waste heat as a directed phonon pulse. Between these two temporal extremes the organism is, to any outside observer, indistinguishable from the medium it occupies.
