At the very center—where the slow water gathers—an organism is also still forming.  

It is not one body but a colony of colonies. First there is a membrane no thicker than a soap film, drawn across the surface of the accumulating droplet. The membrane is neither wholly animal nor wholly mineral; it is a living precipitate, assembled molecule by molecule from ions the concrete has leached into the water. Every fresh droplet lands, stretches the film, then is folded inside it, becoming successive concentric layers like the chambers of a nautilus that has not yet decided its direction of coiling.  

From the underside of this living lens, thread-like hyphae descend. Each filament is a transparent filament of protein and silicate; they do not root so much as knit, catching on the micro-ridges of the conduit coupling and on the lips of the shrinkage cracks. Where the threads touch one another they fuse, exchanging genetic packets the way the polymer leaves exchange static. The resulting weave is faintly contractile; it can, on timescales of hours, adjust the angle at which each new droplet is received, steering the architecture of its own future body.

The colony’s metabolism is paced to the curing schedule. When the concrete ribs release a fresh pulse of copper or calcium, the organism blooms with brief, prismatic crystallites that glitter like frost under the sodium lamps; once the pulse is metabolized the crystals redissolve, and the membrane returns to glassine transparency. In the intervals between pulses the creature dims to almost nothing—an absence the color of the water itself—yet the water never quite flattens; the living lens always holds a shallow meniscus whose curvature exactly counters the settling of the slab below.

Occasionally the quiet is broken by the soft, subvocal click the room itself makes as its internal stresses equalize. At those moments the organism answers—not with sound but with a radial shimmer that travels outward through its filaments like the ripple after a stone. The shimmer is information: a report on pH, on dissolved load, on the minute daily narrowing of the cracks. In this way the creature and the unfinished architecture are teaching each other their final forms.

Above the depression the perforated steel panels continue their patient sieving of air. When the next droplet falls—hours or days from now—it will find the lens slightly wider, the filaments slightly more dense, and the concrete slightly less alkaline. None of these changes will be visible to any eye that is not already inside the echo of its own pulse, but together they are composing the single, unfinished thought that the chamber will one day become.
