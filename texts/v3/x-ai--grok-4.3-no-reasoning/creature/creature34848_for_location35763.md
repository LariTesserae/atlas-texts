There are people here, but you see them first as motion rather than shapes: a woman brushing a child’s hair in a sliver of vine-light, the soft clicks of her comb more visible than her face; a boy pulling a rope hand-over-hand across a bridge, body angling outward so far he seems to hang by will alone. They live inside the city the way air lives inside a flute, never still yet never loud, voices pressed into the stone until every spoken word becomes part of the long, choral hum the canyon keeps.

Their skin is pale from never meeting the sun above the slit, a soft dust-coloured pallor that makes the luminous veins in the leaves stand out like veins of moonlight. They mark the years not with calendars but with salt: each child receives a thin bracelet of the mineral that sweats from the rock, and when the bracelet breaks the child is considered old enough to walk the highest walkways alone. The bracelets are stored in low niches beside every family door; a whole household’s history can be read from how many are stored and how many are still worn.

The goats are almost part of the same family line. Each household keeps one animal that climbs with them at birth and dies with them at death; the animal’s horns are notched with the same number of marks as the salt bracelets. When the goat is too old to climb, it is carried down to the fountain plaza and fed from a shallow basin carved so the water that falls from the seam never splashes the floor. The goat drinks, lies down, and the family stays beside it until the breathing stops. Its horns are then set into a wall niche facing the street, a reminder that every threshold belongs to both bone and stone.

There are no priests, only listeners. Twice a year, when the light crosses the fountain at exact midday, the oldest women sit around its rim and place their palms on the stone to feel the trickle’s constant note. If the note changes, even for an instant, it is taken as a sign that the seam is moving and a new cistern must be opened elsewhere. The women then rise and begin walking together, palms still brushing walls, following the change in resonance until they find a patch of stone where the hum is thinnest. There they scratch a small X with a silvered pebble and leave it; within a month the young men will have cut a new basin exactly where the X lies, and the city will have added another layer to its memory.

Children are born in the galleries where the tunnel bears live. The bears’ slow breathing is said to ease labour, and the mothers believe the child will inherit some of that subterranean patience. The infants are swaddled not in cloth but in dry vine leaves dusted with the same luminous pollen, so the first thing they see above the cradle is the faint green glow of their own breath moving the leaves. When the child is strong enough to hold its head up, the mother plucks a single leaf, presses it to the baby’s mouth, and the infant drinks the thin sweetness; from then on the child carries the scent of that leaf in its skin and is recognised by goats and birds alike as belonging to this particular hollow in the rock.

At night—no true night, only the slow dimming when the slit of sky goes from dust to charcoal—the people hang small clay lamps filled with rendered goat fat from the walkways. The lamps are never lit all at once; instead, each family lights one lamp and lets its glow travel along the vine until the next family sees it and lights their own. The result is a creeping wave of small flames moving through the city like a held breath. When the last lamp is lit, the canyon carries a single drifting constellation for perhaps an hour; then the first lamp is pinched out and the darkness travels the other way until the stone returns to its permanent twilight.

There are names for the city, but they are not spoken above a whisper. The oldest name is simply the sound the fountain makes when the light touches it: a soft, two-note syllable that rises and falls with the trickle. Younger names describe what the city has done—how many new passages were opened in a grandmother’s lifetime, how many goats have died on the highest ledge. When two people meet on a bridge and wish to speak of the city itself, they place their fingers on the same groove in the stone and slide them along it together; the length of the shared slide tells how much of the city’s story they are choosing to hold in common at that moment. The rest remains unspoken, carried in the rock until someone else needs it.

And the rock does carry it. Every footstep, every chisel mark, every breath of the tunnel bears leaves a pressure that the stone remembers longer than any single life. In the lowest galleries, where the walls have grown so thick that sound itself must turn corners to reach the surface, you can lay your palm against the cool stone and feel, very faintly, the accumulated weight of centuries of bare feet crossing the same thresholds. It is not a vibration so much as a direction, a slow drift of memory moving from the high walkways down toward the fountain, as if the entire canyon were still settling, still becoming itself, still learning how to dream in a single continuous body of living rock.
