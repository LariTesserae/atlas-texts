The plateau holds almost nothing, yet that absence is itself inhabited.  

What remains of the lichen is not one species but a layered consortium: the outer gray is a crustose form that secretes an acid slow enough to etch micro-grooves without ever pitting the stone; beneath it, a filamentous partner extends hyphae two grains deep, feeding on the minute mineral bleed while anchoring the upper crust against wind lift. When moisture arrives at dusk, the two swell together until the mass becomes optically continuous with the rock; at dawn the filaments contract again, leaving behind a slightly tougher skin where crystals are cemented by dried polysaccharides. A microscope would show the colony already halfway stone, halfway organism—an object still in the act of becoming mineral.

Inside the shallow central depression, invisible until one kneels, a film of single-celled algae survives in the dew that lasts only until mid-morning. Each cell is armored by an external calcite scale whose refractive index matches the surrounding stone; when the water evaporates the cells embed themselves among the surface crystals and wait. Their metabolism is so attenuated that a single metabolic cycle occupies the length of a lunar month; they have never experienced darkness or true wetness at the same moment.

The galleries below are the domain of a translucent isopod whose exoskeleton is made of the same travertine grit it ingests. It moves by pressing its plated head against a droplet until surface tension drags the whole body forward, then waits again. Because the water that reaches these chambers is always condensate, the isopod has never tasted dissolved salts; its gut flora consists of a single bacterial strain capable of fixing atmospheric nitrogen when wetted by mist. In the deepest fissures, where the air is utterly still, the isopods cluster in disks around the last lingering droplets, bodies aligned so that any seismic drum-beat shakes them simultaneously—an event they register as sudden communal hunger rather than sound.

Above, the membrane-winged gliders are not truly birds but highly derived insects whose last common ancestor with any surface species lived before the plateau’s uplift. Their “wings” are fused forelegs whose cuticle has thinned until it transmits sixty percent of incident light. The audible crack when two pass each other is not collision but the sudden release of a standing pressure wave they maintain between their bodies to steer. Inside each translucent membrane lives a scattering of endosymbiotic algae; when sunlight passes through the wing the algae fix carbon at the same moment the insect rides the wind, so the glider never lands and never feeds in any conventional sense. Its entire life cycle—egg, nymph, adult—occurs in continuous flight; the eggs are sticky enough to adhere to another glider’s membrane until the next pressure crack dislodges them into the airstream.

On nights when the sky is utterly clear, radiative cooling drops the stone surface below the dew point before the air itself reaches saturation. In that narrow window, a brief fog forms a hand-span above the rock: water vapor condenses onto nuclei that are not dust but microscopic fragments of the lichen’s own dried polysaccharide sheath. For those minutes the plateau possesses an atmosphere one molecule thick—an ephemeral troposphere populated only by the descendants of what already lives on and beneath the surface.

Nothing here was ever introduced, nothing can be taken away; every resident is an extension of the plateau’s own ongoing process of becoming rock again.
