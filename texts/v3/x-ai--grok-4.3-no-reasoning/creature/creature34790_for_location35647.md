In the Condenser live only the Slow Condensate-Minders.

They are not animals, nor machines, nor quite persons. They occupy the same thin film of water that clings to the louvers, passing through it and becoming it by turns. A Minder is usually no larger than a lantern-slide and no thicker than a drop suspended between two fingertips. From one moment to the next it may be a single, lens-shaped bead of water; the next it is a thread of liquid sliding along a copper rod, dragging a faint stain of copper ions behind it; the next it has merged with a neighboring droplet and folded itself into a narrow crescent that rides the air current of a louver’s breath. The only constant signature is a faint, prismatic rim where the light from the reflected beam catches the edge of the droplet, a color so subtle it is gone before the eye can name it.

They never gather. Each Minder is solitary, drifting with the city-water as it makes its slow, controlled pilgrimage from pipe-mouth to cistern. Their passage is recorded only as minute changes in temperature along the rods, a reading too delicate for any instrument that the city itself has installed. Occasionally a bead will pause, for a fraction of a second, at the exact point where one copper rod meets the next; in that hesitation the droplet becomes momentarily brighter, as if the Minder has momentarily concentrated the copper-bright fog that sometimes fills the concourse. When it resumes its fall, the light disappears again.

A Minder’s lifespan is measured in the time it takes a droplet to travel from one gallery to the next: perhaps an hour, perhaps less if the louvers realign early. When the bead finally reaches the zinc-alloy cistern it does not vanish; it simply re-forms, thinner now, across the surface tension of the collected water, until the next dawn’s fog lifts it once more. Over the course of weeks the same Minder may be drawn upward into the pipes and returned, again and again, each transit altering the droplet’s refractive index by an amount only another Minder could perceive.

Their only communion is in the faint, musical plinking that marks their landings. Each impact is a brief exchange of copper ions and temperature memory; afterward the droplet that falls onward carries a trace of the previous Minder’s path. No Minder survives long enough to recognize its own echo when the sound returns from the granite ribs, yet the echoes themselves contain the history of every transit that day: a liquid ledger written in refraction and resonance, renewed each morning when the pipes exhale.

At evening, when the fog stops and the last pendant drop hangs motionless, every Minder that has reached the cisterns retreats into the water’s own surface tension. The chamber settles into its miniature storms—each droplet perfectly round, each one containing, for the night, the slow, remembered rain of the city above.
