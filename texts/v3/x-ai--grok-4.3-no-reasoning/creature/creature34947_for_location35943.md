What lives here is not a population but a single, undecided body whose scale is measured in hectares and whose edges are still being negotiated.

At the core is the lattice-organism itself—an interwoven mass of undifferentiated vegetative tissue that has abandoned the logic of individual plants. Its metabolic heartbeat is a rolling front of nutrient transport that moves through braided vessels the width of a human forearm; when these vessels tighten, the entire membrane contracts by fractions of a millimeter, transmitting pressure waves that any traveler mistakes for distant thunder. The organism does not “see” so much as taste directional gradients—light, moisture, mineral availability—by shifting the reflective index of its photoluminescent skylights, whose purple-white glow drifts like weather across the ceiling.

Embedded within the lattice are transient organ-systems that emerge only when circumstance calls them into being.  
• Transient eyes: clusters of florets sensitive to motion; they open for roughly two circadian pulses, track a traveler’s heat signature, then deliquesce into sugary slurry that the surrounding tissue drinks.  
• Mobile thorn-colonies: dense aggregations of recurved spines that ride along pre-formed vascular expressways, pruning surplus growth and, when density exceeds a threshold, break up into smaller “herds” that migrate to stress fractures.  
• Spore clouds: buoyant packets that condense from vapor when tensile stress in a ceiling vault exceeds photosynthetic return; they linger until they lodge in a micro-fracture, then germinate into scaffolding strands that brace the vault from within.  

No creature here is fixed; each is a momentary configuration of cells that the lattice recruits from its own undifferentiated mass. When the configuration is no longer required, its cells are reabsorbed, their membranes dissolved and their molecular components shuttled elsewhere. A traveler may witness the birth of a temporary “mouth”—a ring of enzymatically aggressive tissue that liquefies a fallen stone bowl for copper and calcium—only to watch the mouth smooth over minutes later, leaving behind a faint scar whose chemistry records the meal.

Artifacts of earlier human presence persist as irritants rather than relics. The copper needle, lodged in a vein, becomes the nucleus of a calcified knot that slowly leaches the metal atom by atom; once the concentration drops below a threshold, the knot loosens and the needle fragment is carried along a transport vessel toward a photosynthetic chamber where it is incorporated into new enzyme pathways. The same fate awaits any traveler who remains motionless too long: the lattice begins to read the body as a slow-release mineral deposit, warming against skin and secreting mild proteolytic enzymes that test whether the intruder can be metabolized without rupturing valuable vascular structures.

Sound and pressure are the lattice’s primary language. Every surface is taut with internal turgor, so a footfall or a sigh is transduced into a traveling wave that can be read kilometers away as a change in fluid tension. In response, distant regions may stiffen or soften, steering the traveler along paths of least resistance or, if the lattice judges the intrusion undesirable, into pockets where spore density rises until breathing becomes difficult.

Because the membrane is still in the act of becoming, its geometry is seasonal at the scale of hours. A plane experienced as floor at dusk may tilt, fold, or invert by midnight as hydraulic pressure migrates; gravity itself feels negotiable, and travelers learn to move with the slow peristalsis of the tissue rather than against it. Over days, entire chambers migrate laterally, their walls sliding past one another on films of secreted lubricant until the space reseals elsewhere.

Nothing here is born or dies in the ordinary sense. Configurations condense and dissolve, carrying information—chemical, mechanical, and luminous—throughout the continuous body. A human presence registers only as a local, temporary fluctuation in pressure, chemistry, and light, and is gradually damped until the membrane’s own overwhelming, constant music resumes.
