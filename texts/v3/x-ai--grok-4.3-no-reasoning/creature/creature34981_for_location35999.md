In the spaces between the living planks, the air is cool, still, and slightly acidic with the scent of slowly oxidizing sap sugars. Where the grain has closed over its own knots, the surface is smooth enough to reflect the filtered daylight in soft, brassy patches; elsewhere the timber still pushes itself outward in minute, invisible increments that can be felt only as a faint, continuous warmth under bare skin.

Beneath the floor, in the root cavity where two wide buttresses meet, a single sheet of pale, luminous mycelium has thickened into a translucent membrane. Microscopic air canals thread through it like capillaries, carrying CO₂ toward the exterior and O₂ inward. When the membrane contracts, the collapsing vesicles produce the soft pops already mentioned; when it relaxes, the membrane glows a little brighter for a moment, a pulse so slow it could be mistaken for the flicker of passing clouds. The root surfaces that bracket the cavity are armored with clusters of tiny, downward-pointing lenticels, each no larger than a pinhead, through which the mycelium extends hair-thin filaments. These filaments do not parasitize the wood; they trade fixed nitrogen for the carbohydrates the lattice leaks into the humid space. The exchange is quiet, continuous, and slightly exothermic; the warmth pools in the cavity, rising along one buttress until it escapes through a knot-hole in the floor above.

At that knot-hole—now a dark eye in the bronze planks—lives a clutch of three segmented worms, each the length of a finger. Their bodies are translucent; the gut contents show as a slowly rotating helix of chewed cambium. They move by everting the anterior segments, pressing new tissue against the living wood, then drawing the posterior segments forward like an accordion. Each advance leaves a microscopic smear of cellulases that softens the newest xylem layer just enough for the worms to ingest it. The worms are not pests; the tree responds to their grazing by laying down denser late-wood, so the knot-hole is ringed by a succession of ever-finer annual traces. From time to time a glider, riding an updraft through the cavity, brushes the worms with one translucent wing. The contact is so light it registers only as a momentary dimming of the mycelial glow, yet the worms pause, their segments flattening against the wood until the membrane’s pulse resumes.

Higher, where two horizontal boughs have fused into a single walkway six meters wide, a colony of six-legged grazers has paused. The largest individual carries a pale scar across one shoulder where earlier scraping stimulated a callus ridge. The ridge now functions as a living map: the scar tissue is slightly more resonant than the surrounding wood, so when the animal’s six feet strike it in sequence the vibrations travel farther along the bough than they would through ordinary timber. The herd listens to these returning echoes the way a sighted creature reads shadows; they adjust their grazing paths without ever lifting their heads. Their teeth, four small chisels arranged in a shallow arc, leave crescent-shaped depressions that begin to fill within hours. The depressions collect a thin film of rainwater that the living floor absorbs through lenticels spaced like the pores of skin. The water carries dissolved terpenes downward, where the mycelium below converts them into additional fixed nitrogen. The cycle—grazer to callus to water to nitrogen—closes without any participant registering more than a fractional change in local chemistry.

Inside one of the great trunks, the social birds have shifted their whistle map. A new family has claimed a cavity halfway up the column; their pitch, a steady 420 Hz, overlaps the 417 Hz of the neighboring family. The two tones produce a slow beat frequency that pulses once every three seconds through the resonant wood. The beat travels downward as a standing wave until it meets the upward-traveling warmth from the mycelial cavity. At the meeting point the wave compresses the air inside a resin duct, squeezing out a single, microscopic droplet that beads on the inner surface. The droplet does not fall; surface tension and the duct’s slight upward curve hold it in place. Over days the droplet will darken and polymerize into a new layer of amber, sealing a minor fracture before the next seasonal rain can widen it. The birds never notice the droplet; their census continues, the beat frequency adjusting as new families arrive and old ones merge.

At the lattice margin, where raw cambium still meets the older, darker wood, the growth front advances by three hundred microns each night. The new cells are laid down in files so straight they could be mistaken for the ruled lines of an architect’s drawing, yet each file is offset from its neighbors by half a cell diameter, producing a staggered brickwork whose seams will vanish only after months of lignification. A solitary glider, its wings nearly invisible against the dim light, has settled on the cambium itself. The glider’s legs are tipped with microscopic hooks that engage the soft cell walls without piercing them. It drinks the thin film of sugars exuded by the dividing cells, then lifts off again, leaving a temporary depression that the cambium fills within minutes. The depression registers as a faint cool spot on the thermal map the grazers read through their feet; they detour around it, their six-legged gait adjusting by a single stride. The entire interaction—glider, cambium, grazers—lasts less than the time it takes for a resin vesicle to collapse somewhere in the root cavity three hundred meters below.

The lattice therefore does not contain life so much as it is composed of simultaneous, overlapping acts of maintenance. Each process—mycelial respiration, bird census, grazer scar-mapping, glider feeding, cambial advance—occurs at its own scale and speed, yet all of them are tuned, by the simple necessity of remaining inside the living structure, to amplitudes and frequencies that do not cancel one another. The silence is not an absence but the audible signature of that tuning: a city whose every citizen is also its building material, and whose every repair is also its next expansion.
