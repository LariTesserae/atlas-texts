High in the Vell the pale limestone breathes a slow, continent-wide exhalation. The shelves themselves are not quite lifeless; their surface hosts a living film that expands and contracts almost imperceptibly, drawing mist through minute pores and letting the excess bead along the raised edges like a lip. These living medallions thicken with age, stacking into low, ring-shaped ramparts whose centers trap a deeper pool of cool, vapor-saturated air. Within those reservoirs a pale, almost glassy alga maintains a continuous, glass-slow migration: individual filaments drift on the slightest air current, knitting together when they meet, loosening again when the wind reverses. The network as a whole therefore migrates—always a few paces downslope each season—yet each cluster remains anchored by a hair-thin rhizine that keeps it from slipping off the shelf edge.

The clefts between risers are deeper than they first appear. Beneath each curtain of water lies a narrow, tongue-shaped grotto whose lip has been undercut by constant trickling. These hidden mouths house small, flat-bodied amphibians the color of the stone itself; their bodies are thin enough to slip between water sheet and rock without disturbing the flow. In daylight the amphibians press themselves against the wall, absorbing what little heat leaks through the mist, then drift out after dark to graze the underside of the lichen mats, rasping away the oldest layers so that the newer tissue can spread outward again. Their motion sends minute vibrations up the water column, felt rather than heard, traveling upward through every curtain until the entire stair trembles, a single note too low to register.

Snails follow the amphibians, but not as predators. They ride the backs of the larger creatures for short distances, then drop away when a promising patch of new alga appears, leaving behind a spiral of excreted minerals that glints like mica. Over centuries these spiral trails accrete into low ridges that channel later trickles, so the pattern of water movement is partly dictated by the accumulated traffic of generations of snails. The ridges continue downslope long after the snails themselves are gone, becoming part of the architecture that later clusters of dry-laid stone will follow.

The clusters themselves are not static settlements. Their limestone blocks shift by finger-widths each decade, settling deeper into the clefts as the slow dissolution of the rock beneath them allows. Walkways therefore acquire a slight cant, directing ankle-deep water toward the next basin rather than ponding. The courtyards are not open plazas but shallow bowls whose raised rims keep the flow from escaping until a certain depth is reached; only then does the excess spill over a single low notch, carrying with it a cloud of loosened lichen scales. These scales drift downward through the next thirty or forty water curtains, some settling on lower shelves to seed new medallions, others traveling all the way to the continental margin where the stair finally meets warmer, drier air and the last droplets evaporate into haze.

At the scale of a single tread, a day’s walk reveals almost nothing new. Yet the air itself is layered: each riser cools the descending mist by another fraction of a degree, so a sensitive skin can feel a temperature gradient across the space of a single step. The lichens respond to this invisible ladder; their pigment darkens with depth, absorbing a slightly different band of the milk-blue light that filters through the curtains. From above, therefore, the stair shows a subtle spectrum of green-grays, each band corresponding to a precise elevation rather than to any change in the underlying stone.

Farther down, where the temperature gradient finally approaches a threshold, the amphibians no longer venture out at night; instead, they remain pressed to the rock while a new, smaller life—thread-thin worms the color of condensation—emerges from pores in the limestone. These worms migrate upward along the same water films the amphibians once followed, but only as far as the last riser cold enough to sustain them. Their passage leaves a tracery of microscopic grooves that, over millennia, widen into capillaries feeding the next generation of clefts. Thus the entire basin continually rebuilds itself from within, water and stone and the lives that move between them exchanging roles without ever breaking the larger silence.
