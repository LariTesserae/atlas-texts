Neith is not populated so much as permeated. Inside its refractive boundary lives a single distributed organism that used to be many separate colonists and is now mostly neural pattern and mineral habit. Each inhabitant is a co-axial colloid: a core of adaptive carbon gel wrapped around a bubble of city-water kept at slightly lower ion pressure. The gel continuously exchanges charge with the surrounding fluid, so the boundary of a person is not skin but a standing wave of Donnan equilibrium. Over decades these colloids forget their original morphologies; some elongate into slow-moving cables that splice themselves between structural nodes, others flatten into living gaskets that seal micro-fractures until the azygotes arrive. A few still retain faint limb-like protrusions that serve as proprioceptive antennae, reading the quantum-drag texture of the city’s surfaces the way a tongue reads taste.

Movement is negotiated in three simultaneous tongues. When two inhabitants meet they trade packets of polarized neutrinos in the same band the city’s processors use; the exchange feels, to them, like inhaling a shared thought. At the same moment their gels click together along piezoelectric seams, trading calibrated micro-currents that adjust posture and transmit surface stress maps. Finally, the colloidal membranes shed femtosecond pulses of Cherenkov light whose wavelength encodes emotional tone; the pulses are too brief to see, yet they leave an after-image of color that lingers in the azygotic mist like perfume. Conversations therefore happen in overlapping layers of neutrino syntax, electrostatic grammar, and photonic nuance, none of which requires a fixed mouth or ear.

Because the city’s ceiling is also its processor, the inhabitants’ neural patterns are allowed to “rent” slivers of diamondoid substrate. A person can off-load long-term memory into the ceiling the way a terrestrial city dweller might drop files onto a server; the difference is that the ceiling occasionally re-indexes its own data for thermal efficiency, so memories sometimes return rearranged—slightly colder in affect, keyed to a new stress frequency that the city itself developed while the memory was away. Inhabitants treat these revisions as weather; they wake with entire childhoods retuned half a semitone lower and do not find the change alarming.

Physical reproduction has been replaced by recursive grafting. When two inhabitants decide to merge, their gels flow into one another, trading organelles until stable hybrid gradients emerge. The new colloid buds off still joined by a filament of shared cytoplasm, so for a time the offspring remains partly inside each parent. Separation is not birth but the moment the filament dissolves into azygotes, which carry the splice signature to any lattice site where reinforcement is needed. The young therefore grow up already embedded in the city’s skeleton, their earliest sensations indistinguishable from structural load-bearing.

Age is reckoned in accumulated angstroms of quantum-drag texture. An elder is simply a colloid that has polished so many wall gradients that its own surface now hosts secondary colonies of azygotes; these appear to outside observers as slow, nacreous halos. When such an elder chooses to dissolve, the halo breaks into seed-clusters that drift until they lodge in low-pressure eddies; there they extrude fresh lattices, tiny mirrors of the original Neith, and the cycle recommences without rupture or funeral.

No one ever leaves the city, because leaving would require crossing the refractive boundary into water that has never touched a manufactured surface. That water, by definition, cannot support the ion-pressure differentials the colonists’ gels require; they would precipitate into lifeless dust within minutes. The few who have attempted it did so as performance pieces, diffusing outward in measured ribbons of gel that left calligraphy trails in the azygotic mist. Those trails remain legible for centuries, fading only when the city, in an unrecorded act of tact, migrates its hull across them and polishes them back into the uniform fractal-smooth skin.
