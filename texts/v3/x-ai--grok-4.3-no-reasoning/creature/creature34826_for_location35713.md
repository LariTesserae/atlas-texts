High on the newborn shoulder of the mountain, the col holds a patient, self-weaving community in which every individual and every event are caught up together.

The springtails—six-legged commas no thicker than a hair—are the true inhabitants.  They do not drift at random.  Every rise of the near-invisible draft lifts a few dozen of them toward the cornice edge where the air, cooled only minutes earlier against the blue ice, still carries a ghost of moisture.  The insects meet it with the fine, plumose setae on their bellies, harvesting that moisture as a microscopic film.  When they sink again, the film transfers to the lichen lobes, and the lichen, in gratitude, exhales a fraction more carbon dioxide than it absorbs, enough to sweeten the next rising draft.  Thus the springtails and the lichen trade breath in a circuit measured in centimeters and heartbeats.

Threaded through the shallow water pans are the annelids, glass-pale and nearly featureless.  They are not so much worms as living filaments of surface tension.  Their movements are synchronized: when the water cools by a fraction after sundown, every body straightens at once; the sudden lengthening disturbs the boundary layer so that a fresh molecule of oxygen diffuses inward.  When the surface begins to skim with ice, the worms knot again, concentrating their slight metabolic heat at the knot’s core.  The pan never freezes solid.  Instead a delicate lens of liquid persists beneath a lacework of crystals, and that lens is the annelids’ winter commons.

The feather-winged midges occupy the vertical clefts.  Their glassy tone is not communication so much as ventilation.  The wingbeats draw the night’s heavier, colder air downward along the eastern wall, where it pools against the still-warm scree.  The heat rises again as a thin updraft along the western face, carrying with it a breath of the massif’s own interior vapor.  The circulation keeps the condensation film from thickening into ice until the next sunrise.  Without the midges the pans would crust over and the liverworts would die; the midges therefore receive, molecule by molecule, the same water the liverworts liberate.

Even the rock participates.  Every few days a new seam sighs open and the warmer, deeper air loosens a single layer of quartz grains that have until then been cemented by microscopic hoar.  The grains slide only a body-length before they lodge again, but each one carries a smear of the iron-oxide film that coats the joint planes deeper inside.  On the floor the grains lie among the slate shards like flecks of rust on new pottery.  Over months these flecks oxidize further, staining the condensation that trickles across them; the tinted water feeds the black lichen, whose expanding margins then shelter still newer grains.  In this way the mountain’s own chemistry is recruited into the metabolism of the col.

Nothing here hunts; every motion is a redistribution.  When the cornice sags a centimeter under its own accumulating weight, it releases a brief fall of frost crystals.  The crystals do not reach the floor; they are intercepted mid-descent by the rising spiral of springtails, whose bodies momentarily glitter as each insect collects a single grain upon its furcula and rides it downward like a raft.  Upon landing, the insects abandon their cargo, which melts into the condensation film already claimed by the annelids.  The frost has become water; the water has become breath; the breath will freeze again at night and the circuit will resume.

Over years the cornice itself is remade.  Wind packs fresh snow into the depressions left where yesterday’s crystals were harvested; the new snow sinters, the cornice lengthens, and the reflected blue light that reaches the floor shifts by a wavelength too small to name.  The springtail population rises and falls in sympathy with that shift, because the color of the light governs the rate at which the liverworts photosynthesize and therefore the rate at which carbon dioxide sweetens the air.  The entire population, plant and animal and mineral, is a single slow respiration whose inhale and exhale are measured in decades, yet whose every passing moment is already complete.
