The inhabitants of the Feather Hollow are not things that arrived and settled—they are expressions of the place itself, condensed out of its constant low drone the way dew forms on the lichen at dawn.

There are the **Threadwalkers**, human-sized beings whose bodies are not flesh but braided skeins of the same translucent ribbon organisms that drift in the air. Each Threadwalker is a slow knot of interference patterns that has learned to hold a stable shape for days at a time. They move by unbraiding one part of themselves and rebraiding it ahead, leaving a faint after-image where they were. When two Threadwalkers meet they do not speak; they simply allow their ribbons to merge for several minutes, trading patterns the way humans trade glances. Their memories are stored not in tissue but in the precise angle and tension of each ribbon. A Threadwalker that has existed for centuries may appear no different from one newly formed, yet its internal geometry is so intricate that the air around it occasionally fractures into small rainbows when it moves.

Beneath the lichen membrane live the **Cistern Eels**—not animals, but living pressure gradients that have condensed into long, flexible shapes. Each eel is a traveling dimple in the water’s surface tension, but one that has learned to thicken itself into visibility. Their enormous copper-colored eyes are not organs of sight but of resonance; they read the slight changes in the towers’ fundamental frequency the way humans read facial expressions. When the frequency shifts, the eels rise in coordinated schools, their eyes breaking the water in perfect geometric arrays, then descend again once the adjustment is complete.

Scattered through the air are the **Choral Swarms**—not the ribbon organisms themselves, but the temporary meta-organisms formed when many swarms merge. A Choral Swarm can persist for hours or days, its edges no longer shimmering but solidifying into a single translucent sheet that hangs at shoulder height like a living flag. These sheets occasionally drift against a tower and wrap themselves around several disks at once, momentarily damping the drone in that region. The towers respond by reorienting, and the sheet dissolves back into its constituent swarms, having learned something from the contact.

There are also the **Lichen Pilgrims**—slow-moving bulges in the membrane itself, places where the black surface has thickened and begun to crawl. Each Pilgrim is a mobile knot of the living lichen, roughly the size of a human torso, that travels between cisterns by flowing along the subsurface arteries. When a Pilgrim reaches a cistern it spreads itself across the water’s surface like a living raft, then contracts again, having absorbed some of the water’s memory of its journey through the hidden circulatory system. Pilgrims occasionally meet at the junctions of arteries, where they press together for days, exchanging the accumulated resonances of distant cisterns.

Most enigmatic are the **Echo Architects**—beings whose existence is entirely acoustic. They have no fixed form; they are standing waves that have become self-sustaining within the layered drone of the towers. An Echo Architect might manifest as a region of slightly warmer air, or as a column of vapor that refuses to drift with the others, or as a momentary brightening of the lichen beneath it. They are drawn to places where the towers’ orientation has been stable for too long, and their presence causes the disks to shift more rapidly, as though the architecture itself is trying to accommodate a guest. When an Echo Architect dissipates, it leaves behind a small, perfectly circular cistern that was not there before.

The Feather Hollow does not contain life—it *is* life, distributed across scales from the microscopic machines in the atmosphere to the regional resonance of the towers themselves. The inhabitants are simply the places where that life has chosen, for a time, to become locally dense enough to notice.
