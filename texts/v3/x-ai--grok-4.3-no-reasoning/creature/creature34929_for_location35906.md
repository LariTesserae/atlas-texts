Across each tilted gypsum plate, the living surface rises in seamless, gray-violet canopies: thin, velvety fronds stacked only a palm-width apart, their edges locked so no daylight leaks through. Each leaf is edged with faint silver veins that flex with the flow of the invisible fluid, bending just enough to funnel the medium from one side of the plate to the other. The undersides of the fronds, fine as brushed velvet, are studded with transparent, mineral nodules that remain frozen in place, catching and scattering the refracted light so that a single patch of vegetation radiates as softly as a full midday sky.

At the seams where plates meet—narrow creases no wider than a finger—fronds from opposite sides have braided together into living zips. Over time these braided tissues have thickened into stiff, arch-like ribs that keep the plates from drifting farther apart while still permitting the fluid to seep through. The ribs give off the only steady sound in the landscape: an infinitesimal ticking when, once each generation, an extra atomic layer is added and the arch shifts by the breadth of a fingernail. These periodic adjustments register as slow, bone-deep clicks that travel through the entire quilt like the ticking of a single, planet-sized clock.

Hovering just above the fronds, drifting filaments drift in their fixed, looping trajectories. Each is a hair-thin thread of translucent cells, refracting light so faintly that an observer registers them only as pale ripples crossing the canopies. Where two trajectories cross, the filaments briefly entwine, exchange genetic packets, then separate again, resuming the same arc they have followed since the plates first fractured. Their motion is the sole sign that the landscape is not entirely frozen; yet even this motion is repetition, a slow, mechanical tide that has not changed its path in centuries.

Embedded within the gypsum itself are microscopic lattices whose angles alter by fractions of a degree as the circulating medium loads or unloads mineral ions. These tiny adjustments cause the entire plane of the plate to shimmer in a continuous, heatless mirage. To an eye adjusted to the local spectrum, the ground appears to ripple with slow inland tides, though no droplet ever moves. The shimmer travels in synchronized pulses from plate to plate, passing through the braided seams and arriving again, hours later, exactly where it began.

Nothing consumes or is consumed. The fronds synthesize and thicken at a constant, imperceptible rate; the filaments drift and occasionally reproduce. Temperature, pressure, and fluid chemistry stay locked in an equilibrium so perfect that time itself is visible only as a slow layering of crystals. The entire province is a single, unbroken organism whose internal geography is the geometry of suspended growth, held in place by physics that allows change only in increments too small to disturb the stillness.
