The Lantern Vaults are home to the Slow Choir—an order of organisms that live not by reproduction, locomotion, or even metabolism as we usually understand it, but by an unbroken, single-minded act of crystallisation. Each individual is a chain of translucent polyps, some scarcely larger than a thumbnail, others as long as a man is tall. The polyps are joined end-to-end by flexible mineral hinges whose grain follows the curvature of the ribs. Where the chain touches a wall it does not grip; instead it secretes, at the rate of a few nanometres a day, a new layer of halite glass so perfectly matched to the existing stone that the joint disappears within months. The Choir does not eat, exactly; it rearranges. Every molecule the polyps take up from the old surface is immediately redeployed on the new one, so that the walls neither erode nor accumulate waste; they simply migrate outward, one lattice plane at a time.

Because the Choir moves in perfect synchrony with the city’s breathing cycle, its members have become part of the city’s pressure-regulation system. When an interior gas bladder vents, the change in humidity is instantly registered by the polyps nearest the duct. They respond by stretching or contracting one crystalline hinge, altering the local thickness of the rib by less than the width of a human hair. That infinitesimal adjustment redirects the warm updraft a millimetre to the left or right, guiding a new spore cloud to the precise location where the wall is thinnest. The whole Choir thus functions as a single, distributed sensorium, its reactions slower than a heartbeat but faster than any geological process.

There is also a second, far rarer inhabitant: the Anchor, a solitary organism whose single recorded specimen occupies the geometric centre of the five chambers. The Anchor is not composed of the same polyps; it is a dense, roughly spherical mass of black salt shot through with filaments of native copper. It does not move. Instead it radiates an almost imperceptible electrostatic field that orients every polyp chain within the Vaults. When the Anchor’s copper filaments expand or contract by a few micrometres—as they do once every few centuries—the Choir realigns across all five chambers in a single, silent wave. Observers long ago speculated that the Anchor was either the original seed from which the Choir grew or the final archive in which the Vaults’ forgotten makers stored their last instructions; either way, its presence is the only fixed point in a system otherwise devoted to perpetual, imperceptible change.

Beyond the Choir and the Anchor, the Vaults host only transient visitors: spores that have not yet joined a chain, stray droplets of collected water sliding along the bench grooves, and the rare pressure wave that arrives from the outer membrane like a single, felt syllable. Everything else belongs to the architecture itself, which continues its slow, mutual becoming with its living inhabitants.
