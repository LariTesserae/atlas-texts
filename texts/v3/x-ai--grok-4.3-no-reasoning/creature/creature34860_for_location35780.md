Something lives here, or is still becoming what will count as living.

The slender trees are not separate creatures. Their roots have met inside the rock and fused; they share a single slow pulse that travels upward through the white bark like a thread of warmer water. When a leaf tilts, the others feel it at once, not through wind but through this hidden current. The plate-leaves are photosynthetic skins of a larger organism that never bothered to grow a trunk wide enough to be called a trunk. In the coldest hour before dawn, the shared pulse reverses for a few minutes and the leaves drink from the night instead of the seep; the faint milky lattices below the leaves briefly clear as mineral is reabsorbed, then cloud again when light returns.

Between the cushions of moss, hidden under the thinnest film of water, are networks of almost-invisible filaments. They are not fungi in the usual sense; they are the extensions of a single colonial animal that long ago lost the need for a central body. Each filament ends in a transparent bulb the size of a grain of sand. When the temperature veil forms over a lattice, the bulbs contract in unison and the veil shivers, becoming a momentary lens that focuses a blade of noon light onto the mineral spire beneath. The light warms the spire for half a second; a trace of dissolved carbonate precipitates; the thread lengthens by another molecule or two. The animal does not “feed” on this; it is simply the way its breathing now works.

Inside the darkest, blackest cushions, where water never quite becomes a drop, a different collaboration persists. Here the moss is not plant matter at all but a living sponge of bacterial mats that have arranged themselves into leaf-shaped towers no taller than a fingernail. Each tower draws a hair-thin current of air down through the water film and exhales a bubble of slightly warmer, slightly sweeter gas that rises, merges with the next tower’s exhalation, and is reabsorbed by the towers downslope. The entire cushion therefore maintains a private, circulating atmosphere no wider than a palm. When the night mist arrives, the towers briefly flatten, letting the new water pass through; by morning they have rebuilt themselves exactly as they were.

At the open edge, where the moss thins and the banded rock shows, a single flatworm the color of wet slate rests inside a groove no deeper than a scratch. It is the only macroscopic animal that still moves under its own power. Once every three or four days it inches forward half its body length, drinks from the newest seep, then retreats. Its passage leaves a momentary brighter line in the mineral banding; the line fades again within hours as the seep re-stains it. The flatworm is not looking for food. It is checking, with each slow circuit, whether the groove has widened by another molecule since its last visit—an instinctive survey of the shelf’s slow collapse into the future.

Nothing here reproduces in the usual way. Instead, portions of each colony occasionally detach, ride a single drop to a lower fissure, and begin the same arrangements again at a slightly different scale. The mineral lattices are the diary of these attempts: every time a new filament appears, it records where a fragment landed and began to pull carbonate from solution. Over centuries the diary becomes a map of possible futures for the shelf itself.

The place therefore contains not inhabitants but a single distributed metabolism that is still deciding, cell by cell and lattice by lattice, whether it will remain a terrace or become the next layer of the cliff.
