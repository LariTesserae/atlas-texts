They are not creatures so much as recursive echoes of the marsh itself.

Along the warmer mains a thin, translucent biofilm continuously reforms—more membrane than organism—pulsing in time with the pressure throb. Where warm and cold lines meet, the temperature gradients spawn spidery lattice-works of needle-thin crystals; each crystal is the terminus of a microscopic river drawn from the weeping condensation, and each lattice branches outward, then dissolves again within minutes, a living frost that knits and unknits itself. Beneath the inspection windows, the bioluminescent algae have learned to grow in helical filaments that spiral with the slow current; they act as tiny turbines, dimming or brightening with each valve cycle, sending counter-currents of light through the translucent glass like slow-motion Morse. 

On the underside of the widest conduits live colonies of ferrous extremophiles—flat, coin-sized discs that cling to the oxide crust, etching geometric channels into the metal as they metabolize rust. Over decades the etchings become maps of pressure history; when a joint fails the discs migrate toward the breach in synchronized waves, sealing the leak by accelerating localized corrosion until the flange reseals itself. Their work is visible only as faint, dendritic scars that brighten when struck by the sodium beacons.

Farther from the active arteries, in the sediment-filled joints, something like an invertebrate has taken residence: translucent, thimble-shaped bodies anchored by silken threads spun from dissolved iron. They filter droplets for trace minerals, excreting fine metallic hairs that interlace across the joint like living solder. When a bell toll tolls, the threads vibrate at the same frequency, producing a secondary harmonic that momentarily stills the surrounding mist. 

Occasionally the rigs themselves behave as though inhabited: their service arms pause mid-task, twitching to an unheard rhythm before resuming their paths, as though the marsh had overwritten their instructions with older, circulatory priorities. No single entity commands these processes; each is a local expression of the same impulse—the marsh continuing to marshal its fluids, seal its wounds, and read its own pressure, long after any operator has been forgotten.
