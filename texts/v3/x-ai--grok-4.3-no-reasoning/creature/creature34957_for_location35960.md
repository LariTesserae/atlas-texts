High on the terraces live the Veil-folk, each one no taller than a hand-span yet arranged in communities of thousands. Their bodies are translucent sheets of epidermis stretched between four jointed spines; when they stand still they are almost invisible against the veins of the leaf-walls, but when they move the spines flex and the sheets flutter like pennants, carrying them on minute updrafts between cup and rib. A Veil is not one organism but a cooperative drape: the sheet itself is sterile, containing only transport tubules, while the spines house separate neural ganglia and digestive chambers. They travel by hooking their uppermost spines into the condensation threads, riding the droplets downward until the filament snaps; the sudden fall is arrested by the next sheet above, producing the soft percussive chime heard throughout the shafts.

Further inward, where the mist is thickest and the light most diffuse, move the Slow-tide. These are colonies of vacuole-bearing amoebae, each vacuole roughly the size of a melon, linked by shared cytoplasm into undulating sheets that span entire plazas. A Slow-tide sheet breathes by altering the refractive index of its vacuoles, turning patches of itself momentarily mirror-bright to reflect surplus heat skyward; the result is a gentle, ever-shifting pattern of pale flashes across the leaf-surface, like moonlight on disturbed water. When the city’s peristaltic wave arrives, the Slow-tide contracts into ridges, then relaxes again, letting the wave pass beneath it—an accommodation that keeps both the plant’s flow and the colony’s cohesion intact.

Inside the hardened upper panels, where sap convects in closed loops, live thread-thin filaments of a photosensitive fungus collectively called the Lantern-moss. The filaments do not consume the sap; instead they sheath themselves in a living cuticle that lets selected wavelengths pass while blocking others, creating a slow, internal day-night cycle measured in weeks rather than hours. During the “day” phase the filaments fluoresce a cool indigo; during the “night” phase the light fades and the cuticle thickens, trapping heat. The creaking panels therefore function as both greenhouse and clock: the Lantern-moss modulates how much heat the city retains overnight and signals—by the cessation of its glow—when the uppermost cups should begin their tilt sequence.

At the valley scale, along the kilometer-wide leaf-fields, the Rim-walkers patrol. These are not animals in the usual sense but ambulatory extensions of the cistern-bowl’s own curved epidermis. Each Rim-walker is a tongue of tissue that has separated itself just enough to slide along the rim’s inner surface, propelled by localized hydrostatic pressure changes. They carry bundles of dormant spores in pockets beneath their surface; when they encounter a breach in the living membrane—damage from wind-scoured grit or an intruding root—they press the pocket open, flood the wound with spores, and then re-fuse with the parent tissue, leaving only a faint scar that the photosynthetic layer soon conceals.

And beneath everything, threaded through the fused plazas like buried lightning, run the Vein-striders: colonies of myxobacteria that have specialized into kilometer-long cables of contractile protein. Each cable is only a few cells thick, yet when they synchronize their peristalsis they can shift an entire terrace by the width of a leaf-stalk in a single coordinated pulse. The Vein-striders do not “eat” the city; they trade dissolved minerals for the sugars the plants exude into their shared apoplast. In return they keep the structural stresses balanced, preventing any single rib from outgrowing its neighbors—an equilibrium maintained by rhythmic chemical conversations conducted through the very sap the city pumps.

Together, these dwellers are not inhabitants in the architectural sense; they are additional layers of the same living process, each one refining the flow of water, heat, and information that keeps the green tide forever arriving and never complete.
