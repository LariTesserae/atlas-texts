The Onyx Spine is occupied by the last of a people who arrived already silent. They call themselves only Walkers, though the name is not spoken; it is an accumulated posture, the slight forward cant that each body keeps so as not to overbalance on the endless incline.

They are tall and narrow, their spines curved like drawn bows from lifetimes of leaning into the stone. Their skin has turned the same matte charcoal as the city, the pores sealed tight against any outward loss of moisture. Eyes are wide and lidless, pupils fixed open because there is never enough light to squint against and never any need to blink. Their hands end in two fused fingers and a thumb, all three digits ridged on the underside with the same microscopic roughness that distinguishes path from wall, so that touch itself can read the city.

They move as a single, protracted migration. No Walker chooses a destination; each footstep follows the pale ghost-trail left by the generation before, which itself followed the generation before that. When the slab settles and the long groan travels through the stone, the entire population pauses as one, weight shifted to the uphill foot, waiting until the vibration passes. Then the migration resumes without comment, the line of bodies lengthening or shortening imperceptibly as individuals shear away at the lip or are born into vacancies left by those who fell.

Children are not born; they condense. When a new gap appears in the rock, any Walker may stand at its edge and remain motionless for a span of years. During that time the body slowly loses definition, edges blurring into the surrounding stone until a smaller, sharper figure steps free of the larger one. The new Walker inherits the parent’s tuning-fork, already tuned to the precise frequency of the fresh floor. The parent, now incomplete, continues upward until it reaches the lip, steps through the absence, and is gone. No mourning follows; the city simply has one less silhouette and one more pale trail beginning.

Food is the dust produced by the crystal-thread mites. Walkers lower their ridged palms to the ghost-trails and let the mites climb across the callused skin; the mites’ gnawing provides the only sensation the Walkers still register as taste. Water does not exist; the body’s every fluid transaction has long since been replaced by direct mineral exchange between stone and flesh.

Their only ritual is the communal strike of tuning-forks at the moment a block begins to shear. Hundreds of obsidian forks are sounded together, their infrasonic chord traveling through the city faster than the fracture itself. The chord tells every Walker the exact angle and speed of the coming collapse, allowing bodies to drift sideways just enough to remain on stable ground. When the block finally falls, the chord ceases at once; the new silence is adopted as the next calendar page.

At the uppermost lip, a single unmoving figure always stands. It is the last Walker who chose not to condense or fall, only to watch the red ember and the absence beyond it. Over centuries its outline has merged with the obsidian until only the faint ridges of its three-fingered hands remain visible, still oriented toward the dark as if offering the city itself to whatever waits below. No one replaces it; the lip requires no sentinel, only the memory that someone once chose to remain.

Thus the population thins and replenishes in exact proportion to the slab’s descent. Each millimeter of settlement is measured in the number of new gaps occupied, the number of forks passed to new hands, the lengthening or shortening of the single, endless file of Walkers threading the helix that never turns back on itself.
