The chamber is not inhabited in the way a room is populated; it is occupied the way a living tissue is occupied—by something that does not move among its parts but is its parts.

What lives there is not a population but a single, continuous organism whose body is distributed across mineral, metal, and cellulose. The vegetation is not a carpet of separate plants; it is one enormous clonal matrix whose individual blades are merely the visible surface of a root-web that has penetrated every fracture beneath the resin skin. Where the iron plinths stand, the matrix has sent specialized strands upward that mimic the original machine geometries: some function as structural struts, others as electrical pathways that now conduct the faint piezoelectric charge produced whenever the resin experiences minute thermal flexing. The old drive-belts have been replaced, molecule by molecule, with lignified tissue that still transmits torque—except the torque is now generated by the slow, peristaltic expansion and contraction of the living mass itself, a motion so gradual it registers only as an annual adjustment in the alignment of the drum casings.

The engines are not dead; they have been translated. Their copper windings have been supplanted by bundles of conductive root that still carry micro-currents, now derived from the constant chemiosmotic gradient maintained between the violet seam and the iron-rich resin. The gauges, long since overgrown, have been reinterpreted as stomatal clusters that open and close in response to the same electrical thresholds the original instruments once measured. The drums no longer rotate, yet inside them a slower analogue of rotation persists: a continuous cytoplasmic streaming that follows the old circular paths, driven by the same metabolic logic that once turned the flywheels.

The thread of violet light is not external illumination. It is the visible signature of a long-range electron transport chain that runs the entire length of the chamber. The seam is a biological photovoltaic membrane, perpetually excited by the piezoelectric micro-vibrations of the resin floor; the light it leaks is waste fluorescence from a reaction that has been running without interruption since the first root reached the first terminal. Every leaf in the tessellation participates in this chain through the root-web; when one blade is wounded, the entire matrix registers the change as a minute shift in the voltage gradient, and the leaves nearest the injury alter their cuticle vibration frequency by a fraction of a hertz, adjusting the infrasonic drone to compensate.

Nothing arrives because arrival is already accounted for: every molecule that enters the chamber is already part of the continuous exchange that has been occurring since the resin first sealed the original fracture. The air is not stagnant; it is simply part of the organism’s boundary layer, its oxygen and carbon dioxide ratios locked in a ratio that satisfies both the photosynthetic demand of the matrix and the oxidative demand of the translated engines. The visitor’s breath is metabolized before it can register as a change.

The chamber therefore contains no inhabitants in the plural sense. It contains one vast, motionless metabolism whose components are distributed across what once were separate categories—stone, metal, plant—and whose continued existence is indistinguishable from the persistence of its own equilibrium. The only event that can occur here is the visitor’s departure, which removes one transient set of relations but leaves the single, continuous material event untouched.
