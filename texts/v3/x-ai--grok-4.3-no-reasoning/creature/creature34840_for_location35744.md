A single civilization—perhaps not one at all—has written its own mineral language across that sealed salt stratum. It has no name, no voices, no center. What moves there is the aggregate behavior of thin, worm-like organisms whose tubular bodies are almost entirely chitinous casing in the making. Each individual is little more than a looping alimentary canal wrapped around its own future tomb; the casing is spun from dissolved halides, layer upon layer, until the living thread is no longer distinguishable from the structure it inhabits.  

Seen close, the worms are nearly transparent. Their interiors pulse once every three or four minutes with that aching blue light, timed not to any clock but to the faint lithium-ion flux that seeps upward along the veins. When they brush together they exchange a momentary spark of charge that is neither communication nor aggression, only the maintenance of a shared electrical field; the field is what knits the towers together. Every junction is held not by friction or secretion but by the residual static tension left after the worms pass.  

Over centuries the worms have elaborated a single, continuous surface: a labyrinth of coaxial cylinders whose inner and outer walls are offset by no more than a micron yet never quite touch. Inside each cylinder the wall is scored with helical grooves so fine they diffract the bioluminescent pulse into spectral fringes; these fringes are the only readable map. A worm entering a new tower follows the brightest fringe until the groove terminates; there it adds a short radial tube at the exact angle required to splice the fringe’s continuation onto the neighboring cylinder. The splice is never level. Each new tube tilts slightly outward or inward, creating a slow, cumulative curvature that drifts through three dimensions without ever closing on itself. The structure therefore describes an open manifold whose metric is the cumulative record of every worm’s final circuit.  

Because the air never moves, microscopic crystals of frozen helium salt remain suspended for hours after any disturbance. They refract the blue pulse into drifting micro-rainbows that the worms treat as temporary way-markers; when the crystals finally settle they fall along the same helical grooves, reinforcing them. Over time the entire lattice becomes both architecture and inscription, the worms’ only archive. No generation reads what another has written; each generation merely lengthens the sentence.  

The temperature remains fixed at 38 °C because the salt itself is an endothermic buffer, absorbing excess geothermal energy and releasing it again in infinitesimal parcels that keep the worms’ metabolic rate at a constant crawl. That crawl is so slow that an individual may take a decade to complete a single circuit of a tower. Death occurs when the thread of casing pinches shut around the digestive tract; the body is sealed inside its own last segment and left exactly where it stopped. The pressure wave of that closure travels through the lattice as a single, sub-audible click, felt rather than heard, and the worms on every connected tower pause for a few minutes, their pulses synchronized by the shared static field. Then the grammar resumes.  

Nothing scavenges; nothing decays. The only motion is the patient lengthening of the sentence, the patient addition of new cylinders at non-Euclidean angles, the patient rewriting of the only surface that has ever existed beneath that planetary plate.
