On the living bough-city called Leaf-vein lives no single people but a steady collaboration of three interwoven communities whose daily rounds are shaped to the fig’s own ceaseless re-knitting.

**1. The Limb-walkers.**  
The majority of inhabitants. Their skin is the pale green-grey of inner bark because each generation anoints newborns with a paste of cambium scrapings and lamp-black moss; the stain never washes away and deepens with age. They move in short, soft-soled strides, learned so the sole-cups that renew daily do not bruise beneath their feet. Every Limb-walker carries a short, living cane of the current year’s aerial root; it lengthens with its owner and, when it finally hardens, is ceremonially harvested, hollowed, and kept as the birth-staff of the next child. They work as water-chanellers, roof-lifters, and grafters, always one eye on the next swelling of the roots they tend. At dusk they light the galleries by squeezing luminous beetles between thumb and forefinger; the cold light clings to palms for a quarter-watch before fading.

**2. The Leaf-readers.**  
A smaller, almost reclusive caste who live in hanging bowers woven from the oldest, broadest leaves. Their hearing is tuned to the dry whisper of petioles in the constant wind; from those thousand-voiced rustles they read tomorrow’s weather, root-swelling schedules, and even distant ground-tremors far below the ridge. Most go blind by forty because they spend their nights with pupils pressed to translucent leaf-blades, learning the constellations of veins that shift as sap rises or falls. Their only permanent tools are slivers of obsidian traded up from the valleys, used to etch daily auguries into the undersides of the same leaves they sleep beneath. When a Leaf-reader dies, neighbors press the body between two fresh buttress planks; the fig absorbs the salts and, within a moon, raises a smooth, heartwood scar shaped like an open eye.

**3. The Drip-tenders.**  
Families who never set foot on solid timber if they can help it. Instead they live among the condensation-chains: braided vine cradles slung between drips so that infants are rocked by the measured beat of falling water. Their trade is the gathering of pure leaf-water and the cultivation of crystal-hardened algae films that grow only where droplets strike coldest bark. From these films they press a pigment called ghost-green used for the translucent tattoos that mark marriage alliances between terraces. Drip-tenders alone harvest the thumbnail crabs, farming them in hanging bark troughs so the spiral trails they leave become living maps—routes refreshed every night for anyone who can read the grammar of claw-marks.

**Non-human presences, equally inhabitants:**

• **Shelf-moss**—dense cushions that colonise every level surface. At night they release slow phosphorescence when the temperature drops, painting the plazas in blotches of cold light that shift as the moss is stepped on and springs slowly back.

• **Vein-flutes**—hollow petioles left intact by the Limb-walkers. When wind funnels through them they emit low, mournful chords that the Leaf-readers interpret as countermelodies to their own auguries.

• **Heartwood-ants**—tiny, translucent insects whose mandibles are the only things that can pare heartwood without killing the cambium. Limb-walkers leave shallow troughs of sugar-water each dawn; in exchange the ants keep the wood free of deeper-burrowing grubs.

• **Mist-pale moths**, whose scales are not pigment but stacked layers of living cells that refract lantern light into brief, directionless glimmers—guides for anyone crossing the plazas after dark.

The city is therefore less a structure containing residents than a single four-species metabolism: the fig supplies form and water, the moss supplies light, the ants supply maintenance, and the human triptych supplies intention, memory, and seasonal decisions about which boughs to keep and which to let wither.
