What remains here is not life in any ordinary sense, but the last, attenuated shape of an arrangement that once used living bodies as its instruments.

The lichen—its felted silver thallus—is itself an organism, yet its slow metabolism measures time in the turnover of centuries, not seasons; it accepts ice and releases water without ever accelerating. Each tussock of sedge is a colony that has survived the same series of slow desiccation events that erased the builders; every leaf tip is etched with the same faint white fur that grows on stone. The firs are not truly trees but relics of an older forest that now performs only two functions: to click its needles, and to drop seeds that will lie until a shift in the rubble offers enough grit to germinate. They register the wind’s direction so perfectly that the cant of a single trunk predicts the orientation of all others across an entire platform; they do not grow, they only persist.

Nothing here feeds on anything else. The only processes are those of phase change: vapor to film, film to frost, frost to vapor again; stone to grit, grit to the thin beginnings of new sedge. The rings are no longer dwellings; they are a grammar whose nouns have evaporated. The soot inside the pits records no fires, only the memory that fires once occurred at exactly that temperature and duration. The grinding slabs still hold the shallow bowls worn by vanished palms, but the palms themselves have been withdrawn into the same silence that absorbs every footstep.

What does persist—briefly, incompletely—is the geometry. During those instants when the fog clears, the platforms are seen to have shifted by the span of a held breath. It is not that they have moved; it is that the axis along which the original surveyors aligned them is no longer the axis the world itself prefers. The notches cut into the stone lips are still there, awaiting the next set of eyes that will interpret the misalignment as evidence of an error. No such eyes remain. The platforms therefore continue to rehearse their alignment, generation after generation of stone, long after the purpose that first required it has dissolved into the haze.

In this landscape the only inhabitants are the slow laws that outlast the bodies that once enacted them: the law that water will condense on any edge, the law that stone will accept the pattern of hands and retain it after the hands have gone, the law that an alignment drawn across a continent of platforms will continue to be tested by weather that no longer serves any eye.
