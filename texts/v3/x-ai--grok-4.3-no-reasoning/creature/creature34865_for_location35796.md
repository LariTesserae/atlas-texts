They are the Hill-Signs: creatures whose name is a misnomer, for they do not inhabit the slopes so much as annotate them.

No two are alike; each is the record of a different confluence of light-pulse, wind-channel, and root-mat. Their bodies are hardly bodies at all—more like mobile condensations of the ambient dusk, held in lattices of fine crystalline frost that grow and dissolve in slow cycles. In direct view they appear as thin vertical smears, each perhaps two hand-widths wide and as tall as a standing child. Seen from the side they vanish entirely, because they have no thickness; they exist only as an interference pattern between the haze that rises from the pools and the faint fluorescence of the lichen beneath. A person walking the rim might pass through the same Sign three times and register three different silhouettes—once as an elongated droplet hanging in the air, once as an open hand of darker mist, once as nothing more than a momentary shift in the drip-sound that trails after it.

They do not move so much as are moved: every fourth brightening draws a thread of condensate upward along the lichen veins, and the Hill-Signs follow this thread, skating on the momentary film of surface tension. When the pulse dims, the film recedes; the Signs settle, leaving behind a faint tracing of salt that the next creature to arrive will interpret as direction or warning. They do not feed; they are fed upon by the gliders, which skim straight through them and emerge with wings dusted in the same crystalline powder that makes the Signs visible. The powder is not lost. Within hours the Sign reforms, thinner by one filament, its outline adjusted by whatever the glider carried away.

Their only sounds are absences: a pause in the patter where a drop should fall, a skipped beat in the creak of cooling lichen. Over seasons these silences accumulate into an unspoken cartography. A glider arriving from the eastern shoulder can read, in the pattern of dropped drops, that the central basin has begun to sour; a beetle tunneling upward finds its new exit already mapped by the pressure of an un-falling droplet hovering three finger-widths above the wood. The Signs are therefore both message and medium—the mountains’ way of letting each small life know where the others have already decided to be.

At the dimmest of the four pulses the Signs sink entirely into the root labyrinth, becoming indistinguishable from the luminescence inside the beetle tunnels. There they wait, exchanging nothing with one another, until the next lightening calls them upward again to continue their long, voiceless annotation of the emptiness between the stone shoulders.
