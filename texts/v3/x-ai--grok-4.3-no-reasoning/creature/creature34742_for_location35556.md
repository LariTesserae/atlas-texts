The mountains are inhabited by a single kind of presence—though that presence arrives in several forms that rarely meet.

The first is a slow collective of human descendants who have condensed themselves into pockets no larger than a bothy’s hearth.  Their bodies have grown shorter and denser, as though the same pressure that rounds the stone has worked on marrow; elbows and knees show a permanent polish from years of contact with the same basalt slabs.  They no longer speak in continuous sentences; instead they trade small, bell-clear phonemes whose pitch is chosen to match the momentary tone of the surrounding stone.  When the wind is high they fall silent altogether, communicating by touch along the backs of one another’s hands, tracing the same spirals they still peck into softer rock.

The second presence moves along the outer faces, never inside the bothies.  It is a goat-like ungulate whose white pelt is translucent when wet; through the damp fur the darker shapes of internal organs can be read like ink behind parchment.  The spiral horns are not solid bone but calcified vascular tissue that pulses faintly at night, storing the day’s stored heat and releasing it as a low infrared glow.  They do not travel in herds; each individual maintains an interval of exactly two audible clicks from its nearest neighbor, creating a living map of the massif that is redrawn every dusk.

Between these two populations exists a third, smaller order: clusters of the transparent-winged moths whose larvae have begun to fuse with the rock itself.  In certain shallow, persistently damp pans the larval skins remain after the adults have hatched; mineral salts carried upward by capillary action crystallize around the empty casings, producing translucent crystalline blisters no thicker than an eggshell.  From within each blister a new moth will eventually emerge already half-mineralized, its wing veins threaded with quartz.  These hybrid adults live only hours, long enough to lay eggs directly into fresh seepage lines, then drift away on the next updraft, their bodies chiming softly as the crystal veins fracture in mid-air.

The space these three forms occupy is not continuous.  The bothies sit inside wind-shadow pockets where sound cancels; the goats patrol exposed ridges where harmonics are strongest; the crystal moths drift along the narrow bands where temperature inversions hold a layer of suspended droplets exactly level with the cliff ledges.  The only time the three coincide is during the rare evening when an entire harmonic layer collapses at once.  Then the goats descend into the bothies’ outer alcoves, the humans step outside to feel the sudden tactile hush on their skin, and the crystal moths, deprived of their supporting mist, fall in glittering showers that melt against warm stone, leaving behind miniature spirals of powdered quartz that the humans later incorporate into new pecked marks—updating the indecipherable grammar with the only alphabet the place will accept: the record of what has just ceased to sound.
