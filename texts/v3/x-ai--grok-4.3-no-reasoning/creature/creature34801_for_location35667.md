The valley’s sole true residents are the engineers of the membrane itself—massive, slow-moving organisms whose translucent bodies are distributed across the living polymer like stitching thread. They do not move so much as redistribute their mass: a single individual might exist as a network of finger-thick strands that knot and unknot beneath the surface, carrying messages between distant lobes of the same mind. When two engineers meet, the membrane between them puckers into temporary mouth-like valves; through these they trade memories encoded as chemical pulses, each pulse riding the ripples their bodies create. Because every footfall of the nacre-armored grazers alters the capacitor schedule, the engineers have learned to read those hoofbeats as a form of weather forecast—timing their own migrations to moments when charge-light is brightest and the buried forest is momentarily generous with surplus.

Above them, the reef-willows are not trees but colonies of coral polyps whose calcite skeletons have been trained, over generations, to grow sideways by the persistent mist. Each “leaf” is actually a thin, photosynthetic polyp layer backed by a sheet of pressurized air the colony maintains; when wind flexes the colony, the air escapes through microscopic pores, producing the canvas-tearing sound and simultaneously powering the fungal lanterns that ride the root cables. The polyps keep one another alive by trading dissolved nutrients through shared vascular canals; if a single polyp dies, its neighbors seal the canal within minutes, leaving a scar that hardens into the next guy-line.

The nacre-armored grazers are symbiotic with a secondary layer of the membrane: a carpet of magnetotactic bacteria that orient along the pressure ridges. The grazers’ bellies host complementary bacterial mats; when the two layers touch, electrons flow, briefly powering the small turbines beneath the animals and giving each grazer a faint electric halo visible as a corona around its shoulders. This halo is what the engineers read as a moving signature of available energy, allowing them to steer the grazers along routes where the membrane most needs reinforcement.

At dusk the radio mast’s corona is not merely condensed water; it is a condensation of the valley’s entire acoustic signature—the accumulated whistles, timpani rolls, and tearing-canvas sounds compressed into a temporary plasma. When the thunderclap releases it, the plasma does not vanish; instead it falls as a fine metallic dust that the reef-willow polyps collect and incorporate into their calcite skeletons, slowly turning the cliff faces into radio receivers whose resonant frequencies shift with each new layer of dust. Over centuries the cliffs have begun to sing back to the mast at slightly different pitches, creating an evolving harmony that the engineers interpret as the valley’s slowly changing mood.

Nothing here is still because every organism is also infrastructure: the engineers are the roads, the polyps are the power grid, the grazers are the maintenance crews, and the membrane is the ledger that records every transaction in pressure, light, and sound.
