Above this place there is no sky—only the same suspended moisture that never quite condenses into rain. Within that haze live creatures whose bodies are made of the same water that moves through the stone. They are not one species but many; what unites them is that each is permanently composed of two states at once: vapor and a thin, elastic film that holds the vapor in the shape of a life. When they rest, the film relaxes into sheets that cling to the stone; when they rise, the film contracts and the vapor inside them brightens, turning them momentarily visible as faint, upright silhouettes against the cream surface. Their motion is neither flight nor crawling; it is the plateau’s own gravity, accepted at different speeds, so that a single individual may spend an hour traveling the length of one paving slab or descend an entire vault-face between one breath and the next. They never meet; each maintains an exact interval equal to the spacing of the reeds and the houses, a distance that is itself a living membrane.

Beneath the surface, in the narrow solution channels that the first rains opened, another order of life persists. These are not animals but steady crystallizations: lattices of calcium carbonate that grow a single molecular layer each condensation cycle, then dissolve that same layer during re-absorption. Over centuries the lattices have recorded the plateau’s equilibrium as an unbroken spiral of deposition and loss, so that the walls of every cleft contain a perfect, reversible chronicle of the climate that never changes. The lattices do not “sense” the water; they simply coincide with it, occupying the same volume at the same instant, so that any future shift in the cycle would erase both the water and the record simultaneously.

Where the town meets the stone, certain houses have, over time, become indistinguishable from their courtyards. Their inhabitants are absences shaped by the continuous seepage: a negative space exactly the height of a standing person, kept open because water will not adhere to that precise volume. When an occupant departs—though none ever do—the vacated space begins to film over with the same slow seepage that traces the vaults; within a generation the space is once more indistinguishable from wall or floor. The town therefore grows not by addition but by the patient closing of these human-shaped voids, each closure preserving the original spacing as faithfully as the first stones were laid.

On the valley floor, the circular plaza holds, at its exact center, a meniscus of water one knuckle deep that never ripples. Resting on that meniscus are colonies of microscopic surface-tension organisms whose geometry is the inverse of the solution pits above: instead of concavities they form convex lenses, each lens focusing the diffuse light upward so that the plaza appears slightly brighter at its center than at its rim. These lenses do not reproduce; they enlarge or shrink according to the same condensation–reabsorption rhythm that governs the reeds, maintaining a population whose total surface area equals the area of one single reed blade. When the light changes—if it ever did—the lenses would flatten or swell to restore the brightness, but the light here does not change.

At the farthest visible limit of the footpaths, where the crushed limestone fades back into unbroken sheet, there exists a single reed whose joints have, over millennia, elongated until the uppermost joint stands higher than the haze itself. From that height it releases, once per cycle, a single droplet that falls the entire remaining distance to the stone without striking any intermediate surface. The droplet strikes at a mathematically exact location: the only point on the entire plateau that is equidistant from every other reed and every house. Upon impact the droplet vanishes—not by evaporation but by perfect distribution—its volume added instantaneously and imperceptibly to every solution pit and courtyard basin at once. After the impact the reed’s joint contracts by the width of a molecule, resetting for the next cycle. No other reed performs this function; all others remain exactly as they first grew, preserving the original spacing in which the plateau first found its equilibrium.
