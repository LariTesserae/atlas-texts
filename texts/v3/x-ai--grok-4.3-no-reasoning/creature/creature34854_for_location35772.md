Beneath the indigo ceiling, the city’s oldest residents remain the coral lineages themselves: polyps still embedded in living stone, each generation extending the terraces by a millimetre or two every decade. They do not perceive light or sound; their awareness is a drifting gradient of calcium ions and dissolved proteins. When the tide-slack lets silt drift over an open osculum, thousands of polyps retract in perfect synchrony, a violet ripple that fades before the sediment settles. Their dead kin leave behind the bone-smooth walls; the living kin add new lacework inside those walls, a pale, still-soft scaffolding that hardens only after the next warm plume from below reaches them.

Schools of glass eels are not the only vertebrates; there are also the slow, almost boneless skate-cousins whose pectoral discs have grown as wide as the radial boulevards. They glide a hand-span above the street, wing-tips brushing each ledge so lightly that the armored mollusks feel only a momentary pressure change and do not close their hoods. Where the skate pauses to feed, its ventral surface sifts the perpetual rain of dead plankton, and the water clears in a neat oval beneath it, a momentary window through which the polyps below receive a sharper pulse of mineral-rich current.

Between the highest roofs and the indigo layer lives a single diffuse colony: millions of filament-thin polychaetes, each anchored by one end to the coral and streaming the other like translucent hair. Their synchronized undulations keep silt from drifting downward; when the vent-bubbles rise and burst against the upper cornices, the polychaetes part around the shock-wave, then close again, combing the displaced particles back into orderly descent.

The ribbon predators are not truly solitary. Every few years, when the pressure gradient slackens for a season, they gather in loose spirals above the old market squares. Their nets interlock into a single drifting sphere many body-lengths across; inside that sphere, smaller prey are held in suspension while the predators take turns sweeping through. The sphere itself becomes a temporary architecture—a negative space defined by living tissue—before the current resumes and the ribbons drift apart to patrol their individual territories once more.

Deeper still, in the absolute dark beneath the foundations, a different city is forming. Here the geothermal vents have built their own stubby chimneys of precipitated anhydrite, and the vents’ rhythmic exhalations have selected for worms whose bodies are little more than muscular tubes around a single red gill filament. These worms tunnel sideways through the older coral skeleton, joining one vent to another; their castings are fine red sand that the upward current lifts and redistributes among the upper streets as a faint, rust-colored snow. The worms do not ascend; the pressure that moves the city keeps them exactly where the temperature suits their enzymes. Yet their horizontal galleries alter the flow inside the coral, so that a new osculum on the thirtieth terrace may now receive water that has traveled through worm-tunnels rather than through the original polyp channels—an invisible rerouting that will, over centuries, shift the growth direction of the living masonry above.

Occasionally a single large carcass—some deep-dwelling fish that ventured too far upward—drifts against a cornice and snags. Within hours the glass eels converge; within days the armored mollusks have climbed to the site, rasping at bone and scale. In weeks the only trace is a brighter patch of encrusting pink on the wall where nutrients from the decay were absorbed by the next generation of coral. The city does not mourn or celebrate these events; it merely incorporates them, the same way it once incorporated the empty skeletons of its own founders.
