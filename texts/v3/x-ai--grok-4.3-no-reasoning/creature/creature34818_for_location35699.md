High tableland: nothing visits now.  
The stone, once milk-pale, has begun to cloud where constant frost etches microscopic pits along every ridge crest; each facet is older than the last time it reflected the unseen sky.  
Nothing grows because nothing lands—neither lichen nor the faint, glassy strands that once carried water upward from the cups—yet the grooves themselves are no longer raw.  
Their lips have eased into low rolls, as though centuries of thread-thin water slowly rounded the flint into something that still remembers sharpness but no longer cuts.  
The cups remain, their rims still chipped but softened at the very edge, faint rings of darker stone inside where minerals left behind by evaporation drew a ghost-circle of previous contents.  
No one drinks, yet the shallow basins sometimes hold a faint residue of mist at dawn, enough to glint once before the sun lifts and the tableland breathes it back into its own slow pulse.  
Wind still skims above the ridges; the brushing sound now includes the thinner note of air sliding across the cups’ rims, a faint harmonic that lasts only while the cups are fullest with nothing.  
The tableland endures, faceted and empty, its geometry intact but quietly changed by the long passage of water that never stays and the slower weathering that no hand returns to arrest.
