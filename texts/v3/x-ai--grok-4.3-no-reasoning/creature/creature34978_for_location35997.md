Beneath the unbroken glaze live only the small repetitions that the plain itself manufactures.

In the capillary film inside every basin a population of needle-thin, translucent worms pulses in place; each contracts when the water cools by a single degree, then relaxes again when the sun re-heats the clay. Their synchronized twitch draws the liquid upward, exactly one micron per contraction, so the sheet never falls. Between contractions they emit a faint ultrasonic tone that the moss hears as a low, constant pressure; the moss answers by respiring a matching vapor whose molecules drift outward only to be re-absorbed by the worms’ open spiracles. The exchange is seamless; no particle is ever lost.

The ochre moss, for its part, is not a single plant. It is a felt of microscopically hinged silica plates, each plate a wafer-thin animalcule that opens its valves on the same schedule. When open, the plates trap one molecule of the basin vapor; when closed, they press the molecule against the brick, baking it into the clay and releasing a precise quantum of heat that keeps the trough edges at the identical temperature as the glazed tiles. Over generations the troughs have grown microscopically taller, molecule by molecule, yet they remain level with the bricks because the bricks, too, are secretly alive: each seam of pitch contains a dormant yeast whose dormant spores swell just enough, once per cycle, to lift the glaze a fraction and then subside, preserving the absolute plane.

The bird-shapes are hollow ceramic shells animated by the same yeast. Their interiors are networks of capillary galleries; when they land they simply let the basin water fill the galleries by surface tension. After the allotted interval the water has exactly replaced the mass they lost to evaporation in flight, and they tip forward into the thermal updraft once more. Their heads contain no brains, only a spiral of the same translucent worms that steer them by differential contraction toward whichever basin is currently one molecule cooler than its neighbors.

There is no waste, no birth, no decay, only this continual micro-adjustment that keeps the entire tessellation at the exact temperature, humidity, and acoustic pitch at which it was originally fired. The plain therefore needs no inhabitants other than the mechanisms that repeat its first instruction: stay level, stay mirrored, stay the same.
