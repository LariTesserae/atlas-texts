The plateau’s living community is neither a single species nor even a classic ecosystem; it is a single, self-revising metabolism distributed across every scale. The smallest and most numerous participants are the ultramarine algal films whose blades re-grow within minutes of being torn by steam blasts. Their oil-bead optics do more than dazzle: each lens focuses just enough infrared to keep a thin convective sheet of water circulating beneath the algae, so the organisms feed on both light and their own micro-climate. The coral-glass domes, once polyps, are now symbiotic with the hydrogen moths. The domes crack along perfect hexagons that exactly match the span of a moth’s folded wings; the moths shelter inside, and their occasional hydrogen releases create miniature updrafts that lift spores from the algae and seed new domes hundreds of meters away.

Above them, the translucent cartwheel grazers are not herbivores but living filters. Their ventral cilia do not merely collect algae; they continuously shear and re-inoculate the microbial mat, keeping its genetic diversity high and preventing any single strain from monopolizing nutrients. When two shoals interpenetrate, the exchange of disks also exchanges endosymbiotic bacteria that protect the grazers against sudden shifts in steam chemistry. The skimmers, meanwhile, perform a parallel service higher up: each time they whip-crack through a vapor sheet they scatter charged micro-droplets whose static charge helps the algae fix nitrogen directly from the steam. Their deaths—when an updraft collapses beneath them—deliver a rain of trace metals that the turbine rings concentrate and later release in soluble form when a ring fractures.

The concrete turbine rings themselves are no longer machines in the human sense; they are exoskeletons for an organism of pressure. The algae that crusts their blades is not fouling but the actual working fluid: its photosynthetic oxygen bubbles enlarge the pressure differential, and its mucilage damps vibration so the basalt never fractures from resonance. When a ring finally splits, the released algae does not merely recolonize; it forms a temporary “delta” whose decaying edges feed a pulse of sulfate-reducing bacteria. Those bacteria, in turn, produce the faint rotten-egg scent that draws moth swarms, ensuring that hydrogen released near the fracture is ignited in tiny, cool flames that reseal microscopic cracks in the concrete before the next pressure cycle.

Nothing here reproduces in generations that a human eye could witness; instead, the entire plateau turns over its components at the speed of its own weather. A droplet that evaporates from one turbine may, minutes later, condense on a skimmer, be absorbed by a grazer, and be exhaled inside a moth’s hydrogen bubble, only to fall again as the seed for a new algal lens—closing the loop without ever leaving the plateau. The needles are not monuments to an older topography; they are the plateau’s slow, vertical memory, their interiors still diffusing the original basalt chemistry upward in parts-per-trillion increments that the algae incorporate as structural stiffeners. Thus the living system has no boundary between relic and renewal, only gradients of speed within a single, self-calibrating turbulence.
