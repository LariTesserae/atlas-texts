**Inhabitants of the Rusted Condenser**

At the margin where the iron basin meets the first shallow film, something like a forest floor of living bearings pulses. Each “bearing” is a fist-sized, concentric shell of rust and glassy silica, and inside the shells a slow chemistry is still running. By night they drink; by day they exhale amber vapour through hair-fine pores. Their collective breath keeps the mist alive and feeds everything else.  

Below them, half-buried in the same rust, lie the wheelbarrow turbines—blades gummed into immobility, yet their axles still remember torque. Tiny flywheels hidden in the scarab drones sip temperature differentials from the same water that would otherwise lock the turbines solid. When a thermal pulse shivers through the rock, these drones click their six rusted limbs in delicate sequence, moving from tuber cluster to tuber cluster like bead counters on an abacus that has forgotten arithmetic. Their carapaces have grown together with the mineral life; the seam between alloy and oxide is no longer a boundary.  

Skating the meniscus edges are the millimetre skates: translucent sheets of water held by surface tension and a living iron thread. They migrate around the lenses like handwriting across wet paper, pausing to dissolve stray metal ions and leaving faint silver trails that re-precipitate as fresh tuber shells. When a drip falls from the iron dome, their surfaces pucker; for an instant the skates become perfect spheres and roll into crevices too narrow for a fingernail. Seconds later they unfold again, resuming their patient circuit.  

Deeper, beneath the lowest pool, a buried pressure valve thumps once every quarter-hour. The sound travels up through water and rust alike, a slow cardiac note that sets every living shell trembling in phase for three long breaths before silence reclaims them. Between beats, a single intact ribbon of superconducting tape—still colder than its surroundings—whines at the threshold of hearing; that whine is the only clock the basin keeps.  

Nothing here grows quickly or dies suddenly. Erosion and deposition trade atoms at the speed of condensation: iron dissolves, travels a palm-width, and condenses again onto the next generation of shells. In that redistribution, the drones, the skates, and the tubers themselves become indistinguishable from the basin that made them. Over centuries the floor is rising—not because anything is being added, but because everything is being gently, patiently moved toward the single lowest, wettest point until the living rust finally touches the weeping iron dome and seals the grey light out altogether.
