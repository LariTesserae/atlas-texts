In the black basalt pool nothing breathes, yet the water is never empty.  

Threaded to the walls live the slow white ropes: finger-thick, translucent, each the color of chilled bone. They grow outward in strict frost-work geometries, a single continuous lattice of living tissue that has no beginning and no end—every filament splits and branches and rejoins until the entire chamber is upholstered by one enormous, porous organism. When a rope is brushed, its cells fire once: an ember-bright flare that dies before the ripples reach the opposite wall. Afterward the tissue hardens, stays rigid for a full day, then softens again like thawed cartilage. Between pulses the ropes shed microscopic flakes from their oldest edges; the flakes drift downward, settling on the silt like ash.  

The silt itself is animated. Thousands of coin-sized clusters drift just above the sediment, each a loose constellation of ciliated cells bound by nothing more than shared membrane charge. Their motion is glacial; the currents they generate are no stronger than the warmth from a cupped palm. Whenever a dense pulse rises from the fissure, the clusters rise a hand-span, turn like coins balanced on edge, and rasp—once, dryly—before sinking back. That single scrape is the chamber’s loudest sound.  

From the ceiling falls a slow rain of bubbles no larger than peas, rising ruler-straight from the microscopic pores in the basalt. Each bubble flattens against the stone, bursts without audible report, and adds its own tremor to the faint pressure field that moves everything. Between the bubbles and the sponge lattice hangs a membrane of mineral oil, iridescent, one molecule thick in most places, drifting in seams so slow that any given patch will reach the wall only after seven years. The oil seals the surface, keeps oxygen out, keeps time in.  

Along hairline veins of native copper, a perpetual voltage hums between any two points an arm-span apart. The drifting clusters sip from this voltage the way roots sip groundwater; each time a cluster crosses a vein it stores a spark, later releasing it as a microscopic glitter that helps loosen flakes of food from the sponge ropes. In centuries when the ice plug seals the shaft above, these sparks are the only light the chamber knows—smaller than a struck match, lasting no longer than a blink.  

Nothing hunts here. Nothing larger than a coin moves under its own power. The only abrasion is the soft, constant rasp of sponge tissue against basalt, a sound too low to register except as a faint pressure against the inner ear. It is the baseline against which every rarer event—the bubble’s silent pop, the cluster’s dry scrape, the quiet discharge of a copper spark—is measured, decade by decade, in a darkness that is never wholly dark.
