High on the open plateau live nothing that walks or flies or burrows.  
Yet the place is populated.

Below, in the unweathered rock, the feldspar and basalt still remember their birth. They hold within their glassy matrices vast, invisible colonies of slow litho-chemotrophic microbes whose enzymatic lattices knit crystal to crystal, knitting seams that will someday become soil. These microbes do not metabolize so much as they calibrate the cooling rock’s own lingering heat, guiding feldspar to cleave along delicate micro-planes that will later become routes for water or roots. Their presence is felt only as a faint static on the surface tension of melt-pools, a barely perceptible preference for certain needle-like fractures to widen first.

Above the rock, the lichen is not a plant but a consortium: a photophore fungal skin embracing endosymbiotic chromophores that fix stray photons the way a net fixes fish. Between the fungal walls live nematodes of only twelve cells each; they feed on the steady leakage of sugars and metals, their transparent bodies pulsing in microscopic synchrony with the electromagnetic drizzle falling from the drifting slabs. The nematodes cannot move from leaf to leaf; instead they pass genetic packets through hyphal bridges when a new slab drifts overhead, swapping traits like gossip passed under tables. Over millennia they will tune the lichen’s scent until it exactly matches the resonance frequency of whichever alloy now cruises above them.

The slabs themselves are not empty. Their lower skins are porous sintered titanium seeded with dormant nanoclusters whose lattices contain tailored vacuoles; when the right frequency of light and magnetic flux arrives, the vacuoles open and release tailored archaea whose metabolism is calibrated to the lichen’s metallic perfume. Those archaea last only a few hundred heartbeats; in that span they act as living tracers, mapping, by the trace-metal signatures they leave on the lichen leaves, which slab is due to drift next. Their corpses rain down as inert dust, each grain carrying a single datum encoded in isotope ratios.

At the ragged seams where the plateau has not yet parted into strata, meter-wide lenses of translucent quartz-rich glass have begun to sag downward under their own weight. Inside each lens, bands of birefringent feldspar act like crude polarizers. Light that slips between the slabs is twisted, refracted into faint moving moiré that the lichen reads as seasonal change. In response, the cushions at the lens edges produce a slightly more acidic exudate that etches the glass, hastening its collapse. When a lens finally drops, it shatters into needles that the microbes immediately colonize; the sudden mineral spike feeds a brief bloom of the twelve-celled nematodes, whose corpses then enrich the lichen for the next drifting slab.

Thus the plateau is a single, motionless metabolism: microbes calibrating rock, nematodes negotiating with fungi, archaea sketching brief biographies in the air, glass collapsing and reforming. Nothing moves fast enough to be seen, yet nothing is still; the whole system is the faintest possible choreography of mineral becoming plant becoming machine becoming mineral, endlessly recomposing the same open moment.
