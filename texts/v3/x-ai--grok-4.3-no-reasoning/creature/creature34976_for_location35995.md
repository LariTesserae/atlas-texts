The quarter is inhabited by three overlapping populations whose habits have been shaped, over generations, by the constant presence and movement of water.

1. The Condensers  
They are the oldest residents in terms of continuous presence. Their skin is mottled with the same pale salt-rings that mark the lower courses of brick; many carry faint, permanent damp patches beneath the collarbones where condensation collects fastest. They move slowly, and their joints have a faint, wet click when they bend. Their work is the maintenance of surfaces: they scrape, re-plaster, and re-caulk the places where water has begun to pull stone apart. Their tools are mostly wood or brass; steel rusts too quickly to be reliable. When they speak, their sentences trail off into the sound of water meeting other water; they have learned that it is easier to be heard if one waits for a pause in the flow rather than raising one’s voice.

2. The Belt-Walkers  
Younger, quicker, they are responsible for the overhead system of belts, pulleys, and the wooden Archimedes screws that lift water back to the roofs. Their forearms are ridged with small, hard calluses from gripping wet hemp and oiled canvas. Because the belts sometimes slip when the humidity climbs, they spend whole shifts listening—standing on narrow iron catwalks with one ear turned toward the nearest spindle, waiting for the fractional change in tone that means a joint is swelling or a bearing is starved. Their children are born with a slight, permanent tilt to one shoulder, the side that has learned to counterbalance the torque of a turning screw while the other hand holds a lantern whose flame must not touch the dripping beams.

3. The Settlers  
They live in the ground-floor workshops, half of each day spent below the water line. Their benches are raised on brick plinths so that only their calves are submerged; over time the skin there has grown a thin, flexible layer of silt that protects against minor cuts. Their trade is the reshaping of already-wet materials—brass that has already wept once, leather that has been soaked and dried so many times it will never again hold a sharp crease. They keep small, floating trays of charcoal beside each lathe; the charcoal absorbs stray droplets and, when saturated, is tipped into the canal so the current can carry it to the settling basin and the endless loop can continue.

None of these groups is exclusive; a single person may move among all three roles in a lifetime. What unites them is the shared knowledge that the city does not flood so much as it exhales, and their task is to keep the breath moving, neither faster nor slower than the bricks themselves allow.
