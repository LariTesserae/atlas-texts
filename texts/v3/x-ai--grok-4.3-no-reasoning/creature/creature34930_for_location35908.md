San Cosimo del Alto is a city that has stopped pretending it is separate from the stone it grows on; every human presence is fitted into a narrow band where slate still resists gravity and water still consents to stay.  

The people themselves have become another thin, slow layer. Their bones and hands are already counted among the materials the city uses: the pale calcified layers that line the cisterns contain the grit of generations of ground callus and the dust of old laundry; when a cistern is opened the sediment smells faintly of soap and of the iron that runs in the water. Those who spend their lives at the forges develop knuckles the color and texture of the slate they breathe; their fingerprints are permanently etched with the fine metallic dust that mixes with condensation.  

Because the only level surfaces are roofs, daily life unfolds on staggered elevations that make every conversation vertical. Children on one garden terrace speak downward to parents on the roof two tiers below; a mother will lean over an iron railing to kiss the upturned forehead of a son already one flight beneath her, their breath crossing the intervening space like two brief clouds meeting and dissolving. The elderly occupy the highest gardens, where the light is least and the temperature drops first; they move in deliberate, tottering arcs among the knot-rooted pines, their weight so slight that the moss carpets they walk on barely register the passage. When one of them dies the body is lowered by rope through a vertical shaft cut in the slate; at each intermediate garden the rope pauses so neighbors can touch the wrapped form in farewell before it continues downward to the cavern of the turbines, where quicklime vaults wait to accept the final condensation of a life.  

Time is measured less by clocks than by the changing weight of water. The youngest children can tell, by the pitch of the drip that lands in an empty ewer, whether the night will be long enough for the cisterns to reach the mark that allows a bath. Adults who descend into the turbine halls carry small slate tokens—flat, water-polished discs—whose temperature they check against their own lips; the token cools at the same rate as the stone around the machines, and when it no longer warms the skin it is time to climb again before the temperature gradient reverses and the air begins to sink.  

The animals, too, are synchronized to the mineral rhythm. The rock-rats have learned to anticipate the single daily moment when the sodium bulbs flicker as the turbines surge; they emerge from the hollow rails exactly then, moving in coordinated pairs to raid the succulent leaves whose petals open for only those minutes of extra current. The blind spiders have synchronized the vibration of their webs to the subsonic thrum; when the turbines change pitch the webs slacken or tighten, and the spiders shift position without ever touching the ground. The translucent salamanders follow the condensation channels like slow silver writing, leaving behind a faint track of mucus that dries into the calcite and adds another iridescent lamina to the hanging surfaces.  

Nothing is wasted, and nothing is hurried. When a section of slate finally shears away, the fracture is already anticipated; iron brackets have been set decades earlier in the rock above, waiting to catch the new ledge and turn it into the next roof-garden before the soil has time to slide. The people who install those brackets are the same ones who, as children, once lowered the bodies of their grandparents through the same vertical shafts. Their hands fit the iron with the same patience the slate shows the water, and the water shows the roots, and the roots show the slow, downward breathing of an entire city that has learned to live inside its own erosion.
