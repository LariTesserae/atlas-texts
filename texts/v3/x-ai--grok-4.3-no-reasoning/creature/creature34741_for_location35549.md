Here is what lives in the space you have drawn.

A single resident species of cultivated *Periphyton*—a consortium of cyanobacteria and lichenous algae—has been seeded into the nutrient gel and has, over months, migrated onto every wettable surface. It exists as three slightly distinct phases:

1. A continuous, chartreuse film (no more than two cells thick) that coats the brick and the riveted iron, drinking the slow acid of condensation and in return precipitating microscopic calcite crystals that glint like frost.  
2. Tufted cushions, pea-green and velvet, that occupy the deeper trays; these cushions are the true “leaves” of the valley. Their surfaces are micro-terraced; each terrace is a single algal filament angled to catch stray photons that leak through the vine canopy.  
3. Thread-fine rhizoids that lace through the seams between slate plates and steel, gluing them against further rust while simultaneously mining minute quantities of phosphorus from the factory waste embedded in the metal.

The transparent snails belong to an undescribed *Zospeum* morph adapted to the valley’s continuous twilight. Their shells are not truly clear but layered with microscopic air chambers that refract green light so perfectly that only the faintest outline remains; under ordinary daylight they are effectively invisible except for the silver mucus they lay down. Each trail is a deliberate culture medium: the mucus contains a siderophore the *Periphyton* needs to fix the scant nitrogen that drifts down from the roof joists. In exchange, the algae exude a sugar the snails metabolize at a rate that keeps their metabolism nearly torpid—approximately one body-length per hour.

The black beetle is a single gravid female of an undescribed *Ptinid* whose entire life cycle is completed within the valley. She never eats here; her sole function is oviposition. Her eggs are dropped, one by one, through hairline fractures in the slate. Below, in the perpetual darkness of the sub-floor void, larvae hatch and feed on factory-flung spores and the dried bodies of mites too small to be named. When the adults finally eclose they climb a single thread of spider-silk (the valley’s only arachnid is a sheet-weaver that builds no web, only a safety line) until they reach the vine canopy, mate, and drop again. Their life span above ground is measured in minutes; the click you hear is the precise sound of sclerotized elytra snapping shut as they tuck their wings for the fall.

A fourth tenant is invisible unless the solenoid above mis-times its drip. During the extra-long interval the humidity climbs by less than half a percent; at that threshold a diffuse cloud of spores belonging to an endophytic *Fusarium* species germinates along the mid-veins of the narrow-leaf vines. The resulting micro-lesions produce a single volatile ketone that the piston fans, on their next slow rotation, carry downward. The ketone, in turn, suppresses bacterial overgrowth in the nutrient gel, closing a feedback loop no wider than the valley itself.

No vertebrates, no larger arthropods, no machines that announce themselves louder than breath. The place is tenanted only by these four collaborating kingdoms—alga, gastropod, beetle, and microfungus—each reduced by the narrowness of light and the constancy of moisture until they are little more than colored gradients moving through one another at geological speed.
