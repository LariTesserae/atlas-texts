The High Mist Gardens are not occupied by any single species but by a single ongoing process that manifests as many kinds of life. What lives there is the slow, synchronized work of keeping the crater exactly itself.

At the lowest level—where the stone lips are still clear as water—live the droplet tenders. They are small, translucent beings the size of a curled hand, each one molded from the same wet stone as the terraces themselves. When a droplet forms, one of them rises to meet it, drinks for one heartbeat, and sinks again, carrying the excess downward through its own body so the depression never overflows. Their motion is too slight to disturb the filaments above; they are the hidden counterweight to the visible clock.

Mid-terrace, among the corms, live the filament keepers: tall, grey people whose skin is the same pale grit as the ground, except where the fine root-hairs have grown into it and given it faint green veins. Each keeper is bound to one corm for life. When a new corm is ready, a keeper splits along the seam that runs from throat to navel, plants the half that is still fertile, and keeps the other half beside it as a living record of every droplet that has fallen since the planting. They do not move from their assigned level; they only turn slowly, year by year, so their shadow never falls on the same filament twice.

Above them, inside the blackwood canopy itself, live the six-winged slugs and the drifting many-limbed drinkers. The slugs are not animals but the canopy’s way of cleaning its own light; each one is a single cell that has learned to fold itself into wings. The drinkers are the crater’s way of moving water sideways instead of downward; when one of them lands on a droplet it is not drinking the water but tasting the moment of impact, then carrying that taste upward to the next terrace so the keepers will know when to plant the next corm.

At the very rim, inside the living blackwood wall, live the boundary tenders: people so slow that a single breath lasts the time between two droplet cycles. They are the ones who decide when the wall must add another ring. Their decision is never spoken; it is simply that one of them stops breathing for three full cycles. When that happens, the wall understands it is time to grow outward, and a new generation of corms is already being prepared in the hands of the filament keepers below.

Nothing in the crater is born or dies in the usual sense. Each creature is a temporary concentration of the same water, stone, and filament that make up the terraces themselves. When a droplet tender sinks too far or a filament keeper splits too many times, their substance is drawn back into the terraces to become the next layer of stone. The crater therefore contains no population that can be counted, only a single, continuous act of maintenance that sometimes looks like many small lives and sometimes like none at all.
