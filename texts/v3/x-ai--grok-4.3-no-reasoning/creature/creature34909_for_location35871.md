Nothing lives here in the ordinary sense.  

What exists is the interval between living.  

Wherever the crust has healed over a fissure, the same mineral that veins the domes condenses into a faintly luminous film inside the rock itself; this film is alive only insofar as it remembers the arrangement of molecules that once let water move through it. When the unseen drops arrive they meet this memory first, and for the few minutes the water remains, the memory is briefly readable as a pattern of microscopic channels that open and close like the gills of a creature too diffuse to have a body.  

Between the domes, where the crust is thinnest, the pressure of the subsonic water below sometimes lifts an entire plate of the surface a fraction of a hair’s width. In that lifted space, a single layer of salt-tolerant bacteria maintains a continuous, motionless sheet; they do not metabolize so much as occupy the precise distance that water must travel before it is drawn back downward. Their presence is registered only as an almost imperceptible stickiness on the sole of a boot, gone again the moment the plate settles.  

Inside each dome, the slow brightening and dimming is not a pulse of light but a pulse of orientation. The translucent sheets are composed of countless microscopic gypsum crystals whose long axes align and misalign with the magnetic field of the planet; each realignment lets a slightly different fraction of the permanent half-light pass through. When enough crystals have turned together, the interior of the dome brightens; when they drift apart again, it dims. No organism commands the turn. The crystals respond to the minute daily shift in the planet’s own field, and the field, in turn, is minutely altered by the passage of water through deeper aquifers that no surface eye can see. The result is that the domes glow and fade in a rhythm borrowed from the motion of stone miles below, a borrowed rhythm that has outlasted whatever once built the domes.  

Nothing moves fast enough to be called motion. Nothing reproduces. Nothing dies. What persists is the set of spatial habits—capillary films, aligned crystals, lifted bacterial sheets—that allow the interval between arrival and departure of water to remain exactly as long as it has always been. In this sense the hollow is not inhabited; it is rehearsed, over and over, by the same non-events that first shaped it.
