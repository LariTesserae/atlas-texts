The Moss-Tier is neither a city that houses plants nor a plant that tolerates a city; it is one continuous metabolism that has learned to tolerate the presence of passengers. Its “citizens” are the microscopic symbionts—mostly yeasts and nitrogen-fixing bacteria—that the cambium itself cultivates in specialized pockets along every vein. These populations wax and wane with the slow pulse of sap, each pulse marking a metabolic day that lasts roughly nineteen terrestrial hours. Humans move through the organism like benign parasites; their weight is tolerated because the trunk-moss gains structural fiber from the micro-abrasions their passage leaves behind, like a muscle thickening from repeated use.

The only other permanent residents are the lace-mites. They are not truly animals but colonial biofilms that have taken on the shape of mites: translucent bodies the size of pin-heads, held together by extracellular strands. Each colony maintains a single shared vacuole that functions as a communal lung. When thousands skate across a condensation film they exchange volatiles, creating brief, information-dense plumes that the cambium can sense through chemoreceptors. The ticking sound you hear is the sudden release of these plumes when a droplet falls and disturbs their film.

Occasionally a single human chooses to become a little more than passenger. Such a person cuts a shallow notch into the living wall, slips inside, and allows the cambium to knit over the incision. The body is never consumed; instead the plant threads microscopic root-hairs through skin pores, exchanging dissolved minerals for carbon dioxide. The human remains conscious, sustained at a metabolic rate so low that thought itself slows to the cadence of sap. After some years the cambium grows a new drip-bulb from the former occupant’s mouth; the last sound they make is the soft, regular release of condensed water back into the city’s circulation. No one knows whether the mind inside still dreams.
