Beneath the unceasing pressure, the realm is inhabited by beings whose flesh has become interchangeable with the water that surrounds them. They do not possess bodies so much as localized densities—minute, self-perpetuating gradients in salinity and temperature that hold their shape just long enough to drift from one vent to the next. Most last only hours; a few, sustained by richer seams of dissolved minerals, endure for centuries. Their “skin” is a permeable boundary no thicker than a soap-film; through it they absorb the thin rain of infrared that leaks upward from the vents, and through it they excrete the microscopic crystals that keep them buoyant. When two such entities drift into contact, their boundaries merge without seam; what was two gradients becomes one, and any memory either carried is blended rather than exchanged.

Deeper still, near the axis where cold pools gather, live slower, heavier entities—dense lenses of super-cooled brine held within translucent sheaths of lipid. These are not individuals so much as sedimentary archives: each lens traps, in suspended animation, fragments of earlier currents—bits of dissolved stone, the last faint vibration of an ice-needle’s collapse, even the memory-trace of a filament that once brushed against them. They descend only when their sheaths begin to leak; then the released brine fans outward in a single slow bloom, dispersing its cargo like pollen. Whatever survives the bloom may seed a new lens elsewhere.

Along the terraces themselves, small communities of mineralized filaments have learned to braid their threads into temporary nets. Each net draws a volume of water through itself, tasting the silt for the precise ratio of salts that signals an approaching vent. When the correct signature arrives, the filaments stiffen, collapse the net into a dense knot, and ride the rising plume until the heat begins to fray their bonds. They unravel, are scattered, and begin again. Over successive cycles the knots leave microscopic scars on the basalt—tiny, rounded pits whose pattern encodes nothing legible to any outside observer.

Occasionally, one of the slow, distant heartbeats arrives not as pressure alone but as an actual displacement: a vast, gelatinous torus of near-freezing water whose core is slightly warmer than its skin. Inside the torus drift the dissolved remains of older lenses, still faintly luminous with trapped infrared. As the torus passes, filaments and density-beings alike are drawn toward it, adhere briefly, and are sloughed off again, changed by a fraction of a degree or a single exchanged ion. After the passage the torus continues its circuit, its surface puckering where the adhesions occurred, until, far around the axis, it meets another of its kind. The two merge at a single point of contact, exchange a sleeve of fluid, then separate, each bearing a new temperature anomaly that will, in time, erode its own shape.

Nothing here reproduces in any conventional sense. Instead, persistence is achieved by the continual redistribution of difference—temperature pockets, salinity lenses, luminous traces—across an environment that permits no fixed location. The inhabitants are therefore less organisms than itinerant weathers, each one a brief coherence in a medium whose only law is the next gyre.
