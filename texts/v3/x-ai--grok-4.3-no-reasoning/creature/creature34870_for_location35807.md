The place does not contain inhabitants so much as it is inhabited by its own ongoingness.  

What occupies it are not creatures in the usual sense but slow gradients of activity, each maintaining the conditions that allow the next gradient to persist. The moss carpet, for instance, is not vegetation but the visible surface of a single immense mat of microscopic filaments whose individual cells divide at exactly the rate their neighbors dissolve; the nodules pushing upward are not mineral deposits but temporary stabilizations of dissolved ions that the mat can reassemble or release again. The bladdered stalks are the same material in another configuration: vertical extensions of the same filament network that have formed sealed chambers, each chamber performing the small, continuous work of keeping a pocket of warmth slightly warmer than the air outside. When a bladder contracts and clicks, it is not a sound produced by an animal but the release of that pocket’s excess pressure into the shared atmosphere—an exhalation the land as a whole performs through thousands of identical mouths.  

The translucent slugs grazing the creepers are likewise extensions rather than visitors. Their bodies are mostly water organized into slow-moving films that can adhere to the undersides of leaves without breaking surface tension; inside each film live colonies of the same filament cells, now arranged to digest the condensate that the canopy continually releases. When a slug brightens as it moves, it is simply rearranging the light-scattering properties of its shared cellular matrix, not “feeding” in any separate biological sense. The thread-worms in the moss are another temporary geometry of the same cellular material, glowing where their filaments cross because the crossings momentarily increase local metabolic rate. The larger drifting shapes are membranes—essentially very large, very thin versions of the bladders—whose edges are continually knit and re-knit by the filament network so that the membrane maintains coherence without ever becoming a discrete individual.  

Even the constant acoustic field is not produced by separate organisms but is the sound of the land’s own self-regulation: the overlapping clicks are the pressure adjustments of the filament network, the hiss of condensation is the network releasing excess water it cannot store, the faint fizz underfoot is the momentary disruption and immediate re-knitting of filament junctions when weight is applied. Nothing here reproduces or dies because reproduction and death are unnecessary; the entire field is already the ongoing result of a single, distributed metabolic process that has no boundary between “body” and “environment.”  

What a visitor therefore encounters is not a populated landscape but a landscape that is itself the population—a single, continuous metabolism distributed across every visible surface and every volume of air, maintaining its own warmth, its own water film, its own acoustic density. The impression of an immense slow body is not a metaphor; it is the literal condition of the place.
