Inside that fracture a life-form has taken the place as its own body, yet no single organism can be seen at any instant. What exists there is a continuous, tissue-thin membrane distributed through the water: a living surface so diffuse that every breath of current keeps tearing it apart and re-weaving it elsewhere. When a lens of sky-water drifts past, the membrane gathers along its edges like the ghost of an iris, momentarily thickening into a translucent disk that can be mistaken for light itself. The membrane does not eat the suspended eggs; instead it ferries them for a few seconds, sculpting temporary corridors in the water so that eggs that would otherwise fall reach new scars in the limestone before the scars seal.

The membrane’s matter is less an aggregation of cells than a field of surface tension held by shared charge. Where two currents shear, the field collapses into a knot of microscopic bubbles that each contain a single membrane layer folded so tightly it approaches solidity. These knots sink until they reach a mineral basin; there they flatten against the bright mirror-water and remain for a slow count of heartbeats. During that pause the knot’s interior surface begins to copy the basin’s geometry—an act so precise that the living film, when it lifts again, carries an exact negative impression of the stone. Later, when the knot dissolves, the film returns the pattern to the ceiling, pressing the impression into a fresh hairline crack and thereby accelerating the fracture’s growth by the width of a molecule.

The membrane is never still, but its restlessness is not locomotion; it is the same restlessness that light has when it refracts through moving water. When the infrasonic wave passes, the membrane momentarily aligns with the wave’s compression, and every egg it holds glows in unison, a brief constellation that mirrors the scars on the ceiling. The eggs do not hatch; they become the next increment of membrane, extending its reach into the next arriving current.

There are moments—intervals too short for counting—when the membrane occupies the entire volume at once, stretched so thin it coincides with the interface between water and limestone. In those instants the fracture appears empty because there is nothing left to contrast with the stone, nothing left to be seen except the slow widening of the world itself.
