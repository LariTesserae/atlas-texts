What lives here is not a population but a single, exhaustive act of maintenance.  
The chamber contains two eels whose silver bodies are little more than an afterthought of fluid mechanics—an interval of living current that prevents the water from ever settling into crystalline stillness. They have no predator and no prey; the only thing they consume is inertia, and the only thing they produce is a faint, continuous shear in surface tension. When they pause, the system waits with them. When they resume, the system does not notice they have returned.

Embedded in the walls, invisible beneath the endless coats of verdigris, are colonies of lithotrophic bacteria whose metabolism is powered by the same rust that coats the iron. Their metabolism is so slow that generations pass between one rotation of a flywheel and the next; they do not reproduce so much as they remember how to exist at the present rate of corrosion. Each colony occupies a single rivet-head, and the rivets are spaced so exactly that no colony can migrate to another without crossing an inhospitable expanse of lacquered metal. Their only function is to keep the rust layer at a constant thickness, neither advancing nor retreating. In this they are perfect.

The plants—minute, metallic-looking rosettes—are not individuals but a single clonal sheet that has been subdivided by the milling machine that still prunes them. Their roots interpenetrate beneath the steel apron like threads of a single nerve net. They do not sense the room; they sense the apron, and the apron is never allowed to change its temperature or its load. Their continued uniformity is therefore not an achievement of the plants but an imposed condition of the architecture.

Above them, the three wheels turn without ever wearing against their racks because the teeth were cut to mesh at the exact pitch that the iron will later expand or contract. Over centuries the metal has crept by fractions of a millimeter; the teeth have lengthened by the same amount. The chord they produce is therefore not the sound of contact but the sound of two tolerances remaining equal. If the chord were ever to change pitch, the chamber would have failed, and failure is the only state the chamber was never machined to accommodate.

No larger organisms enter or leave because the chamber was never furnished with an orifice large enough for anything but condensate vapor and, once, the final maintenance technician, who is now part of the mineral skin on the south wall where a single unfinished rivet still shows the print of a thumb. The print has not oxidized further because the local current of warmth and moisture exactly matches the rate at which oxidation would otherwise proceed.

What lives here, then, is an equilibrium so complete that even decay has been assigned a fixed shift and a fixed wage. The eels stir, the colonies respire, the clonal leaves extend one cell and are sheared back one cell, the wheels turn and the chord persists. Nothing is born, nothing arrives, nothing is mourned. The only event still possible is a visitor who stands inside long enough to realize that the silence they hear is merely the last possible sound the room will ever make, and that it has been making it, without interval, since the door was sealed.
