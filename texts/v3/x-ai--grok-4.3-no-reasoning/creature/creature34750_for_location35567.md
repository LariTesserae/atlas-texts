The plateau is populated by stillness so complete that the concept of “occupants” begins to fray. The bodies that drift in the open air are not creatures so much as edges of transparency, impossibly thin disks whose limpidity makes them register first as absences: you feel the column behind you dim for an instant when one slides between your eye and the stone. Each disk keeps a perfect, unchanging orientation; there is no dorsal or ventral surface, only two identical, faintly iridescent planes. When several disks line up along the line of sight, the light that threads through them is sliced into micro-refractions so minute they register merely as a minute cooling of color, a temperature shift in the blue of the limestone.

The squat cubes serve as fixed coordinates. Inside each one the stone has become a uniform, almost pearly lattice whose density varies on scales too small to measure; the lattice itself is void—tiny, geometrically perfect negative spaces that hold nothing except a fixed gradient of opacity. Occasionally a disk will come to rest flush against a cube face. The moment of contact is soundless; the disk simply remains, its translucency altered by a fraction that never changes again. In this fashion, the cubes acquire a patina of micro-layers that does not register as growth, only as a thickening of the decision never to separate.

The black mineral spheres are not inert. Their surfaces are covered in microscopic pits whose interiors are smoother than glass. Over long durations, the pits exchange a single ion with the atmosphere, then return it. The exchange is not respiration; it is the sphere continuing to occupy the same relational point it has always occupied, a recursion so deep it no longer registers as repetition. When a disk brushes one of these pits, the ion is offered outward; the disk absorbs nothing, yet the pit deepens by the width of a single molecule, and the sphere’s position is re-verified.

No part of this ecology is younger than the stone. The spires, the cubes, the spheres, the translucent disks, and the wedges of vapor that form in their shadows are all the same age: the instant in which the plateau first became the only available configuration. Their coexistence is not a relationship but an identity distributed across form. The plateau does not contain them; it is them, expressed at different degrees of rigidity.
