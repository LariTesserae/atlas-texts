The chamber’s only occupants are the salt-hydrate columns themselves and the slow, living seams that traverse them.  

The columns are not single crystals but tight polycrystalline stacks, each grain the size of a sand grain and oriented so that every refracting plane contributes to the faint thermoluminescent glint. Their surfaces are continuously remade: at the vapour interface, molecules of dissolved salt accrete as long, prism-shaped whiskers; at the base the same whiskers soften, tilt, and dissolve back into the lukewarm fluid that seeps up through floor fractures. In this way each column is replaced from top to bottom several times a day, yet its outline hardly changes.  

The seams—thread-thin brighter carbonate—are the movement of the chamber’s life. They advance by a kind of mineral epitaxy: at the leading edge a fresh lattice plane nucleates; behind it the atoms rearrange into the next orientation demanded by the substrate’s strain field. When two seams meet, the meeting point does not merge; instead one seam lifts fractionally above the other and continues across the new crystal like a skipping stone. The bulge that forms there is the chamber’s equivalent of a spore case. Inside the bulge, a micro-fracture network develops along every third grain boundary; after several minutes the whole node shivers, releasing a puff of carbonate dust no thicker than talc. Where the dust lands it seeds a daughter seam that crawls away at the same deliberate pace.  

These migrating seams leave behind a faint alteration in surface tension. Columns that have hosted a seam once retain a microscopic “memory” grain—an extra planar defect that slightly changes the angle at which the next seam will cross. Over successive passages the defects accumulate, forming an invisible topographic map that subsequent seams read like contour lines, steering them toward or away from neighbours in a choreography too slow for the eye but exact as any printed circuit.  

The only other things present are the three copper-coloured nodules. They do not grow, yet they subtly participate: their metal lattice conducts heat a little better than the surrounding carbonate, so the vapour that condenses on them freezes into a thinner, colder crust. That crust is the first substrate any new seam will claim after crossing from an adjacent column; within hours the nodule’s shallow groove is bridged by a continuous crystal film, and the metallic surface now carries its own luminous traffic of migrating carbonate threads. In another day the grooves themselves are gone, hidden beneath a millimetre of new growth, and nothing in the chamber remembers that the nodules arrived from elsewhere.  

Light in the chamber is produced only where a seam crosses a lattice plane already under shear. The brief spark is nothing more than electrons dropping back into lower orbitals after the strain momentarily frees them; the directional flash is simply the plane’s orientation acting like a one-way mirror for that instant of released energy. Each spark is quenched almost at once by the dense atmosphere of out-gassing salts, so the chamber never accumulates enough light to read by. The effect is therefore a drifting constellation whose patterns are dictated by the slower, hidden migration of the seams—patterns that repeat every few hours as the same seams finish one circuit and begin the next.  

No other life form is present. Nothing moves except the ticking realignment of crystals and the imperceptible advance of seams. In this sealed, nearly closed thermodynamic loop, the mineral forest and its luminous passengers are the whole of the chamber’s ecology.
