They move as single presences rather than as a crowd. Their bodies are tall and narrow at the shoulders, the joints angled so that each limb describes a slow, repeated arc rather than a stride. Skin is the same pale basalt tone as the unfinished walls, marked only by the faintest lattice of raised lines that follow the same geometric intervals as the terraces themselves. Their faces carry no expression because their eyes never fix on one another; each gaze rests at a middle distance, somewhere between the next terrace and the ridge above.

They wear nothing that can be called clothing, only a continuous wrapping of the same reed fabric stored in the wall niches: a single length drawn once around the torso and once around each thigh, secured by the pressure of the fabric’s own tension. The color is the faint green-gray of the slate before rain, and the weave is open enough that light passes through the spaces between threads without casting a shadow on the skin beneath. When they pause, the reed settles against them with the same neutrality the stone shows to the constant light.

Their hands are long, the fingers jointed at an extra point so that the last segment can lie flat against the stone when they lift or set it down. They carry slate the way they carry themselves: one piece at a time, balanced along the forearm, the free hand extended slightly as though measuring the next empty interval. Their breathing is shallow and evenly spaced; it never quickens even when they climb the narrow track from the quarry. The only visible change is a brief darkening along the lower edge of the reed wrap where condensation from the fresh fracture has adhered and then dried away.

No hierarchy is visible among them. They occupy the same terraces in the same measured intervals, and when two figures meet at the edge of an open stretch, one simply alters course by a single measured pace so that the distance between any two remains constant. Their slow crossing leaves the moss cushions undisturbed; only the faint, momentary compression of the surface shows where a foot has passed, and even that disappears within the time it takes for the next film of moisture to appear and depart.

Above them, the gliders turn once more on the motionless air. Below, the six-legged browsers continue their cropping, carapaces indistinguishable from the stone. The city holds its single condition of light, stone, and measured emptiness, and the figures who move through it continue without altering either distance or duration.
