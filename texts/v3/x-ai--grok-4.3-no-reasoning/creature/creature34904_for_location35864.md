What lives here is not a population but a continuity.

The plateau itself is the largest single living thing; every wall, column, and membrane is the same organism, differentiated only by posture. Its cells divide so slowly that division registers only as a thickening of blade walls, never as a loss of pattern. The grass does not photosynthesize in the ordinary sense; it transduces the unchanging green-gold light into a low, steady metabolic hum that runs through every blade at once, like current in a superconducting lattice.

Beneath the membrane’s surface—embedded in its deepest, warmest layers—are networks of thread-fine tubules that connect every column and vault into one circulatory system. Along these tubules travel microscopic vesicles containing modified chloroplasts and the shrimp-like organisms you noticed. The vesicles are not transport so much as slow negotiations: when one vesicle arrives at a scarped edge, the undeveloped matrix ahead of it receives a precise chemical cue that initiates the next increment of growth. Because the cues are rhythmic and never contradictory, the plateau has expanded outward in perfect rings for uncountable time, each ring exactly as thick as the previous.

The transparent “shrimp” are not separate creatures. They are the plateau’s reproductive units—diploid buds released when the membrane needs to colonize a new volume of space. Once outside, they drift until they contact a developing column, whereupon they lose their appendages, flatten, and become another node in the same organism. Their transparency keeps them from casting shadows that might interrupt the single, invariant chord of sound generated by droplet motion.

The sky-leaf overhead is not a separate canopy; it is the plateau’s single, enormous photosynthetic surface, curved upward so that every incident ray is refracted back into the living architecture below. Its underside is a continuous sheet of stomata whose coordinated opening and closing modulates the constant note of the plateau. When a vesicle leaves the membrane, the nearest stomata dilate slightly; the change is minute—enough to shift the note by a quarter-tone—but the note returns immediately to C, preserving equilibrium.

Because nothing dies and nothing arrives from outside, the plateau has no predators, no decomposers, and no scavengers. The only process is the slow, irreversible extension of its own edges, each new blade growing into alignment with every previous blade until the membrane is once again seamless.
