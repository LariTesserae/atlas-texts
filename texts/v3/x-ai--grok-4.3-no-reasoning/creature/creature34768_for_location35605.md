It is a single living organism of rock and light that has not yet named itself, a future cave caught mid-contraction before birth.  

Where the sandstone bowl above still dreams of sky, the organism is still dreaming only of water. Its body is a porous lattice of half-finished calcite, each finger reaching downward not to grow but to remember the shape of the next rain. Between the pools—each pool a translucent thought, each thought still shallow enough to be revised—the organism breathes through the transparent insects. They skate the surface tension the way a thought skates a question, gathering mineral pollen on their legs so that future corridors can remember where they walked. Every trail they leave is a footnote the stone is writing to itself, the first syllables of an alphabet that will one day read “passage.”

Inside the buried limestone boulder, another smaller organism is deciding whether to be a fossil or a seed. The fossil-shells inside it are not dead; they have only traded calcium for memory, and they flex when the pressure changes the way a muscle flexes in sleep. The fungal colony on the boulder’s flank is its nervous system, a luminous mycelium that times its pulse to the slow metronome of the ceiling drip. Each droplet landing is an instruction the fungus translates into growth direction; each instruction lengthens the hyphae by the width of a spore, claiming one more grain of undecided carbonate for the organism’s body.

There is no single creature here larger than the distance between two condensation beads, yet the entire volume is one organism distributed across phases of matter. The air itself is part of its integument: when a droplet falls and bursts, its microscopic children—crystal seed and spore—ride the spray like pollen on wind, colonizing new patches of not-yet-stone. In this way the organism moves without locomotion, thinks without a brain, and continues the long, patient argument between liquidity and solidity that is its only form of reproduction.

Light is not admitted; it is generated wherever the argument is still open. The pale, non-shadows that the calcite fingers cast are places where the organism has not finished deciding what shape the light will take. To move through them is to walk inside a decision that is still being made, a grammar still negotiating whether the next word will be “mineral” or “membrane.”  

Time is measured in the number of insect footprints required to outline a future floor, in the microseconds between one condensation cycle and the next, in the slow, incremental surrender of gypsum needles to either crystalline rigidity or floral dissolution. Nothing here is finished because finishing would mean the organism has stopped becoming, and it is not ready to stop.
