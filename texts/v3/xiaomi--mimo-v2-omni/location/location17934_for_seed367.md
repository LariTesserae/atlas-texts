# The Crevice of Vesh

You find it the way everyone finds it — by almost not finding it at all.

A fissure in the escarpment, barely wider than a man's shoulders, choked with pale root-mats and the dried husks of insects. You squeeze through sideways, scraping your pack against walls that sweat a faint mineral warmth, and then the fissure opens — not dramatically, not into a cavern, but into a *space*. A cleft. A wrinkle in the rock that has been there since before rivers learned to run.

---

## The Shape of It

The Crevice of Vesh is perhaps four hundred meters long and never more than thirty wide. It runs crooked through the summit of a mesa so ancient it has forgotten it was ever a mountain. The walls rise thirty, forty, sometimes seventy meters — pale stone, calcium-veined, smoothed by unimaginable ages of wind into surfaces that look almost *melted*. There are no sharp edges here. The erosion is so deep, so old, that everything has been returned to curves. The rock looks like the bones of something that died before bones existed.

Above, the strip of sky is the color of ash in the dim light that reaches down. The sun never fully enters the Crevice. It illuminates the upper rim for a few hours each cycle, sending a warm glow cascading down the walls, but the floor lives in a state of perpetual amber dusk — bright enough to see, dim enough that shadows pool like water in every fold of stone.

The floor is not flat. It is a landscape in miniature — a thicket of ridges, hollows, shelves, and ledges, all carved by the slow dissolution of softer rock. It looks like something poured and left to set, then carefully gnawed. In places the stone has eroded into honeycomb formations thin as fingers, warm to the touch, humming faintly with trapped air. In other places, deep potholes open, round as drilled holes, each one a small world with its own temperature gradient and its own residents.

There is no water. Not a drop, not a seep, not a memory of water. The stone is bone-dry, and the air tastes of dust and warm limestone. How anything lives here is the question the Crevice answers by simply continuing to exist.

---

## The Living Walls

The walls are not bare. They have never been bare.

Every surface that catches a grain of light is covered in vegetation — not green, not the green of wet places, but a deep, bruised maroon shading into silver-grey. The dominant plants are thick-stalked, fleshy things: rosettes of rubbery leaves that press flat against the rock like grasping hands. They are *bryothorps* — a name someone once gave them — and they do not photosynthesize the way surface plants do. They are adapted to this dim, waterless place. They pull what they need from the air itself, from the faint humidity of a hundred thousand breathing creatures, from the slow outgassing of the ancient stone. Their roots are shallow, almost decorative. They cling with adhesive pads that, when pulled free, make a sound like a quiet kiss.

Between the rosettes, a fine moss — hair-thin, rust-colored — grows in dense mats. It is dry to the touch, almost brittle, but alive in a patient, geological way. It has been growing here for longer than the people have existed. In places, the moss mats are half a meter thick, layered like sediment, and the lower layers have compressed into something almost like leather. The people of Vesh cut strips of this old moss-mat and use it for everything: binding, padding, crude clothing, wadding for their sleeping hollows.

Higher up, where the walls narrow and the light is dimmer, trailing structures descend — not vines, but fungal cords, pale and thick as fingers, hanging in curtains from ledges where detritus has accumulated. They are the connective tissue of the Crevice. They break down dead organic matter — and there is a great deal of it — and redistribute nutrients through a network that threads through the walls themselves. You can sometimes see their pale glow in the deepest dark, a bioluminescence so faint it might be imagined.

The overall effect is of walls that are *alive*. Not just inhabited but *constituted* by living matter. The stone is a skeleton, and the maroon-and-rust vegetation is its flesh. The Crevice breathes. You feel it on your skin — a faint warmth that is not merely thermal. Something is exhaling. The air has a weight to it, a thick, organic quality, as though you are inside the lung of some vast creature that settled into this shape a billion years ago and has never needed to move again.

---

## The People of the Crevice

They call themselves the *Veshkin*, or rather, they make a sound that approximates that — a soft, clicking word spoken from the back of the throat. They are not many. Perhaps two thousand live within the Crevice at any time, though the number fluctuates with seasons they barely understand and events they track in ways that have nothing to do with calendars.

They are short, dense-boned, and have the sun-darkened, wind-smoothed look of people who have always lived in a place that is warm but not hot, dry but not barren. Their skin carries a faint reddish undertone, possibly from the constant proximity to the maroon vegetation, possibly from diet, possibly from ten thousand years of breathing this particular air. Their eyes are large — adapted to the dimness — and their hands are extraordinary: broad, calloused, with fingers that seem to have an extra joint, or at least an extra *dexterity*. They can feel vibrations in the stone the way others hear sound.

They build in the only material available: the soft, eroded limestone and the dried biological matter of the walls. Their structures are not separate from the rock — they are *extensions* of it. They carve, they plaster, they shape the existing formations into rooms, passages, chambers, and ledges. A Veshkin dwelling looks like something that grew rather than something that was built: smooth, rounded, organic, with walls that slope and curve and meet without clear corners. The plaster they use is a mixture of crushed stone, fungal cord, and a secretion from certain of the wall-rosettes that dries to a surface as hard and smooth as shell.

The Crevice is *dense* with these structures. They stack. They overlap. They tunnel through and around each other. A single vertical section of wall might contain six or seven levels of habitation, connected by carved steps, holds, and short climbing passages. From a distance, the inhabited walls look like termite mounds — or like the interior of a lung, riddled with chambers and passages. There is no ground-level streets. There are no streets at all. Movement is vertical as much as horizontal, and a Veshkin child can climb better than most surface creatures can walk.

They have no metal. They have never needed it. The stone is soft enough to work with sharpened bone, obsidian traded from somewhere far above, and the dense, woody stalks of the larger wall-plants, which can be hardened by fire. Their tools are simple: scrapers, gougers, needles, wedges, hammers of shaped stone. They make them beautifully. The Veshkin have a craftsman's culture — the person who makes the best scraper is admired, spoken of, remembered. Their tools are their art and their currency. A well-made obsidian-edged gouge is worth more than anything else in the Crevice.

They eat the Crevice itself: the wall-rosettes (bitter, but nourishing), the moss-mat (ground into a paste, pressed into flat cakes), the fungal cords (pulled up in strips, chewed slowly), and the overwhelming fauna, which provides the bulk of their diet.

---

## The Fauna

This is where the Crevice becomes difficult to describe in terms that make surface-dwellers comfortable.

The Crevice is *dense* with life. Not just populated — *packed*. Every surface, every crevice, every ledge is occupied, contested, fought over. The dominant fauna are arthropods — six-legged and eight-legged creatures of astonishing variety, most of them small, many of them tiny, some of them not. The air hums. The walls rustle. The sound is constant.

In the dim light, clouds of small winged creatures drift in patterns that almost look choreographed — swarms of translucent, gnat-like things that feed on the fungal spores and are, in turn, fed on by everything else. Their wings catch what little light reaches the floor and scatter it into faint, shifting mosaics on the walls. It is the closest thing to beauty in a place that is otherwise deeply, viscerally alive in ways that challenge the uninitiated.

Larger creatures occupy the ledges and the honeycomb formations. Beetle-analogues the size of a man's fist, their carapaces the color of the stone, nearly invisible until they move. They graze the moss-mat, their mandibles working in a rhythm that contributes to the Crevice's sound — a constant, soft rasping, as though the walls themselves are being slowly sanded. Larger still: segmented, scorpion-like predators that wait in the potholes, their sensory hairs twitching, striking with a speed that makes the eye stutter. The Veshkin respect these. Children are taught to read the stone for the signs of a nesting predator before they are taught to speak in full sentences.

Birds exist here too — or something like birds. Small, dense, with reduced wings and powerful legs, adapted to climbing and hopping rather than flight. They nest in the upper reaches where the light is best and the insect clouds are thickest. Their calls contribute to the sound of the Crevice: a layered, clicking, whistling chorus that never stops. At certain hours — triggered by light levels the Veshkin track with care — the chorus intensifies, and the Crevice rings with it. The sound bounces off the smooth walls, overlaps with itself, becomes a texture rather than a collection of individual voices.

The Crevice is *loud*. Not violently loud, not the loud of a storm or a battle, but *persistently* loud. The layered sounds of thousands of creatures in a narrow, resonant space create a drone that you feel in your chest. Newcomers describe headaches, a disorientation, a feeling of being *inside something*. The Veshkin do not hear it. They have never heard anything else.

And beneath it all, the slow, barely perceptible sounds of the geological process itself: the soft settling of stone, the faint crack of thermal expansion, the whisper of wind far above filtering down through the fissure. The Crevice is tectonically stable — it has been still for longer than life has existed on the surface — but it is not silent. It sighs. It settles. It is alive in the mineral sense, the way a mountain is alive: slowly, on a timescale that makes the frantic chatter of its biological inhabitants seem like the briefest flicker.

---

## The World Above and Below

The Crevice has no river, no lake, no aquifer. The Veshkin do not *drink* in the way surface peoples would understand. Their biology has adapted to this over deep time. They extract moisture from the organisms they eat, and their bodies are ruthlessly efficient at conservation. They do not sweat. Their waste is nearly dry. They are, in a sense, partially lithotrophic — the mineral-rich dust that pervades everything is consumed incidentally and provides trace nutrients and moisture bound in crystal structures. It is not water as a surface-dweller would recognize it, but it is enough.

The Crevice is sealed — not artificially, but by its own geometry. The fissure you squeezed through is the only entrance. The walls are too smooth, too vertical, and too high for any other access. Above, the mesa rim is wind-blasted and lifeless, scoured by a dryness that makes even the most barren surface deserts seem lush. Below, the honeycomb formations extend deep, but they dead-end in solid stone.

The Veshkin do not leave. The concept is not quite alien to them — they know, in an abstract way, that there is an outside — but it has no emotional weight. The Crevice is the world. The strip of ash-colored sky is the ceiling of the world. The warm, living walls are the skin of the world. The sound, the smell, the pressure of it — this is what existence *is*.

---

## Time in the Crevice

The erosion parameter is the most telling thing about this place.

The Crevice of Vesh is *old*. Not old the way a human settlement is old, not old the way a surface city with its rebuilt walls and layered foundations is old. Old in the geological sense. The smoothness of the walls is not the work of centuries. It is the work of epochs. This fissure has existed, in something close to its current shape, for longer than vertebrates have existed on the surface. The biological community within it has had time — *immense* time — to adapt, to specialize, to settle into a stable state so deeply optimized that it barely changes anymore.

The ecosystem is not climax in the way a forest is climax. It is climax in the way a *mineral* is climax — a final form. The balance of predator and prey, of vegetation and fauna, of the slow breakdown of stone and its incorporation into living systems, has been reached so long ago that the equations have become invisible. Nothing is in transition. Everything is in its place. The Crevice does not evolve anymore. It *persists*.

The Veshkin, for all their tool-use and culture and social complexity, are part of this persistence. They are not its masters. They are its current expression — the latest arrangement of matter in a place that has expressed matter in countless arrangements before them and will, in time, express it in others. Their bones will become the soil that feeds the moss-mat. Their tools will be reabsorbed into the stone. Their words will join the sound.

This is not a sad thought to them. It is not a happy thought. It is simply the shape of things. The Crevice endures, and they are inside it, and that is enough.

---

*The light shifts. The amber dusk moves along the walls as the sun crosses the narrow strip of sky overhead. The swarms adjust their patterns. The predators in the potholes tense and relax. The Veshkin move through their vertical city, their large eyes catching the dim light, their hands feeling the vibrations of ten thousand lives in the stone beneath their fingers. The Crevice sighs. The sound goes on.*
