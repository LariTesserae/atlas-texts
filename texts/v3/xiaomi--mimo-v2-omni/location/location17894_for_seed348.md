It does not rain here, not truly. The moisture is a constant exhalation, beading on every cold surface in a fine, unwavering condensation. It collects in shallow, brackish runnels that trace the cracks in the ferrocrete slabs, pooling in depressions that have held the same inch of oily water for centuries. The ground is a low, fractured plain of industry, a settlement built upon its own ruins, and now locked in perfect, terrible stasis.

The air hums. Not with life, but with resonant frequency. The overwhelming fauna is not biological, but kinetic and metallic. They are the **Locomotive Herds**—hundreds of rusted, articulated engines, no larger than horses, that move through the skeletal streets and flooded yards. They have no wheels; they proceed by a slow, grinding oscillation of their own chassis, their steel pistons pumping in silent, synchronized rhythms that generate a deep, bone-shaking hum. This is the source of the perpetual sound: a layered drone of vibration, the shriek of unlubricated metal on metal, the percussive *thrum* of immense flywheels turning in locked chambers. It is a loudness felt more than heard, a saturation of the air itself.

They are the only "life," and they are everywhere. They graze on the corroded girders of collapsed warehouses, their mandible-like couplers scraping off flakes of rust which fall like brown snow into the water below. They nest in the shells of old turbines, their constant vibration causing the surrounding structures to slowly, perpetually crumble—a fine powder of concrete and brick dust always drifting from some unseen edge. They are densest in the central district, where the layers of construction are deepest, a palimpsest of forgotten factories now fused into a single, jagged mountain range of machinery.

There is no plant, no fungus, no recognized biology. The moisture feeds nothing but corrosion. The "ecology" is a closed circuit of decay and reformation. A Locomotive Herd-member, when its internal motion finally grinds to a halt, is simply nudged by its kin into a deep basin. There, under the constant drip, it slowly dissolves into a new stratum of iron-rich sediment, its unique resonant frequency absorbed back into the ambient hum. New forms are not born; they are slowly precipitated from the solution of the old, coalescing in crystalline growths deep in the flooded mines beneath the city. The process is glacial, a geological blink, and utterly unchanging.

The light is normal, a flat, diffuse grey that casts no sharp shadows. It seems to emanate from the moisture in the air itself, reflecting evenly off every wet surface. This allows the true, strange geography to be seen: a city scaled for giants, but now inhabited by these small, fervent engines. Arches tall enough for airships are choked with their clustered bodies. Grand plazas are now shallow lakes, their fountains long silent, serving only as gathering pools where the Herds stand motionless for years, their resonant hums harmonizing into a single, deafening chord before they shudder back into motion.

Time is not a river here. It is a frozen lake. The erosion is complete, the balance struck. The condensation falls, the Herds vibrate and scrape, the structures settle by a millimeter per century, and the sound never, ever changes. It is a place at the end of causality, an industrial ecosystem that has achieved a perfect, loud, strange, and permanent equilibrium. It is not waiting. It is simply, eternally, *is*.
