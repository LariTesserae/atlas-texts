The place has no name you would recognize. It is a work in progress, an unfinished thought in a material that is not stone, not metal, not flesh.

It is called the **Kilnplain**.

The ground is the first and most absolute fact. It is *made*. There is no dirt, no bedrock. The substrate is a broad, cracked expanse of a substance likeite calcium carbonate oriteite dried resin, bleached pale under a normal, relentless sun. It is warm to the touch, radiating a stored heat that makes the air above it shimmer. This material is not uniform; it bears the faint, colossal textures of its own making—long, sweeping curves, as if extruded by a slow, planetary-scale nozzle, and vast, shallow dimples where settling has occurred. The cracks are not random; they form polygonal plates, a mosaic baked into being. In the deepest cracks, a different, darker material is exposed, still cool and damp, smelling of ozone and wet chalk.

Water is a secret here. It does not flow in rivers. It appears as a sudden, shocking dampness on the pale surface after the nightly condensation, a dew that leaves dark stains which steam away by mid-morning. It pools in the dimples, creating ephemeral, brackish mirrors that last a few hours before being drunk by the thirsty ground or the air. There are no springs, only seepage from the substrate itself, as if the material is slowly, reluctantly exuding its own primordial moisture. This intermittent dampness is the only form of growth’s catalyst.

Vegetation is a sparse, tenacious accident. It consists of low, crustose growths—part lichen, part fungal mat—that cling to the shaded sides of cracks or colonize the transient pools. They are the color of verdigris and rust, and when the moisture comes, they swell with a faint, leathery sigh. In the vast spaces between, there is nothing but the pale, hot ground and the empty sky. There are no trees, no grasses, only these scattered, resilient scabs of life.

The scale is what undoes the mind. The Kilnplain extends to every horizon, a continent of this unfinished material. The slight elevation means it is a basin, but so vast that its edges are invisible, lost in atmospheric haze. This immensity generates its own weather. The daytime heat creates thermals that lift the condensing moisture into high, thin clouds that race at tremendous speed, casting fleeting, racing shadows. By late afternoon, these clouds can coalesce into brief, violent squalls that lash the ground with warm, mineral-rich rain, a sound like static or distant applause on the hard surface. The rain is the main sculptor, for erosion is just beginning. It has etched shallow, braided channels—arroyos that are more suggestions than gullies—where the water has found the path of least resistance across the made ground. These channels are smooth, polished, and disappear into the distance.

The silence is profound, but not absolute. The active dynamics of the place produce a minimal soundscape: the *tink* of cooling material contracting in the evening, the whisper of wind across vast, unobstructed spaces, the sudden *plop* of a condensation droplet falling from an overhanging lip into a pool. Most haunting is the occasional, deep, grinding *thrum* that seems to come from the ground itself—a tectonic shift in the substrate, the slow settling of a continent-sized construct, still finding its final form.

There is no animal life. No insects buzz over the pools, no birds cross the sky. The active dynamics are geological, chemical, and meteorological alone. The construction process is ongoing; in some areas, the ground feels newly set, soft at the edges of cracks. In others, it is ancient and brittle. The "built" nature is total—you are walking on a manufactured surface, a plain of petrified secretion or colossal, abandoned architecture. Yet the technology is absent because the builder is not a tool-user. The builder *is* the material, the process. Perhaps it was a biological colony on a planetary scale, a living cement that grew and then died, leaving its body as the landscape. Or perhaps it is still alive, in a geological, microbial sense, continuing its slow exhalation of ground.

The light is normal, but the lack of familiar reference points makes it strange. Shadows are sharp and black. The pale ground reflects the sun, creating a blinding, diffuse glare. At sunset, the entire plain glows like an ember, the cracks turning into veins of shadow, before the rapid cooling sets in and the condensation begins its nightly weeping.

You stand in the Kilnplain. You are surrounded by the works of a forgotten or not-yet-finished process. The air is hot and dry, then briefly wet. The ground is hard, then damp. The scale is inhuman. The silence is punctuated only by the sounds of the place making and unmaking itself. It is normal in its physics, but utterly alien in its origin and purpose. It is a vast, hot, sparse, quiet, active, and entirely made emptiness, still under construction.
