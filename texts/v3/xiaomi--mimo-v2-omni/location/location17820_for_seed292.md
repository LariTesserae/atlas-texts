The world is a churning, lightless ocean punctuated by peaks. Not islands, but the drowned summits of mountains that once stood in open air, now reduced to jagged archipelagos in a sea that has no horizon. The water is not blue; it is a dense, nutrient-rich black, thick with suspended sediment and the endless fall of organic matter. It moves not in tides, but in vast, slow, planetary gyres—a permanent, world-spanning storm system where current meets counter-current. The surface is a realm of perpetual, violent weather: sheets of rain driven horizontally by katabatic winds pouring off the cold highlands, waves that are less crests and more continuous, frothing upheavals. There is no calm. The sound is a layered roar: the deep, tectonic groan of shifting water masses, the hiss of spray, the percussive drumming of rain on every exposed surface, and beneath it all, a low-frequency hum, almost felt in the bones, the vibration of the planet's own restless engine.

The "land," where it exists, is a testament to the supreme, ancient power of water. Every surface is smoothed, scalloped, and fluted by eons of erosion. The peaks are not sharp, but rounded, like the knuckles of a gargantuan, submerged hand. They are composed of the hardest, most metamorphosed rocks—basalts and gneisses—that have finally yielded to the patient, absolute dominance of the liquid world. In the shallow drowned valleys between these peaks, where the water is a mere hundred meters deep, life explodes in a dense, vertical tapestry.

Lush is an understatement. This is a world of submarine rainforests. Great rafts of buoyant, lily-like plants, some the size of houses, float on the turbulent surface, their roots dangling meters down into the gloom, hosting entire ecosystems of clinging algae, sponges, and filter-feeders. Below, the slopes of the drowned mountains are sheathed in forests of kelp and choral analogues that grow not toward a sun, but toward the faint, diffuse light filtering down from the storm-wracked surface. They form dense, swaying thickets, their fronds and branches near-touching, creating a layered canopy that descends into darkness. Bioluminescence is the true sun here. Colonies of organisms pulse with cold blues and greens along the steeper cliffs, mapping the terrain in living light. The water itself is thick with planktonic blooms, turning the already dim light into a hazy, particulate soup.

The fauna is abundant and adapted to pressure, darkness, and turbulence. Armored, trilobite-like grazers scour the kelp forests. Schools of fish, their bodies streamlined to an extreme, move as single, shimmering entities through the currents, their scales reflecting the bioluminescent light in dazzling, disorienting patterns. In the deeper trenches that plunge into absolute blackness, larger, more languid shapes move—leviathans that navigate by electromagnetic sense and sonar, their calls the deep, resonant pings and clicks that punctuate the watery roar. On the occasional, wave-battered rocky shelf above the waterline—a true rarity—scattered remnants of a different age persist. A single, rusted stanchion of what might have been a watchtower. The corner of a foundation, its concrete cracked and pitted by salt and wind. The faint, ghostly grid of a paved road, visible only at the lowest tides, now a path for scavenging crustaceans. These are not settlements; they are epitaphs.

The industry here is not of fire, but of water and pressure. Great, barnacle-encrusted turbines, anchored to the mountain peaks, harness the unending fury of the currents and storms. Their housings vibrate with a constant, low-power hum, channeling energy through corroded conduits that snake down the slopes into the depths. There are no factories, only automated, submerged processing stations—hives of activity where mineral-rich silt is filtered and compressed by hydraulic presses into dense ingots, to be hauled by submersible barges to unknown destinations. The tech is brutal, functional, and entirely integrated into the aquatic environment; it looks more like a natural growth of metal and ceramic than a human creation.

The rules of this world are simple and absolute. **Water is the medium of all travel, all trade, all life.** The old maps are myths; terrain is measured in depth and current strength. **The storm never ends.** To be on the surface is to accept constant assault. Settlement and thought happen below, in the relative stability of the abyssal plains or the sheltered canyons between the drowned peaks. **The past is a buried layer.** History is not studied, it is dredged for—sifting through the sediment for artifacts, for hard metals, for the rare, preserved data-core sealed in a pressure-proof casing. **Energy is harvested, not generated.** To fight the world's turbulence is to be destroyed; to channel it, to live.

It is a world of sublime and terrible power, where every living thing exists in a state of dynamic, turbulent equilibrium with the all-encompassing sea. It is loud, dark, dense, and alive—absolutely, irreversibly alive in its ancient, fluid form.
