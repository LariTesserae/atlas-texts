The place is a high, cold plateau of immense scale, a world unto itself under a pale, normal light. The air is thin and carries the bite of permanent winter, though the cold is not the blistering sort of lowland blizzards; it is a dry, still cold, the cold of altitude and stone. Mist is the closest thing to weather here—it gathers in the hollows and ravines, a pale, condensing breath that slicks the rock with intermittent moisture and beads on the sparse lichen. There is no true rain, only this seeping dampness, as if the landscape itself is slowly exhaling.

The ground is a young, sharp-edged expanse of fractured basalt and dark slate, uplifted not long ago in the planet's time. The erosion is minimal; cliffs are jagged, scree slopes are unstable, and the peaks around the plateau's rim are needle-like. Between the stone, in cracks and shallow depressions, life persists. It is sparse, but tenacious: cushions of moss in a hundred shades of grey-green and rust, crustose lichens that paint the rock in maps of ochre and silver, and tough, low shrubs no taller than a hand, their leaves small and waxy to conserve what little moisture the mist provides.

Set into the living rock of the plateau’s central massif is the city—or rather, the sprawl. It is dense, a honeycomb of hand-hewn dwellings, storehouses, and gathering halls stacked upon one another, following the natural fissures and ledges of the cliff face. The technology is primitive but ingenious. There is no metal here; tools are of shaped stone, bone, and hardened wood. Structures are built of the same dark basalt as the plateau, mortared with clay and moss, their outlines blurring into the mountain itself. Ropes of woven fiber link different levels, and wooden scaffolds cling to the sheer faces. It is a vertical urbanism, born of the need to conserve heat and shelter from the eternal wind that whispers across the heights.

The quiet is profound, broken by specific sounds: the hollow *tock* of a stone tool striking stone, the distant, melodic clang of a hollow-log bell marking a shift, the creak of fiber rope, and the ever-present, sighing wind. From the sparse scrub come the chitters of small, furred creatures—rodent-like and bird-like things with dense plumage. Abundant life here means hardy life: herds of six-legged, shaggy grazers that move like slow clouds across the higher meadows, picking at the moss; immense, silent birds of prey that ride the updrafts on three-meter wingspans; and the ubiquitous, insect-like swarms that fill the crevices, a crucial part of the cycle. Their noise is a sub-audible hum you feel in your bones more than hear.

Everything moves with a slow, patient dynamic. The mist forms and dissipates over hours. The grazers take a full day to cross a visible slope. The city grows by inches each season, a new dwelling chipped from the rock, a new path worn smooth. This is a place of immense scale and dense habitation, yet it feels empty and full at the same time—a vast, cold, stone world upon which a patient, primitive urban life has etched itself into every available crack. It endures in a state of quiet, damp, high-altitude stasis, where the only true urgency is the preservation of warmth and the careful harvest of the sparse, tenacious life the plateau offers.
