The landscape is a breathing continent of vegetation, a world built entirely from living tissue that has grown, thickened, and settled into its final form over an age that defies memory. There is no ground, only root. The elevation is high—a vast, elevated plateau that creates its own weather systems—yet the terrain is not rocky. It is a dense, woven mat of roots, trunks, and aerial structures, all fused into a single, continuous organism.

The dominant architecture is that of the **Great Weepers**. These are colossal, tree-like forms, but their "trunks" are lattices of aerial roots and hardened sap, and their canopies are not leaves but immense, drooping curtains of moss and epiphytic mats that descend for hundreds of meters until they merge with the floor. Water weeps constantly from their pores and from the condensing clouds that cling to their upper reaches. The atmosphere is perpetually damp, heavy with the smell of wet earth, decaying matter, and the sharp green scent of chlorophyll. Every surface is slick. The air itself feels thick, a warm, humid blanket that muffles sound and diffuses the dim, green-tinged light into a perpetual twilight.

Underfoot, the "soil" is a springy, porous matrix of living and dead roots, sphagnum, and fungal hyphae. It holds water like a sponge, and in places, it gives way to dark, peat-filled pools where the roots thin out. The sound is one of patient, quiet process: the steady drip of condensation from leaf to leaf, the faint gurgle of water moving through hidden channels within the root-mass, the distant, resonant *thrum* of wind passing through the vast, hollow lattices of the Weepers’ trunks. Occasionally, a deeper, woody groan echoes as the entire structure settles—a millimeter of growth or decay over a century.

Fauna is abundant but secretive, evolved to this vegetal world. Insects with iridescent, waterproof carapaces swarm in the mist. Small, six-limbed mammals with prehensile tails navigate the root labyrinths, feeding on fungi and nectar. In the darker pools, amphibious creatures with bioluminescent patches drift like living jewels. Birds are rare, but there are things that glide on membrane-wings between the great trunks, their calls sharp and chittering in the damp air. All life here is integrated, a layer of mobile consciousness upon the vast, stationary plant-body.

The strangeness lies in the **scale of the biology**. There are no individual trees in the human sense; the entire continent is one interconnected super-organism. A "river" is not a body of water but a flowing, vascular bundle of water-conducting tissues on the surface, a river of sap and moisture moving with glacial slowness. In the far distance, where the plateau shrouds itself in cloud, the shapes of the Weepers become indistinct, suggesting columns holding up a sky that is itself a ceiling of mist and living fronds.

The marks of any past civilization are almost gone. Here and there, a stone—too regular, too angular—protrudes from the root-mass, swallowed by lichen and parasitic vines. It might be a cornerstone, a fragment of a plinth. In one deep hollow, a series of arches made of a dark, glassy material rises only to be half-consumed by a buttress root, their purpose and origin utterly forgotten by the patient, growing world. They are merely another substrate.

This is a place at the end of geologic time, where life has won so completely it has become the landscape itself. Change is measured in the slow heave of growth, the gradual sinking of ancient structures, and the silent, eternal weeping of water into its own green, living depths.
