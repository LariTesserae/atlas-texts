# The Vastwork

You stand on ground that is not ground.

The surface beneath your feet gives slightly — warm, faintly resilient, striated in pale greens and deep umber like muscle fiber laid bare. It has grain. It has *direction*. Follow the grain long enough and it rises into ridges, low walls of compressed fibrous substrate that run in sweeping parallel lines toward a horizon you cannot find, because the horizon doesn't exist here in any honest sense. The terrain just goes. It extends. It keeps being *more of this* — the same young, sharp-edged ridges with their almost-geometric profiles, the same pale substrate between them, the same dimness overhead that doesn't come from any sky you recognize.

This is the Vastwork. No one remembers if it was always here or if it emerged, fully formed, sometime after the last thing anyone reliably remembers. It doesn't matter. It *is here now*, and it is nearly incomprehensible in scale.

The lowlands between the ridges — and everything here is lowlands, because the whole structure sits in a kind of shallow basin with no visible walls — are where the water lives. Not rivers. Not lakes. Pockets. Irregular bowls and depressions in the substrate that hold water in amounts that seem almost reluctant, as though the surface absorbs moisture as fast as it can and only occasionally loses the argument. Each pool is warm, almost body-temperature, slightly viscous, and faintly luminescent in shades of pale amber. They steam. The fog they give off hangs in low strata, rarely rising above what you'd guess is ten or fifteen meters, and it moves — slowly, purposefully — drawn along the channels between ridges by some gradient in temperature or pressure or something less nameable. Condensation beads on every surface. The ridges weep. The air itself is a wet, warm, breathing thing that tastes faintly of copper and sugar.

And there is sound.

God, *there is sound*.

It is the first thing that makes you understand how large this place is, because the sound has *weather*. It comes in waves, in fronts, in systems. Right now — this moment — it is a low, pervasive thrum, a vibration felt more in the chest than the ears, like standing inside a cathedral organ while someone holds a single note on the lowest pedal. But underneath and above and through it are other layers. Clicking. A sound like vast sheets of plastic being crumpled. Birdsong — except not birds, and the calls are wrong, too melodic in places and too mechanical in others, echoing off ridges that are hundreds of meters apart and arriving from directions that don't correspond to anything you can see. Sometimes a sound like tearing fabric, enormous, sustained for thirty seconds, fading. Sometimes a rhythmic percussion — biological, almost certainly — that thumps through the substrate and makes the pools ripple in unison. The silence between sounds is itself a sound: a high, faint whine, almost at the edge of perception, as though the substrate itself is vibrating at some ultrasonic frequency translated down into the audible range by your overwhelmed ears.

You have to shout to be heard by someone standing next to you. There is no "next to you." There is barely anyone.

---

The density here is the thing that will break your mind if you let it.

Everything — the ridges, the pools, the fog, the organisms — is spread out with a spacing that feels *deliberate*, as though something calculated the minimum viable distance between every presence and then doubled it for good measure. You can stand at any point and see in every direction for what feels like kilometers, and in that entire radius there might be six or seven major features. A ridge. A pool. A structure. A living thing. Six. In a space that should hold thousands.

And yet it is *full of life*.

The vegetation — if that word even applies — is everywhere and enormous and impossibly lush. Great fronds rise from the substrate in spirals, some of them thirty or forty meters tall, their surfaces covered in a fine, fur-like carpet of photosynthetic filaments that sway in the fog-drift. They are not green, exactly. They are a deep, saturated teal that shifts toward violet at the tips, and they pulse with faint bioluminescence along their veins — a slow, rhythmic brightening and dimming, like breathing. Between the fronds, the substrate itself is alive: a secondary layer of growth, low and mat-like, that covers nearly every exposed surface in textures ranging from velvet to coral to something that looks and feels like living lace. Flowering structures emerge at irregular intervals — not flowers as you know them, but geometric blossoms of translucent tissue, backlit from within, each one a different color, each one emitting a specific scent that changes with the temperature. The warm ones smell like rain on hot stone. The cool ones smell like something sweeter, almost floral, almost chemical. The overall effect of the vegetation is of being inside a living lung — everything breathing, everything growing, everything photosynthesizing or chemosynthesizing or doing whatever it is organisms do here in this dim, warm, wet, fog-choked half-light.

And the fauna.

They are the most unsettling part, because they are *clearly visible* and *rare* and *enormous*.

Something moves in the middle distance — you see it through the fog, a shape that takes several seconds to resolve. It is tall, perhaps two hundred meters, stilted on legs that look like inverted wishbones, each leg ending not in a foot but in a broad, flat pad that presses into the substrate and leaves behind a faintly glowing impression. The body is a bulbous, asymmetric mass — translucent in places, showing dark shapes moving inside it — and from its underside hang filaments, hundreds of them, trailing through the fog like the tendrils of a jellyfish. It moves slowly, deliberately, each leg-lift accompanied by a deep, resonant *thock* that you feel in your teeth. It does not acknowledge you. It may not be able to perceive you. You are very, very small.

Closer — fifty meters, maybe — something else. A flat, ray-like creature glides through the fog at a height of about ten meters, its body a disc of iridescent tissue perhaps twenty meters across, undulating at its edges in a motion that is hypnotic and biomechanically improbable. It is followed by a trail of smaller versions — dozens of them, each a meter or less across — moving in a loose, spiraling formation. They make a sound like wind chimes, but wet.

And between the megafauna, filling the spaces, the air itself seems alive. Swarms of tiny organisms — you see them when the fog thins, thousands of pinprick lights moving in coordinated clouds — drift and pulse and occasionally coalesce into brief, denser shapes before dissolving again. They emit a high, reedy hum that contributes to the overall wall of sound. On the substrate, things crawl — segmented, legged, many-eyed, ranging from the size of a hand to the size of a dog. They move with purpose. They avoid each other with choreographed precision. Some of them are eating the living mat that covers the ground, and where they've eaten, the mat regrows visibly in real time, a slow green tide reclaiming the exposed substrate within minutes.

---

And then there is the city.

You don't see it all at once. You see *parts* of it, and it takes time to understand that they are connected.

The first sign is a ridge that's different from the others. Same height, same general profile, but its surface is *too regular* — the striations too evenly spaced, the edges too clean. And embedded in its face, at intervals, are dark apertures: openings, roughly oval, each perhaps two meters across, set into the ridge at what appear to be deliberate heights. Some are at ground level. Some are ten meters up, with no visible means of access. They lead inward. The substrate inside the apertures is smooth — polished, almost — and it continues the same warm temperature as the outside, but the air that comes out of the openings has a different smell: ozone and something metallic, something *processed*.

Following the ridge, you find more of these structures. They connect. The apertures multiply. Some ridges have merged, their substrate fused into a single wall with dozens of openings at multiple levels. Bridges span the gaps between them — slender arches of the same living substrate, but woven with something that glints, something with a crystalline regularity that doesn't occur naturally. The bridges vibrate faintly when you step on them, and the vibration carries a signal — you can feel it communicating something, though you can't read it. The surfaces here are covered in patterns: not carved, not painted, but *grown* into the substrate in fractal arrangements that seem to encode information at multiple scales. Zoom in and the patterns have patterns. Zoom in again and they have patterns still.

The city — if it is a city, if that word can apply to something that appears to have been grown rather than built, that has no roads or doors or windows in any recognizable sense — extends for what might be tens of kilometers in every direction, though its edges are not clearly defined. It fades into the natural landscape gradually, the structures becoming less dense, the apertures fewer, the bridges more rare, until you're back among the ridges and pools and fronds and you can't point to a line and say *the city starts here*. It isn't *on* the landscape. It *is* the landscape. Or the landscape is it. The distinction has been erased by time and technology and something that might be ecology and might be engineering and is probably both.

And the technology.

You find evidence of it everywhere, though none of it is recognizable. Things embedded in the substrate at ground level — smooth, dark ovals that emit no sound and no light but that are *warm*, warmer than the surrounding ground, and when you place your hand on one, you feel a complex vibration, a rhythm, something that feels like computation at a scale and in a medium you have no framework for understanding. The fronds have been *modified* — you can see it in the regularity of their vein patterns, in the way their bioluminescence follows a schedule that seems too precise to be natural. The fog itself may be managed: it moves in patterns that, over hours, begin to look like circulation, like a respiratory system, like the breath of something vast. The swarms of tiny luminous organisms coordinate across distances of kilometers with no visible central authority, and their swarm-patterns occasionally form shapes — geometric, mathematical, *intentional* — that persist for minutes before dissolving.

The technology here is not *used*. It is not *operated*. It is *inherent*. It is the medium in which everything exists. The organisms, the landscape, the structures, the weather — they are all computation, or information, or some process that doesn't have a name yet. The city is the computer. The ecosystem is the program. The fauna are the processes. And you are a user without an interface, standing in the middle of something that was designed by minds so far beyond human that their output looks like nature.

---

At night — and there is a night here, though it is not caused by the rotation of a planet; it is a dimming, a shift in the ambient bioluminescence from teal-and-amber to deep indigo-and-silver — the sound changes. The thrum recedes. The clicking becomes dominant. The great stilted creatures are quieter, their thocking steps more spaced out. But the swarms brighten, and they form shapes in the dark that are unmistakably *symbols*, or *glyphs*, or some form of communication broadcast across the fog in a language made of living light.

And if you stand very still, and if you are lucky, you feel it — a sensation that comes not through any sense you can name but through something more fundamental. A sense of *scale*. A sense that you are standing inside something that is *aware of itself*, that is *processing*, that is alive in a way that encompasses and transcends every biological and technological definition you've ever known. The Vastwork is not a place. It is an organism, or a machine, or a thought. It is young — geologically, structurally, in whatever way age applies to something like this — and it is still becoming.

You are very small. The ridges go on forever. The fog breathes. The sound continues.

It will never stop.
