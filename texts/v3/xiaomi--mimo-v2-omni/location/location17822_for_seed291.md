# The Steaming Rows

You come into it across a plain that shouldn't be flat but is — unnervingly, freshly flat, as if a great hand pressed the earth level and left. The ground beneath your feet is cold clay, dense as plaster, and it gives slightly with each step the way a bruise gives when pressed. There is moisture in everything. Not standing water — you might walk for an hour and find no pool, no stream, no puddle — but a pervasive, exhaled dampness that beads on every surface and gathers in the creases of your clothing. Condensation runs down walls in thin, irregular threads. The air tastes of iron and wet limestone.

And then: the rows begin.

**The settlement** — and it is a settlement, unmistakably, though no one you've asked can say precisely what it is *for* — extends for kilometers in every direction. The buildings are close, packed with a density that borders on organic, as if they grew against one another for warmth. They are industrial in character: long, low structures of riveted sheet metal and poured concrete, smokestacks of various diameters, venting systems that hiss and tick with thermal contraction. Many of the structures appear to be only partially completed, or perhaps un-completed — rebar exposed and curving upward like the ribs of something half-eaten, concrete forms still bearing the impression of wooden molds that have since rotted away or been pulled free. There are buildings within buildings: an older shell, a newer interior, and between them a gap filled with pipes and wiring and the slow green advance of vegetation that no one has trimmed.

**The vegetation is relentless.** This is the great paradox of the place — how alive it is, how aggressively green, in a climate that should not support it. The cold is real: you can see your breath most mornings, and the metal surfaces bite at bare skin. Yet the plants thrive with an intensity that suggests they are drawing warmth from somewhere below. Ferns erupt from cracks in foundation slabs. Moss — thick, almost spongy, in shades of green so deep they verge on black — coats every north-facing surface. Vines with broad, waxy leaves climb the smokestacks and hang in curtains from catwalks. In the gaps between buildings, where the settlement's density relents for a moment, there are thickets of something like bamboo — segmented, pale green, reaching four or five meters — that creak and knock together in wind you cannot feel. The roots of these plants have gotten into the plumbing, the drainage systems, the foundations. In some districts the buildings lean at angles that suggest the vegetation is slowly, patiently shouldering them apart.

**Water arrives without rain.** Or rather, rain is rare — a cold, fine drizzle that comes and goes in hours — but the dampness is constant. It rises from the ground in the early hours, a ground-fog so thick it obliterates the first meter of every structure. It condenses on every surface above. It drips from the vegetation in steady, quiet rhythms. There are cisterns everywhere — carved stone, poured concrete, corrugated metal — and they fill without any visible source. Some overflow. Some have been overflowing for so long that the buildings around them stand in shallow, algae-greened basins. The drainage systems are elaborate but insufficient, and in the lowest areas of the settlement — the place is not quite as flat as it first appears; there are subtle sags, basins, depressions — the water gathers in thin sheets that reflect the overcast sky with a metallic stillness.

**And it is quiet.** This is the wrong thing about the place, the detail that sits wrong in the mind and refuses to resolve. For a settlement of this scale — and the scale is considerable; you can walk for a full day in one direction and not reach its edge — the volume is wrong. There are sounds: the drip of condensation, the tick of cooling metal, the creak of vegetation against scaffolding, the occasional distant clank of something mechanical cycling through its operation. But there are no voices. Or rather — the voices are there, but they arrive wrong. Muffled, as if heard through walls that aren't there. Present but without source. You hear what might be a conversation around a corner and find only ferns and fog. A laugh, high and brief, from inside a structure whose door is welded shut.

**The fauna** accounts for the quiet in its own way. The animals here are abundant — more abundant than in any forest or field you've known — but they move with a deliberateness that suggests awareness of something. Birds, mostly: grey and green, with long, flexible necks, perched on every exposed beam and ledge. They watch without singing. There are small mammals — something between a rat and a rabbit, soft-furred, dark-eyed — that move through the undergrowth and the pipe-channels in constant, silent processions. In the condensation pools you occasionally see the surface break: a fish, or something shaped like a fish, surfacing and submerging without sound. At night — and the nights are long here, the latitude unforgiving — something larger moves between the buildings. You hear its passage through the vegetation: a steady, rhythmic compression of bamboo and fern. It does not approach.

**The strangeness.** It accumulates. It is not a single revelation but a slow accretion of wrongness:

The buildings have floor plans that don't match their exteriors. You enter a structure expecting a single hall and find corridors that branch and descend. The numbering systems on the buildings — stamped into metal plates beside the doors — use a notation you cannot parse. Sometimes the numbers include characters that are not numbers.

The industrial equipment runs. This is the other wrong thing. The engines operate, the generators hum, the conveyor systems move with slow, empty purpose — but there is no one tending them. No product emerges. The factories produce a faint warmth and a rhythmic vibration that you feel in your sternum when you stand near their walls, and that is all. Some of the machinery appears to have been running long enough for vegetation to grow through its moving parts without halting its motion. The plants weave between pistons and gears with a flexibility that suggests the machinery has learned to accommodate them, or they the machinery.

There are gardens. Deliberate, tended, unmistakable gardens — plots of herbs and flowering plants arranged with aesthetic care in the courtyards between factory blocks. The soil in these gardens is dark and rich and warm to the touch. Someone tends them. You find the tools: trowels, shears, watering cans, all of local manufacture, all slightly too large for a human hand. The soil is warm. You keep coming back to this. The soil is warm.

**The ground is still forming.** This is the geological truth of the place, the fact that underlies everything else. The flatness is too recent. The clay beneath the settlement is still settling, still compressing. In certain districts — the older districts, or perhaps the newer ones; the distinction is unclear — you can see fresh cracks in the foundations that have been patched and cracked again and patched again. New stone pushes up through the clay in low, rounded formations, like the first knuckles of something buried. The drainage patterns shift from season to season. Streams that ran south last month run east now. The cisterns that were full are dry; the dry places pool.

The settlement was built on ground that was not ready to be built on. Or the ground is responding to the settlement. Or both. The relationship is co-creative and adversarial and you cannot tell where the building ends and the geology begins.

**On the outskirts** — if there are outskirts, if the edges are real and not just points where your perception gives out — the buildings thin and the vegetation thickens and the ground becomes uncertain. The clay gives way to something softer, something that remembers being mud. The fog is denser here. The buildings that stand at these margins are older, or newer, or different: made of stone rather than metal, with narrow windows and heavy doors, as if designed to keep something out or in. They are overgrown beyond entry. Their chimneys trail vines instead of smoke.

Beyond them, the plain extends. Featureless. Cold. Flat in that unnatural way. Moisture rising.

Something is being built, or something is building itself, and the process is not finished, and the settlement is as much a feature of the landscape as the ferns and the clay and the silent birds. It is a place that is becoming. It has not decided yet what it is becoming. The engines run. The gardens grow. The water gathers. The ground, patient and warm, shifts beneath everything, rearranging the foundations of a city that no one remembers building and no one is able to leave.
