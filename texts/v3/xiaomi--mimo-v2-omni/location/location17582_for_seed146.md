# The Underbelly

You are deep. Not in the way a cellar is deep, or a mine — those are human ideas, vertical shafts with ladders and purpose. This is deep the way a body is deep. You are inside something, and it does not know you are here, and it would not care if it did.

## The Cavern That Is Not a Cavern

The space extends in every direction farther than your eyes can resolve. The darkness is not total — that is the first strangeness. There is a faint, sourceless warmth to the light, a dim umber glow that seems to come from the stone itself, as if the rock remembers being magma and can't quite forget its luminosity. You can see perhaps thirty or forty paces in any direction. Beyond that, shapes become suggestions. Beyond suggestions, there is nothing you can name.

The ceiling — if it can be called that — is somewhere between two hundred and five hundred paces above. It is impossible to determine with certainty. The geometry does not invite measurement. Arches and vaults and inverted mountain-shapes descend from above like the architecture of a cathedral designed by something that had never seen the sky and did not know what a roof was for. Some of these formations nearly touch the ground. Others vanish upward into the glow-and-darkness, unresolved, forever ascending. Stalactites the color of old bone and new rust hang in clusters so dense they form secondary ceilings, lower canopies of mineral, and between them hang threads — not spider-silk, not web, but something fibrous and pale, like nerve tissue or the branching of a river delta pressed into three dimensions and suspended in air. These threads are part of the ecology. More on that shortly.

The floor is not flat. It is not rough, either. It is *convoluted*. Rolling ridges of pale mineral, like the surface of a brain scaled up to the size of a landscape. Between the ridges: troughs, shallow valleys, some only a body-length deep, others plunging into shadowed crevices that go down further than the light can follow. The stone underfoot is warm — not hot, but warm the way skin is warm. It has a living temperature. If you press your ear to it (and people have; there are smooth depressions in the ridges that suggest this has been done for generations), you can hear something. Not a heartbeat. A kind of slow, granular shifting, as if the entire mass of the world is settling one grain at a time into a position it will never quite reach.

The features are sharp. Young. The ridges have crisp edges, not softened by time or weather. There is no weather here in any conventional sense — no rain, no wind, no frost. But there is something that plays the role of weather. In the far distance, in directions you cannot orient, there are drifts of fine powder —ite dust,ite crystal — that move in slow, vast cycles, piling against one ridge, then months later eroding away and堆积 against another. A tide without water. The mineral dust catches the faint luminescence and, from a distance, looks like fog rolling across a dark plain. It is not fog. If you walk through it, it stings. It tastes of copper and alkaline salt.

## The Dryness

There is no water. This must be stated plainly, because the mind reaches for water the way the tongue reaches for moisture, and here the reaching is never satisfied. Not a drop. Not a seep. Not a condensation on the walls, not a dampness in the air. The humidity is not low — it is *absent*. The concept does not apply. The air is something else: dry in a way that transcends aridity. It is air that has been *cured*, like leather, like meat. It has a weight to it, a density, and a smell — not unpleasant, but alien. Ozone. Warm basalt. A faint sweetness like the resin of a tree that has no wood.

There is no standing liquid of any kind. The mineral chemistry of this place operates in the space between solid and gas. Certain formations on the walls appear to *sublimate* — delicate crystal flowers that seem to grow in real-time, or rather, in a time so slow that watching them for an hour reveals nothing, but returning after a year reveals a new lattice, a new bloom. The walls are alive with this process. It is the closest thing this world has to vegetation, and it is not vegetation. It is chemistry happening in the open, unashamed, without the machinery of roots or leaves to disguise it.

## The Layered Dark

The density of the place is physical. You feel it in your chest. The space is not empty — it is *full*, but full of things that are easy to overlook. The pale threads hanging from the ceiling-formations are everywhere, and they are difficult to see because they are nearly the same color as the dim air. They form curtains, nets, cat's-cradles strung between the stalactites at varying heights. Some are as fine as hair. Others are thick as a man's arm, braided, with a texture like gut. They are not alive — or they are not *alive* in any way that biology would recognize — but they are not inert. They vibrate. Occasionally, a thread will thrum once, a low tone at the very bottom of hearing, and then go still. This is one of the few sounds in this place.

Sound, here, is strange. It does not carry. It does not echo. It is *absorbed* — swallowed by the dense air, by the mineral walls, by the threads. A shout reaches perhaps ten paces and then simply stops, not fading but ceasing, as if the sound wave walked into a wall it could not see. Conversation is possible, but only at close range, and only in low tones. This suits the fauna. It suits whatever else is here.

The few sounds that persist are: the sub-audible hum of the ground itself; the occasional thrum of a thread; and a clicking, intermittent and arrhythmic, that comes from the fauna. More on the fauna.

## What Lives Here

It lives. In abundance. In the dark, without water, in the warm dry dense air of this place, something has found a way to not just persist but *proliferate*.

The creatures are — how to describe them — they are not insects, though some move with that jointed, many-legged precision. They are not mammals, though some are warm (warmer than the stone, warmer than the air) and move with a soft, furred deliberateness. They are not fungi, though some grow in clusters from the walls and ceilings, sessile, pulsing faintly, harvesting something from the mineral-rich air. They are *convergent*. They have arrived at similar solutions — locomotion, consumption, reproduction — from a completely alien starting point, so they look like things you almost recognize, things your mind tries to categorize as *beetle* or *slug* or *bat* and then fails, because the legs are wrong, the body plan is wrong, the eyes (those that have eyes) are arranged in patterns that suggest a sensory relationship with the world that you cannot intuit.

Most of them are small. The smallest are like mites, nearly invisible, clustering on the crystal-blooms on the walls, and they are everywhere — a fine, crawling dust of life on every surface. The medium-sized are the most common: creatures the length of a forearm, pale, chitinous or ceramic in texture, with too many limbs that move in alternating waves. They navigate the thread-network with ease, gripping the fibers, moving along them upside-down, pausing to — feed? Listen? Perform some action that has no human analogue. They click. This is the clicking you hear: a language, or a navigation method, or a byproduct of their joints. It has patterns. It is not random. But it is not speech.

The largest creatures are rarely seen and never fully resolved in the dim light. They move in the far distance, in the troughs between the brain-ridges, or in the vertical spaces near the ceiling. They are slow. Their outlines suggest something between a ray and a centipede — wide, flat, undulating, with edges that ripple. They glow faintly, a colder light than the stone's warmth, blue-white, and they leave trails of disturbed mineral dust in their wake, slow dunes rearranged by passage. There may be five of these creatures in this cavern-system. There may be five hundred. The space is too large and the light too poor to know.

## The Builders

And then: evidence of intelligence. Not evidence of *current* intelligence — evidence of past intelligence, or of an intelligence so alien and so slow that it expresses itself in ways that look like geology.

Scattered across the landscape — sometimes in the troughs, sometimes perched on the ridges like cairns — are structures. They are small, mostly. A pile of precisely fitted mineral blocks, each one the size of a fist, arranged in spirals. A notch carved into a wall at regular intervals, climbing upward for thirty paces and then stopping, for no apparent reason, at a smooth and featureless stretch of stone. A pit, perfectly circular, ten paces across and two deep, with walls so smooth they look machined, and at the bottom: nothing. An offering, perhaps, to something that did not come.

The material of these structures is the same mineral as the surrounding stone, but *worked*. There are tool-marks. The marks are simple — gouges, scrapes, impacts — consistent with stone tools or perhaps soft metal tools, though no metal has been found. The builders, whoever they were or are, did not have complex technology. They had patience. They had repetition. Some of the structures are clearly very old — the sharp edges of the tool-marks softened by the slow sublimation of crystal-growth, the blocks fused to each other and to the ground by a patina of mineral bloom. Others look fresh. Fresh in geological terms, at least — which here might mean a thousand years, or might mean last week. The dynamics of this place offer no help in dating. Nothing changes. The conditions that existed when a structure was built are the conditions that exist now.

There are no bodies. No bones (the dryness would preserve them, if they existed). No remains. Just the structures, and the tool-marks, and the implication of hands.

## The Stillness

The world does not change. This is the deepest and most unsettling quality of the place. Not that it changes slowly — it does not change. The crystal-blooms grow, but if you photograph them and return after a year, the growth is indistinguishable from measurement error. The dust-drifts move, but they return to the same positions in the same slow cycles, as if on rails. The fauna follow patterns so consistent they might be programmed. The threads hum at intervals that seem random but, over long observation, reveal themselves to be periodic — cycles so long that no human lifespan would be sufficient to see them repeat.

There is no erosion. There is no weather. There is no decay. The creatures that die — and they do die; the small ones most frequently, their pale carapaces accumulating in thin strata in the troughs — do not rot. They dry. They become part of the mineral record. Their bodies are gradually incorporated into the crystal-bloom on the walls, becoming scaffolding for new growth, becoming indistinguishable from the stone. The place is not a graveyard. It is a sediment. Every surface is a compressed history of everything that has ever lived and died and been absorbed here, which is to say: the stone *is* the history, and the history is the stone, and there is no distinction between the living and the built and the geological.

Time, here, is flat. Not frozen — frozen implies something that was once fluid. This is a place where the concept of *before* and *after* has been smoothed to nothing, like a pebble worn not by water (there is no water) but by the sheer weight of unchanging duration. You could live here for a year and see nothing new. You could live here for a century and see nothing new. The clicking of the creatures would continue. The threads would hum their long, slow notes. The ground would warm your ear with its granular settling. The dark would hold.

## What It Feels Like

It feels, paradoxically, like being inside something alive — not alive in the way a cave is sometimes described as *breathing*, with drafts and echoes, but alive in a cellular, metabolic sense. The warmth is the warmth of a body. The density is the density of tissue. The sounds are the sounds of a system operating at its own pace, indifferent to observation. You are inside something that does not know you are here. You are, at best, a mineral inclusion. A foreign body that will eventually be absorbed, your atoms joining the crystal-bloom, your tool-marks (if you left any) softened by the patient chemistry of the walls, your history becoming indistinguishable from the stone.

It is not hostile. Hostility would require attention, and this place does not attend. It simply *is*, and it is vast, and it is warm, and it is dry beyond any dryness you have known, and it is full of things that click and crawl and pulse and glow and have been doing so for longer than you can fathom and will continue to do so after you are gone, in the same way, in the same places, in the same dim light that is not light but memory of light, held in the stone forever.
