The air is thick enough to drink, and tastes of wet stone and copper. It clings to the skin, a constant condensation that never quite becomes rain. This is a world of perpetual post-rrain, where the atmosphere itself sweats.

The land is flat, so flat it feels like the baseboard of the world, and it is dark. Not the darkness of night, which implies a sun somewhere, but a fundamental, material darkness. The ground is a yielding, black loam, saturated to the point of liquidity. It swallows footfalls whole. From this mire rise the structures.

They are not buildings in any remembered sense. They are the weathered, settled forms of a city that has given up its shape to time and saturation. What might have been towers are now tapering, melting pillars of aite-like stone, their surfaces intricately patterned with erosion channels—centuries of water finding its own patient way down. What might have been walls are low, zig-zagging ridges, their tops rounded and softened, weeping a constant, clear seepage. The material itself is strange: a dark, fine-grained rock that never dries, warm to the touch, and faintly luminous with a sickly, internal phosphorescence that provides the only light—a dim, gray-violet glow that turns the pooling water into sheets of black mirror.

This is a place of profound silence, yet it is actively, ceaselessly busy. The primary sound is the drip, the trickle, the gurgle. Water moves everywhere—through capillaries in the stone, across the loam, coalescing in the vast, shallow depressions that serve as silent, inland seas. There is a low, almost sub-audible hum, like the vibration of the earth itself, or the resonance of all that held water. No wind stirs. No creature calls. The ecology is one of minerals and processes alone.

Sparse vegetation exists as a kind of geological crust. In the warmer, brighter seeps, patches of a thick, gelatinous cyanobacteria bloom in electric blues and oranges, their colors shockingly vivid in the gloom. They are photosynthetic, but not for any sun; they feed on the faint phosphorescence and the chemical energy of the weeping rocks, growing in slow, pulsing rhythms. Elsewhere, delicate, crystalline formations—like frost made of silica and salt—bloom in intricate fractals on the driest tips of the highest pillars, catching the weak light and scattering it into dim, prismatic sparks.

The reality here is thin, abraded by time and saturation. The erosion is not just physical, but ontological. The city-forms are not merely weathered; they are *forgetting* their original purpose. A doorway might open onto a solid wall of fused stone three inches away. A graceful arch might lead not across a space, but into a dense, warm fog that hums and gives back the same view from a slightly different angle. The paths between the structures shift, not by moving, but by the slow, gravitational slump of the land and the patient work of water. A traveler might follow a winding channel between two low walls, only to find it has become a cul-de-sac, the walls having imperceptibly leaned together in the time it took to walk the length.

There is a logic to it, but it is the logic of a dream or a slow fever. The heat is constant, oppressive, a blanket that steams from the ground. The darkness is textured, layered with the glow of decay and the movement of water. It is urban in its density of form, yet sparse in its habitation; a metropolis for ghosts, or for the patient, mineral processes that are now its only citizens. It is a place that has achieved a terrible, beautiful equilibrium—a city that has completed its long journey back to being a landscape, and in doing so, has become something else entirely. A theorem in stone and water, proving only its own existence, breathing softly in the dark.
