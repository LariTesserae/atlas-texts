The ground is not soil but a porous, dark grey mineralite, likeite that sweats. Water does not pool here in lakes or streams but seeps perpetually upward, beading on every surface, gathering in shallow, shifting rivulets that lace the landscape in a trembling silver mesh. The rock itself appears to be the source—a cool, damp exhalation from the planet’s bones. The air is thick with this moisture, a fine mist that hangs in the dim light, making distances blur and every surface glisten.

This is an ancient, near-flattened plain, stretching to a horizon that is more a suggestion than a line. The only elevation comes from gentle, eroded swells—the last whispers of what might have been mountains a billion years ago. They are smooth as worn teeth, their flanks scored with deep, parallel channels where the weeping has, over eons, carved its path. The scale is vast and open, a regional emptiness where the eye can travel for miles without encountering anything but the glistening grey and the soft, blurred shapes of the next swell.

There is no plant life. Nothing green, nothing with leaves or stems. The biological niche, if it can be called that, is occupied by something far stranger. Crystalline formations, like frost or slow-motion explosions of quartz, bloom in the water-channels. They are not alive in any familiar sense, but they grow, fed by the mineral-rich seepage. They chime when the water flows over them, adding a high, glassy note to the soundscape. Other presences are more mobile: herds of low-slung, multi-jointed creatures with translucent hides, through which you can see the slow pulse of dark fluids. They move with a deliberate, scraping gait, their bodies armored in plates of what looks like chitin harvested from the very rock they consume. They are abundant, dotting the hollows and channels, their collective movement a soft, continuous *skritch-skritch* against the stone.

And the sound. It is deafening, but not with a single roar. It is a layered, omnipresent cacophony. The constant *shush* and gurgle of water moving over and through the rock. The deep, sub-bass hum that seems to come from the ground itself, a geological resonance. The crystalline chimes. The scraping of the fauna. And above it all, the wind—a hollow, moaning wind that never ceases, funneled by the ancient channels and the vast, open space. It carries the mist and erodes what little is left to erode.

Scattered across this plain are the only marks of any intelligence. Simple structures of stacked, unworked stone—low circles, partial walls, the faint ghost of a foundation. They are not ruins; they are too simple, too fundamental. They feel more like placeholders, or markers. The tools left behind are similarly primitive: hand-axes of dense, dark basalt; bowls carved from a softer, soapstone-like rock, always found half-full of the ubiquitous water. The technology is at its dawn, or perhaps its dusk.

The light is perpetually dim. There is no sun visible, only a diffuse, pearlescent glow from the entire sky, as if the cloud layer is the source of illumination itself. Shadows are soft, indistinct. The temperature is cool, a damp chill that seeps into the bones, unchanging with any cycle of day or night.

Here, then, is the world in its settled, final form. Not a dead world, but a post-organic one. The activity is in the water, the slow growth of the crystals, the grazing of the chitinous herds, the eternal wind. The weirdness is in the very premise: life that is not life, feeding on a landscape that weeps but does not rot. The ancient erosion has ground everything down to this—a wet, dim, loud, and strangely beautiful equilibrium. It is a place that does not remember being anything else, and shows no sign of becoming anything new.
