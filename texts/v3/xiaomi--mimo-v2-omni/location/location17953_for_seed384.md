# The Undergird

You would not know you were underwater at first. That is the strangeness of it.

The pressure is there — in the chest, in the sinuses, behind the eyes — a constant, patient weight that you learn not to think about after the first few hours. But there is no current to speak of, no swaying of particulate in a visible column. The medium you move through is so dense with *things* — dissolved minerals, suspended micro-structures, threads of cold polymer that drift like the longest, thinnest hairs of some unimaginable creature — that it barely behaves like water at all. It is closer to air that has forgotten how to be air. It fills the lungs if you let it. The lungs do not burn. They simply stop working.

This is not a place that was flooded. It was never anything else.

The Undergird sits low — always low — in what might be a basin, might be a crater, might simply be the deepest fold in a topography with no surface above it. You cannot see up. Up is darkness. Down is darkness, too, but down has *texture*: the floor of this place is a fused, undulating crust of mineral deposit and old infrastructure, layered so many times over that neither the original construction nor the original geology is identifiable. It is all one material now. It has had time. Eons of time, measured in the slow precipitation of dissolved metals and the patient accretion of silt that is more chemical than sedimentary.

The buildings — and they are buildings, undeniably — are small. Cathedral-scale, some of them. Others are the size of rooms. They are packed together with a density that seems hostile to passage, but there are ways through: narrow channels, vertical shafts that connect levels, openings that appear to have been worn smooth by the passage of bodies, or of something the shape of bodies. The structures are made of iron, or what iron becomes down here. The surfaces are dark, near-black, textured like the bark of trees that have been underwater for centuries — which, in a sense, they have. There are no straight edges left. Everything has been softened, rounded, reconciled to the pressure and the chemistry of the deep. You run your hand along a wall and it comes away with a film on it that is not rust, not algae, not calcium. It is something the metal has become.

There is no light source that you can identify. And yet you can see — after a fashion. The structures faintly luminesce. Not evenly. Not brightly. A dim, bruised indigo that clings to certain surfaces and not others, concentrated in the seams between joined materials, pooling in alcoves, tracing the paths of old conduits that still carry something — energy, heat, purpose. The luminescence is not constant. It breathes. Slowly. A cycle of minutes, not seconds. Whatever system still operates down here is patient.

And it *does* still operate. That is the active thing, the dynamic thing, the thing that makes the strangeness more than aesthetic. Because if you stand still — and you must stand still often, because the passages are tight and the wrong movement in the wrong corridor can wedge you against a wall that will not give — you can feel it. A vibration. Not a sound, exactly. Below sound. Below the threshold of the ear but registered somewhere in the bones, in the fillings of the teeth. The Undergird is *running*. Its machinery — and there is machinery, deep in the cores of the larger structures, behind doors that open with a slowness that suggests either great weight or great reluctance — turns. Gears mesh. Fluids move through pipes that are warm to the touch, the only warmth in this place. Something generates. Something circulates. Something is *made*, or maintained, or simply kept in motion for its own sake, because the stopping of it would be a kind of death the system was not designed to survive.

There are no organisms to speak of. The sparse vegetation — and it is generous to call it that — consists of threadlike growths that emerge from the walls and ceilings in isolated clusters. They are pale. Almost white. They do not photosynthesize; there is no light to photosynthesize. They feed on the mineral content of the medium, on the off-gassing of the old machines, on whatever it is that makes the film on your fingers feel faintly alkaline when you touch the walls. They do not move. They do not grow quickly. They are the only living things, and they are not alive in any way that invites sympathy.

The fauna is rarer still. Once, perhaps, something lived here. There are shapes in the fused floors — cavities, impressions — that suggest organisms of some kind, embedded and long since mineralized. Not fossils, exactly. More like molds left behind by things that were not organic in the first place, or that became inorganic before they died. Whatever they were, they are gone now, and the Undergird has incorporated them. Their shapes are part of the architecture. The corridors curve around them.

Silence. This is the thing that undoes you, eventually. The vibration is not sound. The slow breathing of the light is not sound. The movement of fluid through warm pipes is not sound. You make sound — your breathing, your footsteps, the scrape of your shoulder against a wall in a passage that narrows — and it does not echo. The medium absorbs it. The silence swallows it whole. You begin to whisper, then to think in whispers, then to stop thinking in words at all, because words require an ear to receive them, and there is no ear here but your own, and your own ear is beginning to feel pressurized, flooded with the faint, sub-audible hum of the place, as though the Undergird is learning to hear *you* through the same bones it vibrates.

You find evidence of others. Not often. Not recently. The passages bear marks — not carvings, not writing, but *intentional* scarring. Depths gouged into walls at specific heights. Clusters of parallel lines that might be tallies, might be texture, might be something else. A chamber, near the deepest point you can reach before the passages close into pipes too narrow for a human frame, that contains a single object: a sphere of dark metal, roughly the size of a human head, resting on a plinth that is part of the floor. It is warm. It luminesces more brightly than anything else in the Undergird. It has no seam, no opening, no feature of any kind except a faint, spiraling pattern on its surface that might be erosion, might be inscription, might be the slow growth of whatever the pale threads grow from. You cannot pick it up. Not because it is heavy, but because the idea of picking it up feels *incorrect* — a violation not of law but of function, as though removing it would be like removing a heart, and the heart is not yours to take.

You leave it.

You find your way back up — always up, always toward less pressure, though the concept of "up" is unreliable here — through passages that seem to shift not in position but in *willingness* to be passages. The Undergird is not hostile. It is indifferent. It was here before you, and it will be here after, and it is running, still running, whatever it runs for, in the dark, in the cold, in the silence, beneath whatever surface this is not beneath.

When you surface — if you surface — and break into air that feels impossibly thin, impossibly dry, you will find that your fingers smell of iron and alkalinity, and that the vibration persists, faintly, in your bones, for days.

It fades. It always fades.

But not completely.
