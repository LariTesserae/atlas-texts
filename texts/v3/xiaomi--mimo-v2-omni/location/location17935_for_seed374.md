The ground is wet. Not with open water, but with a constant, weeping saturation that never pools, never runs. It is the color of wet slate and new bruises—purples, greys, and a deep, oxide red. The surface has a dull, matte sheen, like leather left in the rain. There are no plants. Not a moss, not a lichen, not a single blade of anything green or photosynthetic. The world is mineral and fungal, or perhaps something older than either. What life there is, is abundant, and it moves.

Low, dense structures crowd the landscape, rising from the damp earth like hardened secretions or erupted geology. They are not built from quarried stone or forged metal, but appear to have been *grown* or *precipitated* from the environment itself—low, bulbous, labyrinthine warrens of a substance like calciumite or chitin, riddled with pores and narrow passages. They are settlements, clearly, but of a primitive and organic urbanism. There are no straight lines, no right angles. The architecture is all curves, bulges, and clustered domes, pressing against each other in a dense, tactile mosaic. Tools and artifacts are simple: cups and bins of the same precipitated material, cordage woven from fibrous fungal strands, blades of sharpened flint or obsidian. The technology is early, manual, and intimately tied to the local material.

The air is warm and thick, heavy with humidity that condenses on every surface, tracing slow, silvery runnels down the curved walls. This is the source of the wetness—not rain, but the atmosphere itself perpetually sweating. The light is dim, a diffuse, coppery twilight that seems to emanate from the cloud-heavy sky itself, with no distinct sun. It filters through the constant mist, softening all edges, making distances hazy.

And the sound. The sound is immense, a foundational roar that you feel in your bones and teeth. It is not a chaotic noise, but a layered, perpetual drone. It comes from the earth itself: a deep, tectonic groan, the shriek of mineral stressed under slow, eternal pressure. It comes from the structures: a chitinous rattling and humming as they minutely expand and contract with the temperature. And it comes from the fauna. The fauna is everywhere, and it is *loud*. Creatures with exoskeletons of glossy chitin or porous ceramic click and scrape across the wet ground. Others, soft-bodied and slug-like, leave trails of luminous, phosphorescent slime as they vocalize in deep, resonant thrums. Winged things—not insects, but things with membranous sails of translucent tissue—flutter in the dim air, producing a constant, whispering rustle. There are no birdsong, no mammalian calls. This is a cacophony of clicks, scrapes, hums, and resonant pulses, a deafening bio-acoustic landscape that never, ever pauses.

Yet, nothing changes. The sound is eternal, the moisture constant, the slow growth of the structures imperceptible. There is no weather, only the perpetual state. No storms pass through, no seasons turn. The deep groans of the earth are not preludes to eruption, but the fixed tone of the world. The dense, bustling activity of the fauna follows rigid, unchanging patterns—a swarm of ceramic-shelled grazers moving across a fungal bloom, a pack of clicking hunters flowing between warrens—in loops of behavior that have repeated for millennia and will repeat for millennia more. It is a world of tremendous energy and sound, locked in a perfect, deafening stasis. A living, breathing, roaring equilibrium, caught in the amber of its own perpetual now.
