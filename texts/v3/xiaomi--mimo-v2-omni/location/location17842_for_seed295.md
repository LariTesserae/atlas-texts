# The Built Plateau

You are inside something. You realize this not from any single cue but from the cumulative sense of warmth, of enclosure, of the air being thick with spore-smell and exhalation, the way the walls around you seem to *secrete* their own faint dampness. Everything here was made, and what was made is alive, and what is alive was made by something smaller still.

---

The ground is not stone. It is compacted chitin, calcium carbonate, hardened resin, and compressed layers of organic sediment laid down over millions of years. It feels faintly warm underfoot — not hot, but the warmth of breath, of metabolic hum. It has the look of old bone that has been rained on and dried a thousand times, but it is not bone. It was built, layer by microscopic layer, by organisms so numerous and so small that their individual efforts were meaningless and their collective achievement was *this*: an entire constructed landscape stretching in every direction, as far as you could walk, as deep as you could descend.

Every surface is layered. The walls are not smooth — they are encrusted, beaded, laminated, covered in fractal textures that repeat at every scale. From a distance, the great pillars and ridges look weathered and rounded, like limestone karst sculpted by eons of rain. Up close, you can see that the weathering is biological: tiny organisms have chewed, dissolved, and reshaped the surface endlessly, and other organisms have rebuilt it behind them. This is the erosion here — not wind and water alone, but mouths and acids and the slow, patient secretion of new material over old scars. The landscape has existed in this state for longer than you can reckon. It is settled. It is in balance. It has been itself for a very long time.

---

It is dark.

Not absolute — there is no sky, and yet it is not a sealed cave. The darkness is warm and moving, thick with humid air that carries heat from somewhere below, from deep geothermal sources that this place has learned to use, to channel, to keep itself warm without a sun. In the blackness, you hear everything.

The sound is **deafening**. It is not one sound but thousands, stacked in layers that merge into a single, unending roar. The high-pitched chittering of insects — billions of them, trillions — fills the upper register like static. Beneath that, a mid-range wash of clicking, rustling, the wet scrape of things moving over surfaces, the papery flutter of winged creatures in the open spaces above. Below that, a bass drone that you feel more than hear, a vibration in the ground and the walls and the air itself. It is the sound of the structure. Of the slow grinding and settling of billions of tons of built material. Of the organisms that live *inside* the walls — which are hollow, threaded with tunnels, inhabited from within. And beneath the drone, if you press your hand to the ground and hold your breath, there is something deeper still: a pulse, a rhythmic thrum, so slow it is almost geological. The building heartbeat of a place that has been growing for longer than any single creature in it has lived.

---

The scale is vast. You stand in what might be called a valley, though it has no sky — the walls rise around you and then curve inward overhead, sometimes meeting, sometimes opening into cavernous voids that extend upward for hundreds of meters. The "open" sky here is the interior of the structure itself: immense vaulted spaces, cathedral-chambers, the negative space left between walls of construction. From a high ridge you can see across a plain that stretches for miles, but it is a plain *inside*, its surface bumpy and irregular, covered in growth, its horizon defined not by the curvature of the earth but by the sloping walls of the built world closing in at the distance.

There are towers. Great tapering pillars of layered construction that rise from the floor and merge with the ceiling, structural supports that were grown rather than placed. They are hundreds of meters across at the base and threaded with millions of internal chambers. They are warm. They hum. They have the faintest luminescence — not visible light, but something your skin perceives as warmth, as presence, the radiant heat of dense biological activity happening just beneath the surface.

---

The vegetation is lush, but there are no plants in the way you would recognize them. The fungal gardens are the green of this place: vast, cultivated mats of mycelium that spread across every surface, tended by the smaller fauna with an almost agricultural attentiveness. Some are bioluminescent, producing faint cold glows in blue and green and pale amber — not enough to see by, but enough to register, to remind you that you are surrounded. The fungal growths come in hundreds of forms: flat crusts, dangling filaments, bulbous fruiting bodies the size of trees, fine cobweb-like sheets that drift in the warm air currents. They are everywhere. They are the basis of everything here — food, material, atmosphere.

Bacterial colonies coat the wetter surfaces in thick, slippery mats of color you cannot see but could feel: rust-red, deep green, the yellow of sulfur. They process the moisture, break down waste, contribute to the slow chemical cycle that feeds the builders. Hanging gardens of fibrous strands descend from ceilings in curtains, covered in their own micro-ecosystems of mite and spore. The walls, if you press close, are faintly damp and faintly *alive* — not just colonized by life but *made of* life, or at least of life's remains, compacted and calcified and constantly being renewed.

---

The moisture is intermittent but persistent. It is not rain — there is no weather here, no sky to produce it. Instead, condensation gathers on cooler surfaces and runs in thin sheets down the walls. Water seeps in from outside, from cracks in the structure that connect to surface rivers or aquifers far above or far below, channeled through engineered conduits that the builders created long ago. It pools in shallow depressions on the floor, warm and mineral-rich, and it feeds the gardens. Occasionally, a channel will break or a reservoir will overflow, and water will cascade down a wall or flood a low passage — an event that ripples through the local ecology as organisms rush to the fresh moisture. Then it subsides. The water finds new channels. The pools refill slowly. The dripping continues.

---

The fauna is overwhelming. You cannot stand still for a moment without something moving across your boot, your arm, the surface beside you. The air is thick with flying creatures — not birds, but chitinous, multi-winged things that navigate by sound and touch, their wingbeats contributing a high-frequency whine to the ambient roar. The ground is covered in crawling things: segmented, many-legged, ranging from the size of a grain of rice to the size of a large dog. They move in streams, in columns, in purposeful traffic patterns that follow the contours of the built landscape like roads. Some carry fragments of material in their mandibles. Some tend the fungal gardens with meticulous care, pruning and replanting. Some do nothing you can discern except exist in incomprehensible numbers.

The larger creatures are rare but present. You hear them more than see them — deep resonant calls that vibrate in your chest, the heavy scrape of substantial bodies moving through larger tunnels, the occasional percussive boom that might be a limb striking a wall or a sound made deliberately for communication. They are the size of cattle, perhaps larger, and they move through the structure's main corridors like elders in a city, unbothered by the millions of smaller lives parting around them. Their role is opaque to you. They may be architects. They may simply be old.

And then there are the ones inside the walls. You cannot see them, but you can hear them — the faint scratching and chewing that comes from within every surface, the constant labor of organisms that live in the space between the structure's outer and inner skins, maintaining it from within, consuming old material and replacing it with new. The walls are *perpetually under construction*. The landscape is not static; it is in the act of being made, always, at a rate so slow that to stand and watch would be to see nothing at all. But return in a thousand years, and the ridge you stand on would be wider, the valley shallower, the tower broader. Everything here grows. Everything thickens. The structure becomes more itself, slowly, patiently, the way a coral reef becomes more reef.

---

The water, the warmth, the darkness, the sound, the life, the construction — all of it is one system. It has been running for so long that it no longer needs the world outside. The geothermal heat drives the chemistry. The chemistry feeds the fungi. The fungi feed the fauna. The fauna builds the structure. The structure channels the water. The water feeds the fungi. The cycle turns. It has been turning.

You stand in the middle of something that was built by creatures with no tools, no plans, no awareness of what they were making — and what they made is a world. A warm, dark, deafening, teeming, living world, growing in the slow dark, miles above the surface it was built upon, or perhaps miles below it. You cannot tell. It does not matter. This place has no interest in the world outside itself. It is busy. It is building. It is the sound you hear and the ground you stand on and the air that warms your lungs. It has been here for a long time, and it will be here long after you leave, continuing its slow work of becoming, adding another layer, another cell, another breath to the vast and patient architecture of everything.
