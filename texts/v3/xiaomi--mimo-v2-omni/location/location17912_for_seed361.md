The plateau is not a summit but a vast, ancient shoulder of the world, eroded over aeons into a gently undulating plain. The air here is thick and warm, holding the heat of forgotten suns, and it carries the constant, mineral scent of wet stone and the pervasive sweetness of decay. Water is not a river or a lake, but a condition. It beads on every surface—on the backs of leathery leaves, in the shimmering filaments of fungal blooms, in the slow, fat droplets that fall from condensation cliffs with a soft *tick* every few minutes. Shallow, warm pools, no larger than a room, gather in the shallow basins of the stone, their surfaces often hidden beneath mats of floating, emerald moss.

The vegetation is a riotous, slow-motion explosion. Towering, scale-barked trees with canopies like shattered umbrellas are spaced far apart, their roots delving deep into fissures. Between them, the ground is a tapestry of giant, velvety mosses, ferns with fronds like stained glass, and flowers that seem to pulse with a faint, internal light, their petals fleshy and warm to the touch. This is not a dense jungle, but a spacious, curated garden of immense, individual specimens, each given room to express its bizarre and magnificent form in the humid air.

The true overwhelming presence, however, is the fauna. In the silence between sounds, you realize the space is not empty but *occupied*. Drifting through the air are things like translucent, balloon-like jellyfish, trailing luminous filaments that sting and feed on airborne microbes. On the ground, beetle-like creatures the size of small dogs move with a deliberate, clicking slowness, their carapaces inlaid with patterns that look like circuit boards. In the canopy, something with too many limbs and a face like a carved mask watches with patient, amber eyes. The noise is a dense, layered symphony: the deep, resonant thrum of insect wings, the crystalline chime of the balloon-creatures' filaments, the distant, melodic roar of a predator you never see, and the constant, underlying whisper of water condensing and dripping.

Embedded in this ancient landscape are the scattered remains of a technology that feels both impossibly old and unimaginably advanced. A single, perfect arch of a dark, non-reflective material stands on the plain, its surface cool and impossibly smooth, humming at a frequency you feel in your teeth. Nearby, a low wall of fitted, hexagonal stones—no mortar, just perfect geometry—emerges from the moss, its surface etched with lines that rearrange themselves if you look away and then back. You find a small, fist-sized node of milky crystal half-buried in the loam; when you brush the dirt from it, it projects a three-dimensional, silent map of constellations unknown to any human sky before dissolving into mist.

The strangeness here is not loud, but woven into the fabric of the place. The light from the normal sun seems to split into constituent colors as it passes through the humid air, casting faint, prismatic shadows. The large, fungal growths on the northern sides of the trees occasionally emit a soft, warm glow of their own. And there is a palpable sense of intelligence to the ecosystem—not human, but a vast, biological computation. The creatures, the plants, the water cycle, and the ruins are all interconnected in a system that has been running, self-repairing, and evolving for longer than civilization has existed on the lowlands. It is active, this place: a slow, loud, warm, and profoundly alive machine operating at the edge of comprehension, its original purpose lost, its current function simply to *be*, magnificently and eternally.
