The place is a basin. Not a valley—there are no slopes to speak of, just a vast, shallow depression where the horizon lies so flat and close it seems to press down. The ground is a mosaic of dark, vitreous stone, worn smooth and shallowly pocked by ages of a rain that is no longer there. It is warm to the touch, holding the memory of a sun that now hangs in a hazy, white-gold sky. The stone is not one material but a consortium: basalt, obsidian, and some other, glassier substance that veins the rock in pale, milky threads.

Water is a persistent rumor here. It does not flow in rivers or gather in lakes. Instead, it emerges from the rock itself—a slow, capillary weep from countless microscopic fissures. In the mornings, the entire plain is coated in a trembling, momentary dew that beads on the non-absorbent stone. By noon, it is gone, drawn back into the air or wicked away to subsurface channels, only to reappear as condensation in the deeper, shadowed pits. You can hear it: not a trickle, but a faint, collective hiss, a million tiny voices of evaporation and condensation happening simultaneously.

There is no soil. No grass, no lichen, not even the hardiest moss. Life, in the green and growing sense, never found purchase here. The world is mineral, and it sings with a different biology.

The sound is the landscape’s defining feature. It is not wind or animal call. It is a deep, resonant hum, a subsonic vibration that you feel in your bones before you hear it with your ears. It emanates from the great, gear-like formations scattered sparsely across the plain—massive, interlocking rings of stone, some dozens of feet across, that sit half-buried in the basalt. They are not constructed; they have grown, crystallized over millennia into shapes that uncannily resemble machinery. Their surfaces are etched with fractal patterns, and where they mesh, there is a constant, grinding friction—not of stone on stone, but of energy on matter. The hum is their song, the frequency of their perpetual, glacial turning. They are powered by geothermal gradients and the planet’s own magnetic pulse, a slow-motion industrial process that has been running since before the concept of industry existed.

Rarely, you see the fauna. They are not animals as we know them. They are crystalline entities, slow-moving and translucent, that feed directly on the piezoelectric energy generated by the stress in the rocks and the hum of the great gears. A six-legged thing of quartz and shadow, clicking softly over the stone, its interior refracting the white light into prismatic flares. A drifting, jellyfish-like form of silicate membranes, buoyed on thermal updrafts, its tendrils trailing and absorbing static charge. They are sparse, solitary, and utterly silent.

The atmosphere is thick with moisture and heat, but it is clean, lacking the scent of decay or pollen. It smells of wet stone and ozone, a sharp, electric tang from the air itself, ionized by the constant energetic processes.

Nothing here changes. The gears turn at a rate imperceptible to the eye. The dew forms and vanishes. The crystal creatures follow their immutable, metabolic paths. Erosion finished its work eons ago, leaving this balanced, settled state. The light does not shift; the haze is constant. It is a world of profound stability, yet it is not quiet. It is loud with the sound of its own eternal, unwavering function—a low, planetary groan of energy being transformed, of stone living a life of slow, grinding, mineral purpose. It is a factory with no builders, a machine with no makers, running on forever in warm, damp, luminous silence.
