# The Upper Tanneries

You arrive at the city by climbing. The water does not care that you are tired.

It falls around you in heavy, warm-threaded curtains — not rain exactly, but a constant downward rivering from the sky-mass above, so dense and so vertical it feels architectural, like passing through a thousand hanging veils. The water is cool. It has been cool for longer than anyone remembers. It finds your clothing, your skin, the creases of your hands, and it does not stop.

The city is built into and of a single material: **mangrove-wood**, cultivated over generations into shapes that serve. Not carved — *grown*. The people here do not cut. They bend. They bind. They whisper to the tips of young shoots in the dark hours and come back in a season to find a wall where there was none, a floor where there was only the suggestion of one. The wood is pale, almost white where it dries above the waterline, and dark — nearly black — where it stays wet. Nearly everything stays wet.

---

## The Structure of Things

The city sits high. This matters. This is why the water falls instead of rises.

It occupies a ridge, or perhaps a plateau — it is difficult to say where the landform ends and the growth begins. The roots of the great trees descend through what was once stone and is now a fibrous tangle, hundreds of meters down, into a darkness that swallows light. Above, the canopy — or rather the *citytop* — opens into a wide, sparse arrangement of platforms and living chambers, each connected by root-bridges and vine-ladders. There is space between things. You can stand on a central platform and see, in most directions, the pale lattice of the next structure thirty or forty meters away, with nothing but falling water and the grey sky between.

This is not a crowded city. It is a scattered one. The buildings breathe.

Each structure is a room, or a cluster of rooms, grown inside the burl and bole of an ancient mangrove crown. The walls are smooth, curved, alive — you can feel them pulse faintly if you press your ear to the wood in the quiet hours (there are no quiet hours, but you understand). Ceilings dome upward into darkness where the wood still reaches. Floors are woven root-mat, springy and warm despite the cool air, because the living wood generates its own modest heat. Doors are not doors. They are openings where the wood was taught not to grow, and the edges remain soft, pinkish, slightly moist, like the inside of a mouth.

There are perhaps forty of these structures across the citytop. Some are large enough to hold a family. Some are single chambers where one person tends a particular cultivation or keeps tools. The tools are bone and woven fiber and occasionally copper, soft enough to bend, used more for guiding growth than for cutting. A knife here is a flat stone used to score a line in young bark, after which the bark thickens along the score and separates on its own, forming a natural shelf or lip.

---

## The Sound

It is loud. It has always been loud.

The water is the first sound — the million-threaded downward rush, hitting root-mat and branch and pool, splashing into the gaps between things, falling through the canopy into the abyss below. It is a roar, but layered: you hear the high hiss of fine spray and, beneath it, the deep percussive thud of heavier streams hitting standing water far below the city. It is the sound of a place that is not quite a waterfall and not quite a rainstorm but something in between, permanent and total.

The fauna adds the second stratum. Birds — not many, but loud, species with enormous syrinxes evolved to be heard over the water — send sharp, ringing calls from the outermost branches. Tree-frogs in the tens of thousands create a low, throbbing hum in the walls themselves, because the walls are the trees, and the frogs are inside the walls. Insects — mosquitos, midges, things with no common name — add a high, whining frequency that you stop hearing after a day and start hearing again after a week, always slightly worse than you remembered.

And beneath it all, the growth. If you are quiet, and you press yourself against the wood and hold your breath, you can hear the trees. Not creaking. Not groaning. A slow, fibrous *stretching*, like a tendon lengthening over the course of a year. The sound of cell walls dividing. It is the quietest and most unsettling sound in the city, because it reminds you that the floor is alive, the walls are alive, the ceiling is alive, and they are all *going somewhere*, very slowly, with a patience that does not include you.

## The People and Their Work

They are few. Perhaps two hundred. They speak a language with more vowels than consonants, which carries well over water.

Their days are spent in cultivation, in weaving, in the slow craft of coaxing growth. A builder will select a young shoot, tie it to a frame of bone pegs, and return each morning to adjust the ties, whisper the shaping words, score the bark where a window should open. A single wall takes three years. A room takes ten. A full structure, with chambers and dome and root-anchors driven deep enough to withstand the storms that come once in a generation — a lifetime. Several lifetimes, if you want it done well.

They eat the trees. Not the heartwood, but the cambium — the soft, slick inner bark, which they scrape away in thin strips and cook into a pale paste that tastes of rain and something faintly sweet. They eat the frogs, which are abundant and stupid and easy to catch. They eat the birds when they can, which is not often, because the birds are clever and loud and tend to announce the approach of a hunter from a great distance. Fish exist in the pools below the city, in the deep water that collects on the forest floor far beneath the roots, but descending is dangerous and done only in certain seasons, by certain people, with rope-vines long enough to climb back up.

---

## The Ecology

Everything here is entangled. The birds nest in the trees, which are the buildings, which are the city, and their droppings feed the insects, which feed the frogs, which live in the walls, which are the buildings, which are the trees. The water falls, feeds the roots, descends through the root-mass into the deep, rises again as mist, condenses in the canopy, and falls once more. It is a closed system, almost — a city that is also an organism, or a forest that is also a city, depending on where you stand and whether you consider the people part of the ecology or a temporary accident of it.

The trees are old. The oldest structures in the city are several hundred years old, and their roots go deeper than anyone has explored. There are chambers in the lower levels — below the habitable city, in the dark tangle where the root-mass thickens into something almost geological — that no one has entered in living memory. The wood down there is hard as stone, black, and warm. Sometimes, in the deepest quiet, the whole city hums, and people say it is the old roots, still growing, still reaching downward, still finding new stone to split and new darkness to fill.

The balance is the thing. The erosion — the slow dissolution of whatever stone once existed here — has been completed, replaced entirely by the fibrous architecture of the mangroves. The city is in its own state. It is not growing quickly. It is not dying. It is maintaining, adjusting, responding to the water and the weather and the weight of its own accumulated life with a slowness that looks, from the outside, like stillness.

It is not still.

---

## What Is Strange

You will notice, after a few days, that the city shifts. Not visibly — not in any way you could point to and say *there, that moved*. But a doorway you used yesterday is slightly lower today. A root-bridge you crossed this morning has an extra handhold woven into its rail. The room you sleep in is subtly larger than it was when you arrived, the walls having retreated a centimeter in the night, as if making room for you, or perhaps simply *growing away from you*, the way a thing does when it is alive and you are not part of its plan.

The people do not discuss this. It is not strange to them. The city is alive. It grows. It responds. It is not a place they inhabit — it is a place that tolerates them, that has been taught to tolerate them, and that continues to tolerate them because they continue to tend it, to whisper to its young shoots, to feed its roots, to live inside its body and add their warmth to its slow, fibrous pulse.

The water falls. The frogs hum. The wood stretches.

You are inside something that is going somewhere, very slowly, and it will be a very long time before it arrives.
