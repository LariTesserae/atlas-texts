This is the Hollowworks.

You are standing in a space that feels both immense and intimate. The scale is wrong, or perhaps inverted. Above, the ceiling—or what serves as one—vaults away into engineered darkness, a complex lattice of girders and conduits that might be a hundred meters high or a thousand. The distance is deceptive. The true boundary is not up, but *out*. The chamber, or canyon, or gallery, you occupy is only about fifty meters across. It is a profound, vertical slot, a cut into a world that is entirely made.

Everything here is constructed. The ground is not soil but a deep, layered aggregate of vitrified slag, ceramic dust, and fused mineral wool, compacted by centuries of slow settlement into a stone harder than granite. It is warm to the touch, a residual heat bleeding from the colossal geothermal engines that labor in the planet’s marrow, far below. This warmth, coupled with a constant, managed humidity—a dampness that beads on every surface, a condensation born of temperature differentials between the deep machinery and the filtered upper atmospheres—feeds the astonishing, desperate lushness.

Vegetation is not an afterthought here; it is the primary architecture. The Hollowworks were built for it, or it has adapted to the build with a fervent, monstrous grace. Great, ropey columns of a self-generated composite—part root, part polymer secretion—descend from the ceiling lattice, anchoring themselves deep in the aggregate floor. From these columns, and from the walls themselves, spills a riot of broad-leafed fronds, velvety mosses in electric greens and deep purples, and carnivorous, trumpet-shaped blooms that sigh open and shut with a slow, hydraulic patience. The plants are engineered, or have become so. Their roots tap the warm moisture and the mineral runoff from the machinery; their leaves comb particulate matter from the air. They are both crop and ecosystem, a cultivated wilderness thriving in the girders of a dead or sleeping industry.

The sound is the first thing you notice, and the last. It is a physical presence, a deafening, multi-layered roar that never ceases. It is not the sound of wind or water, but of the Works themselves. Deep, subsonic throbs from the geothermal taps vibrate in your bones. The hiss and drip of condensation cycling through vast, internal piping systems runs like a metallic river. Closer, the *thrum* of immense flywheels and the distant, grinding song of tectonic stabilizers create a constant, harmonic pressure. The acoustics are designed—or have evolved—to channel and amplify this noise. The walls are lined with a porous, resonant stone that turns the entire slot into a gigantic, roaring flute. Yet, within this cacophony, there is a strange order. The rhythms are slow, predictable, geological. The sound does not change; it simply *is*, the auditory equivalent of gravity.

Fauna exists in the interstices. You see no mammals, no birds. Life here is specialized. Clouds of gossamer-winged insects, engineered as pollinators and decomposers, swarm in the warm, moist air, their collective buzzing a high, silvery counterpoint to the industrial bass. In the deeper pools of condensation that gather in depressions on the floor, translucent, eel-like creatures—blinds, they are called—navigate by electrical sense, feeding on algae and fallen biomass. The most striking are the Casing-borers, beetle-like creatures with diamond-hard mandibles that spend their centuries-long lives slowly sculpting the aggregate walls, following veins of specific minerals, leaving behind intricate, fractal galleries that become homes for the insects and mosses. They are the patient architects of this slow, biological decay.

This is a place of established balance, an equilibrium between the immense, slow-motion processes of the constructed world and the tenacious, adaptive life it nurtures. Erosion here is not of wind and rain, but of chemical seepage, biological secretion, and the imperceptible grinding of the Works’ own movement. It is a landscape in a state of perpetual, gentle becoming, where the distinction between machine and organism has been erased by time and purpose. The strangeness is not in glaring anomalies, but in the seamless fusion of the industrial and the organic. Water flows through channels that are also root systems. The light, a dim, amber glow filtered through the ceiling lattice and the leafy canopy, is powered by geothermal-generated bioluminescence in the walls themselves. The world is hollow, vast in its emptiness between the structured walls, and deafeningly, magnificently alive.
