The place is a high, cold terracescape, carved into the slopes of a young mountain range. The peaks above are jagged, freshly torn from the earth's crust, their flanks too steep and new for soil to cling. Below them, where the gradient eases for a breath, the first terraces appear. They are not gentle, rolling farmlands, but sharp, geometric steps of dark stone and packed earth, hewn directly from the mountainside with primitive, patient tools.

The air is damp and cool, a perpetual state of condensation. Mist doesn't roll in; it simply coalesces from the air, beading on every surface—on the lichen-crusted stone walls, on the broad, waxy leaves of the terrace-gardens, on the coarse, woven fibers of the ropes and nets strung between structures. There is no true rain, only this constant, weeping dew that collects in hand-chiseled stone channels and trickles into communal cisterns. The light is normal, but diffused, a flat, silver-grey wash that eliminates shadows and makes distances hard to judge.

The urban settlement clings to the terraces in a dense, vertical sprawl. Houses are built of the same dark mountain stone, their walls thick and sloping, roofs made of layered slate or tightly-woven thatch from the hardy, reed-like grasses that grow in the damper hollows. There are no straight streets, only stepped alleys and covered walkways that bridge the gaps between levels. Infrastructure is simple but profound: every drop of moisture is managed. Wooden aqueducts, mossy and dripping, guide water from seepage points higher up. Stone gutters spiral down the central plazas. The entire city is a hydraulic machine, built to harvest the ambient damp.

The vegetation is lush but constrained. On the terraces themselves, broad-leafed vegetables, thick-stalked fungi, and mats of a moss-like grain grow in careful, cultivated abundance. In the uncultivated spaces between structures, life is stranger: great clusters of a bell-shaped, translucent flower that hums faintly when the dew is heaviest; vines with leaves like plates of green glass that climb the walls; groves of stunted, wind-twisted trees whose bark peels in aromatic, papery curls, their roots gripping the stone with visible tendons.

Despite the quiet, the fauna is abundant. Small, six-legged creatures with iridescent shells scuttle in the mossy cracks. In the reed-beds that cluster around the cisterns, amphibians with skin like polished jade call in short, liquid bursts. Above, the sky is ruled by the high-soaring *kethra*, broad-winged scavengers with feathers like slate, who ride the updrafts in silent circles. The only truly loud creatures are the *stone-borers*, blind, mole-like mammals that gnaw on mineral deposits deep within the mountain, their gnawing a constant, sub-audible vibration felt more in the bones than heard.

And yet, for all this life, nothing changes. The dynamic is zero, a perfect, frozen equilibrium. The water cycle is closed, the population of *kethra* never increases or decreases, the gnawing of the stone-borers neither deepens nor shallows their tunnels. The terraces are not being expanded; the stone channels are not being repaired, though they never erode. The people—or whatever they are—move with a slow, deliberate grace, their actions repetitive and purposeful, yet leaving no cumulative mark. A weaver works at a loom, the cloth growing by a thread each day, but never reaching completion. A carver shapes the same block of soft stone, its form subtly shifting in an endless cycle between mask and tool and mask again.

The world feels ancient and young at once. The features are sharp, the mountain raw, but the scene is immutable. The silence is a tapestry woven from the drip of condensation, the distant gnawing of the stone-borers, the rustle of waxy leaves, and the profound, patient breath of the mist moving through the terraces. It is a place where time has been captured in amber, a single, damp, cool, and fertile moment, held forever in the high, quiet air.
