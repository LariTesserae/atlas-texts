The land is high, a plateau raised toward the sky, and the air holds the damp, warm weight of a sponge left in the sun. It does not rain here, not precisely. Instead, the atmosphere itself seems to weep. Moisture condenses on every surface—on the sharp, unweathered faces of dark basalt columns that jut from the earth like the broken teeth of some buried giant, on the pale veins of marble that lace the ground, on the broad, waxy leaves of the vegetation that clings to life in the fissures. Water is not an event here; it is a constant, gentle presence. It beads, it drips, it collects in shallow, silver sheets that mirror the overcast sky before seeping back into the porous stone.

The ground is young. The angles are severe, the fractures clean. There are no soft, rolling hills, only stark terraces and sudden drops where the earth has recently cracked open and heaved itself upward. Erosion has barely begun its patient work; the edges are still sharp, the scars of the planet’s convulsions still fresh. Between the dark stone, the soil is thin and gravelly, a pale grit that crunches faintly underfoot.

Life here is sparse, but tenacious. Low, sprawling thickets of a moss-like shrub, its leaves a deep and glossy green, root into every crack and seam where a hint of soil gathers. They form isolated islands of vegetation in a sea of stone, their growth slow and deliberate. There are no trees, only these patient, low-lying clusters that drink perpetually from the condensing air. The green is vivid against the dark rock, but it does not dominate. It is a punctuation, not a narrative.

And within this stark, wet stone garden, there is a town. It is not a city of grandeur, but one of necessity and primitive logic. The structures are built from the same dark basalt, quarried from the nearby fractures and stacked with a simple, mortar-less precision. The buildings are low, single-storied, with roofs sloped to guide the ceaseless condensation into carved stone channels that lead to central cisterns. There are no towers, no monuments—only clusters of square dwellings, open-sided work shelters, and enclosures for storing whatever sparse resources can be gathered. The layout is organic, following the contours of the stone terraces, connected by paths of packed grit and laid flagstone. It feels less built upon the land and more *extracted* from it, a logical extension of the geology.

And here is the profound silence. No bird calls from the shrubs. No insect hums in the humid air. No dog barks in the alleys, no livestock lowing in pens. The only sounds are the soft, eternal drip of water from leaf to stone, the whisper of it trickling through the channels, and, if one is very still, the faint, sub-audible groan of the land itself, settling almost imperceptibly. The people who built this town—and they are here, their presence inferred in the neatly stacked tools, the smoothed edges of doorways, the lingering warmth in sheltered corners—are the sole source of complex movement. They move with a quiet economy, their faces turned upward often, as if listening for a sound that never comes.

This is a world in equilibrium. The water cycle is a closed, intimate loop between stone and air. The vegetation grows at a rate measured in decades. The town stands as it has always stood, its people engaged in the endless, manual craft of surviving in a place that gives water but withholds almost everything else. Time here does not flow; it pools. The light is normal, a flat, diffuse silver that casts no sharp shadows, rendering every detail with equal clarity and making the whole scene feel like a perfectly preserved moment, a diorama under glass. There is no decay, because there is nothing to truly decay. There is only the slow, silent process of endurance, and the patient waiting of the stone.
