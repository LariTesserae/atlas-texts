You are standing—though the word is strange here—in a world that is, primarily, *water*. But this is not the ocean’s floor, nor a sunlit sea. The water is the atmosphere. It is the air and the sky and the ground. You are submerged in a medium that is not quite liquid, not quite vapor, but something in between—a dense, cool suspension that presses against you with a gentle, insistent weight. It carries light, a diffuse, silver-grey luminescence that seems to emanate from everywhere and nowhere, like the memory of a noon sun trapped in pearl. This light has no source. It simply *is*.

Above, below, in all directions, there is no horizon. There is only depth, and within that depth, the terrain. You stand upon—or rather, your presence is stabilized near—a spire of something that is not quite rock. It is a dark, vitreous material, like obsidian that has been melted and then frozen in a state of hurried cooling. Its edges are not sharp, but rounded, as if being perpetually smoothed by the current that never ceases. This current is the world’s breath and its heartbeat. It is a constant, multi-directional flow that tugs at you, warm in one moment (a brief, startling anomaly), chilling the next. It is not a tide; it is a turbulent, internal weather.

The spire you are near is one of many. They rise from a plain of fine, metallic silt that lies in shifting dunes and valleys. These spires are sparse, lonely monoliths. They are not tall—the scale here is intimate, pocket-sized, as if you are looking at a model of a mountain range held in a grotto of water. Some are mere nubs, embryonic, just pushing through the silt. Others are taller, branched with delicate, fractal-like filaments that vibrate in the flow, emitting a faint, phosphorescent glow. This is the “vegetation”—not plant, but mineral growth, a kind of crystalline, abiogenic life. It feeds on the minerals in the silt and the energy in the turbulent current. Where it grows, the silt is swept clean, revealing patches of the dark, glassy bedrock beneath.

And the sound. It is deafening, but not with noise as you know it. It is a pressure on the skull, a vibration in the bones. It is the symphony of the current itself: a deep, groaning bass of shifting silt-beds, overlaid with the high, chittering pings of the mineral filaments striking each other, and woven through with the constant, rushing roar of the water-weather. It is a sound so pervasive it becomes a kind of silence, a blanket of sensation that drowns out all internal thought. You do not hear it with your ears; you are *immersed* in it.

Life is rare, but present. A creature like a translucent ribbon, meters long but no wider than your hand, undulates through the mid-water. It has no eyes, only shimmering electroreceptor patches along its flanks. It is an automaton of the current, feeding on the drifting motes of mineral and the occasional, hapless spore of the crystalline growth. In the silt, things shift. Not fauna, but perhaps their memory: you find fragments, smooth and worked. A shard of something that was once a tool, its edge too symmetrical to be natural, made of a copper-alloy that has patinated to a ghostly green. A section of what might be a wall, or a vessel, fused into the base of a spire, its purpose lost. These are the scattered remnants of a *built* presence, a people who once worked with this primitive metal and this same glassy stone. They are gone. Only these faint marks remain, being slowly consumed by the embryonic geology.

The strangeness here is in the physics. The water does not feel wet. It has a texture, like oil, but leaves no moisture on your skin. Sometimes, the light coalesces into brief, phantom shapes—a door, a hand, a spiraling tower—that exist for a second before dissolving back into the silver haze. The turbulent current occasionally synchronizes, and for a moment, all the mineral filaments on all the spires will chime in perfect, harmonic unison—a single, clear bell-tone that cuts through the roar—before descending back into chaos. It is a world in a state of hyperactive genesis, a storm of creation that never settles. The silt-beds heave and collapse in minutes. New glass spires can be seen, from a distance, nucleating and growing over the course of an hour, only to be sheared off by a violent eddy.

You are in a grotto. A vast, submerged, pressurized grotto that is both a cradle and a tomb. It is a single, self-contained feature, a micro-environment hidden within some larger, unfathomable whole. It is almost empty—vast spaces of water and silt lie between the lonely spires. Yet that emptiness is active, violent, and creative. You are witnessing the raw, beating heart of a world still deciding what it wants to be, under a sky of water, in cool, deafening, turbulent light. And then, for one second, the roar cuts out. Absolute, profound silence. In that silence, you hear a faint, rhythmic *tap-tap-tap* from within the silt nearby. The work of a remnant tool? Or the first heartbeat of the next thing to be born here? The roar returns, and the answer is washed away.
