# The Resonance Vats

You descend through a shaft cut into calcite andite andite and something else — something with a faint iridescence, like oil on wet stone, except the stone is bone-dry and has been for longer than anyone can account for. The stairs are original. They are not human. They are too regular for that, too perfectly scaled to a leg slightly shorter than yours, and each tread is scooped concave from what might be use or might be the way they were grown. The air warms as you go down. Not unpleasantly. It settles on your skin like the breath of a machine that has been running all day.

You arrive.

The chamber is perhaps eighty meters across and no more than twelve high. It feels compressed — a ceiling that presses even as it holds. The walls are close and they are *dense*. Not smooth. Not empty. Every surface is covered.

---

**The growth.** It is not vegetation in any traditional sense, but it is alive, or was, or is something adjacent to alive. A crystalline latticework — coral, if coral had been designed by engineers with no interest in oceans — climbs the walls in fractal buttresses and shelves. It is the color of rusted bronze and pale bone, and in the dim light it is difficult to tell where the mineral ends and the biological begins. The structures are layered: older growth encrusted by newer growth, each generation slightly different in geometry, as though the rules had shifted. Some branches are thin as wire and hum faintly when you pass. Others are thick, columnar, load-bearing — they hold the ceiling the way flying buttresses do, but they pulse, very slowly, with a rhythm that is not quite mechanical and not quite cardiac. Sparse — it does not carpet every surface, but where it exists it exists in thick, tangled profusion, knotted over itself. In the gaps you see the walls beneath: scored with tool marks, or claw marks, or both, in overlapping layers going back.

There is no moisture anywhere. Not a trace. The air is dry in a way that goes beyond low humidity — it is actively, chemically *thirsty*. Your lips crack within minutes. The crystalline growth thrives in this. It does not need water. It needs something else. The warmth. The sound.

**The fauna.** They are everywhere. Small — most of them no larger than a hand — but there are thousands. They move through the latticework like weevils through grain. You hear them before you see them: a dry chitinous rustling, a ticking, a sound like someone slowly crumpling cellophane. They are pale and segmented, these creatures, with too many legs that move in wave patterns, and they navigate the crystal growth with an ease that suggests co-evolution or ownership. Some are larger. The size of a cat, perhaps, though shaped nothing like one — elongated, flattened, with mandibles that open and close in a slow, rhythmic chew on nothing. They do not flee from you. They do not seem to register you. Their eyes, where they have eyes, are faceted and dull and aimed inward.

The sound they make — all of them, collectively — is considerable. It fills the chamber like the hum of a ventilation system, a constant layered noise: clicking, scraping, the high thin whistle of air moving through narrow thoraxes. And beneath it, or woven through it, a resonance. The crystal structures vibrate. The walls vibrate. The sound is being *used*. You can feel it in your sternum. It is not unpleasant, but it is insistent, and it does not change. It has not changed. It will not change.

**The machinery.** Scattered through the chamber, half-swallowed by the growth, are the remnants — or the organs — of industry. You cannot tell if they were built or grown, these structures. They are made of metal, or what has become metal: iron oxide, copper verdigris, and alloys you cannot name, all of it coated in a fine mineral dust that never settles because there is no air current strong enough to move it. There are things that look like engines — flywheels frozen mid-rotation, pistons seized in cylinders, belts gone rigid as leather turned toite. They are small. Nothing here is built for human scale. The largest complete mechanism is the size of a chest freezer, and it is embedded in the wall with crystal growth flowing over and through it, incorporating it, or perhaps it was always part of the wall and only looks engineered.

There are marks on the floor. Not writing, exactly. Patterns. Concentric circles, spirals, grids — scratched into the stone with something hard and deliberate. They are partially obscured by the chitin-dust and the shed carapaces of the fauna and the mineral detritus that coats everything in a fine, warm, bone-dry powder. Some of the patterns have clearly been there for a very long time. Others look fresh, though there is no one here to have made them.

**The light.** There is no source. The chamber is dim — just enough to see by, never enough to see clearly. The latticework itself produces a faint luminescence, a bioluminescence or a phosphorescence, cold and steady, the color of bruised plums and tarnished silver. It is not enough to cast shadows. Everything exists in a soft, depthless half-visibility. Your eyes never fully adjust because there is nothing to adjust *to*. The dimness is constant, unchanging, like the sound.

**The equilibrium.** This is the thing that unsettles most. Nothing here is in process. Nothing is growing or dying or decaying or building. The creatures chew on nothing and swallow nothing and do not change in number. The crystal structures have reached some final configuration and hold it. The machines — if that is what they are — are frozen in a state that is neither broken nor functional but simply *completed*. The sound does not modulate. The light does not flicker. The temperature, warm and constant, has no gradient, no direction, no source. The air does not move. The dust does not settle because it is already settled. Everything is at rest in the way that a bell is at rest while it is ringing.

You realize, standing in the center of the chamber with the dry clicking of ten thousand small mouths surrounding you and the resonance pressing against your ribs like a second heartbeat, that this place is not waiting. It is not a ruin. It is not abandoned. It is *finished*. Whatever process produced it — engineering or evolution or some third thing that has no word — reached its conclusion, and the conclusion is this: a closed system, dense and loud and dry and dim, humming with a frequency that has not changed in perhaps millennia and will not change for millennia more. The fauna maintain it. The crystal maintains it. The machines, if they ever were machines, have been *absorbed* by what maintains it. It is a room the size of a cathedral that functions as a single organism or a single instrument, and it is playing one note, forever, in perfect stability.

You can leave. The stairs are still there. The shaft still goes up. But the sound follows you for days, and in the silence of your sleep you feel it still — a warmth behind your sternum, a pressure, a vibration just below the threshold of hearing, as though the place has recalibrated you to its frequency and you will now always, at some level, be listening for the moment it stops.

It will not stop.
