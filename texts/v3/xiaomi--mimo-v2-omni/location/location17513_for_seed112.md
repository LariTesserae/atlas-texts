The world is a lung that never empties.

You stand on a ridge of compressed cellulose, spongy and faintly warm underfoot, that stretches into the hazy distance in every direction. There is no water here. Not a drop. The air is dry, crisp, and cool, tasting of ozone and crushed green matter. It moves. Oh, how it moves. A constant, churning wind, born from the planet’s own vast metabolism, screams through the landscape. It is a sound that is less heard than felt—a tectonic grinding of leaf against leaf, a deep hydraulic rush of sap and gas through colossal trunks, and a high, keening whistle where fronds thin to filaments and whip in the gale. It is deafening, a white-noise roar that swallows all silence.

The vegetation *is* the landscape. There is no soil, only a kilometers-deep, interlocking mat of roots, rhizomes, and fungal threads, a living substrate that has been growing and self-composting for eons. The "ground" pulses faintly with warmth from geothermal gradients and its own frantic respiration. Towering above you are the primary structures: not trees, but **Arboreal Spires**. They are like colossal, fluted cacti crossed with pipe organs, their surfaces a lattice of ridges and pores that inhale the carbon-rich, water-vapor-thin atmosphere. Their color is a muted, dusty jade, veined with channels of slower, darker sap. They don’t bear leaves in any conventional sense; instead, their entire surface is photosynthetic, a vast, living solar panel.

At their bases, and climbing their flanks in slow, centuries-long spirals, are the **Lithophylls**. These are plant-animals, a symbiotic hybrid. Their lower halves are anchor-like roots that secrete a mild acid, dissolving the cellulose substrate for nutrients. Their upper halves are fronds of a startling, iridescent blue, which flutter and click constantly in the wind. They are the fauna—abundant, but sparse in their distribution. You might see a cluster of twenty on a spire that is itself a kilometer wide, each one a lonely splash of metallic color in the green-grey vastness. Their clicking is a secondary sound layer beneath the wind’s roar, a frantic, insectile communication.

The scale is continental. The Arboreal Spires form mountain ranges and canyon systems. What you might mistake for distant, snow-capped peaks are the sun-bleached, dead tips of the oldest spires, their photosynthetic skins sloughed off to reveal a bone-white, lignite-like interior. The canyons are not carved by water, but by **shear winds**—jet streams of air channeled by the arrangement of the spires, scouring the sides of the living rock. The erosion is established, ancient. The landscape is in a dynamic equilibrium: growth on the windward sides, constant abrasion on the leeward, and a slow, tectonic subsidence as the root-mat below grows too heavy and sinks, dragging whole regions down into the planet’s warm, dark interior to be recycled.

This is an industrial world, but the industry is biological. The roar you hear is the sound of a planetary-scale bio-reactor. The "engines" are the vast, internal pumping systems of the spires, moving gallons of viscous sap per second. The "factories" are the chlorophyll factories in every cell, working under the bright, diffuse light of a sun filtered through a permanent, fine pollen-haze. The "electrification" is the piezoelectric charge generated by the flexing of fibrous structures in the wind, a low-level bio-electric field that makes the hair on your arms stand up and occasionally causes the lithophylls to glow with a faint, ghostly St. Elmo's fire.

The turbulence is constant. New growth erupts in explosive, sped-up time-lapses of days—pale, tender shoots shoving their way out of the substrate, only to be hardened and weathered by the wind within a week. Old sections of spire, weakened by internal fungal rot or a shift in the wind-pattern, collapse in slow-motion avalanches of fibrous debris, creating new clearings in the dense, vertical jungle. These clearings are not empty; they are filled with a riot of pioneer species: fast-growing, vine-like things that mat the ground in a furious race to claim the new sunlight before the spires’ shadows reclaim it.

There is no sky, only a bright, luminous ceiling of haze, glowing with a uniform, pearl-grey light. There is no horizon, only the gradual fading of colossal green forms into the misty distance. The air smells of growth and decay, of chlorophyll and compost, of lightning-struck ozone from the constant bio-electric discharge.

You are in a place that has never known water, yet is more alive than any forest. It is a single, screaming, growing organism, a world-sized engine of cellulose and light, building and destroying itself in a deafening, eternal loop. The ground beneath you shudders with a deep, internal tremor—a spire somewhere beyond the haze is drinking a new vein of nutrient, or perhaps one is finally succumbing to gravity, its fall a muted thunder that you feel in your bones. You are a speck on the skin of a god that is both the mountain and the mason, and it does not know you are here.
