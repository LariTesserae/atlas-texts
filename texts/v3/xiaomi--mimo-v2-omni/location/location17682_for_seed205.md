This world is a lung.

You understand this not from any map or teaching, but from the first breath you take upon arrival, and the next, and every one after. The air is thick, warm, and wet—not with the wetness of rain or a lake, but with the exhalation of a trillion living things. It is the interior of a vast, green organism, and you are standing on the spongy, living floor of its throat.

The heat is a blanket, constant and close, generated not by a distant sun but by the metabolic fury of the biomass itself. The light is diffuse, a green-gold gloom filtered through hundreds of meters of layered canopy, leaf-lattice, and drifting seed-pods. It is never fully day, never fully night; the cycle is one of condensation and evaporation, a slow, rhythmic sigh.

**The Plants:** There is no soil here. There is only root, stem, and frond, woven together into a continent-spanning architecture. The ground is a resilient, fibrous mat of interlaced roots and fallen, half-decayed matter that never fully decays, held in a state of perpetual, damp compost. From this mat rise the Pillars: not trees in any familiar sense, but vertical ecosystems. They are trunks of woven vine and hardened sap, hundreds of feet in girth, their surfaces a riot of epiphytes, phosphorescent fungi, and pitcher-shaped hollows that collect condensation. Their crowns are not leaves but vast, flat pads of photosynthetic tissue, interlocking like scales to form the primary canopy, miles high.

Between the Pillars, the air itself is a medium for growth. "Sky-vines" bridge the distances, thick as your waist, their surfaces velvety with moss. Great, dangling curtains of aerial roots descend, dripping with the intermittent moisture that is the world's only rain—a constant, soft patter and drip from canopy to sub-canopy to forest floor, a sound that never stops. This is the water: not in rivers or seas, but in the air, on every surface, in the swollen, translucent bladders of specific plants that store it. You drink by piercing the skin of a "dew-bulb" and sipping the cool, slightly sweet water within.

**The Fauna:** The overwhelming life is not confined to the plants. The deafening sound is their chorus: the *chitinous scrape* of creatures the size of dogs, with mandibles of obsidian-like crystal, harvesting fungal growths from the Pillar bark; the *deep, resonant thrum* of flying leathery wings, the size of sail-cloths, as they navigate the canyons between the Pillars; the *shrieking, melodic calls* of things that might be birds, but with too many eyes and prehensile tails, flitting from bloom to bloom. The creatures here are not separate from the plant-mass; they are its mobile organs. Pollinators the size of your hand, with iridescent carapaces. Burrowing things that aerate the root-mat, leaving temporary tunnels that fill with condensation. Predators that mimic the dangling vines, their stillness absolute until a passing grazer brushes their trigger-hairs. Life is not abundant; it is *invasive*, every niche filled, every movement part of a frantic, slow-motion frenzy of consumption, growth, and decomposition.

**The Strange & The Ancient:** The weirdness is in the scale and the purpose. The Pillars are not random. In the far distance, you see them arranged in deliberate spirals and concentric rings, patterns visible only when the canopy thins for a moment. The moisture cycle is too regular, too engineered. The air sometimes carries not just the scent of humus and pollen, but a metallic, ozone tang, like the air after a lightning strike that never comes. And in the deepest, most shadowed hollows of the root-mat, you find the signs of the *built*. Not cities, but hints: a section of root-mat that is unnaturally level, as if compressed by a giant's palm; a series of perfectly spherical hollows in a Pillar's side, arranged in a geometric lattice; a shard of something smooth and dark, neither stone nor metal, that hums faintly when you touch it. These are the relics of a prior intelligence, long since consumed, digested, and incorporated into the ecosystem. Their technology was primitive to our eyes—stone and wood and simple alloys—but their understanding of growth was absolute. They didn't build *on* this world; they built *with* it, and were ultimately built *into* it.

**The Rules:** Here, the rules are biological. Water is hoarded and recycled in a closed system. Energy comes from the slow, relentless digestion of the planetary mass itself. The "weather" is the collective respiration and perspiration of the biosphere—vast, slow clouds of spores released in synchronized bursts, seasonal "dry spells" where the canopy tightens, reducing moisture to trigger certain reproductive cycles. The sound is communication, a constant bio-acoustic network of clicks, thrums, and calls that coordinate migrations, warn of predators, and mark territory on a continental scale. To survive here, you must learn its rhythms. You must know which plants offer water, which offer food (often the same one, at different stages), and which will dissolve your skin with digestive acids if you brush against them. You must listen to the sound, not just as noise, but as information. The great, terrifying truth is that the ecosystem is aware of you. Not as an individual, but as a novel stimulus. You are a minor irritant in the great lung, and the lung is slowly, patiently, trying to incorporate you—to break you down into your component nutrients and add your matter to the mat. Your only defense is movement, and the faint, ancient knowledge whispered by the humming shards: the patterns of the Pillars, the cycles of the canopy, the secret paths through the root-labyrinth where the growth is slowest. You are a ghost in the green machine, and the machine is vast, hot, deafening, and very, very hungry.
