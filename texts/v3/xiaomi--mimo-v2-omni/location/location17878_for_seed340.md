You do not hear it at first. You *feel* it—a pressure in the bones, a vibration that travels up through the soles of your feet and hums in your teeth. Then the sound resolves itself, not as a single noise, but as the sum of all noise: a cacophony so dense and total it becomes a new kind of silence, a deafening roar that is the baseline state of this place.

This is the Underbloom. A world without open water, without sky, without the mercy of a surface. You stand on a floor that is not stone, but a compacted, spongy mat of root-matter, fungal hyphae, and the shed integuments of countless living things. It gives slightly underfoot, warm and faintly yielding, like walking on a sun-heated animal’s hide. The air is thick, humid with transpiration and the sweet-sharp scent of growth and decay happening simultaneously and at an impossible rate. It is warm, a constant, enveloping warmth that smells of crushed green stems, spores, and hot chlorophyll.

The light is not normal, not really. It is a diffuse, sourceless luminescence, a soft jade-green glow that emanates from everything. Bioluminescent lichen coats every surface in pulsating veins. Fungal caps, some the size of dinner tables, shed spores that glitter in the air like dust motes in a sunbeam. Great, slow-beating flowers, fleshy and pale as blind cavefish, exhale clouds of phosphorescent pollen that drift and settle like eerie snow. There are no shadows here, only gradients of green and gold gloom.

And the growth. By the gods, the *growth*. Vegetation is not a feature; it is the substrate, the architecture, the very geography. Massive root-columns, thicker than ancient redwoods, descend from the unseen ceiling far above, anchoring the entire world. They are wrapped in parasitic vines that bloom with chattering, bell-like flowers, adding their high-pitched clatter to the roar. Entire biomes exist on a single vertical root-face: mats of velvet moss that digest falling detritus; shelf-fungi that glow with internal fire; air-plants with leaves like translucent membranes, filtering nutrients directly from the thick, spore-laden air. There is no dirt, only layers of accumulated life: shed bark, dead leaves, empty chrysalises, all being consumed and rebuilt in real-time by a riot of decomposers.

This is a world in a state of violent, turbulent genesis. Erosion is a foreign concept; here, the only force is accretion. New structures of plant-matter are constantly being woven into existence. You can watch it: a vine elongates at the speed of a growing fingernail, questing into a new space. A fungal tower erupts from the mat with a soft, wet *pop*, expanding to the size of a hut in a matter of minutes. The landscape is never the same twice. It is an anthill, a coral reef, a storm of life, all compressed into a perpetual "now" of hyperactive creation.

And the fauna. Overwhelming is an understatement. The noise is largely their doing. Insects the size of your fist with crystalline wings create a harmonic drone. Grub-like creatures, pale and fat, burrow through the root-mat with rasping sounds. Things that might be mammals, but wrong—many-limbed, furred in bioluminescent filaments—skitter in the middle distances, their eyes reflecting the eternal green glow. In the deeper gloom, larger shapes move with slow deliberation, their bodies encrusted with the very plants they consume, becoming walking gardens. They are all part of the system, not separate from it. Their waste feeds the plants, their bodies become the substrate when they die, their movements spread spores and seeds. It is a closed, frantic loop.

Amidst this primal chaos, you find the faintest hints of a struggle to impose order. A circle of stones, blackened by fire, surrounding the ashes of a long-dead blaze. A crude alcove carved into a root-column, its interior smoothed by repeated contact, a few primitive bone tools left behind—a scraper, an awl. A length of braided vine, strong as rope, hanging from a high branch. These are the scattered artifacts of beings who once tried to live here, or perhaps still do, in pockets of desperate habitation. Their technology is simple, organic: tools of bone, stone, and hardened sap. They did not build structures; they modified the living architecture, carving shelters into the roots, weaving bridges from vines. It is a world that operates on a different principle: life as a geologic force, happening at a speed that defies comprehension. The air itself feels *alive*, a particulate soup of genetic material, hungry for a place to land and become something else. To be here is to be constantly brushed by tendrils, pelted by spores, watched by countless alien eyes. It is beautiful and terrifying, a testament to the sheer, mindless, deafening power of existence, sealed away in the dark, warm, and infinitely fertile womb of the world. You are an anomaly here, a solid, slow, water-based thing in a realm of churning, photosynthetic fury. And the Underbloom, in its deafening, turbulent indifference, is already beginning to decide what to make of you.
