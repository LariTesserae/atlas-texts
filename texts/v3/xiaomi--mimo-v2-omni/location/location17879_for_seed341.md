The air is thick, breathing steam. It condenses on every surface, a perpetual dew that clots into runnels and drips with a soft, percussive patter onto stone already dark with moisture. This is the Geyser Quarter, though the name is an old joke—there are no geysers here, only the city itself that sweats.

The heat is a blanket, wet and heavy, trapping the smells of damp ferrous stone and the lush, almost aggressive vegetation that claims every crack and crevice. Great, veiny fronds of something between fern and moss climb the walls of the narrow streets, their undersides glowing with a faint, phosphorescent blue light that adds to the general murk. The sun, when it can be seen through the perpetual haze, is a swollen, white disc, offering no relief, only illumination for the steam.

It is a low city, built in a shallow basin that collects the water and the heat. The buildings are not tall, but they are dense and layered, a geological stratification of habitation. The oldest foundations are of a black, porous stone that seems to drink the moisture and weep it back out, staining the walls in long, mineral-rich streaks of ochre and verdigris. Upon these, later generations have built with brick, with poured concrete, with sheets of riveted iron now blooming with rust. Walkways and bridges arc between structures at dizzying heights, a tangled web of infrastructure half-swallowed by vines.

The sound is constant, a liquid roar. It is the symphony of the Quarter: water coursing through countless open sluices and broken pipes that line the streets like veins; the deep, resonant *thrum* of great, hidden pumps working ceaselessly beneath the foundations to keep the Quarter from drowning completely; the hiss of steam venting from cracked geothermal pipes; and the steady, metallic *ping* of cooling iron from the old foundry works. There is no silence here. Even in the relative stillness of a courtyard, the sound is a physical presence, a vibration felt in the bones.

The industry is ancient, automated, and mindless. The foundry stacks belch not smoke, but plumes of pure, hot vapor. Waterwheels, grown over with algae and slime, turn in sluggish canals, powering nothing but their own rotation, their gears grinding with a sound like chewing bones. Looms in abandoned workshops clack and shuttle of their own accord, weaving shrouds of raw, undyed thread from cotton that grows wild on the balconies. The machines have outlived their purpose and their masters, persisting in a slow, decaying loop of activity.

And it is active, profoundly so. The decay is not still. Rust blooms like orange lichen. Masonry crumbles in slow motion, feeding the sediment in the canals. The vegetation grows with visible speed, its tendrils exploring every seam, prying stone apart with patient, green fingers. The water changes the landscape daily, carving new runnels, depositing silt in new mounds. It is an ecosystem of erosion and growth, but one devoid of fauna. There are no birds in the steaming air, no rats in the alleys, no insects buzzing at the phosphorescent moss. The only life is the plant life, and the terrible, mechanical life of the enduring machines.

The strangeness here is not a sudden intrusion, but the state of the place itself. It is the fact that the moss *listens*, its faint glow pulsing in rhythm with the thrum of the deep pumps. It is the way the runoff from certain walls, when collected, forms perfect, glassy spheres that roll uphill toward the source of a particular harmonic vibration. It is the whispers in the steam—complex, non-repeating patterns of sound that seem almost linguistic but resolve into nothing, a phantom of the city’s overtaxed acoustics.

It is a place that has achieved a kind of equilibrium. The machines maintain the drainage that prevents total inundation. The water feeds the plants that break down the structures. The heat catalyzes both decay and growth. The scale is intimate, a warren of cul-de-sacs and hidden courtyards where the sound is deafening and the steam obscures everything more than twenty paces away. You are always in a room here, even outdoors—a room of sound, of vapor, and of encroaching, silent green. It is a cathedral to persistence, and its hymn is the relentless noise of its own slow, wet unraveling.
