# The Young Reaches

The ground is new here. That is the first thing you understand, not from reading or instruction, but from the way the rock feels underfoot — too sharp, too clean, still cooling from whatever deep heat birthed it. The stone has not yet been softened by time. There are no soil fields, no loam, no gentle accumulation of the dead feeding the living. There is mineral. There is grit. There is the raw face of basalt and calcium salts and iron-stained quartzite that has never known rain until now.

And there is so much water.

It seeps from everywhere and nowhere. The rock *weeps*. Every crevice, every fracture in the young stone runs with condensation, with groundwater forced upward by pressure that hasn't yet found its equilibrium. Shallow puddles spread across flats of dark stone and glint under a sky that never fully brightens — a sky the color of tin, pressing low, more vapor than cloud. Streams thread through channels that look freshly cut, as if the water itself is still deciding where to go. In the distance, the terrain rises and rises: ridgelines of naked rock climbing to peaks that have no name because no one has stood on them, their flanks streaked with wet mineral — black, rust, chalk-white — like the exposed anatomy of the earth before skin was invented.

It is cold. Not the settled cold of a northern winter but the thin cold of altitude, of air that has too little mass to hold warmth. Your breath clouds. The water is near-freezing where it pools, and where it moves fast it is colder still. But it does not freeze — something in the mineral content, the constant subtle warmth still radiating from below, keeps it liquid. The landscape is drenched and shivering and alive with a kind of geological fever.

And the machines — though *machines* is the wrong word, because there are no machines. There is nothing built here. No cairn, no post, no marker. What there is, is *mechanism*. The mountain is a factory that has no architect. Great tectonic presses groan beneath the surface at intervals you feel in the bones of your feet — not earthquakes exactly, but the rhythmic clenching of rock being born, plates shoving upward by millimeters, the raw industrial process of orogeny continuing still. You can hear it sometimes in the quiet: a deep percussive thrumming from below, like a mill engine, like the heartbeat of something too large to see. Hydrothermal vents dot the higher slopes, hissing thin plumes of sulfurous steam — natural chimneys — and around their rims the rock is stained in absurd chemical colors, blues and yellows, the palette of a refinery. Mineral-rich water pours from fissures in quantities that seem impossible for the altitude, fed by pressure systems deep in the crust. The mountain is manufacturing its own hydrology. It is building its own watershed in real time, and the process sounds like industry: hissing, grinding, the low groan of stone under load.

The fauna came before the soil did.

They are everywhere, these small persistent lives, and they are baffling in their abundance given how little grows here. Insects first — swarms of midges and springtails clouding over the warm wet patches near the vents, their wings catching the dim light like flecks of mica. Higher animals follow: pale arthropods with translucent bodies navigating the wet stone by vibration and chemical trail. Small mammals — something like voles, something like pikas — burrow into the loose scree, feeding on whatever the insects leave behind. Birds circle the thermals rising from the vents: thin, sharp-beaked things that dive for insects mid-air with mechanical precision. The ecology is simple and brutal and fast, like the landscape itself — everything is still in its early phase, still figuring out the rules, and there is an almost industrial efficiency to the food chain: vent heat feeds microbes, microbes feed insects, insects feed everything else. No wasted motion. No excess.

Sparse vegetation clings in places where mineral deposits have accumulated into something almost like soil — cushions of moss, pale green, almost luminous against the dark rock. A few grasses, low and tough, rooted in cracks where water pools. They are pioneer species, the very first, and they look uncertain about their own existence, as if they know the ground beneath them is still rising, still shifting, that the patch of grit they've claimed may be three feet higher next year. They do not spread. They persist.

The scale of the place is hard to take in because there is so little in it to measure by. The eye travels across expanses of wet dark stone and finds nothing to stop on — no tree, no structure, no landmark — and the mind stutters. Ridges fold into ridges. Valleys descend into valleys. The spaces between features are enormous and empty, filled only with thin air and the sound of water moving over new rock. It is a landscape of *almost nothing per volume*, and the emptiness has a quality that is not quite loneliness but is adjacent to it — the emptiness of a room before furniture, a sentence before meaning. The world here is *under construction*, and the workers have left no scaffolding, no blueprints, only the raw result: height, cold, wet stone, and the machinery of geology grinding on.

At dusk the light — which was never more than a diffused silver-grey — dims further, and the vents become more visible: thin lines of steam catching the last illumination, rising like smoke from a city that was never built. The thrumming from below seems louder in the gathering dark. The midges settle. The small mammals go still in their burrows. And for a moment the landscape feels like what it is: an enormous process, industrial in scale and indifference, still in its early chapters, building itself from raw material with no intention and no audience, the way a body builds itself in the womb — complex, purposeful-looking, and utterly without a designer.

In the morning the water will still be running. The peaks will have risen another fraction of a millimeter. The insects will swarm again over the warm places. And the rock will still be too young, too clean, too new — still cooling, still weeping, still deciding what it wants to become.
