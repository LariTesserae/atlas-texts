# The Steaming Terraces of Numin

The land rises. That is the first thing you understand about this place — not that it *has* elevation, but that elevation is its defining gesture, its reason for existing. The terrain ascends in broad, irregular shelves, each one a plateau maybe a quarter-mile across before the ground humps upward again, steep and slick, to the next level. You climb without meaning to. The terraces stack and stack and the horizon keeps lifting until you realize you're standing on a mountainside that has been worn, over deep time, into something resembling a staircase built by something patient and mineral-minded.

The stone is dark. Basaltic in origin, but so old and so worked-over by water and chemistry that its surface has become something else — a kind of ceramic skin, iron-dark and faintly iridescent when wet, which is always. Every surface weeps. Not dramatically, not as springs or gushers, but as a constant exudation: water finding its way through the porous rock from some vast reservoir deep below, condensing from the thick air onto every cooler surface, running in thin films down every vertical face. The terraces themselves are shallowly concave, each one holding a skin of warm water an inch or two deep — natural basins edged with lips of mineral deposit. Travertine, but not the pale white of limestone pools. These deposits are rust-and-manganese-dark, banded in ochre and greenish-black, built up over millennia into smooth, rounded rims that look almost deliberate, almost shaped by hands. They weren't. The chemistry does this. Iron-rich water, slightly alkaline, meeting slightly different water, slightly different mineral load, and precipitating its excess at the boundary where two flows merge. The rims grow a millimeter a century. They have been growing for a very long time.

Each basin is a world.

The water in them is warm — not hot, not scalding, but body-warm, the temperature of living things. It arrives carrying dissolved minerals from deep strata, and as it sits in the shallow pans under the heavy tropical sun, it concentrates. Silica deposits a barely-visible film on the basin floors. Iron oxide stains the edges in terraced rings of orange and brown. In some of the larger pools, where the water has had time to stagnate and interact with the biology that inhabits it, there are microbial mats — thick, rubbery, rust-red and olive-green — that coat the stone and soften every edge. They smell faintly sulfurous, not enough to offend, enough to remind you that the earth below is active, is cooking, is cycling materials upward through this whole system like a slow metabolism.

The vegetation is ferocious. At the lower terraces, where the ground is broader and the soil — what soil there is, mostly broken-down volcanic rock amended by centuries of dead plant matter — can support real root systems, the growth is extravagant. Broad-leafed plants with leaves the size of your torso crowd against each other, their surfaces glossy with moisture, their edges dripping. Ferns explode from every crevice, some of them tree-sized, their fronds ten feet long, arching over the pool-edges and trailing their tips in the water. Moss everywhere, on every surface that isn't actively underwater — thick, spongy moss in electric green, holding water in itself so that pressing a hand against it releases a small flood. Higher up, where the terraces narrow and the soil thins to almost nothing, the plants change. They become specialists: things with thick, waxy leaves that can root directly into the mineral-crusted stone, with aerial roots that hang down seeking moisture in the air itself, which they always find. The humidity here never drops below eighty percent. You could wring the atmosphere out like a wet cloth.

Trees exist but not in the way you expect. They don't grow straight up — the wind at this elevation, when it moves, won't allow it. Instead they grow *outward*, spreading laterally from their bases, their trunks splitting low into multiple thick limbs that press flat against the rock and follow the contours of the terraces like things trying to hold on rather than reach up. Their roots are visible everywhere: thick, ropy, searching, running along the surface of the stone and occasionally plunging into a crack and vanishing. Where they meet the mineral-laden water, they calcify partially — their lower surfaces crusted with the same dark deposit that edges the pools, so that it becomes hard to tell where the plant ends and the geology begins. Some of the oldest trees have trunks so mineralized at their base that they are effectively stone columns with a living skin. They creak in the rare wind. They are three, four, five hundred years old. They have been slowly incorporating the mountain into their bodies for their entire lives.

The fauna is present but understated. You hear it more than you see it. Small things in the undergrowth — rustlings, the occasional sharp click of one chitinous body against another. Insects are abundant, as you'd expect: they fill the air above the pools in low, shimmering clouds, the light catching their wings so that each swarm looks briefly like a spray of sparks. Some of them are large — dragonfly-analogues with six-inch wingspans that patrol the pool edges with mechanical precision, their bodies iridescent blue-green, catching the light and throwing it back in brief flashes. Frogs, or frog-like things, call from the margins of the larger basins, but their calls are low and resonant, almost subsonic, more felt in the sternum than heard with the ears. You become aware that a constant, deep, irregular pulse underlies the quiet of this place — a basso profundo of amphibian throats vibrating in concert with something geological, some tectonic hum that the land itself produces. You can't be sure where one ends and the other begins.

Birds exist here but are rare and strange. They don't sing so much as produce single, clear, bell-like tones that carry across the terraces and echo off the wet stone. One note each, spaced minutes apart, coming from somewhere in the middle distance. You never locate the source. The sound is beautiful and lonely and makes the silence around it deeper by contrast.

The heat. It is immense and enveloping. Not the dry heat of desert but a saturated, pressing, almost *liquid* heat — the temperature of a hot spring, extended to fill the entire atmosphere. The sun at this elevation would be punishing on bare rock, but the terraces and their vegetation create pockets of shade everywhere, so that you can always find somewhere out of the direct light. The problem isn't the sun. It's the ambient temperature, which sits around 105 Fahrenheit and does not drop at night, because the ground itself radiates warmth, because the water is warm, because the humidity holds the heat like a blanket. Sweat doesn't evaporate here. It simply accumulates. You become part of the wetness.

There is no sound of industry here, no engines, no mechanisms. And yet the place *feels* industrial. That is its strangeness, its slight weirdness — the sense that you are standing inside a process, a system of such complexity and such relentless throughput that it resembles a factory designed by something that has no concept of efficiency in the human sense. The water cycles: up from below as geothermal seepage, across the terraces as flow, into the air as evaporation, back down as rain from the almost-permanent cloud cover that sits just above the highest peaks, and then through the porous rock again, picking up minerals, bringing them up, depositing them, building the rims a fraction of a grain at a time. The biology cycles: the microbial mats consume the minerals and produce compounds that other organisms eat, which feed the plants, which feed the insects, which feed the frogs, which feed the... nothing, really, nothing large, nothing at the top. The system is strangely self-contained, as if it has no predators because it has no need of population control — everything is in balance, everything is *in its own state*, has been for so long that the balance has become permanent, geological, a feature of the landscape rather than something happening within it.

The terraces extend. From the highest point you can reach — a broad, wind-scoured shelf perhaps eight thousand feet above the valley floor, where the pools are smaller and the mineral deposits lighter in color, almost white, because different compounds precipitate at the cooler temperatures — you can look down and see the system in its entirety. It spreads below you like a topographic map made real, layer after layer of dark stone and bright water and impossible green, each terrace catching the light differently, some of the pools reflecting the sky so perfectly that they look like holes punched through the mountain to somewhere else. The scale of it is regional. It covers the entire mountainside, a system maybe fifteen miles across and six thousand feet deep, and it has been here, in this state, for longer than any human presence, longer probably than any human species, slowly building itself upward and outward, an edifice of mineral and water and life that is still under construction and will never be finished.

The quiet. The heat. The water moving everywhere and going nowhere. You sit on a warm, wet stone ledge and watch the mineral rims grow, imperceptibly, and feel the mountain breathe beneath you — a slow expansion and contraction, barely detectable, that is either the tides of some deep aquifer or the slow pulse of magma chambers miles below. The insects flash. A single bell-tone sounds from the middle distance and fades. The steam from the largest pools rises and joins the clouds and will fall again as rain on the upper terraces tomorrow, or the day after, or tonight, and the cycle will continue, mechanical in its regularity, organic in its form, industrial in its scale, and utterly, stubbornly, without any trace of human presence or human intention.

The mountain builds itself. The water brings the stone up in solution and lays it down in walls. The plants drink from both and give back their bodies to feed the next layer of growth. It is a place that has no edges, no boundaries, no clear beginning or end — just process, continuing, hot and wet and alive and so old that its age has become a kind of geography of its own.
