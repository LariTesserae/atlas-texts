The air is thick enough to drink, and scalding. It tastes of hot metal and wet stone. You stand on a causeway of fused black rock, its surface cracked into a mosaic of hexagonal plates that radiate a deep, residual heat. Before you, and in every direction, the landscape stretches out in a state of humid, geologic exuberance.

This is a world without green. The ground is a tapestry of mineral stain: vast sheets of rust-ochre and verdigris, streaked with veins of milky quartz and colonies of slow-growing, metallic crystal that glitter in the perpetual twilight. There are no trees, no grasses, only the relentless architecture of stone and seepage.

Water is everywhere, but not as rivers or lakes. It weeps from the land itself. From fissures in the hexagonal plates, from the porous faces of towering, weathered obelisks, and from the cavernous mouths of structures that are neither wholly natural nor built, a constant, hot condensation drips, trickles, and steams. The air is a permanent mist, each droplet catching the dim, diffuse light that filters from a sky the color of a bruised plum. The sun is a vague, molten smear behind the perpetual vapor haze, casting no sharp shadows.

The structures dominate the middle distance. They are urban in scale and density, but not in intent. They rise like colossal, eroded honeycombs or the abandoned, half-melted cathedrals of a forgotten industry. Walls of slaggy, fused stone are pierced with regular, circular openings, from which warm, damp gusts exhale with a low, sighing sound. Great, rusted chains, thick as old-growth trunks, hang slack between towers, disappearing into the mist. There is no glass, no wood, no sign of delicate artifice—only the heavy, enduring grammar of furnace and quarry. Pipes, some as wide as tunnels, snake across the ground and up the structures, groaning softly with the pressure of whatever hot fluid they transport. A deep, subsonic thrum vibrates through the rock underfoot—the heartbeat of some vast, subterranean engine that never sleeps.

Life, in any familiar sense, is absent. But there is biology. In the perpetual damp, on the shaded sides of the obelisks and within the sheltered arches of the built forms, grow the only competitors to the mineral kingdom: vast, slow-motion colonies of lithophagic fungi. They are sheets of living crust, some the texture of wet felt in shades of ghostly white and violet, others forming hard, scalloped shelves of a substance like chalite. They digest the stone itself, their hyphae tracing faint, lighter patterns through the dark rock, and in doing so, they release the moisture that contributes to the weeping air. Their presence is announced not by color or movement, but by a faint, earthy scent and a soft, almost sub-audible clicking—the sound of crystals being methodically unbonded. Fauna is rare, but here and there, a multi-legged creature resembling a slow, armored isopod made of polished hematite picks its way across the causeway, its claws making a delicate *tick-tick-tick* on the hot plates before it disappears into a fissure to drink from the condensate.

The stillness is profound, but not silent. It is a quiet composed of small, active sounds: the *plink* of hot water hitting cooling stone, the rhythmic groan of a settling pipe, the distant, echoing drip from a high archway, the subterranean hum. The dominant processes are geological and chemical—erosion, condensation, crystallization, and the patient, fungal digestion of the world. It is a landscape in balance, but a balance of extreme states: heat and water, emptiness and monumental construction, silence and ceaseless, slow activity.

The scale is overwhelming. The causeway leads toward the hazy urban mass, but distances are deceptive in the mist. Features that seem a short walk away reveal themselves to be miles distant upon a second glance. The emptiness between these points is absolute—a near-void of shimmering, hot air and stained, unadorned ground. It is a place built for a purpose you cannot grasp, now left to the dominion of stone, water, heat, and the patient, hungry fungi. It is utterly strange, and it feels, in its deep, humming bones, ancient.
