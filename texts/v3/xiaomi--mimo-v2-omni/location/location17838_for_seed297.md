# The Bloomfield Parishes

You come upon it and there is no edge. No transition zone, no gradient of thinning soil or struggling pioneers. The land simply becomes — all at once, across a horizon you could fit a city between — a dense, low, living floor of green so tightly packed it reads first as texture, then as substance, then as something closer to a geological feature.

The ground is not wet. It has never been wet. Run your hand through the upper layer and what comes away is dry, faintly warm, and almost powdery — a tracery of filaments thinner than hair, interlocked at every scale you care to examine. Down deeper it compacts into something firm and almost mineral, a pale calcium-white lattice threaded through with chlorophyll veins. There is no soil here in any conventional sense. The entire substrate *is* the organism, or the colony, or the machine. You are standing on something that took the concept of a root system and extended it, without interruption, across roughly nine hundred square kilometers of near-perfectly flat terrain.

And it is lush. Obscenely, impossibly lush.

---

## The Structure of the Living Floor

Nothing here grows taller than your waist, which is the first strange mercy. The density compensates for the lack of height. What looks from a distance like a uniform meadow of deep emerald and chartreuse resolves, at walking scale, into thousands of distinct morphologies pressed against one another like coral in a reef.

There are flat, photosynthetic pads edged in copper, overlapping like shingles. There are vertical spires no thicker than a pencil that end in tight nodules — these vibrate faintly if you breathe on them. There are rosettes that unfurl in fractal spirals, their innermost curls too small for your eye to resolve. Some structures resemble flowers in the way that a cathedral resembles a cave: the resemblance is there, the logic is entirely alien. Petal-analogues are rigid, glassy, and translucent, ringing with a faint chime when the warm air moves across them — though the air rarely moves much. Between and beneath all of these, the mat: the interwoven fibrous base layer that is simultaneously foundation, plumbing, nervous system, and ground.

The palette runs from deep forest green through every shade of yellow-green, punctuated by the copper, the translucent whites, and occasional veins of something almost iridescent — blue-black, shifting, like oil on a surface that is itself alive.

The warmth comes from below. Not dramatically. Just a steady, even ambient heat, two or three degrees above what your skin expects, as though the ground is running at a low and constant metabolic rate. It never fluctuates. You can press your palm flat against the living mat and hold it there and the temperature will not change by the width of a hair over hours, days, longer.

---

## The Silence

The quiet is the thing you notice second, after the sheer visual density, and it unsettles more.

There is sound here. It is simply not the sound of living places. No birds. No insects. No wind through leaves — because the structures that might act as leaves are rigid and do not rustle. What you hear is:

A faint, pervasive hum. Low-frequency, felt in the sternum more than heard in the ear. It comes from everywhere and nowhere, as if the ground itself is resonating at some barely-audible frequency.

The click-and-settle of the glassy petal structures expanding microscopically in the warmth, a sound like distant static.

Your own footsteps, which sound wrong — muffled and slightly adhesive, as if the ground is gently, constantly adjusting around the pressure of your sole.

Nothing else. No animal calls. No movement in the undergrowth, because there is no undergrowth, only the single continuous growth, and nothing visible lives within it. You might — *might* — if you stand perfectly still for ten minutes, see a faint ripple propagate across a patch of the rosette structures, as though something has passed beneath. But you will never see what caused it.

---

## The Edges of Sense

The light is clear and ample and seems to come from everywhere at once. There is a sun — or something like a sun — overhead, but the illumination has a quality that doesn't quite match direct solar radiation. Shadows exist but are soft, diffused, as though the air itself carries a faint luminosity. The air is dry. Absurdly dry. Your lips crack within the first hour. There is no humidity here and never has been, and yet everything is green, and thriving, and pressed so close together that you have to push through it at certain points, the rigid structures brushing against your clothes with a papery sound.

The smell is minimal — a dry, faintly mineral scent, like crushed chalk with an undertone of something almost chemical. Not unpleasant. Not organic, exactly. If you breathe deeply enough to try to identify it, the scent seems to become more abstract, harder to pin to any memory.

And then there is the thing you feel before you can name it.

The place is *still*. Not calm — still. You have been in calm places before, places where the wind dies and the world holds its breath. This is not that. This is a place where change has been solved. Where the question of what happens next has been answered with *this, exactly this*, and the answer is not a moment but a permanent state. The warmth will not rise or fall. The green will not grow or recede. The hum will not vary. If you leave a mark — scuff the surface, break a glassy petal — you will return to it in a day, a week, a year, and find the mark has been gently, perfectly filled, not repaired but *re-integrated*, as though it never marked a boundary between the organism and not-organism at all.

Time, in the Bloomfield Parishes, moves. You move through it. But the Parishes themselves do not participate.

## The Question of Origin

There are no structures here. No ruins, no tools, no痕迹 of any intelligence that built and left. And yet the technology rating of this place would break any instrument you brought with it — not because you can see it, but because you can *measure* it. Spectral analysis of the living mat returns results that do not correspond to any known biological or chemical process. The fibrous base layer is conducting something that is not quite electricity and not quite chemistry. The fractal rosettes are computing — you are not sure how you know this, but if you sit with one and watch its spirals, you begin to understand that their geometry is *processing* information, that each level of recursion is a calculation, and that the whole field, all nine hundred square kilometers of it, is one slow and patient thought that has been thinking itself for longer than you have a frame of reference to measure.

It was not built. It was not planted. It arrived — or it *emerged*. Perhaps it was always latent in the physics of this place, a phase change waiting for the right conditions. Perhaps it was seeded from somewhere else, by something whose technology and intent are so far past your comprehension that the distinction between biology and engineering dissolved long before you arrived to draw it.

The only certainty is that it is complete. It does not need you. It does not need anything. It is a closed system, running on energy that does not degrade, locked in an equilibrium that does not erode, doing something that may be photosynthesis or may be thought or may be a kind of worship, and it will be here, exactly like this, when you are not.

---

You can walk for days in any direction and the landscape will not change in any way that matters. The rosettes may give way to spires for a kilometer. The copper-edged pads may thin. The hum may shift half a tone. But the place holds. It is a living thing frozen at the height of its own expression, dense and warm and dry and utterly, profoundly still — a green and breathing machine that has, for whatever reason, decided to be a meadow.
