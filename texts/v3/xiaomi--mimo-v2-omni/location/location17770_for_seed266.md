The first thing is the scale of it, and the heat. You stand on a plain of fused, glassy regolith that stretches toward a horizon so distant it feels less like a curve and more like a slow, warm fading of detail. Above, the sky is a permanent, hazy amber, the light of a sun filtered through a permanent suspension of fine mineral dust. There is no blue. The warmth is dry and penetrating, radiating up from the ground and down from the sky, a constant, gentle bake that never peaks, never ebbs. It is the warmth of a world that has forgotten how to cool.

The ground beneath your feet is not dirt. It is a mosaic of fused silicate plates, cracked into vast polygons by some ancient, universal stress. In the cracks, there is no green life, only deeper darkness and a faint, metallic glint. The air carries a taste of ozone and hot stone, and a sound—the only persistent sound—is a deep, sub-audible hum. It is the vibration of the world itself, the resonance of its slow rotation and the groan of its cooling core, felt in the bones more than heard by the ear.

This is a world of stone and metal, and of life that has learned to thrive upon them. The fauna is abundant, but it is a fauna of silicate and crystal. From a distance, the low, mesa-like formations that dot the plains might be geological features. But stay awhile, and you see the imperceptible shifts. These are the **Lithovores**, great, slow creatures whose bodies are built of interlocking mineral plates, drawing energy directly from the geothermal gradients and the ambient static charge in the dust-filled air. They move in cycles measured in decades, their passage marked not by footprints but by the subtle re-fusing of the glassy ground behind them. Smaller things skitter in the cracks: **Spinnerets** that weave filaments of spun basalt fiber into protective nets, and **Chime-Swarms**, insectoid collectives whose crystalline carapaces resonate with the world-hum, creating eerie, harmonic choruses that rise and fall with the planet's weak magnetic tides.

And then, there is the city.

You see it not as a cluster of buildings, but as a **geological feature**. It rises from the plain like a mountain range that has been carefully, violently sculpted. This is **Kharos-Dur**, the Forge-Mountain. It is a single, continuous structure of layered metal, slag-concrete, and carved basalt, miles across and miles high. Its towers are not built; they are **excavated** from monolithic stone spires, their windows glowing with the amber of contained furnace-light. Bridges of stressed metal and fused rock span chasms that plunge miles into the world's crust, where the deep hum is loudest and the heat is a tangible, shimmering force.

This is an industrial civilization, but one that operates on a geologic timetable. The factories are not noisy, belching places. They are vast, silent chambers where chemical reactions happen over centuries, slowly growing structural beams from catalyzed magma. The "engines" are tectonic brakes and geothermal taps, systems that harness the planet's own slow, immense power. The inhabitants—descendants of whatever built this place—are a sparse populace, their lives long and deliberate, their movements paced to the rhythms of their world. They are engineers of deep time, architects of erosion.

The strangeness is in the details. **Gravity** here feels slightly *off*, not weaker or stronger, but *selective*. Certain veins of ore in the city's foundations cause localized pockets where dust falls upward in lazy spirals. **Light** behaves oddly at the edges of the great chasms, bending and smearing as if the air itself were a flawed crystal. The city's infrastructure is layered; you can see the eons of its construction. The deepest levels are primordial, natural lava tubes reinforced with forgotten tech. The middle layers are brutalist, efficient carvings from the industrial age. The upper spires are newer, more delicate lattices of alloy, reaching into the hazy sky, as if the city, like the Lithovores, is slowly, inexorably climbing.

There is no weather as we know it. No rain, no clouds. But there are **Dust Falls**—periodic, slow-motion avalanches of fine particulate from the upper atmosphere, settling on the city's shoulders like a grey snow, to be painstakingly collected and purified. There are **Resonance Storms**, where the world-hum spikes in frequency, causing the chime-swarms to shriek in unison and certain metals to sing with a painful, clear tone. The cycles are slow, the changes glacial. A storm might build over a year and subside over a decade.

This is a world in balance, but it is the balance of a knife's edge on a geological scale. It is vast, sparse, and old. It is a sealed system of heat, stone, metal, and the strange life that has learned to call it home. The silence is not empty; it is the sound of immense, patient processes at work. To stand here is to feel time dilate, to understand that growth and change are not measured in springs, but in the slow, warm, grinding turn of a world toward its own distant, amber horizon.
