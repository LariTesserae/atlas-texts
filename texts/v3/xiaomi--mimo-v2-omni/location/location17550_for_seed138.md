The air does not move. It hangs, thick and hot, a blanket of vapor that condenses on every surface and drips in a constant, pattering rain that is not weather but atmosphere. This is the Basin of Condensate, a low, sunken pocket of the world where the ground sweats perpetually. The heat is immense, a physical weight that presses from all directions, yet the sky is a dim, pearl-grey ceiling, the light diffuse and shadowless, as if the sun itself has been boiled into a soft, omnipresent glow.

The landscape is one of sharp, young geology. There are no rolling hills, only angular ridges and sudden, flat mesas of dark basalt and greenish soapstone, their surfaces slick and gleaming. Water weeps from every fissure, not in streams but in a million tiny trickles, feeding a network of shallow, steaming canals and warm, still ponds that mirror the grey sky. The ground between is a mix of gritty, volcanic sand and porous rock, warm to the touch, and everywhere there is the smell of wet stone, hot metal, and a faint, mineral tang of salt.

Into this primordial steambath, industry has carved its mark. The scale is not of towering spires but of dense, clustered habitation—hamlets and workshops built directly into the rock faces, or perched on stilts over the warmer ponds. These are not pretty towns. They are agglomerations of dark iron, soot-stained brick, and rough-cut stone, linked by networks of pipes, sluices, and clattering elevated railways. Great, slow-moving waterwheels churn in the canals, their groaning axle-songs a bass note beneath the constant hiss of steam vents and the percussive *clang-clang-clang* of drop-hammers from forges built into the cliffs. Electricity is here, but it is a young, crackling thing—arcs of blue light jumping between copper poles, powering pulsing dynamo-houses that hum with a nervous energy.

Vegetation is sparse, but stubborn. In the cracks of the forges, where condensation provides a drip, clusters of pale, translucent moss thrive. On the shaded northern faces of the basalt ridges, leathery, heat-loving ferns unfurl in tight spirals, their fronds sweating the same moisture as the rocks. There are no trees, only thickets of wire-like, rust-colored reeds at the canal edges, their roots filtering the mineral-rich water.

The fauna, however, is abundant and has adapted profoundly. Clouds of tiny, metallic-winged flies swarm around the warmer pools, their buzzing a high-frequency drone that mixes with the industrial din. In the canals, blind, eel-like creatures with heat-sensing pits navigate the murky water, feeding on chemotrophic bacteria that bloom on the submerged hot stones. On land, the dominant creatures are the **Kettlegaits**: six-limbed, armored arthropods the size of small dogs. They are scavengers and engineers, their chitinous shells the color of rusted iron. They move in slow, deliberate caravans, harvesting mineral deposits and stray bits of industrial waste, their multiple limbs clicking and scraping on the stone. They are the sound of the place when the machines pause—a dry, rhythmic skittering. Occasionally, a larger predator, a low-slung, multi-eyed **Boiler-Cat**, will stalk the edges of a settlement, its fur matted with condensation, waiting for an unwary Ketlegait or a dropped scrap of meat.

The world here is in a state of perfect, frozen equilibrium. The water that evaporates in the heat condenses and falls back within the same hour. The machines run at a constant, unchanging pace, their rhythms as eternal as the dripping rocks. The Kettlegaits follow the same paths their ancestors did. No gradient, no progress, no decay—only the sustained, loud, wet, and hot moment, forever. It is a place of immense, oppressive energy that never changes, a steam-powered terrarium where industry and geology have reached a searing, stable, and loud détente.
