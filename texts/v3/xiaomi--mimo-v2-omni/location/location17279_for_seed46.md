This place exists at a high, hot altitude where the concept of “water” has been replaced by something older and stranger. The air is thin, dry, and breathes with a geothermal heat that radiates not from a sun above, but from the ground itself. It is a highland plateau of dark, vitreous rock—obsidian and basalt—that cools in fractured plates where the heat bleeds into the thin atmosphere, but never quite chills. There are no rivers, no lakes, no dew. Moisture is a foreign concept, a memory of a different world.

And yet, it is lush.

Life here does not drink. It *breathes* the heat, the mineral exhalations, the raw energy seeping from the planet’s bones. What could be called vegetation grows in sprawling, interconnected mats and towering, slow-moving forms. Think less of leaves and more of photosynthetic lichen, of heat-loving fungal blooms, and crystalline stalks that conduct geothermal energy into their fibrous, silicon-rich structures. Colors are deep and burnished: rust-red, copper, a blue-black sheen, and sudden bursts of phosphorescent gold where chemical reactions glow in the gloaming. The “canopy” is a latticework of these slow, reaching structures, casting intricate, shifting shadows on the glassy ground.

The erosion is embryonic. This landscape is not being worn down; it is still being *forged*. New rock plates, slick and dark, push up from fissures with glacial patience, grinding against older sections. The edges are sharp, unweathered. Crystals bloom in hollows like frost, growing visibly over a season—not by accretion, but by a kind of geological secretion. The ground hums.

This is where the hidden scale reveals itself. The vast, hot plateau is an illusion. Your perception shifts, and you see it for what it is: a single, immense feature contained within a bubble of warped reality. A pocket world. The “sky” is a blurred, shimmering membrane not far above the tallest growths. The horizon curves upward. This entire ecosystem is embedded within something larger, a vast and possibly dead interior, like a geode the size of a mountain range.

And here, the industrial and the weird become one. There are no factories, no structures. The technology is geological. Vast, slow-moving pistons of stone and iron ore, miles wide, throb in rhythm with the planet’s core, their movement the only source of the pervasive hum. Conduits of naturally-formed, superconducting mineral veins pulse with light, carrying not electricity, but concentrated thermal and kinetic energy. This is the planet’s industrial nervous system, and the lush, heat-fed growths are its symbiotic circuitry. They cluster along the conduits, tapping into the flow, their bioluminescence flickering in time with the deep, tectonic heartbeat.

The sound is mostly quiet, but never silent. It is the sub-bass groan of moving stone, the high, crystalline *tink* of growing minerals, the sizzle of chemical reactions in the fungal mats. It is the sound of a world thinking, processing, building itself according to a logic that is both industrial and profoundly alien.

There is no animal life to witness this. The dynamism is slow, gradual—a growth of crystal, a shift in a stone piston, a new vein of light awakening over decades. Reality here is thin, unstable. At the edges of your vision, the geometry of the rock plates might momentarily tessellate into impossible patterns. A tree-like structure might reflect a landscape that isn’t there—a forest under a blue sky, a ghost of a water-world, a flickering memory of rain. Then it’s gone, leaving only the dry, hot, vibrant, and machine-like pulse of this sealed and secret heart.
