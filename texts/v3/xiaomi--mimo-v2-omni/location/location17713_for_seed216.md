# The Hollow Phytum

You are standing inside a world that grew.

There is no ground here—not as you would recognize it. Beneath your feet, the surface gives slightly, warm and faintly yielding, composed of root-matter woven so densely over such vast spans of time that it has become architectural. It *is* the architecture. The floor, the walls that curve upward at enormous distances, the vaulted ceiling so far above that it carries its own weather—clouds of condensation forming and drifting in the upper dark—all of it is plant. All of it has always been plant.

They call it the Phytum, or some word older than that, untranslatable. It is a world grown rather than formed, and it is impossibly, achingly empty for how much of it there is.

## The Structure

The scale defies comfortable reckoning. The interior spaces of the Phytum are continental. You stand in what might be called a chamber, though it is larger than most geological formations on worlds of solid rock. The nearest wall—a slow-curving surface of woven lignic tissue, bark-smooth and darkened to the color of old copper—might be three days' walk away. The ceiling is farther than that. You cannot see it clearly. The light does not reach that far.

And yet the space is not solid. This is the paradox that unsettles newcomers most. The Phytum is *hollow*. Not hollow like a cave, where stone surrounds you, but hollow like a framework, a skeleton, a lattice expanded to planetary scale. Between the great structural members—trunks the size of mountain ranges, buttress-roots that descend from heights you cannot see—there is *nothing*. Open air. Vast distances of warm, dim atmosphere in every direction. You can look outward from certain vantage points and see other structures, other chambers, other vaulted spaces, separated by gulfs of emptiness so large they develop their own weather systems. Wind moves through these voids on schedules dictated by temperature differentials between chambers. Some of these winds are old. Some have names.

The density of this world, measured in matter-per-volume, would scandalize a geologist. It is mostly air. The plant-structure that constitutes the Phytum is efficient, tendoned, reduced to what is necessary. Everything unnecessary has been pruned away by time. What remains is the load-bearing reality: the minimum architecture required to maintain a world-sized organism in a state of equilibrium.

## The Materials

Everything is cellulose and lignin and vascular tissue at some scale, but the variations are immense. The floor is springy, fibrous, warm to the touch—like walking on a living thing, because you are. It perspires. Moisture beads on every surface here, condensing from the warm, humid atmosphere onto the cooler plant-matter, tracing rivulets that follow the grain of the tissue downward into capillary channels. The Phytum drinks itself. Water moves through it constantly, pumped by processes you can feel if you press your palm flat against any structural surface: a faint pulse, slow as meditation, cycling water from the marshy low-points up through vascular columns so enormous they have their own internal rivers, and then releasing it again as vapor into the vast interior atmospheres.

The marshes—the wet places—are the oldest parts. They collect in the bowls and depressions of the structural floor where water pools before it can be reabsorbed. They smell of anaerobic decay and new growth simultaneously. Fungal colonies bloom at their margins in pale, luminescent clusters. The water is tea-dark, stained by tannins leaching from ancient plant-matter, and warm—almost hot—because the metabolic activity of decomposition in these pools is constant and vigorous.

Other materials exist, but only as plant-derivatives. There are places where the lignic tissue has hardened over uncounted ages into something approaching wood-stone, dense and resonant if you strike it. The primitive inhabitants—if *inhabitant* is the right word for things that may be extensions of the Phytum itself—use tools shaped from this material: wedges, mallets, lever-sticks, cutting edges formed from flakes of silicified bark. Nothing metal. Nothing forged. Everything carved, grown, split, or whittled. Their constructions are indistinguishable from the Phytum's own growth, because they are the same process at a different scale.

## The Light

It is dim. Not dark—you can see—but dim in the way of a forest floor at the end of a cloudy afternoon. The Phytum's upper vault absorbs nearly all external light. What filters through is green-shifted, modified by the living tissue overhead into a spectrum dominated by deep amber and chlorophyll-shadow. There are bioluminescent elements—fungal threads, certain symbiotic algae in the wet places, occasional blooms of phosphorescent spore-mass that light chambers in brief, ghost-blue episodes—but these are sporadic and unreliable. Most of the time, you navigate by the dim, sourceless amber that pervades everything.

Your eyes adjust. They always adjust. But you never stop noticing the dimness. It makes distance ambiguous. The far walls of any chamber are suggestions, hazes of darker color against lighter dark. You learn to read proximity by sound and touch more than sight.

## The Sound

There is almost none.

This is the quality that most disturbs visitors. For a world so large, so structurally complex, so biome-rich, the Phytum is profoundly quiet. The atmosphere is thick and humid—sound travels strangely in it, muffled and close, as if the air itself absorbs. Footsteps on the living floor make a soft, wet compression sound and nothing more. The pulse of vascular flow is subsonic; you feel it in your bones but cannot hear it. Wind in the voids between structures produces a low, almost sub-audible moan that takes on harmonic qualities in certain chambers—chords that shift over hours as wind-speed changes—but it is felt more than heard.

The fauna is abundant but quiet. Creatures move through the structural lattice with an economy born of deep adaptation. There are things like enormous slow-moving manta shapes that drift through the voids on thermal currents, membrane-wings translucent against the amber dim, feeding on airborne spores. They make no sound. There are smaller things—multi-legged, trunk-climbing organisms with chitin like the plant-matter itself—that feed on surface tissues, and their feeding is a whisper, a dry crackle barely perceptible. In the marshes, there are pulses of life: the quick darting of things that hunt by vibration, the wet plop of amphibian-analogues that have lived so long in darkness they have no eyes, only skin that senses pressure changes in the water.

Everything here has learned silence, or was built that way.

## The Ecology

The Phytum is not a place where plants grow. The Phytum *is* the plant. It is a single organism—or a colonial superorganism so integrated that the distinction is meaningless—that constitutes the entire structural, biological, and atmospheric basis of the world. Every other living thing exists in relationship to it.

Some relationships are symbiotic. The fungal networks in the marshes break down dead tissue and return nutrients to the vascular system. The spore-feeding drifters in the voids concentrate atmospheric minerals and deposit them on surfaces in their waste, fertilizing growth-zones. The trunk-climbers excrete compounds that stimulate bark-hardening in the structures they inhabit, strengthening the architecture they depend on.

Some relationships are parasitic. There are borers—organisms that tunnel into the structural tissue and feed on it, creating cavities and weaknesses. The Phytum responds by sealing off damaged areas, flooding them with defensive compounds, growing new tissue around the wound. This is slow. The timescale of injury and repair here is generational. A borer-wound might take centuries to seal. The Phytum does not rush. It has no reason to rush. It has been here for longer than you can comfortably think about.

## The Weirdness

And then there are the places where the world does not hold together properly.

You notice it first as a wrongness in the distance. A wall that should be curved is curved *incorrectly*—the arc tightens as you follow it, folding in a direction that is not a direction, the geometry pressing against something your visual cortex cannot process. You look away and look back, and it is simply a wall again, but the afterimage of impossibility lingers.

In certain chambers, the scale shifts without warning. You walk across the living floor for hours, the far wall always a day's journey away, and then you turn a corner around a structural buttress and the chamber is *small*. Wall to wall, ceiling low, the space intimate and close and *wrong* in a way that makes your breath catch. You look back the way you came, and the entrance is gone. Not sealed—*gone*, as if it never existed. You are in a small room that has always been a small room. Your footprints in the surface tissue behind you are not there.

Then you walk forward, and the wall ahead opens, and you step into a void so large that your sense of balance fails. There is no floor. There is no ceiling. There are structural elements at distances your mind refuses to compute, and between them—nothing. Not darkness. Not fog. *Nothing*. A visual absence that the eye slides off of like water off oiled skin. You do not fall. You are standing on solid surface. But the surface and the void exist in the same space, and your legs tell you one thing while your eyes tell you something that your eyes should not be able to tell you.

These episodes pass. They always pass. The world reasserts its geometry, its gentle amber dimness, its warm and humid quiet. You continue walking. But you have begun to understand that the Phytum is not entirely subject to the laws you brought with you. It has its own laws—older, slower, and less concerned with your ability to perceive them correctly.

The inhabitants—if they are separate from the Phytum—do not seem troubled by these passages. They mark certain areas with carved symbols in the hardened bark-stone: warnings, or perhaps invitations. It is hard to tell. Their language is grown rather than written, each symbol a living glyph that shifts subtly over time, and translation requires a patience you may not have.

## The Atmosphere

Warm. Humid. Close. The air in any chamber is rich with spores, water vapor, the metabolic breath of the Phytum itself. It smells of green growing things and deep tannin and the faint sweetness of fermentation. Breathing it is like breathing inside a living body, which you are. There is a slight dizziness that visitors develop after a few days—not dangerous, not toxic, just a gentle loosening of the boundaries between self and environment. You begin to feel your own moisture, your own warmth, your own biological kinship with the world around you. Whether this is the atmosphere or the weirdness or simply the truth of the situation is unclear.

Weather happens *inside*. In the great voids between structural elements, temperature differentials drive convection. Warm air rises from metabolic surfaces, cools against distant vaults, condenses. Rain falls in the interiors of the Phytum—warm, tannin-tinted rain that feeds the marshes and keeps the vascular system cycling. Storms develop in the largest voids, moving through spaces bigger than nations, their lightning brief and silent at the distances involved. You see the flash, count no seconds, hear nothing.

## The Rules

You learn, or you are taught, or you simply begin to know:

Do not drink from the marshes without preparation. The microbial life in the tannin-water is not hostile to you, but it is *alien* in its biochemistry, and your gut will protest.

Do not trust the small rooms. They are not traps, but they are not destinations either. They are *transitions*. Move through them without stopping. The Phytum is reconfiguring around you, and stillness is not a state it recognizes.

Mark your path on the surface with the bark-stone tools. The marks will persist for a few hours before the living floor grows over them. This is not the Phytum erasing you. This is the Phytum *healing*. You are a wound. It is not angry about this, but it will heal nonetheless.

Listen to the silence. The absence of sound is information. When the silence changes—when it thickens, or thins, or acquires a quality you cannot name—the Phytum is doing something. A vascular flush. A structural shift. A growth-event in a nearby chamber. The silence speaks. You just have to learn its grammar.

Do not look directly at the voids for too long. They are not empty. They are *open*. And what is open can sometimes look back.

## What It Is

The Phytum is a world that decided to be alive and followed through completely. There is no substrate beneath it. No rock, no mineral earth, no geological foundation. It is plant-root all the way down, all the way through, a planetary-scale organism that has been growing and sustaining itself for epochs long enough to develop equilibrium with its star, its orbit, its own internal dynamics.

It is primitive not because it is simple but because it has never needed to be anything else. Its technology is its biology. Its architecture is its anatomy. Its inhabitants are its proprioception—its awareness of itself, moving through its own body, maintaining its own systems with tools made from its own tissue.

It is vast and empty and warm and dim and wet and alive and strange, and it has been here since before your species had a word for what a plant is.

You are standing on it. You are inside it. You are, in ways you may never fully understand, *part* of it now.

The silence holds you like a cupped hand.
