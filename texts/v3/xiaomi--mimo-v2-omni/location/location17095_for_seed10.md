It is a high plateau, or perhaps the eroded crown of a mountain range so ancient its peaks have been worn flat. The scale is regional; you can see for leagues in every direction, and the landscape rolls away in gentle, water-carved undulations. There is no sharp horizon, only a soft blurring where the dim, lavender-grey sky meets the land.

Everything here is alive, and wet.

The ground is not soil, but a deep, resilient mat of plant matter—spongy, fibrous, and thrumming with moisture. It gives slightly underfoot with a soft, sucking sound, releasing a breath of cold, chlorophyll-rich air. Water doesn't pool so much as *exude*. It wells up from countless pores in the vegetation, trickles down living slopes, and gathers in shallow, shifting depressions that are less ponds than temporary organs of the landscape, filling and draining according to some vegetative pulse. The air itself is a condensing mist, a perpetual post-rain chill that beads on every surface. Rocks—if they are rocks—are few and far between, and they weep. They are rounded, ancient stones that seem to sweat a clear, cold fluid, their surfaces perpetually slick and furred with a moss that glows with a faint, internal phosphorescence, contributing to the dim, crepuscular light.

This is a pure-plant ecosystem. There are no trees in the conventional sense, no shrubs, no grasses. There are only *masses*. Great, slow-moving continents of interlocked plant life: some resemble colossal, flattened fungi with the texture of wet leather; others are dense, bristling thickets of what might be heather, but each needle-tip drips with condensation; still others are vast, flat rosettes of succulent-like plates, their edges ruffling in the ceaseless, damp breeze. They grow over and into each other, a seamless, living architecture. What appears to be a valley is actually the fold between two colossal, mossy growths. A ridge is the hardened spine of a plant that has been growing here for millennia.

And it is turbulent. This is not a still life. The landscape *moves*. Not quickly, but with a grinding, inexorable purpose. One moment, a section of the mat-land a hundred paces wide will heave upward with a wet, tearing sound, water gushing from the newly exposed undersides, as some deep-rooted organism reshapes itself. Minutes later, a different sector will subside with a long, gurgling sigh, the mist swirling into the new depression. The "weeping rocks" are not stationary; they are slowly sinking back into the vegetative substrate, to be digested and reformed. The entire plateau is in a state of slow, chaotic metamorphosis—a Solaris-like churn of creation and decay, but rendered in root, fiber, and cold water.

The fauna is abundant, but it is difficult to separate from the plant-substrate. Creatures that are more mossy lump than animal drift across the surface, grazing on the exuded moisture and the spores that hang thick in the air. They leave behind shimmering trails of digestate that are immediately colonized by a new, brilliantly blue mold. In the taller, bristling thickets, you might glimpse things that resemble feathered serpents, but they move with the slow grace of vines, and their "feathers" are actually a form of symbiotic lichen that photosynthesizes even in this dim light. The true animals are small, insectile, and frantic, buzzing between the great plant-masses like sparks from a slow, green fire. Their sound is part of the loud, constant atmosphere: a deep, sub-bass hum from the shifting plant tectonics, overlaid with the high, chittering whine of the insectiles, the drip and trickle of water, and the sudden, guttural *gloop* of a gas-pore opening in some fleshy growth.

There is no technology, no artifact, no structure built by hands. But there is *industry*. It is the industry of the ecosystem itself. Mycelial networks, thick as cables, pulse with faint light beneath the surface, transmitting nutrients and information at a pace that feels geological but is, in its own way, mechanized and scaled. The turbulence is the labor. The condensation is the exhaust. The entire place operates like a vast, biological factory, its production being itself, endlessly refined and reconstituted. The weirdness is in its fundamental logic. Cause and effect are softened. You might see a growth bloom and wither in the space of a minute, its brief life a response to a change that won't be felt for a decade. The air sometimes tastes of metal, or of memories of salt oceans that vanished before the plateau was born. Reflections in the water-depressions don't always match what stands above them.

This is a place at the end of a very long road. It is ancient beyond measure, settled into a final, dynamic form where erosion has done its ultimate work: not to flatten, but to simplify into a primal, wet, cold, living engine. It is sparse not in life, but in *variety*. The emptiness between the great plant-presences is an emptiness of difference, not of matter. It is a world that has dreamt itself into a single, complex organism, and now shivers and churns in the depths of its own, slow dream.
