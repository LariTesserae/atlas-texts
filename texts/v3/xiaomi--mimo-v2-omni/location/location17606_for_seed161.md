**Gloomth**

The place is called Gloomth by those who live within it, and the name is less a title than a description of the air itself. It is a vast, sealed cathedral of a world, its boundaries felt rather than seen—a constant, subtle pressure of enclosure against the mind. The scale is intimate yet grand; one could walk from one "wall" of the environment to the other in a single day, yet the verticality is staggering. Sheer, near-vertical spires and shelves of porous, bone-colored rock rise from the dim floor, climbing upward into a gloom so thick it feels like a medium in itself. There is no sky, only a diffuse, sourceless amber twilight that seems to emanate from the very substance of the rock and the life that clings to it.

The air is warm, dry, and fragrant with the thick, sweetish scent of decay and growth, a perfume of rich humus and alien pollen. It moves slowly, a languid respiration that carries sound with profound clarity and resonance. There is no water here—not a drop, not a dew. The hydrologic cycle is absent, and the concept of rain is a myth. Instead, life drinks from the air itself, which is saturated with moisture exhaled by the dense ecology and perhaps drawn from some deep, chemical reaction within the rock.

And that ecology is everywhere, a plush, voracious tapestry. Every surface, from the cavern floor to the dizzying heights of the spires, is covered in a thick, spongy carpet of **fungal life**. This is the vegetation: not plants, but a kingdom of its own. Velvet mats of mycelium in shades of bruised purple, grey-green, and deepest blue cling to the stone. From them sprout the "fruits": towering, tree-like structures with caps that glow with a faint, bioluminescent vein-pattern, casting pools of cold blue light. There are delicate, feathery strands that hang like chandeliers, and robust, shelf-like conks that ring like porcelain when struck. The air is filled with their spores, a constant, glittering dust motes in the dim light.

The fauna is abundant and wholly adapted to this fungal forest. Six-limbed grazers with tough, rasping mouthparts move slowly up the spire-stems, cropping the glowing caps. Their shells are patterned like lichen, and they are followed by smaller, faster symbionts that pick parasites from their hides. In the crevices and on the sheltered undersides of shelves, colonies of dangling, worm-like creatures filter the spore-rich air. The loudest sound in Gloomth is the constant, soft, abrasive chorus of a million mouths rasping on fungal flesh—a sound like gentle, perpetual sanding, underscored by the deep, almost sub-audible thrum of the largest mycelial networks pulsing with nutrients.

The erosion here is not of wind or water, but of time and life itself. This is a primordial, settled state. The rock, aiteiteite calcium-carbonate analog, is slowly digested from within by the mycelial networks, which excrete mild acids. The spires are not jagged peaks but softened, rounded forms, their surfaces pitted and honeycombed. The entire landscape is near-flattened at its base by the constant, slow rain of dead fungal matter, creating a deep, springy substrate. This is the end of life's chaotic formation; now is the long, slow maintenance.

Here, in the stable warmth and the dim, spore-hazed air, a civilization has arisen. They are the **Grown-Tenders**. Their technology is primitive in material but profound in understanding. They have no metal. Their tools are shaped from the hardened, petrified stems of ancient fungi, their edges sharpened with obsidian-like flakes of the local rock. Their homes, their temples, their very towns are not built, but *cultivated*. They have learned to guide the growth of the massive, tree-fungi, encouraging them to form arches, walls, and spiraling towers. Their city is a single, continuous, living organism—a warren of chambers and bridges that glows from within with the blue light of cultivated bioluminescence. Infrastructure is mycelial; waste is fed back into the living floor, nutrients are cycled through deliberate inoculations. It is an urban settlement of immense density, layered upon itself, a three-dimensional maze grown over millennia.

Life here is slow, patient, and cyclical. The changes are glacial. A new chamber in the city might take a generation to fully form. The great grazing herds follow a single spire's cap-cycle for decades. The loudest moments are the "Sighs"—periodic, slow exhalations of gas from deep within the planet that sweep through the chambers, rich in nutrients, causing a burst of growth and a symphony of creaking, stretching sounds from the living architecture.

Gloomth is a world of symbiosis, sealed in time. It is warm, dim, loud with the sound of feeding, and profoundly alive in a slow, deliberate, and utterly alien way. The rules are simple: tend the fungi, harvest the fruits, listen to the rock, and do not break the ancient, settled balance. Outside is a void. Inside is everything.
