# The Steaming Terraces of Kehl Summit

You come up through a cut in the rock — a road, if you can call it that — and the heat hits like opening a furnace. Not radiant, not like sunlight. The air itself is hot, the way air is hot above a kettle. It smells ofite and calcium and something faintly metallic, like blood left on a stove.

The landscape opens in every direction, and it is *full*. That is the first and most disorienting thing: there is no empty ground. Every square meter is occupied. Terraces of pale stone descend and ascend in vast spiraling steps, carved into the mountains over millennia and then — the uncanny part — *re-carved*, re-terraced, re-sequenced by human industry that came later and left its own geometry on top of the geological one. The result is a density of form that feels almost textual, almost script-like, as if someone wrote on the mountain in two different languages and you're reading both at once.

From a distance, the terraces look like an enormous amphitheater built for an audience that never arrived. Up close, each level is packed: packed with stone, with the husks of processing equipment, with the remains of conveyor housings and crushing stations that have been still for decades. Metalwork in rust-red and oxidized green clings to the terrace walls. Some of it is structural, some of it decorative — the old miners etched patterns into their machinery housings, small spirals and animal shapes, as though the work needed ornamentation to justify itself.

The industrial remains are scattered but persistent. You'll walk thirty meters and find a collapsed pump station. Another fifty and there's a row of hopper foundations, their chutes still aimed at nothing. They are small presences, individually — a beam here, a bracket there — but they are *everywhere*, the way bones are everywhere at a dig site. You begin to understand that the density parameter is not metaphorical. There is truly no room between the presences. Stone terrace, metal remnant, mineral crust, dried sludge, and then stone again. Layer and layer.

---

The water. There is almost no surface water — a few seeps that emerge from the lower terraces where the mountain's interior heat meets cooler rock strata. These seeps are warm. Not hot, but warm enough that steam curls from them in the early morning before the ambient heat takes over. They are small: rivulets no wider than your hand, tracing mineral-white paths across the stone before pooling in shallow depressions and evaporating. You learn to watch for the white trails. They tell you where the water was yesterday, even if it's gone today. Occasionally — perhaps once a day — a seam somewhere deep opens and sends a pulse of water across the middle terraces, a quick shallow flood that steams and shrinks over the course of an hour. The fauna knows. You learn to watch the fauna.

---

The fauna is abundant and everywhere and largely quiet, which is the second disorienting thing. The birds here — and there are birds, more than you'd expect — have short, clipped calls. No sustained song. Small mammals move between the machinery ruins with a silence that feels learned, not natural, as though the generations that survived here were the ones who kept still. Lizards — many lizards — drape themselves over warm metal in the mornings, their coloring almost perfectly matched to rust and verdigris. A species of ground-nesting bird, plump and pale, uses the old hopper chutes as shelter. You hear a kind of breathing texture to the place — the in-and-out of small lives, wings shifting, claws on stone — but no single sound dominates. It is *mostly quiet* the way a library is mostly quiet: full of presence, restrained in volume.

A larger predator moves through. You don't see it often, but you see its signs: tracks in the dried seep-paths, a dragged line through the mineral dust. Something with weight. The prey animals know. Their stillness is strategic.

---

The heat does not relent. Even at altitude — and you are *high*, the horizon distant and shimmering, the air thin enough that you breathe consciously — the temperature holds. The terraces act as thermal mass, absorbing solar energy all day and radiating it all night. There is no cool hour. You sweat, and the sweat evaporates so fast you barely notice it until the salt crust on your skin. The sparse vegetation — scrubby, pale-leaved, waxy — grows in the cracks between terraces where moisture lingers an extra hour. It doesn't grow *tall*. It grows *wide*, spreading flat against the stone like it's hiding from the sky.

---

The established erosion is the oldest language here. The terraces the mountain wore itself into — the natural fracturing and weathering — are still visible beneath the human terracing. The mountain was already a staircase before anyone decided to climb it. The wind, which is constant and dry and comes from the northwest without variation, has rounded every edge that the miners left sharp. In another century it will finish the job. The ruins and the geology are converging. In places you genuinely cannot tell where the mountain ends and the industry begins. This is the *settled-form* quality: nothing here is new. Everything has found its state and is slowly, slowly becoming the same material.

---

The weirdness is subtle. It lives in the fact that the terraces seem to *organize* the heat. On certain levels the air shimmers more than others. The animals cluster on specific terraces at specific times, as if following a calendar you can't read. The water pulses seem rhythmic — not random, but *timed*, like the mountain is breathing on a schedule that predates anything human and will continue long after. Once, in the early morning, you hear a deep sound — not loud, not alarming — a resonance from somewhere far below, like a tuning fork the size of a building. It lasts four seconds and stops. The lizards do not react. They have heard it before.

---

You stand on the upper terrace and look out. The scale is regional: the terraces extend across three visible peaks, descending into a broad valley where the haze of heat makes the far wall shimmer and dissolve. The road you came up is one of dozens — faint traces, carved into the rock, switchbacking between levels. The whole mountainside is a town's footprint without the town. Foundations, terraces, channels, marks. The built environment is scattered enough that you keep thinking you've seen the last of it, and then another bracket, another etched symbol, another foundation appears.

Below, in the valley floor, there is a dark line — the only surface water of any size, perhaps a kilometer away. It glints once, then the heat-haze swallows it. You think about going down to it. The lizards on your terrace shift, almost imperceptibly, toward the morning sun. The mountain holds its heat. The birds say nothing.
