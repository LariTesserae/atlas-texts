# The Growing Tableland

You stand on a world that built itself from root and cambium and never stopped.

The ground beneath your feet is not stone. It is compressed heartwood — ancient, lacquered, sealed by decades of sap flow into something harder than oak but still faintly alive. You can feel it breathe: a slow, hot expansion and contraction, like the flank of a sleeping animal. This is the substrate of the entire plateau — a living floor, stretching in every direction, cracked here and there where newer growth has forced it apart. The cracks weep warm, clear fluid that smells faintly of resin and iron. Everything is wet. Not flooded — wet. The way skin is wet after a bath you haven't fully dried from. The air itself is a warm compress, dense with moisture, and it condenses on every surface in fat, slow droplets that slide rather than fall. You are always, everywhere, ankle-deep in the runoff of the atmosphere.

The plateau is high. You know this because the horizon drops away at enormous distances, and beyond the edge you can see other formations — pillars and shelves and cantilevered shelves of living material, rising from valleys you cannot see the bottoms of, their upper surfaces colonized by the same constructions that surround you here. The air is thinner up here but does not feel thin; it feels *pressurized* by heat and wetness, like the inside of a mouth.

And the constructions.

They are not buildings. They are not natural, either. They are *grown* — deliberately, it seems, but by no intelligence you can recognize. Everywhere across the plateau, spaced wide apart in the sparse arrangement that defines this landscape, there are structures of breathtaking complexity. Some are columnar: bundles of fibrous trunks, each as wide as a room, braided together into towers that rise forty or fifty meters and then — impossibly — spread into flat, parasol-like canopies of woven tissue, taut as drumheads. Rain collects on these canopies and channels down the braided columns in rivers, feeding the base, which roots deeper into the living floor. Some structures are horizontal: vast lattice-shells, like the ribcages of leviathans, woven from flexible stems and plated with overlapping scales of bark so thin they are translucent. Light filters through them the way it filters through stained glass, casting the interiors in amber and green.

Some structures are incomplete. Some are in the process of collapsing. Some are in the process of *both at once* — a tower whose upper third has torn open and is unfurling new lateral growth even as its base buckles, the dead material being reabsorbed, softened, reclaimed by the substrate, while new shoots erupt from the ruin with visible speed, reaching, curling, finding the light. You can *watch* the growth. It is fast enough to see, like watching ivy in a time-lapse, but jerkier, more arrhythmic. A branch will shoot out a meter in a minute, then stop for an hour, then continue. The whole plateau shudders with this energy. It is not stable. Nothing here has decided what it wants to be yet.

The sound.

It is the loudest quiet you have ever experienced. There are no animals. No birds, no insects, nothing that moves by choice through the air or across the ground. And yet the place is *deafening*. The structures groan — deep, wooden, tectonic sounds, like hull timber under stress. The weeping ground hisses where water meets hot surface. The wind — and there is always wind, hot and wet, pouring across the plateau — vibrates the great canopy-drums and the lattice-shells and the translucent bark-plates, and each structure resonates at its own frequency. The effect is a droning, multi-tonal chord that never resolves, that shifts as the wind shifts, that is sometimes joined by sharp cracking reports when a growth-spurt splits a seam or a new shoot forces its way through compressed substrate. Underneath it all, there is a sound like breathing — the collective expansion and contraction of the living ground, a bass rhythm, slow and enormous, as if the plateau itself has lungs.

The strangeness of the place is not in any single feature. It is in the *totality* — in the realization that there is no inorganic matter anywhere you can see. No stone. No metal. No dust that is not pollen, no sediment that is not decayed cellulose. Everything was grown. Everything is still growing. The architecture is deliberate but not designed — or designed by a logic so far from human that it might as well be geological. Why does that tower spiral while its neighbor branches? Why do some structures live for centuries, their bases now petrified and massive as ziggurats, while others bloom for a single season, fruit violently, and dissolve? There is no answer in the air. There is no one to ask. The animals were never here. The plants did everything themselves, and they did it in the dark, without witnesses, for longer than you can calculate.

The light is ordinary. That might be the strangest thing. A sun — a normal, yellow-white sun, unremarkable — hangs at an angle that suggests mid-afternoon, and it lights the plateau evenly, without drama. The wet surfaces gleam. The translucent bark glows from within. The shadows are soft-edged and green. It is the light of a place that has no reason to be ominous and isn't — only profoundly, irreducibly *other*.

At the edge of the plateau, where the living floor breaks apart into the first great fissures, you can look down into the deeper architecture. Below the rim, the structures grow denser and wilder, tangled together, competing for light, forming — at extreme depth, where your eye loses resolution — a solid mass of intergrown tissue. A jungle, but vertical. A cathedral built without worshipers by an organism that does not know it is building.

Here on the plateau, where the young sharp-featured formations still stand sparse and separate, the wind moves freely between them, and the heat rises from the ground in visible ripples, and the water condenses on everything, and the structures groan and split and reach and grow, and the whole living surface breathes its slow, massive breath, and there is no one — nothing — alive here but the architecture itself.

It is enough. It has always been enough.
