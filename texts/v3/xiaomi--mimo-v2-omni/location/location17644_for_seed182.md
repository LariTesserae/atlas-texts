It begins in darkness, not of night, but of mass. A buried space, a city of caverns pressing down under kilometers of stone. The air is thick, humid, almost chewable, held at a constant, feverish warmth that seeps from the rock itself. This is not the heat of the sun, but of the planet’s slow digestion.

Water is the lifeblood here, but it is stingy and strategic. It does not fall as rain; it condenses. It gathers in beads on the cooler, metallic surfaces of vast, forgotten machinery and drips with a deliberate, metronomic patience. It weeps from fissures in the vaulted ceilings, tracing pale mineral trails down fluted columns of ancient, rust-scarred iron. It pools in shallow, warm depressions on the floor, black and still, reflecting the dim glow of bioluminescence.

That glow is the primary light. It does not shine; it smolders. Fungi and lichens in impossible, lush profusion coat every surface that isn’t actively hot or moving. They form thick, velvety carpets in mossy greens and deep, bruised purples across the packed earth floors. They climb in feathery, fluorescent fronds up the sides of derelict refinery towers and condensation vats, their soft light revealing a density of construction that feels less like a city built and more like a city *grown* or *excreted*. There is no space left. Buildings—if they can be called that—are fused together, their original brick and concrete now entombed in a calcareous slime, in fungal buttresses, in the root-like tendrils of plants that have no need for the sun. Walkways are narrow, winding tunnels between bulbous, organic-feeling structures. Pipes, still dripping, run overhead like metallic vines, some pulsing faintly with the slow movement of viscous fluids.

The industry here is not of the forge, but of the filter, the pump, and the slow chemical vat. Great, sluggish pistons, the size of houses, cycle with a grinding sigh every few minutes, stirring the warm pools. Filtration membranes, vast and leathery, flutter in imperceptible breezes drawn through ventilation shafts that lead to the forgotten surface. The work is automated, silent, and eternal. It produces nothing for export. It maintains only the equilibrium: the warmth, the damp, the carefully calibrated nutrient mists that feed the clinging greenery.

Life is sparse, but attuned. The fauna is not animal in a familiar sense. Pale, eyeless things, like a cross between a salamander and a root, slide through the warm mud, their bodies translucent, showing the slow pulse of ingested minerals. In the air, only the occasional gnat-like swarm, drawn to a patch of brighter fungus, breaks the stillness. The silence is profound, broken only by the drip, the distant piston’s sigh, and a faint, pervasive hum—the frequency of the rock itself, of the buried machinery, a vibration felt more in the bones than in the ears.

This is a place of processes, not events. Change is measured in the slow build-up of mineral deposits, in the gradual encroachment of one fungal colony over another, in the centuries-long arc of a corroding girder finally yielding to the weight of its lush, green shroud. The strangeness is not in sudden anomalies, but in the very logic of the place: a sealed, hot, wet, dense, green, industrial crypt, humming its quiet song to itself in the dark, forever.
