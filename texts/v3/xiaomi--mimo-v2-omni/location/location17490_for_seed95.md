# The Lichen Tableland

You arrive at the edge of something that has no business being here, or perhaps has every right to be. A plateau so old it has nearly forgotten it was once mountains. The uplift happened so long ago that the forces of wind and water have had time to wear the high stone down to a vast, rolling flatness — not smooth, but *settled*, the way a body settles into stillness after a long illness. The rock is basalt, or what was basalt, exhumed and re-exposed and re-weathered until it has taken on a quality closer to soil than stone, though it will not grow anything.

## The Ground

The surface is dense with structure. This is not emptiness. Every square meter is occupied — packed — with stone formations that rise between knee-height and the top of a man's head. Columns and slabs and bulbous pillars, none of them intact, all of them eroded into forms that suggest organs, joints, vertebrae. The ancient lava cooled into hexagonal columns long ago, and those columns have since fractured and slumped and been sculpted further by wind carrying grit across this high place for longer than any forest has existed anywhere on the continent. The result is a terrain you must walk through rather than over. A city of stone stumps, close-set, with narrow winding passages between them. Dense. Occupied. Every volume filled.

The stone is warm underfoot even in the grey hours. The plateau holds heat like a kiln that was fired once and never fully cooled. In the crevices between formations, moisture collects — not streams, not puddles in any conventional sense, but a *welling*. Condensation from the clouds that snag on this high edge of the world, channeled by the geometry of the stone into the cracks and pores and honeycomb cavities of the eroded basalt. You press your hand into a shallow depression and it comes away wet. There is water *inside* the rock here, in networks of tiny channels too small to see, and it weeps out through fractures. In the low angles of morning light, the entire plateau gleams faintly, as if sweating. There are no rivers. There are no springs. But the stone is never dry. It is damp the way a living skin is damp.

## What Grows

Almost nothing. Patches of dark lichen — so dark they appear black — crust the north-facing sides of the taller columns. In the deeper crevices where moisture concentrates, there are filaments: a pale, branching growth, not quite moss, not quite fungus, that threads through the narrowest cracks and sometimes bridges the gap between two formations. It grows slowly. It grows so slowly that the oldest strands may be centuries old, and they have the appearance of something mineral rather than organic — translucent, stiff, faintly mineralized. The sparse vegetation here has made its peace with stone. It has become stone-adjacent. It does not photosynthesize with any enthusiasm. It is enough that it exists at all on this high, wind-scoured table where the air is thin and the rain comes only as mist and condensation and the occasional grey drizzle that lasts an hour and then withdraws.

No trees. No grasses. No flowers. The plateau is too high, too exposed, too ancient for anything so ambitious as a root system. What grows here grows in the seams.

## What Lives

And yet — the place is *full*.

They are small. Most of them. The overwhelming presence of fauna on this plateau is not the kind you expected. There are no herds. No flocks. Nothing large enough to see from a distance and recognize as animal. What there is, is *everywhere*, if you look.

Insects, yes — but not the biting kind, not the conspicuous kind. Dark-bodied creatures the length of a thumb, with an oily iridescence, that move in slow processions along the crevices. They follow the moisture. They feed on the lichen and the pale filament-growth, and they are so numerous that if you crouch and watch a single column base for a minute, you will see dozens of them, each on its own deliberate path, the columns between them threaded with their tiny traffic. They make no sound except a faint, collective rustle, like dry leaves stirred by a breath.

But they are only the most visible layer. Beneath the surface — literally within the porous, weathered basalt — there are things living in the wet crevices. Organisms you hesitate to call animals, some of them. Tiny translucent things, almost amoebic, that coat the damp interior walls of the stone cavities. You see them only when you break open a piece of the softer rock and find the inner surfaces glistening with a faintly luminescent film. Whether this is chemiluminescence or something else, you cannot tell. It is cold light. It is barely there. But it is *pervasive*. Every interior surface carries it. The plateau, broken open, glows faintly from within like a body with a low fever.

And there are spiders — or things that resemble spiders — with elongated legs adapted to the narrow crevices, who build no webs but hunt by touch along the stone channels. There are creatures that look like bits of rock until they move, flattened and angular, pressed into the eroded fluting of the columns like living inlays. Their camouflage is so thorough that you will not see them until one shifts position and the illusion breaks for just a moment before reassembling.

The biomass is immense and hidden. The plateau *teems*, but it teems in its cracks and pores and interior spaces. From above — if you could see it from above — it would look like bare stone. Standing on it, surrounded by it, you begin to feel the density of life the way you feel pressure changes in your ears. The air moves with tiny stirrings. The stone vibrates faintly with a thousand small economies running in parallel. Every surface is occupied. Every crevice is a habitat. The ecosystem has compressed itself into the geometry of eroded basalt, and it is *saturated*.

## The Quiet

And yet it is nearly silent. The insects do not buzz. The crevice-hunters make no sound at all. The wind, when it comes, is muffled by the density of the stone formations — they break it apart, scatter it, reduce it to a pressure against your skin rather than a noise in your ears. The only consistent sound is a faint mineral ticking — the sound of the rock itself contracting and expanding with the slow thermal cycle of the day, the ancient basalt adjusting itself by increments so small they manifest only as sound. Ticking. Clicking. A sound like a clock made of stone, measuring time in units too large to care about.

The quiet is not empty. It is occupied by patience.

## The Artifacts

They are scattered, and they are old. Older than any culture you know.

In a crevice between two columns, half-fused into the lichen crust, there is a shape that is almost certainly a bowl. It has been eroded to near-nothing — its rim is soft, its interior is filled with the dark lichen and the pale filaments — but the curvature is too regular, the wall thickness too consistent, to be natural. It has been here so long that the stone has accepted it, incorporated it, stopped distinguishing between the artifact and the landscape.

Elsewhere, a line of slightly raised stone across a narrow passage. A wall? A boundary? It is three courses high at most, barely knee-height, and so weathered that it merges with the natural fractured geometry of the basalt unless you look carefully and notice that the stones are *chosen* — fitted, deliberately, in a way the natural columns are not. Something was enclosed here, once. A small space. A shelter, perhaps, though the shelter would have been smaller than the crevice it was built within.

In a shallow depression — a natural pan in the rock where water collects — there is a darkness to the stone that is different from the surrounding basalt. A stain. An old stain, soaked deep, of a mineral or organic origin that the weather has not quite erased. Something was processed here. Crushed, ground, mixed with water. Pigment, maybe. Or food. The depression is the size of a paired set of hands, and the stain has a faintly ochre quality, almost warm against the grey-black of the basalt.

There are perhaps a dozen such artifacts across the area you can walk in a day. No more. Whatever culture left them was small, patient, and has been gone for an unimaginable time. The plateau has half-reclaimed them. In another millennium, the rest will dissolve.

## The Strangeness

You notice it gradually, the way you notice a change in someone's face when you've been looking at them a long time.

The formations are not random.

You had assumed they were — the usual product of columnar jointing and differential erosion, the basalt breaking along its natural cooling fractures, wind and water doing the rest. And that is what it is. That is mechanically, geologically, exactly what has happened here. But the *pattern* of erosion across this section of plateau is... suspicious. The columns cluster in ways that suggest intention without confirming it. There are clearings between them — too regular, perhaps, or perhaps not, because water would collect and flow and erode more in certain spots and less in others, and of course it would look ordered. Of course it would.

But when you stand in one of these clearings and look at the arrangement of stone around you, you feel — and this is not a rational observation, and you know it — that you are standing in a room. An open room with no roof. The columns are walls. The clearing is a floor. The space has a quality of *interiority* that you cannot account for with geology alone, though you try.

And the filaments — the pale, stiff, not-quite-fungal growth — they are denser here. In the clearings. They trace patterns on the floor of the stone, branching and re-branching, and if you follow the patterns with your eyes they seem to converge on points that align with specific columns, as if the growth is mapping something, or remembering something, or... communicating. The filaments are too rigid for that, though. They are too mineral. They are fossils of a process that may never have been alive.

At night — and you should not stay here at night, but if you did — the interior luminescence of the stone would become visible. The plateau would glow from within. Not brightly. Not enough to see by. But enough to know that the stone is occupied, that the wet cavities are full of cold light, that the entire high flat surface of this ancient, eroded, patient place is *watching* with a thousand dim, biological eyes that are not eyes, in a language that is not language, in a time-scale that makes your life look like a single heartbeat of a long and very quiet song.

## The Edges

The plateau does not end in cliffs. It ends in a long, gradual slope of broken stone — talus and scree that has been falling from the edge for so long that the slope itself has stabilized, grown its own crust of lichen and filaments and patient insects. You can walk down it easily. The air warms as you descend. The formations thin out. The dense fauna of the plateau loosens, disperses, becomes ordinary. The strangeness fades like a signal losing strength with distance.

By the time you reach the valley below, the rock is just rock. The insects are just insects. The quiet is just quiet.

But you will carry the impression of that high, old, occupied place — its density, its patience, its dim interior light — the way you carry the memory of a voice heard through a wall: clear enough to know someone was there, too muffled to know what they were saying.
