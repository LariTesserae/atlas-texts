The world is a drowned mountain range.

You are in the high places, but the high places are under a sea. There is no surface above, only a gradient of light—a diffuse, aquamarine twilight that filters down from an unreachable sky, turning to sapphire and then to indigo as your gaze follows the water downward. The concept of "elevation" here is a memory of stone. You drift between peaks of black basalt and glass-smooth obsidian, their summits truncated by the flood, their flanks sheared into sharp, young cliffs and jagged spires. The water itself is the architecture. It fills every canyon, every crevasse, pressing against the stone with a cold, permanent weight. It is not an ocean in the traditional sense; there are no tides, no currents born of wind. The water is a vast, stratified body, a liquid atmosphere with its own slow, geologic breathing.

The density is overwhelming. Not just of water, but of presence. The liquid medium is thick with life, but none of it is green. There are no plants, no kelp forests, no meadows of seagrass. The mineral world reigns, and life has risen to meet it in a fanatical, crystalline fashion. Drifting through the water column are swarms of **Lithozoa**—creatures that are less flesh and more living mineral lattice. Some resemble delicate, rotating fractals of silica, their bodies filtering the water for microscopic particles, catching the faint light on prismatic edges. Others are colonial, forming floating, sponge-like masses of opalescent tubes that hum with a low, resonant frequency. They are the fauna of the deep, and they are abundant, a glittering, drifting snow of life that turns every beam of light into a dazzling, chaotic pathway.

The terrain is a testament to youth and violence. The stone is not worn smooth by time; it is fractured, fresh. Sharp-edged shelves of andesite jut out from slopes, creating labyrinthine overhangs and alcoves. In these places, the sound is a constant, pressurized symphony. It is not the roar of a surface storm, but a deep, enveloping **drone**. It is the sound of the water itself, a billion tons of it, resonating against the stone. Layered over this are the clicks, chirps, and melodic pulses of the Lithozoa—a dense, overlapping chatter that serves no purpose you can discern, other than to *be*. And beneath it all, a rhythmic, tectonic **groan**, as the young mountains settle, millimeter by millimeter, under the colossal weight of the flood. It is loud. The pressure is a physical presence on your skin, a reminder that you are in a world that is not meant for air-breathers.

The light, at this depth, is normal only in its consistency. It has no source you can see. It emanates from the water itself, a bioluminescent glow from the countless Lithozoa, combined with a faint, ambient luminescence from the minerals leaching from the rock. It paints everything in shades of cobalt, teal, and phosphorescent silver. Shadows are soft, indistinct. The world glows from within.

There is no technology here, no artifacts, yet there is a sense of immense, slow **industry**. It is the industry of geology and biology intertwined. You see vast, submerged terraces where the Lithozoa have, over centuries, accreted themselves onto the rock, building lattices and filigrees of living mineral that resemble frozen, industrial cities. They are not built, but grown, a form of autocatalytic construction. In places, these growths form great, chimney-like structures that vent warmer, mineral-rich water from the planet's interior. Around these vents, the density of life explodes—swirling clouds of spiraling crystal-organisms, predatory shards that dart and flash, and vast, slow-moving grazer-colonies that look like floating cathedrals of bone. This is the engine: geothermal energy and chemical gradients, driving an ecosystem of bizarre, mineralogical life.

The "weirdness" is in the rules. Here, biology is a subset of geology. Life forms grow by incorporating the minerals of the drowned mountains directly into their bodies. Reproduction is often a process of fragmentation and recrystallization. Sound is not just communication but a tool for shaping growth; the resonant frequencies of the larger structures can influence how the Lithozoa colonies accrete upon them. The dynamic is slow, patient. A new spire might take a thousand years to form. A colony might drift for a century before finding a suitable ledge to anchor and begin its construction. Change is measured in the gradual filling of a canyon with crystalline life, or the rare, thunderous collapse of an unstable cliff face, which sends shockwaves through the water and a rain of stone and startled life downward into the deeper dark.

You are a bubble of warmth and air in a cool, high, dense, and utterly alien realm. A realm where the mountains drowned, and the life that followed learned to wear their bones as its own. It is a silent war of accretion, a loud symphony of pressure and pulse, played out in the eternal, glowing twilight of the high deep.
