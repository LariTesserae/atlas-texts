The landscape is a vast, high-altitude plateau of powdered mineral—a mixture ofite dust,ite salt, and fragments ofite crystal that stretches to every horizon under a sky of pale, unchanging blue. The air is cool and thin, carrying no moisture, and the light is flat, shadowless, as if the sun hangs in permanent, hazy noon. There is no wind. The silence is absolute, a pressure against the ears, broken only by the faintest, intermittent *tink* of crystalline structures shifting under their own weight, or the dry rustle of countless small bodies moving across the dust.

The terrain is one of profound, ancient erosion. What might have once been towering mountain ranges have been worn down to a dense forest of spires, pinnacles, and mesas—geological remnants packed so closely together that the spaces between them are narrow canyons and labyrinthine passages. The stone is striated in faded bands of ochre, grey, and bleached white, smoothed by time into organic, melting forms. The ground is not flat but sloped in gentle, immense gradients, as if the plateau itself is the inside of a shallow, eroded bowl.

This is a world of overwhelming, silent life. The fauna is not merely abundant but *ubiquitous*. Millions of small, chitinous creatures—somewhere between a beetle and a crustacean—blanket the ground and crawl in slow, deliberate columns up the sides of the spires. They are the color of dust, nearly invisible until they move. Their bodies are adapted to the utter dryness, drawing trace condensation from the cool air through minute, capillary-like structures. They do not chirp or buzz. Their communication, if any, is through subtle shifts in posture and the collective sound of their passage: a whispering, granular flow, like sand slipping through an hourglass. Occasionally, a larger, more solitary creature—a six-legged, armadillo-like thing with a shell of fused crystal—will be seen wedged in a crevice, perfectly still, as if hibernating for millennia.

Scattered amongst the spires are the faint marks of a primitive presence. Not cities, but signs: a section of spire wall smoothed unnaturally flat on one side; a circle of unworked stones, half-buried; a single, crudely flaked blade of obsidian lying in the open, its edges still sharp. There is no charcoal, no bone, no pottery. The technology is so simple it feels more like intention than habitation—a touch upon the land, not a conquest.

And then, the weirdness.

The erosion is too perfect. The spires, when looked at directly, seem solid, but in the periphery of vision, they appear to twist, to elongate, their shadows behaving incorrectly. The dense columns of insectoids occasionally freeze in unison, their myriad bodies forming a geometric pattern on the ground that resembles a script, before dissolving back into chaotic motion. The *tink* of shifting crystal seems to come from inside your own skull. The normal light has no source. The silence feels *conscious*.

Most profoundly, the place does not change. The dust does not settle further. The spires do not erode another micron. The insectoids are born and die, but their total number, their patterns, their behavior, remain a constant. It is an equilibrium achieved not through balance, but through suspension. Time here is not a river; it is a frozen lake. The reality of the place—its geology, its life, its scant memories of builders—has settled into a final, stable form. It is the end of a process, a world that has finished becoming itself and now simply *is*, a vast, silent, teeming, and subtly impossible monument to a state of permanent conclusion.
