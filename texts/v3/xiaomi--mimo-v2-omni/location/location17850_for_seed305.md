# The Keening Plateaus

The light is the first thing you cannot bear.

It comes from everywhere and nowhere — not a sun, not a sky-source, but a pervasive white incandescence that seems to emanate from the air itself, from the molecular structure of the atmosphere at this altitude. The plateaus exist so high that whatever protects the world below has thinned to translucence, and the light of adjacent stars (there are several, in varying stages of furious fusion) does not so much *fall* on this landscape as *saturate* it. Every surface is bleached at its edges. Shadows are not dark here — they are merely *less white*, pale blue indentations in a world that has nearly forgotten contrast.

And yet things grow.

---

## The Living Rock

The plateaus are young — geologically speaking, they are still *becoming*. The stone is sharp, barely weathered, its fracture-planes clean as broken glass. Basalt and something stranger: a mineral with the lattice-structure of living tissue, translucent and faintly warm to the touch, threaded with capillary channels that carry water in slow, rhythmic pulses. The locals — there are no locals, nothing intelligent, no structures, no artifacts — but the *inhabitants* of this place seem to include the geology itself. The rock breathes. Not metaphorically. Every few minutes, the stone surfaces contract almost imperceptibly, drawing moisture up from below, and then relax. Condensation beads on the dark glass-faces of the outcrops, trembles, and is reabsorbed. The whole plateau is a lung.

The water here is intimate. There are no rivers, no lakes, no open bodies of water in the conventional sense. Instead: moisture *lives* in the stone, in the root-mats, in the air itself, which is thick with a near-fog that never quite resolves into droplets. It clings. It seeps. A stream here is just a seam in the rock where the pulsing runs faster, where a thin film of water slides perpetually downhill in a sheet no thicker than a coin. Stand on one and you feel the vibration of it — a subsonic hum that travels up through your legs.

---

## The Thickets

Lush is too gentle a word.

The vegetation has no reason to be this dense, this *desperate*, at this altitude, in this cold, but it is. Every surface that is not sheer or actively shearing is occupied. The plants here are not green — the light won't allow it, is too much, too white. They are *dark*: near-black, deep violet, a bruise-purple that absorbs what it can from the overbright world and converts it into slow, stubborn growth. They grow in mats, in curtains, in hanging gardens that drape the cliff-faces like pelts. The dominant forms are something between moss and fungal lattice — a network-organism that extends rhizomes through the stone itself, cracking it from within, accelerating the youth of the terrain into a feedback loop: the plants break the rock, the fresh rock provides new surface, the plants colonize it. The whole plateau is slowly, silently *unraveling* from the top down.

Among the dark mats: flowers. Or structures that serve the function of flowers, if not the form. Bulbous, pale, nearly luminous against the dark foliage — they open in slow spirals over the course of days and emit a scent that is less a fragrance than a *pressure*, a weight in the nasal passages, something between ozone and the mineral smell of wet slate. They pulse faintly. They are warm. In the perpetual cold, they are the only things that feel like they have blood.

The density is oppressive. There is no open ground. Every step lands on living surface — springy, damp, slightly giving. The rhizome-mats are ankle-deep at minimum, waist-deep in the gullies between rock-shelves. Walking here is not traversal but *wading through life*. The vegetation clings to boots, to skin, to anything still. Leave a hand on a mat for a minute and it will begin to be *accepted* by the surface, the rhizomes testing, tasting, reaching.

---

## The Fauna

There is no quiet way to describe this.

The plateaus are *inhabited* — not by a few adapted species in sparse numbers, but by a volume of living creatures that defies the apparent poverty of the environment. They are everywhere. In the mats, beneath the stone, in the air, on every surface. Most are small — insects, arthropods, things with too many legs or not enough — but they exist in numbers that merge into a collective *presence*, a seething texture over every landscape feature. The dark mats of vegetation are not solid; they are *shuddering*, constantly moving, every square meter hosting thousands of small bodies going about the incomprehensible business of their lives.

Larger things, too. Creatures that look like slugs the size of dogs, pale and translucent, dragging themselves across the rock with muscular contractions that are audible — a wet, rhythmic *shlk-shlk-shlk* that combines with a thousand others to form a base-note of sound beneath everything. Birds, or things that fulfill the role of birds: winged, yes, but with too many wings, or with wings that are also legs, or with wings that don't fold but remain perpetually extended, catching the updrafts from the plateau's thermals. They do not sing. They *click* — a rapid, percussive chatter that rises and falls in waves across the plateau, passing from individual to individual in chains of call-and-response that can take hours to traverse a single valley.

The overwhelming quality of the fauna is not aggression but *indifference*. Nothing here is interested in you, because everything here is already engaged in a life so dense, so *full*, that there is no room for curiosity. The ecosystem does not have space for an outsider. You are simply *additional surface*, and the small things will colonize you in time.

---

## The Sound

The plateau is loud.

Not with any single noise, but with the cumulative weight of every living thing, every pulsing stone, every sheet of water-film vibrating in its channels. It is a roar without source — a white-noise of *existence* that rises from every direction and has no center, no origin point you could face. The clicking of the winged things. The shlk-shlk of the slug-things. The deep, nearly-subsonic hum of the stone-lungs breathing. The crack of sharp rock-faces expanding and contracting with temperature. And beneath it all: a sound like a turbine, like a vast engine turning over slowly in the earth beneath your feet.

This is the industrial element, and it is not a construction.

---

## The Engines

They are geological.

Beneath the plateaus, at depths that range from a few meters to several hundred, there are structures — natural structures, but *structured* nonetheless — where the rock has formed into regular, repeating geometries. Cylinders. Pistons. Chambers. The young stone, still finding its form, has crystallized in places along patterns that are disturbingly mechanical: rotors of mineral, stators of compressed basalt, channels through which heated water is driven at pressure by the geothermal gradients below. These are not machines. No one built them. But they *function* — turning, pushing, driving. They generate the subsonic hum. They push water upward through the plateau's stone-lungs. They create the thermals that the winged things ride. The entire landscape is, in a sense, an engine — a slow, vast, geologically young mechanism that is still assembling itself, still finding its tolerances, still grinding into operational form over millennia.

You can hear it. In quiet moments — if such moments exist here — you can hear the deep *thrum* of a chamber pressurizing somewhere below, the *crack* of a mineral rotor turning against its bearing, the sustained *whine* of water forced through a narrowing in the rock. It is not human. It is not *for* anyone. It is simply what this young, high, bright, cold, densely living world *does*: it turns, it breathes, it processes, it *runs*. An engine without an operator. A machine that is also an ecosystem. The fauna has adapted to its rhythms. The vegetation grows along its thermal vents. The water moves through it like coolant.

---

## The Cold

Do not forget the cold.

It is the first and last truth of this place. The light is blinding but it warms nothing. The stone-lungs are cool to the touch even at their warmest. The air is thin and cuts. The fog-moisture does not freeze because something in the mineral composition of the rock suppresses crystallization — but it *should* freeze, by any reasonable thermodynamic standard, and the fact that it doesn't is part of the strangeness here, part of the sense that the rules are *almost* right but not quite. Your breath comes visible. Your fingers go numb. The dark vegetation underfoot is body-temperature — it generates a small, desperate warmth from its own metabolic processes — but it is not enough. You are always, here, losing heat to the bright, white, loud, living plateau.

---

## The Slow Turn

Nothing here moves fast. The engines grind in cycles measured in hours. The vegetation grows in increments measured in days. The slug-things cross a rock-face in a week. Even the clicking of the winged-things, rapid as it is in the moment, forms patterns that shift over the course of seasons. The plateau is still forming, still rising, and the sharp-featured stone at its edges is new enough that it still rings when struck — but this newness is *geological* newness, measured in centuries. The erosion is minimal, the landscape barely committed to its own shape. Give it time. Give it millennia. The engines will find their groove. The stone will smooth. The ecosystem will settle into its own strange equilibrium.

For now, it is young, and loud, and bright, and full, and cold, and turning slowly beneath your feet like a world-sized machine that has not yet learned what it is for.
