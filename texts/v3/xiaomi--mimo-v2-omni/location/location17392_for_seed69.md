The first thing is the dark. Not the dark of night, but a permanent, starless, lidless dark—the absolute absence of any sun or moon. And yet, the place is not invisible. It is revealed in other ways.

You stand on a high plateau, but “plateau” is too small a word. It is a continental shelf, a vast and elevated plain that stretches to a horizon that doesn’t exist in the dark. The ground beneath your feet is not soil, but a spongy, yielding mat of pale, phosphorescent moss and deep-purple fungal lattices, saturated and weeping. The air is thick, warm, and heavy with moisture; it condenses on every surface in fat, glowing droplets that trace slow, luminous trails down rock and stem before being reabsorbed. This is a world of perpetual post-rain, of weeping stone and breathing earth.

The terrain is ancient, settled into its own profound rhythm. Great, weathered mesas of basalt and quartz rise from the moss-sea, their slopes carved not by wind, but by millennia of water seepage and the slow, relentless growth of root systems. These formations are the bones of the land, smoothed and stained by time. Between them, shallow valleys hold shallow, warm lakes that are more soup than water, thick with algae and the glittering shed carapaces of a trillion insects. The water table is barely an inch below the surface everywhere; to dig is to find a spring.

And here, life is dense, abundant, and utterly strange. Forests of towering, bone-white fungi, some reaching the height of redwoods, crowd the mesa bases. Their caps are leathery and veined with channels that carry warm, mineral-rich water upward, which then weeps from their gills in a constant, glowing drizzle. Vine-like plants with translucent, fluid-filled stems spiral up the fungi, their only light-source being the chemical reactions within their own sap. This is a lushness of a different kind—not of green leaves, but of pulsating, veined, and dripping forms.

The fauna has adapted to the total dark and the constant, saturated warmth. Things click and chitter in the fungal forests—armored, multi-legged grazers that scrape phosphorescent lichen from rock, their shells rasping like sandpaper. In the weeping lakes, blind, eel-like creatures with sensitive lateral lines and skin that carries faint, bioluminescent patterns hunt by vibration and electric sense. The air is filled with the loud, layered sounds of their existence: the deep, subsonic hum of the fungus-growth itself, the wet *shluck* of expanding root networks, the chorus of clicks from the grazers, and the sudden, tearing shriek of a predator—a sleek, six-limbed hunter with a mouth that glows faintly blue from symbiotic bacteria, its only true light in the world.

And then there is the Industry. Not built upon the landscape, but grown from it. Scattered across the plateaus and nestled into mesa-sides are structures that are half-factory, half-organism. Great, rusted iron pillars, sweating condensation, straddle fungal groves. They are not ruins; they are active. Slow, piston-driven pumps draw the warm, mineral-soup from the earth and channel it through networks of pitted, verdigris-coated pipes. These pipes throb with a slow, mechanical heartbeat. They feed into vast, settling tanks where the water is agitated by geared, paddle-wheel mechanisms, their grinding a constant, low-frequency drone that blends with the ecological sounds. This is not clean, efficient industry. It is loud, messy, and utterly integrated. The factories leak, they weep, they are overgrown with the very phosphorescent moss they seem to be processing. They are weathered into the landscape, as much a part of the erosion and balance as the mesas themselves. They are not building a world; they are metabolizing it.

The rules of this place are clear: nothing is wasted. Energy comes from chemical reactions, from geothermal warmth, from the slow decay and recycling of everything. Light is a tool, a byproduct, a biological lure, but never a gift from above. The vastness is filled with the dense, active processes of life and machine, each exploiting the wet, warm, dark conditions in a perpetual, loud, and strange dance. You feel the scale in the echo of a distant pump, in the way the sound of the grazers becomes a continent-wide static, in the realization that the weeping moss-mat extends for thousands of miles in every direction, under the same eternal, starless dark. It is a world that has turned inward, and found in its own damp, roaring, phosphorescent depths a universe of being.
