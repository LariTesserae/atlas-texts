# The Root Halls

You are below.

The cold is absolute — not the cutting cold of a winter wind but the settled, permanent cold of stone that has never known the sun. It lives in every surface. It presses against the skin of anything that enters and does not relent. Your breath does not so much fog as crystallize, becoming a faint rattle of ice motes that drift downward in the still air. But the stillness is a lie. The sound fills everything.

**The sound.** It is not a roar. It is not a crash. It is the combined voice of a billion billion micro-fractures — ice splitting in hairline threads through living tissue, water expanding in cellulose channels, fibers contracting as temperature drops another fraction of a degree. It is a sound like glass being drawn on by a thousand bowstrings at once. A high, crystalline, shimmering constant. It has no rhythm. It has no break. It fills the cavern the way water fills a vessel, pressing into every hollow, and it does not stop. Standing in it, you feel your thoughts go thin. Speech is impossible. Thought itself becomes an act of effort. The cracking hum of frozen wood is the baseline of existence here, and everything else — every footfall, every breath, every distant mechanical thud — must push against it to be known.

This is the Root Halls. They extend for kilometers in every direction, a buried landscape the size of a city's worth of valley, open and vaulted and impossibly spacious for a place with no sky. The ceiling — if that word applies — is forty, sixty, sometimes a hundred meters overhead, and it is made of roots. Not stone. Not earth. Roots. Dense, interlocking, frozen solid, grown so thickly over centuries that they have become structural, architectural. They branch and rebranch and braid together until the canopy overhead looks like the cross-section of a mind, every strand purposeful, every fork a decision made in slow time by something without a brain. Between the roots, darkness. Below them, everything.

The floor is a mix of bare stone — pale, sharp-edged, recently exposed — and the deeper roots of the great plants above, which plunge down through fractures in the rock and grip everything. The ground is uneven. You step over a root the width of a doorway. You step around a fissure where stone has cracked and a younger root has wedged itself in, still flexing, still growing despite the cold. The rock here is young. The caverns opened not long ago in geological terms — the features are angular, not smoothed by time. Ceiling collapses are a fact of life; piles of rubble sit where sections of root-mat gave way and crashed down, some of them still alive, still growing from where they landed, the broken ends of their root-masses sending out exploratory tendrils across the stone.

**The plants.** There is nothing else. No soil in the traditional sense — just accumulated root-fiber, dead and living both, compressed into a dense peat that is itself part of the living network. The plants here have no names you would recognize, because they are not separate organisms. They are one thing. A single interconnected growth that has adapted to this lightless cold with the patience of a glacier. The great roots overhead are its skeleton. The fine white hairs that coat every surface — visible only when you press close, and then only in the dim glow of whatever light-filtering or phosphorescence makes this place navigable at all — are its sensory organs, tasting the air for moisture, for minerals, for the faint chemical signatures of the machines. The whole cavern system is the body of one organism, or perhaps a colonial super-organism, and it is alive and it is growing and it does not care that you are here.

In the open stretches — the plazas, the atriums, the wide spaces where the root-ceiling vaults up to its highest — the growth thins. This is what passes for sparse in a place that is entirely alive. Here the root-mat on the ground is only ankle-deep, and you can see the bare stone beneath, slick with condensation. The moisture pools in depressions and collects along the edges of the larger roots, forming thin films, tiny channels, pockets no deeper than a hand where water gathers and freezes and thaws and freezes again in some subtle cycle driven by the machines' waste heat. You can follow these threads of water. They lead somewhere. They always lead to the machines.

**The machines.** They are old. Not ancient — a century, perhaps two — but old enough that the roots have found them. The largest is a compressor station, or what remains of one: a housing the size of a small building, its outer shell of riveted steel still mostly intact, its internal mechanisms seized and frozen in mid-cycle. The roots have grown around it, under it, through it. They have threaded through every opening, filled every cavity, and in some cases replaced the internal components entirely — a piston housing packed with root-fiber instead of metal, valves made of hardened plant-stem fitted so precisely into the original fittings that you cannot tell where engineering ends and biology begins. The machines are not abandoned. They have been incorporated. And some of them still run.

Not well. Not efficiently. But the deep rhythmic pounding — a sound distinct from the crystalline cracking, a low percussive thud that you feel in your sternum — comes from these half-absorbed engines. They cycle slowly. A stroke every few minutes, sometimes less. Something is still powering them: geothermal heat, perhaps, or the metabolic waste of the root-network itself, channeled through pathways that were once copper piping and are now conduits of living tissue. The machines pump. They push water through the root-system, or air, or something that has the consistency of sap. They are organs now, and they are failing, and the failing is so gradual — a piston stroke lost per decade — that the organism compensates and adapts and the cycle continues.

You pass a smaller installation: a row of electrical transformers, maybe eight of them, set on concrete pads that jut from the root-mat like islands. The transformers are dark. Their cooling fins are clogged with root-fiber. One of them has been split open — not by damage but by growth, the casing bowed outward and eventually cracked by the pressure of the plant-mass that filled its interior. Spilling out of it is not wire or coil but a dense, pale growth, almost white, that spreads across the ground for several meters in every direction before thinning into the general root-mat. It is wet. It is faintly warm to the touch — the only warmth in this place that does not come from your own body.

**The light.** There is almost none. What exists is ambient, sourceless, a faint grey-blue luminescence that might be bioluminescent activity in the root-hairs, or might be phosphorescence in the mineral-rich stone, or might be something the industrial installations are producing in their slow death — a residual glow in doped glass, a chemical seepage. It is enough to see by, barely, if your eyes have adjusted. Shapes are clear. Color is not. Everything exists in gradients of grey and black and the faintest blue, like a photograph of a place that was never meant to be photographed.

**What lives here.** Almost nothing, and what does live here does so quietly. You will not see fauna unless you are very patient and very still. There are insects — small, pale, moving through the root-lattice with the surety of something born to it. They are the color of ice. There are things that might be spiders but are not, their bodies translucent, their legs long and thin and indistinguishable from the root-hairs they walk among. Occasionally, in the deeper pools — those pockets of water that collect in the root-mat's hollows and do not freeze because of some thermal influence — you might see movement. Something eel-like, perhaps, or a larval form of something that has no adult stage in this cold. The fauna is rare. It does not call attention to itself. **The remains.** Scattered. Sparse. You find a boot, leather, stiff with cold, half-buried in the root-mat. You find a desk — metal, industrial, the kind bolted to a factory floor — standing alone in an open stretch of cavern, its surface coated in a thin rime of ice, a ledger beneath it still legible if you hold it close and squint. You find markings on the rock: survey lines, numbered tags, the kind of notations mining engineers leave behind. They are crisp. The young stone holds them well. But the root-mat is growing toward them, and within decades it will reach them and cover them, and within centuries it will have consumed the stone itself, and the only record that anyone was ever here will be the machines, already half-plant, already dreaming their slow mechanical dreams in the dark.

The whole place is patient. The cold is patient. The roots are patient. The machines are patient in the way that a dying thing is patient — not by choice but by incapacity. Everything here moves at the speed of growth, which is to say: you will not see anything happen. But things are happening. The roots are advancing. The machines are declining. The water is moving. The sound — that endless, shimmering, impossible sound of ice cracking in living wood — continues, and it will continue, and it does not need you to hear it.

It is the sound of a world that does not know you are in it.
