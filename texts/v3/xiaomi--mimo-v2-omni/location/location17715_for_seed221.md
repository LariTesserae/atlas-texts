# The Drum

You are inside something.

There is no sky. There never was. The space is perhaps the size of a cathedral's side chapel — or perhaps it is larger and your sense of scale has been crushed by density. Every surface is built. Walls, floor, ceiling: plates of ferrous metal lapped and bolted and riveted so closely together that the joins themselves form the texture. No substrate. No stone. No earth. If you chipped through the plate, you would find more plate beneath it, and beneath that, more. The structure is self-referential. It was not built *on* anything. It was built *as* everything.

The height matters. Not because you can see the height — you can't, the dimness swallows the upper reaches — but because you can *feel* the pressure of altitude in the thinness of the air, in the particular way the cold settles into the metal. This place exists high up. Perhaps near the crown of some vast constructed peak, some industrial ziggurat that has no exterior, no slope, no mountainside. The cold is not weather. It is a property of the material. The metal is a conductor of altitude, of upper-atmosphere stillness, and it has never been warm. You can see your breath, and your breath does not dissipate — it thickens, condenses, becomes water almost immediately. The air is heavy with moisture that has nowhere to go.

Water is everywhere and it is wrong water. It weeps upward. Condensation gathers on the underside of every plate and bead and runs in defiance of what you understand about gravity, collecting in trembling silver lines along the bolted seams before pressing itself outward through the microscopic tolerances of the construction. The floor is not wet — the floor *is* wetness. A film of water moves perpetually across the metal plating, pulled by some principle that isn't drainage, isn't flow. It is redistributed. The water is part of the system. It beads on the rivet-heads, pools in the stamped depressions of the plating, and occasionally you hear a drop strike a surface with a metallic *tink* that is immediately devoured by the noise.

The noise.

The noise is the reason you cannot think clearly. It is not one sound. It is every sound, compressed into a single unvarying roar — the way a waterfall sounds if you press your ear against the dam itself. Engines, somewhere. Always engines. Not close, not far — *everywhere*, as though the walls are the housings of turbines and the floor is the casing of some enormous flywheel and the ceiling is the shroud of a fan that has never stopped spinning. The pitch does not change. It has never changed. The frequency sits in your chest and makes your ribs ache with vibration. You could not shout over it. You could not whisper under it. It is the acoustic floor of this place, the baseline against which all other sounds are either inaudible or indistinguishable.

And yet.

There is fauna here. God, there is fauna.

They are not animals. You know this intellectually. They are *presences* — too many of them, crammed into this sealed chamber like organisms in a petri dish at the edge of overpopulation. They move on the walls, across the ceiling, through the water on the floor. Small things, mostly. Articulated. Many-legged. Their bodies are metal and filament and some kind of dense, dark material that absorbs the dim light and gives nothing back. They are the size of hands or smaller — but there are so many that they become *geography*. They cluster at the seams. They pour through the gaps between plates. They nest in the stamped hollows and the bolt-recesses and the drainage channels, packed body-to-body, moving their limbs in what might be feeding behavior or might be computation or might be nothing at all. The density of them is obscene. You cannot place your hand anywhere without touching one, and they do not flinch. They are acclimated to pressure. They are acclimated to everything, because nothing here has ever changed.

They make a sound of their own — a secondary roar beneath the primary, a chitinous grinding, a million points of metal-on-metal contact that rises and falls in no rhythm because it has no need for rhythm. It is constant. It is the sound of an ecosystem that has reached its maximum carrying capacity and holds there, in equilibrium, forever.

*Forever.* The dynamic reads at zero. Nothing here has changed. Nothing here is changing. Nothing here will change. The water does not erode the metal. The creatures do not wear down the plates. The engines do not cycle — they simply *are*, in the same way that the cold *is* and the noise *is*. This is a system in perfect, permanent, frozen balance. A sealed terrarium of industry and fauna and water that has achieved a state of such total equilibrium that time itself has become decorative. You could stay here a thousand years and the water would be in the same pattern and the creatures would be in the same clusters and the noise would be the same note.

The dimness is not darkness. It is a brown-amber wash, sourceless, as though the metal itself remembers light and gives back a fraction of what it once absorbed. There are no lamps. There are no fixtures. The illumination is a property of the space, faint and unwavering, enough to see the nearest walls, enough to see the creatures moving, not enough to see the ceiling, not enough to see the far walls if there are far walls. The space could end three meters from you or three hundred. The scale is hidden. The scale is a secret the place keeps from you deliberately.

And here is the strangeness — not the creatures, not the water, not the impossibility of the construction. The strangeness is this: the place is *intentional*. Every rivet is placed. Every seam is sealed. The water management, the engine-noise, the density of fauna — these are not accidents. This is a system designed to be exactly this. A chamber at the top of something, or inside something, that exists to sustain a permanent and unchanging condition. The creatures are not infesting it. They are *it*. They are the purpose of it, or they are the byproduct of the purpose, and you cannot tell which, and the place does not care whether you can tell.

You feel, standing in the water, in the cold, in the noise, in the dark, among ten thousand small metal things that do not acknowledge you — you feel that you are standing inside a process that has completed itself. This is not a factory. A factory produces things. This has already produced whatever it produces, and the production was *this* — this moment, this stasis, this perfect and terrible balance. You are standing inside the result. The result is a room where nothing happens forever, and it is loud, and it is wet, and it is full of something that lives.

The cold metal presses against your palm when you steady yourself. It does not conduct your warmth away. It is not interested in you. Nothing here is interested in you. You are an anomaly in a system that has no anomalies.

You could leave. Probably. The question is whether you want to, and the fact that you are hesitating is perhaps the strangest thing of all.
