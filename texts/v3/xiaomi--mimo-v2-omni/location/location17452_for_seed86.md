It is a country of porous stone.

You stand—or rather, you exist—in a shallow, wind-scoured depression, a vast basin whose far rim is a hazy line against a perpetually dim sky. The light is not night, but the perpetual twilight of a sun veiled by high, thin cloud and a atmosphere heavy with suspended moisture. It seeps from the rock itself, this damp. The ground underfoot is not soil, but a gritty, calcium-rich grit, the powdered remains of the plateau that once was. It is cool to the touch, a deep, mineral coolness that never leaves.

The erosion here is young, sharp. The landscape is a network of shallow, angular channels—arroyos not carved by water, but by some slower, more pervasive process of dissolution and collapse. Their edges are crisp, their floors littered with fresh, angular scree. From the porous stone, water weeps. Not in streams, but in a constant, silent condensation. It beads on every shadowed surface, collects in shallow, glassy pans in the hollows, and follows the intricate, fractal cracks downward, darkening the stone in weeping trails. The air tastes of wet limestone and ozone.

And it is loud. Not with wind or weather, for the air is still, but with a dense, layered chorus of life. This is the overwhelming fact of the place: fauna. It is a landscape of creatures. From the gravelly grit spring spindly, pale growths—not plants, but calcified colonies of some ancient, symbiotic organism. They are sparse, each standing a few feet from the other, leafless, resembling more the bleached antlers of some buried beast. They are the anchors.

Upon and around them, life seethes. Chitinous insects, the size of a human hand, with iridescent carapaces, click and scrape across the stone, their mandibles working ceaselessly on the mineral film. Flocks of eyeless, bat-like creatures with translucent wings cling to the undersides of the slight overhangs, emitting a constant, high-pitched echolocation chatter that merges into a shimmering wall of sound. In the weeping pans, blind, eel-like things, no longer than a finger, wriggle through the shallow water, feeding on the microbial slimes. The air is thick with the drone of flying beetles and the skittering of countless unseen legs.

Yet, for all this teeming, there is no vegetation as we know it, no hunt, no dramatic predation. It is an ecosystem of scavengers and filterers, of grazers upon stone and mist. The cycle is slow, closed, and utterly self-contained. The overwhelming impression is not of violence, but of a relentless, murmuring industry. The world is a workshop, and every creature is a laborer.

There is no mark of hand or tool. No shard of flint shows a knapped edge. The primitive tech suggested by the parameters is not an artifact of intelligence, but of biology: the shaping of stone by the ceaseless, abrasive motion of a million chitin limbs; the polishing of pools by the passage of soft-bodied dwellers; the deep, resonant chambers in the rock not dug, but grown, by colonies of the coraline growths that vibrate with the collective sound of the place.

It is a world in perfect, frozen equilibrium. The erosion happens, but at a pace imperceptible to a fleeting visitor. The water weeps, but the reservoir in the stone seems infinite. The fauna lives and dies, but the numbers never change, the balance never tips. The sound never rises or falls. The dim light never brightens nor darkens. It is a single, sustained moment, stretched across an eternity. You are not in a place that *is*, but in a place that *persists*. To be here is to feel the profound, indifferent weight of a system that needs nothing from you, that does not see you, and that will continue its soft, loud, damp, and sharp-featured existence long after you are powdered grit yourself.
