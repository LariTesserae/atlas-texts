The place is a basin, a vast, shallow depression that holds itself like a cupped palm catching something precious and foul. It is a city, or the memory of one. The heat is the first fact of it—a dense, wet heat that clings to the skin and sits heavy in the lungs, a perpetual post-rain steam rising not from soil, but from the stone and slag that compose everything. The ground underfoot is never dry. It sweats a thin, mineral-slick condensation, gathering in rivulets that trace ancient, eroded paths between structures, feeding a network of sluggish, warm canals. The rocks themselves weep, their surfaces glistening under a sky that is perpetually dim, a bruised twilight filtered through a high, dirty haze of industrial particulate and suspended moisture.

There are no plants. Not a single blade of moss or filament of lichen. The world is rendered in mineral and metal: the dark, vitreous sheen of basalt, the rust-orange of oxidizing iron, the chalky white of deposited salts lining the waterways. The architecture is brutal and settled, grown from this material. Factories and processing plants, their forms weathered by centuries of acidic rain and heat, rise like geological features. Their walls are thick with corrosion and condensation, their vast, empty windows staring out over the basin like dead eyes. There are no leaves to rustle; the silence is profound, broken only by the sounds of industry and its ghosts.

The emptiness is palpable. You can walk for minutes between the great, hulking structures, crossing open plazas of cracked pavement and slag, the space between them so vast it swallows sound. This is a low-density urbanity, a city spaced for giants or machines, where the buildings are monuments to a process, not a population. Yet, the place is not lifeless. It is overwhelmingly inhabited.

They move in the steam and the dim light, the fauna. Not animals as once known, but things of adapted biology and industrial accident. Great, slug-like forms, their bodies translucent and veined with coolant or acid, ooze along the weeping walls, scraping mineral growths with rasping mouthparts. In the warm, stagnant canals, eel-like creatures with bioluminescent patches pulse slowly through the murk, feeding on the chemotactic bacteria that bloom in the effluent. Above, in the heat-shimmer of the upper air, leathery-winged shapes, more glider than flyer, ride the thermals between smokestacks, their screeches the only high, sharp sounds in the gloom. They nest in the rafters of the great factories, their guano adding a new, pungent layer to the ancient decay. This is their equilibrium, their settled state. They are the overwhelming, teeming consequence of this place, perfectly adapted to its wet heat, its mineral diet, its toxic quiet.

The soundscape is a deep, low-frequency hum. It is the sound of massive, slow-moving machinery still operating somewhere in the heart of the great plants—pumps cycling viscous fluids, turbines turning in flooded chambers, the groan of cooling metal. It is a sound felt in the bones as much as heard. Occasionally, a crash echoes across the basin—a slagheap finally giving way, a corroded support structure failing—followed by a long, settling silence, quickly reclaimed by the hum.

This is a place in its own state, a mature system. The erosion is not violent but a slow, chemical digestion, a settling. The heat is constant, the water cycle local and intimate, born from the earth and industry itself. The light is always fading, always dim. The overwhelming fauna is not a chaotic swarm but a complex, slow-motion ecosystem of scavengers and filters, thriving in the absence of all but the hardiest, most alien forms of life. It is a normal, functioning world—the normal, functioning world that remains. It is industrial, urban, wet, hot, and empty of everything except the life that learned to call this home. It breathes, it weeps, it hums. It is at peace with itself.
