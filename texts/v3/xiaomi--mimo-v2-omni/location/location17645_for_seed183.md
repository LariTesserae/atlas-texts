The place is a high, bright emptiness that hums.

You stand upon a plain of vitrified obsidian, cracked into a mosaic of black plates by some ancient, colossal stress. Between the cracks, there is no soil, only a grit of powdered basalt and glass. The air is cool and carries a heavy, metallic tang, like the taste of a lightning strike. It is also wet—not with rain, but with a constant, weeping condensation that beads on every surface and gathers in the fractures, forming a labyrinth of shallow, steaming channels. The water is unnaturally pure and warm to the touch, as if drawn up from a geothermal heart rather than falling from the sky.

The horizon is too close, and too sharp. It does not curve away but is a jagged, serrated line where the plateau abruptly ends, hinting at abyssal drops on all sides. This is a summit, but of what? There are no slopes leading down, only the suggestion of infinite vertical space beyond the edge. The light is brilliant and shadowless, a diffuse, sourceless glare that seems to emanate from the very air itself, turning the wet glass of the ground into a blinding, fractured mirror.

And the landscape *sounds*. It is not the sound of wind or water, but of resonance. The ground vibrates with a deep, subsonic thrum, the voice of the planet’s bones. From the weeping channels, a higher-pitched chime rises as the water quivers, a perpetual, random music. In the distance, where the ground swells into low, melted-looking ridges of columnar basalt, the air shimmers and *sings*—a chord that wavers and splits, as if the light itself is under tension.

There is no green. No life as it is commonly understood. But the world is not inert. The dense, layered mineral formations are alive with slow process. Veins of quartz, milky and fracture-filled, pulse with a faint inner luminescence, a rhythm out of sync with the thrumming ground. The air itself occasionally coalesces into fleeting, crystalline motes that drift for a moment before dissolving. These are not organisms, but patterns—autocatalytic mineral growths, energy differentials made manifest in the saturated atmosphere.

Scattered across the plain are the remnants of something else. Not buildings, but *marks*. Perfect right-angles etched into the obsidian. Shallow, circular depressions arranged in geometric spirals. Occasionally, a pillar of fused sand and glass, waist-high, its surface swirling with frozen patterns, stands alone. These are artifacts of a primitive, perhaps mineral, intelligence. Tools of leverage and calculation, left behind. They are being slowly claimed by the weeping processes: the right-angles soften under deposits of silicate scale, the circles fill with the warm, chiming water.

This is a world in a state of active, strange equilibrium. Erosion is not from wind, but from this constant chemical weeping and the deep vibrations that occasionally cause a flake of glass to shear off with a sharp *crack*. The dynamic is in the condensation-evaporation cycle, the slow growth of crystal veins, the eternal, resonant song of the place. It is dense with presences: the presence of water, of vibration, of light, of geometric memory, of a reality that has folded in on itself and now exists by its own, cool, bright, and loudly humming rules. It is a place that has settled into its own breakdown, and is beautifully, terribly alive with it.
