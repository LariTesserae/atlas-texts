The world is below, and it is alive.

Not with green things—there are no plants here, no chlorophyll or wood or pollen. The walls, the floors, the ceilings of this colossal, interconnected cavern system are bare mineral: basalt, feldspar, glittering veins of quartz, and great slumped flows of ancient, cooled magma now polished smooth by constant, damp abrasion. The air is warm, a humid 20 degrees Celsius that never wavers, and it smells of wet stone, ozone, and a faint, coppery tang that coats the back of the throat.

Water is the constant artist and architect. It doesn't fall from a sky—there is no sky—but weeps from every pore and fissure. Condensation gathers on the vaulted ceilings into fat, trembling droplets that fall with a steady *plip... plip...* into shallow, brackish pools below. These pools are not still; they are fed by and feed into a network of seeping, gurgling rivulets that carve delicate, ever-shifting channels into the soft mineral floors. The sound is a dense tapestry: a constant low roar of distant, rushing underground rivers, the closer drip and trickle of condensation, the gurgle of syphons, and over it all, the **sound**—the overwhelming, living sound.

The fauna. It is the dominant fact of this place. There is no empty space. From the microscopic to the monumental, life fills every cubic meter. The air itself is a shimmering haze of midges, gnats, and smaller, unnamed fliers with iridescent carapaces, their wings creating a subsonic hum you feel in your bones. On the walls, in dense, multi-layered carpets, are things that resemble beetle-larvae, slug-like creatures, and intricate, crawling filaments of colonial organisms. They are not insects as we know them, but mineral-world analogues: silicon-shelled, chitin-armored, feeding on the rock itself, on the films of bacteria and chemotrophic moss-analogues (the "fungal-or-simpler" life that substitutes for vegetation), and on each other. The density is overwhelming; to touch a wall is to feel a thousand tiny, scuttling bodies yield and then re-colonize the space your fingers just occupied.

The scale is vast and regional—a valley-size bubble of interconnected caverns, chambers, and tunnels stretching beyond sight in the normal, diffuse light that seems to emanate from the very rock and the bioluminescence of the fauna. There is no single sun; there is a pervasive, pearlescent glow, brightest around the larger, wetter congregations of life, casting soft, overlapping shadows that writhe with movement.

And into this churning, primal basin, there are signs of a **built** and **primitive** world. On a rare, elevated plateau of harder rock, away from the worst of the seepage, are the remains of a settlement. Not a city, but a scattering: a circle of stone slabs, blackened by fire; a pile of deliberately fractured, tool-marked basalt; the framework of a shelter woven from the tough, fibrous husks of shed carapaces. The tools are simple: a hand-axe of chert, a bowl carved from a giant, empty gastropod shell. These are the marks of a people who arrived, or perhaps emerged, here, who learned to harvest the endless, crawling protein, to boil the brackish water, to find shelter in the niches of this overwhelming world. They are gone now, or perhaps they are simply deeper in, part of the churn.

For the dynamic here is **turbulence**. This is not a static ecosystem. The water seeps change course, drowning a colony in minutes, only to expose new, virgin rock for colonization. Mass swarms of fliers erupt from one chamber in a chaotic, dark cloud, only to be decimated by predatory, bat-like creatures with sonar-clicks that pierce the din. Rockfalls are common, not with a crash but a slow, grinding collapse, instantly swarmed by detritivores. New fissures crack open, breathing out hot, mineral-rich steam and creating instant, teeming new biomes. It is a world in a state of perpetual, violent birth and consumption.

The **weirdness** is in the logic of it. The life here does not recognize our biological categories. The "fauna" have behaviors that blur the line between animal, fungus, and mineral. Some creatures secrete resinous shells that slowly, over generations, build up crystalline structures. Others move in slow, geologic waves, taking years to cross a chamber. The entire ecosystem feels like a single, churning organism, and the ancient human tools are just one more component, being slowly buried under a layer of shell-grit and phosphorescent slime, becoming another stratum in this dense, warm, loud, and utterly alien world beneath the world.
