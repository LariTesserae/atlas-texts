The world is a vertical desert of heat and darkness.

You stand—or rather, you *persist*—on a ledge of something that is not quite rock. It is a warm, granular expanse, a crust of fused silicates and metallic dust that gives underfoot with a faint, glassy crunch. There is no sand here as Earth knows it; the particles are sharp, angular, and cold to the touch despite the pervasive warmth that radiates from below. This is the ground, and it extends in every direction, not as a plain, but as a near-vertical face. The concept of “elevation” here is absolute. You are clinging to the side of a world-sized precipice, looking down into a darkness that has no bottom and up into a darkness that has no sky.

The scale is agoraphobic. The “ground” is a tapestry of mega-features: colossal, un-eroded buttresses the size of mountain ranges; sheer, kilometer-high faces of raw, unweathered basalt; vast, sloping plains of that warm, gritty dust. The features are sharp-edged, geometric, as if the world was crystallized in a moment of violent pressure and then left untouched. There is no water, no ice, no wind to soften these edges. Erosion is not a process here; it is a nascent idea, a potential energy waiting for a trigger that may never come. The world feels *new*, not in a verdant sense, but in a geologic one—like a freshly fractured geode, its internal structures pristine and unscratched.

And yet, it is not still.

Beneath your feet, through the soles of your boots (if you had them), you feel a deep, arrhythmic pulse. A tremor. Not an earthquake, but a *shifting*. The entire cliffside is alive with a sub-audible turbulence. Grains of the warm dust trickle constantly in faint, dry streams. Occasional, larger fragments—a slab of obsidian-like material, a chunk of porous, iron-rich regolith—detach from somewhere above and begin a long, silent fall into the abyss, never to be heard from. The dynamic is not of weather, but of structure. The world is slowly, restlessly rearranging itself at the edges.

In this sparse emptiness, you encounter the “scattered built.” They are not buildings. They are formations, or perhaps artifacts, of such ambiguity that the distinction collapses. Here, a spire of flawless, black crystal, three stories tall, juts from the slope at an impossible angle, its surface humming with a vibration too low to hear but felt in the teeth. There, a cluster of perfect, meter-wide spheres, made of a dull, copper-colored metal, rests in a shallow scoop of the dust, as if placed. Further down, a series of parallel grooves, each a meter deep and kilometers long, score a face of basalt, too regular for fracture, too ancient for any tool you can imagine. They are remnants of a process, or a purpose, that left no other trace.

The fauna is a concept, not a creature. In the utter, inky darkness, your eyes (adjusting for an eternity) begin to perceive… *heat*. The world glows in the spectrum of warmth. Vast, slow-moving patches of deeper infrared crawl across the cliff-face—thermal blooms from the world’s turbulent core. Smaller, sharper points of heat flare and die in the distance: the impacts of those falling stones, converting kinetic energy to brief, silent bonfires. The ecology is one of thermodynamics. There are heat-lichen, you might call them: fractal patterns of slightly different temperatures that spread and compete on the surfaces of the warmer spires, consuming the gradients, growing and decaying in a silent, invisible dance.

The sound is the negation of sound. The dominant noise is a deep, pervasive *hush*—the acoustic shadow of absolute emptiness. But within that hush, if you listen with your bones, there is the sub-bass rumble of the world’s internal churn. The tiny, sharp *tink* of a cooling crystal fracturing a centimeter away. The almost-psychic whisper of dust grains colliding in their slow slide. It is a soundscape of immense pressure and patience.

The light is not black. It is a thick, velvety absence that you can feel pressing against your retinas. But the heat blooms cast their own illumination in the mind’s eye. A deep, brooding maroon glow from a massive thermal shift far below; a sudden, angry orange smear where a boulder finally shatters on a ledge a mile down. These are not lights you see with, but lights you *know*. They etch the landscape in afterimages of temperature, painting the darkness with a geography of warmth and cold.

The weirdness is in the logic. This place obeys physics, but a stripped-down, stark version of it. There is no biology, no hydrology, no atmosphere to speak of, only geology and thermodynamics in a pure, brutal dialogue. The warmth comes from the world’s own decaying core, not a sun. The “scattered built” formations might be the natural result of pressure and crystallization, or they might be the architecture of a process so alien it is indistinguishable from geology. The turbulence is not chaos, but the steady, relentless activity of a world still cooling, still settling, still *becoming*—a becoming that will take eons, measured in millimeters of dust and the slow, cold glow of falling stars in an endless, waterless dark.
