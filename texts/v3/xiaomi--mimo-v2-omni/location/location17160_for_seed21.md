The plateau is a wound in the sky, a recently upthrust slab of dark basaltic rock that still carries the sharpness of its birth. Its edges shear away into mist, confining the space to a single, high clearing roughly the footprint of a grand cathedral. The air is thick and hot, a heavy blanket that carries the scent of wet stone and ozone, and it weeps. Condensation gathers on every surface, beading on the angular rock faces and trickling down in countless silver rivulets. The ground is a mosaic: expanses of bare, glistening stone alternate with shallow pools and patches of the most vibrant, emerald-green moss and ferns. This vegetation is lush in its health, each frond perfectly formed, yet it grows in isolated clusters, leaving much of the plateau as open, reflective space, balanced with emptiness.

Scattered across this clearing are the artifacts. They are not ruins, but active presences. Crystalline obelisks, no taller than a person, hum with a frequency felt in the bones rather than heard. They grow directly from the rock, their facets refracting the normal daylight into prismatic patterns that dance across the wet ground. Elsewhere, spheres of a dull, mercurial metal hover a meter above the surface, drifting in slow, deliberate arcs. Occasionally, one will pause, and a filament of light will lash out to touch a fern, which will then shiver and grow at a perceptible rate. These are the marks of a built environment—scattered, enigmatic, and operating on principles that feel post-modern, bordering on the cosmic.

The soundscape is a study in near-silence. The dominant noise is the drip and trickle of water. Beneath that, a profound quiet, broken only by the deep, subsonic rumble of geothermal activity far below the young crust. The hum of the crystal obelisks provides a constant, soft undernote. From the hovering spheres comes the occasional, delicate chime, like glass struck in the distance. Fauna is rare; once in a long while, a creature like a glass lizard, with skin that mirrors its surroundings, will slide from one moss patch to another, leaving no trace.

The weirdness is in the details. The water does not always obey gravity; in places, it beads and climbs upward along invisible seams in the rock, feeding the mosses in defiance of logic. The shadows of the crystal towers do not correspond to the position of the sun, but instead stretch toward the nearest sphere. The ferns pulse with a slow, bioluminescent glow in the moments after a hover-sphere bathes them in light, a growth so rapid it seems like a memory played in fast-forward.

Despite the quiet, the place is dynamic. The water cycle is feverish: pools steam and evaporate in the heat, only to recondense moments later as a fine mist that clings to the air. The mosses visibly expand, reclaiming bare rock centimeter by centimeter, only to be pruned back by the deliberate light-tendrils of the spheres. The hovering devices map and remap their paths, and the crystal obelisks occasionally rotate their facets in unison, as if tracking something beyond the sky. It is a laboratory, a garden, a relic, and a frontier, all at once—a small, boundless stage where geology, hydrology, and an inscrutable technology play out their intertwined processes under a hot, weeping sky.
