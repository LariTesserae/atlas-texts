The place is known, to those who live within it, as the Terraces of Lyr. It is not a landscape in any natural sense. It is a constructed world, a megastructure so vast and so old that its original purpose has eroded into myth, leaving only the layered, living, mechanical reality of its present state.

**The Framework: Structure and Scale**
The entire world is built. There is no raw earth, no bedrock that was not placed, cut, and fitted by some forgotten industry. The substrate is a labyrinthine, continent-sized assembly of metallic composites, vitrified stone, and polymerized organics, all fused and weathered over eons into a settled, stable form. The elevation is extreme not because of mountains, but because the structure ascends in colossal, stepped terraces. From the lowest gloom of the Underpump to the sun-blasted rims of the Upper Orchards, the vertical drop is measured in kilometers. Each terrace is a broad, open plain or a gently rolling hillscape unto itself, but the edges are dramatic: sheer walls hundreds of meters high, crisscrossed with colossal piping, vine-choked buttresses, and the silent, gaping mouths of aqueducts.

**The Living Mantle: Vegetation and Ecology**
Upon this skeletal world, life has been meticulously, perhaps even accidentally, seeded. The vegetation is lush, a dense, saturated tapestry of greens, purples, and phosphorescent blues that clings to every surface. This is not wild growth, but a cultivated, or perhaps naturalized, biome. Great ferns with silica-hardened fronds unfurl in the perpetual warm, bright air. Mats of velvety, moisture-wicking moss cover the plazas and floors, softening the industrial geometry. Flowering vines with trumpet-shaped blooms scale the superstructure, their roots probing into ancient seams where nutrient-rich gels still weep. The flora has adapted to the built environment; trees have roots that wrap around conduits for support, and aerial plants grow directly from the condensation that beads on towering heat exchangers.

The fauna is abundant and, like the flora, seems purpose-built for this world. Herds of six-legged grazers, their carapaces a dull bronze, move slowly across the terraces, browsing on the dense foliage. They are the visible pulse of the ecosystem. In the air, flocks of avian mimics—creatures with the bodies of lizards and the iridescent wings of insects—dart and shriek, their calls adding a high, piercing layer to the soundscape. Smaller, quicker things skitter in the undergrowth: beetle-like scavengers, bioluminescent pollinators that drift like embers at dusk. There are no predators of great size; the balance is maintained by competition and the sheer complexity of the habitat.

**The Water Cycle: Dampness and Engineering**
Water is ever-present but never free. The damp parameter is realized as a perpetual, engineered cycle. Condensation runs in rivulets down the warm walls of the megastructure, collected in channels that feed the vast, shallow wetlands of the middle terraces. These are not rivers, but controlled flows—sluggish, brown with dissolved nutrients, their banks thick with reeds and drinking fauna. The air itself holds a warm, humid weight, and most surfaces are slick with a thin film of moisture or beaded droplets. Occasional, scheduled rains fall from cloud-forming condensers high above, a drenching, warm downpour that turns the terraces into a symphony of dripping, gurgling, and rushing water before it is all meticulously channeled away again.

**Atmosphere, Sound, and Light**
The light is bright, almost relentless. Massive, sun-mimicking orbs are embedded in the vault of the sky-structure, casting a diffuse, shadowless glare that eliminates true night. Their brightness is constant, shifting only in a slow, daily rhythm from a sharp white to a deep amber. There is no weather, only the controlled climate: uniformly warm, with gentle, predictable breezes that whisper through the megastructure's canyons.

The soundscape is loud, but layered and constant, a droning baseline for life. The dominant sound is the low, sub-audible hum of the world-machinery: the slow turn of continent-spanning gears, the thrum of geothermal pumps, the sigh of atmospheric processors. Upon this, life adds its own density: the rustle of a billion leaves, the grating calls of the avians, the splash of grazers in the wetlands, the distant, rhythmic crash of a scheduled rain deluge against metal and leaf. It is a sound that has never known silence.

**Rules of the World and its Weirdness**
The world operates on slow, patient processes. Change is glacial. The erosion is not of rock, but of function: a conduit slowly narrowing with mineral deposits, a terrace floor gradually sinking under millennia of organic mulch, a light-orb dimming over centuries. The dynamic is one of cycles, not events. The weirdness is low, but palpable—a sense of *designed* wrongness. Some terraces seem to defy gravity, curving upward at their edges. Certain pools of water show impossible, geometric reflections. The grazers sometimes move in perfect, synchronized patterns for no apparent reason. These are not horrors, but accepted quirks of the old, deep logic of the place.

To stand on a Terrace of Lyr is to be immersed in a beautiful, overgrown machine. It is warm, bright, loud, and teeming with life that has forgotten it is not native. It is a testament not to nature's triumph, but to a built order so ancient it has become a new kind of nature—one of damp metal, humming silence, and the slow, patient growth of a forest on the bones of a factory.
