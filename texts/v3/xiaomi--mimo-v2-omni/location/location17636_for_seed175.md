The air is thick and warm, saturated with a moisture that never quite resolves into rain. It condenses on every surface, drawing a perpetual sweat from the stone. This is a high, wet place—a plateau or the broad shoulder of a mountain—where the land has been settled into its own form for eons. Erosion here is not a violent force but a gentle, endless polishing.

The ground is a dark, peaty loam, spongy underfoot, but the true bones of the place are stone: a pale, fine-grained rock, like forgotten marble, that emerges in rounded humps and long, low ridges. The stone *weeps*. From every fissure and hollow, water wells up, not in springs but as a slow, uniform exudation, coating everything in a shimmering, unbroken film. It gathers in shallow, clear pools that mirror the diffuse, silver-grey sky, then overflows in a thousand tiny rivulets that trace the stone’s contours before vanishing again into the moss.

And the moss is the vegetation. Not a single kind, but a thousand variations in texture and hue, from the deep, velvet emerald of the saturated hollows to the pale, luminous sage of the higher ridges. It smothers the stone in thick, plush carpets, hangs in dripping curtains from overhangs, and builds miniature, sponge-like forests in every crevice. Ferns unfurl in slow, spiraling patience, their fronds heavy with beads of condensation. Broad-leafed plants, like waxen hostas, sit spaced far apart in the clearings, each one a solitary statement of life. There are no trees, only this low, lush, and meticulously spaced garden of non-vascular and shade-loving life.

Here, in this warm, wet silence, there are no birds, no insects, no scuttling things. The fauna is an absence you can feel. The only sounds are the liquid ones: the soft *tap* of a water droplet falling from a fern tip onto a moss cushion, the barely-audible gurgle of a rivulet finding its path, and beneath it all, a profound, resonant quiet that seems to amplify the minute noises of water. It is not an empty silence, but a full one, saturated with the slow pulse of the land breathing out moisture.

The strangeness seeps in not as a shock, but as a gradual realization. The patterns of the moss growth are too deliberate, forming perfect concentric circles around the weeping points. The water, when you cup it in your hands, feels oddly *alive*, viscous and mineral-rich, carrying a faint, clean scent of ozone and crushed stone. The light, even at midday, is a soft, pearlescent glow, as if filtered through a constant, high-altitude mist, casting no sharp shadows. Time here is measured in the expansion of a moss colony, the deepening of a water-polished groove in the marble. It is a place of immense, patient equilibrium, a self-contained world where stone, water, and plant exist in a slow, wet, and silent conversation that has been going on for millennia, and will continue for millennia more, utterly indifferent and beautifully complete.
