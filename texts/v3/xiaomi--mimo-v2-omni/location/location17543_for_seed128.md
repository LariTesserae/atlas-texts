# The Reservoir Throat

You are inside something.

That is the first certainty. Not inside a building — inside a *body*, if bodies can be made of fused ceramic and programmable stone and a material like bone but warmer, slightly yielding under the pressure of a hand held against the wall. The walls breathe. Not metaphorically. The surface beneath your fingertips expands and contracts on a cycle of roughly four seconds, and each exhalation pushes a film of warm water across the interior, so that everything glistens, everything weeps. The entire chamber is slick. Has always been slick. Will always be slick.

The space is not large. That's the second thing. You could measure it — perhaps eight meters across, perhaps ten — but measurement feels beside the point here, because the chamber doesn't quite agree on where its own boundaries are. The walls curve inward at what you'd call chest height, then ease outward again near the ceiling, which sits just above head-height, close enough that you feel the warmth of it radiating down. The room is intimate. Enclosing. The architectural equivalent of a held breath.

And it is *loud*.

---

Sound fills this place the way water fills a glass — completely, pressurized, with nowhere else to go. It is not one sound. It is a dense weave of many sounds, layered so thickly that they become a single roar, a presence you feel in the cavity of your chest and the soft tissues of your throat. There is a low hum — not mechanical, not exactly, more like the resonance of a vast structure settling under its own impossible weight, the way a cathedral hums after a choir has stopped singing but the stones are still remembering the frequency. Beneath that, a pulse: regular, organic, wet. Something is being pumped through the walls themselves. You can hear it — feel it — in the floor, in the ceiling, a network of fluid moving through channels too small to see, but too numerous and too fast to ignore. The walls are vascular. The room is perfused.

And then there are the creatures.

They are everywhere, and they are small, and they are *busy*. You hear them before you properly see them — a high, ceaseless clicking, like ten thousand fingernails tapping on wet glass. They move in the water-filmed channels of the walls, dozens per square meter, their bodies pressed flat against the ceramic-bone substrate, translucent and faintly luminous in the dim light. They are roughly the shape of limpets, if limpets had fringed mouths that opened and closed in rapid pulses, each opening harvesting something invisible from the water that beads and runs along the walls. Some are the size of a thumbnail. Some are smaller. Some, in the deeper channels where the water collects into rivulets, are larger — the size of a palm — and these move more slowly, trailing filaments from their undersides that anchor them in the flow. The clicking comes from their feeding. From the collective, mechanical rhythm of ten thousand mouths opening and closing in something that is almost but not quite synchrony, so that the sound shimmers, beats against itself, creates interference patterns that rise and fall in volume even though the individual mouths never stop.

You look down. The floor is not flat. It is scored with a pattern of channels — not carved, *grown*, like the grooves in a leaf — all of them angling toward a central depression where water collects in a shallow, warm pool no more than ankle-deep. The channels are packed with the creatures. Walking here would mean stepping on hundreds of them. They would crunch and release a fluid that smells faintly amber, faintly resinous, like sap from a tree that doesn't exist.

---

The light comes from the walls.

Not from any single source. The ceramic-bone material itself is dimly luminous — a murky, amber-brown glow, like sunlight through river water, like the inside of a held seashell. It is not enough light to see by comfortably. Your eyes adjust, but they never fully resolve the space into clarity. Everything remains slightly soft, slightly suggestion. The luminance shifts subtly over minutes — a slow wave of brightening that moves across the ceiling from east to west (if these directions mean anything here), then fades, then returns from the opposite direction. A circadian rhythm built into the architecture. The room is keeping time. The room is *alive*, or at least metabolically active in ways that blur the boundary between structure and organism.

In the dimness, you notice the texture of the walls more than their color. They are not smooth. They are layered — thousands of thin strata, each perhaps a millimeter thick, each slightly different in composition. Some layers are hard and glassy. Some are porous, drinking in the water that condenses on them. Some are fibrous, woven with threads of something that might be metal or might be tendon. The cumulative effect is of a surface that has been built up over an immense period of time, layer by layer, like a pearl, like a reef, like a sedimentary record of a process that has no name. Erosion has settled the older layers into smoothness. The newer layers near the ceiling are rougher, still forming, still *active*. You can see the newest stratum at the junction of wall and ceiling: a wet, glistening film, barely there, still being deposited by mechanisms you cannot identify.

---

Here is what you begin to understand, standing in the warm dimness with water running down your neck and the clicking of ten thousand mouths filling your skull:

This place was made. All of it. Every surface, every channel, every creature. This is not a natural cave that something has colonized. This is an *intention* — a space designed, constructed, and seeded with life that serves a function you cannot yet discern. The technology that built it is invisible because it has become the substrate itself. The walls are not walls *and also* machinery. The walls *are* the machinery. The creatures are not wildlife. They are components. The water is not an environmental condition. It is a medium — a carrier, a solvent, a circulatory fluid for a system whose purpose remains opaque.

And the system is old. Not crumbling — stable, balanced, settled into its own equilibrium — but old in the way that a deep geological formation is old, as though this chamber has been here, operating, weeping, clicking, humming, for longer than any civilization you can name. It has been here long enough that the distinction between the constructed and the emergent has dissolved. The makers are gone, or the makers are indistinguishable from the made, or the makers are still present in some distributed sense that the architecture itself constitutes.

The warm water reaches your ankles in the central depression. Something moves beneath the surface — something larger than the limpet-things, something with the sinuous weight of an eel. You feel it brush your calf. It does not stop. It belongs here more than you do.

The room breathes. The walls drip. The clicking continues.

You are inside something, and it is aware of you in the way that a body is aware of a foreign particle — not with attention, but with *process*. The water on the walls has thickened slightly where you stand. The luminance near your body has dimmed by a fraction. The creatures have shifted their distribution, pulling away from you in a slow, organic tide, leaving clear patches on the wall like negative space, like an image of your presence rendered in absence.

The system is adjusting.

It will decide what to do with you in its own time.
