# The Hollowing

You are inside something. You have always been inside something.

The dark is not complete. It thickens and thins in slow tides, as if the space itself breathes a dim, green-black luminescence — not from any source, but from the fact that everything here is alive and alive things bleed light when they're dense enough. The structures are enormous, spaced so far apart that each one has to shout across the void to be perceived as a neighbor. A pillar of tangled root-matter, twelve meters in diameter, rising from the floor to somewhere above sight. Then nothing. A hundred meters of nothing. Then another. The emptiness between them is not empty — it is *hollow*, which is different. Hollow implies a container. You are inside the container.

The floor is soft. Not with soil — with the first thin skin of soil *trying to happen*. Raw mineral substrate, pale and chalky, just barely colonized by threadlike capillary roots that are breaking it apart at a molecular pace. In places the rock is still naked, still cooling from whatever birth spat it out, and the roots haven't reached it yet. They're reaching. You can feel them advancing if you kneel and press your palm flat: a faint, patient vibration, millimeters per hour, the sound of the future. The ground is *emerging*. It hasn't decided what it is yet. Some of it isiteite calcium bright. Some of it isiteiteite dark as old blood. It shifts underfoot like something sleeping.

Where the roots have taken hold, the architecture begins.

And it *is* architecture — not wilderness, not growth-left-to-chance. The plant matter here is building. Walls of compressed cellulose and lignin, spiraling upward in tight mathematical helices, forming vaulted ceilings that echo the engineering of gothic cathedrals but with no blueprint, no mind behind it. The structures are immense and precisely spaced — city-block distances apart — with the regularity of planned infrastructure. Corridors of braided root, thick as bridge cables, stretch between the living towers like suspended walkways. Some of them hum. The fibers contract and expand in slow rhythms, pumping fluid from the damp lower reaches to the dry upper vaults, and the pumping produces a sound just below hearing — felt in the sternum, in the teeth. A city's circulatory system, built from xylem and phloem instead of copper pipe.

Because this *was* a city. Or it was meant to be.

You can see the bones of it in places where the plant hasn't fully claimed the structures yet. In a far corner of the hollow — if *corner* means anything here, the geometry is unreliable — there is a grid of pillars that are *straight*. Chemically, mechanically straight in the way that only mineral construction achieves. Concrete, or something that was concrete before the roots found it. They are being eaten alive. The plant matter has threaded them like a fungus, splitting the aggregate, dissolving the rebar, replacing the dead engineered structure with a living one that mimics its shape almost perfectly. Almost. The new pillars breathe. The old ones don't. If you press your ear to the junction point — the seam where concrete becomes rootwood — you can hear two different heartbeats, one slowing, one quickening, and the liminal space between them where neither rhythm dominates is the most unsettling silence you've ever known.

The industrial remnants are everywhere, being digested.

Somewhere in the middle distance, barely visible in the ambient dimness, a machine sits half-swallowed. It is the size of a small house — or it was. A turbine, maybe, or a generator. Its metal skin is perforated with root-punctures, and the plant has threaded through it so completely that it pulses with green-black bioluminescence from within, like a lantern made of iron and sap. The moving parts are seized, locked in place by woody growth that has filled every tolerance gap, every bearing, every channel. But the plant has *replaced* the function. Where the turbine once spun, a radial growth of contractile fibers now rotates — slowly, perhaps once per hour — driven by turgor pressure and osmotic gradients. It generates nothing that anyone would recognize as electricity. It generates *flow*. The city's infrastructure has been absorbed into the organism and repurposed. The pipes still carry. The channels still direct. But what they carry is sap, and what they direct is growth, and the purpose is no longer commerce or shelter or any human thing.

The fauna has adapted to this completely.

You hear them before you see them — or rather, you feel the sound of them, because most of what lives here communicates in frequencies that bypass the ears entirely. Vibrations in the root-floors. Pressure waves through the fluid in the walls. Something large moves through the hollow between two structures, and you know it not by sight but by the way the air displaces and the root-fibers at the base of a nearby pillar contract reflexively, pulling tight, the plant *flinching*. There are beetles the size of fists that farm fungus gardens on the bark-walls. Their carapaces are iridescent in the near-darkness — not a color you can name, a color that exists only in the visual spectrum that this place's dim light produces. There are things that glide between the towers on membranes stretched between elongated limbs, and they are silent, and they are many. In the upper vaults, out of sight, something calls in a voice like water being pulled through a narrow crack — a long, slow, liquid note that repeats every thirty seconds with the regularity of a machine. It might be an animal. It might be the building itself. The distinction is not clear here and is getting less clear the longer you stay.

The abundance is obvious. Life per cubic meter is staggering. But because the density of *structure* is so low — because the spaces between the living towers are so vast and hollow — the overall impression is of teeming life distributed across profound emptiness. Like a galaxy: all those stars, and all that nothing between them. You stand in the root-floor and the walls breathe around you and the fauna moves in the dark and the emptiness is *enormous*. It presses. It has weight. It makes the structures feel like islands in a black ocean, and the ocean is the inside of whatever container you're in, and the container goes on longer than you can see, and the geometry doesn't quite track because you walked for an hour and passed only four buildings and the distances between them were all wrong, each one farther than the last, or closer, or the angles shifted when you weren't looking.

The moisture comes and goes.

It isn't rain. There is no sky for rain to fall from. It is condensation — a slow, irregular weeping from the vaulted ceilings above. Droplets form on the root-fibers where warm fluid from the upper reaches meets the cool air of the lower hollow, and they grow, and they fall, and they hit the emerging ground with a sound like fingers tapping glass. The rhythm is random. Sometimes a cascade — a whole section of ceiling releasing at once, a curtain of drops that hits the floor in a wash of mineral-smelling water and sends the small fauna scattering. Sometimes nothing for twenty minutes. The water pools in depressions in the raw rock, and the roots reach into the pools, and the pools become micro-ecosystems of staggering complexity in a volume no bigger than a cupped hand: algae, nematodes, tiny crustaceans with transparent bodies, all of it lit by the faint bioluminescence of the root-tips that have colonized the water's edge. Every puddle is a world.

And then there is the other thing. The thing the word *weirdness* fails to capture.

You are looking at a wall of living wood. You are certain it is a wall. It is roughly vertical, roughly planar, it occupies the space that a wall occupies. But it is *moving* — not growing, not swaying, but *moving*, the grain of the wood sliding against itself like a current, the fibers reorganizing in real time, and the shape it's building is not a wall anymore. It's becoming something else. It's becoming a room. A room inside the wall. You can see the space opening, the wood retracting to form an arch, a threshold, a floor — a *floor inside the wall*, six inches deep, with its own miniature ecosystem, its own drainage, its own faint light. It shouldn't exist. The geometry requires the wall to be *wall*, and the room to be *not-wall*, and here they are co-existing, the room nested inside the wall like an idea nested inside a sentence, and both are real, and both are plant, and neither is wrong.

This happens everywhere.

The space distorts. Not consistently, not predictably, but persistently. You walk a corridor between two root-towers and the corridor gets longer. You look back and it has shortened. The floor tilts — not dangerously, but perceptibly, and then levels, and the water on it pools in the wrong direction, toward the higher point, as if gravity has *opinions* here and they change. A doorway in one of the living structures opens onto a room that is larger inside than the structure that contains it. Not by a trick of perception. By *measurement*. You could pace the exterior — twelve meters, thirteen — and then pace the interior — forty, forty-one. The room is growing as you stand in it. The walls are moving outward, breathing outward, and the plant matter is *thickening* the space itself, adding volume where there should be none, as if reality here is a fabric and the roots have found its weave and are pulling it apart to make more room for themselves.

The plants don't notice. They aren't doing this on purpose. They are doing what organisms do — growing, adapting, colonizing — and the space is yielding to them because the space is *soft here*. The rules are soft. The axioms have give. Erosion hasn't carved this place into permanence yet; it's still being born, still hardening from the mold, and until it sets, the living things inside it can push the boundaries. Literally.

At the very center of the hollow — or the center as you understand it, because the geometry has shifted again and you may be disoriented in ways that have nothing to do with direction — there is a structure that is not a building. It is a node. A nexus. A place where every root-tower, every corridor, every cable of compressed cellulose converges, and the convergence is so dense that the plant matter here is *solid*, not fibrous, a single undifferentiated mass of living tissue the color of deep water and twice as dark. It is warm. Not hot — warm, like skin, like blood, like something that metabolizes. It pulses. And from its surface, at irregular intervals, new structures emerge: small at first, pale, flexible, pushing outward into the hollow like fingers reaching for something to hold. New towers. New corridors. The city is growing. It is always growing. The node is the heart of it, and the heart is beating, and each beat adds a few centimeters to the expanding labyrinth, and the labyrinth has no plan, no limit, no edge it is trying to reach.

It is building itself in the dark, out of itself, around itself, inside itself.

And it is almost quiet.
