The place is called the Underloom by those who think of it at all, which is not often, for it is not a place that invites visitors.

It is a world of stone and life, without water. The ground is not earth but a compacted, granular substrate—like sand that has never known the kiss of rain, ground from the rock above over eons. It gives underfoot with a soft, powdery crunch. There are no rivers, no lakes, no seas. Moisture is a memory sealed in the geology itself. Yet, it is lush. This is the central, silent paradox.

The vegetation is fungal and lithophytic. Immense, shelf-like structures of a chitinous, coral-white material erupt from the walls and floor of what can only be called a cavern, but a cavern of continental scale. The ceiling is lost in a permanent, cool twilight, a vaulted darkness from which hangs no stalactite but vast, interconnected webs of mycelial rope, some thick as ancient tree trunks, others fine as spun silk, all pulsing with a faint, internal bioluminescence—the primary source of light. This light is a deep, bruised purple and a cold, arterial blue, casting long, sharp shadows that never quite align with the objects that make them.

The "plants" are not green. They are bone-white, charcoal-grey, and the deep violet of a livid bruise. They grow in fractal patterns—crystalline ferns that are rigid and sharp, great fans of porous, sponge-like matter that sigh and shudder in the faint air currents. There are forests of what look like petrified weeping willows, their "leaves" being thousands of delicate, dangling mineral filaments that chime softly against one another, a sound that forms the base layer of the deafening noise.

For it is not quiet here. The scale is open, a valley-sized chamber stretching into further chambers and vaults, a subterranean geography. And in this space, sound travels and amplifies. The primary source of the deafening roar is the air itself. This is not wind as understood on the surface. It is a slow, massive, thermal exchange. Cool air from the deep, porous rock exhales into the slightly warmer, mushroom-scented air of the chambers, creating vast, glacial-pressure systems. The air doesn't gust; it *moves*, a constant, sub-audible pressure wave that becomes audible as it interacts with the landscape. It becomes a deep, resonant drone, a bass note that you feel in your chest and the bones of your skull. Layered upon this is the chime of the mineral filaments, the occasional crack of a thermal shift in the rock, and the calls of the fauna.

The fauna is abundant and large. They are slow, patient creatures, adapted to a world without rapid change. Great, armadillo-like grazers, their shells plated with the same calcium-oxalate as the mineral ferns, lumber through the fungal forests, scraping nutrient-rich spores and silica-rich "moss" from the ground with rasping, stone-like tongues. In the air, though no true flying creature exists here, there are drifters—flat, ray-like beings with vast, membranous wings that catch the slow thermal currents, navigating the dark upper vaults on silent, gliding hunts for the smaller, skittering things that live in the crevices of the mycelial ropes. There are no predators in a frenzy; the hunt is a game of patience measured in days.

The erosion is ancient, primordial. Every edge is softened. The chamber floors are not jagged but rolling, like dunes turned to stone. The walls are smooth, scooped out by some unimaginably slow process of chemical exfoliation and the tireless, abrasive motion of the air over a billion years. It feels settled into its final form, a world at the end of its becoming.

And here is the urban element. The great, columnar basalt formations that support the upper vaults are not random. They are spaced with a disturbing regularity, and from them, in regular intervals, branch the shelf-like fungi, creating levels and platforms. The mycelial webs connect these columns in a network that looks like a city's infrastructure, a transit system for spores and small creatures. The "buildings" are the enormous, porous, sponge-like growths, riddled with tunnels and chambers used by the fauna. It is a city built by biology, not hands, following the cold logic of structural efficiency and spore dispersal. There is no technology, only a profound, organic architecture.

The dynamic is slow. Change here is geological. A new fungal shelf might grow a centimeter in a year. A thermal shift in the air current might take a century to become permanent. The light pulses, but over hours, a slow, breathing rhythm. To stand here is to feel time dilate, to understand patience as a physical force.

It is a normal place. There is no magic, no weirdness beyond the profound strangeness of its own rules. A dry, lush, loud, silent, ancient, and enduring world, breathing its own dust in the dark, a city of life built in the absolute absence of water, under a sky it will never see.
