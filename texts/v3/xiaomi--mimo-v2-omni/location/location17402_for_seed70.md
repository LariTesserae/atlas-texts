# The Kept Highlands

You come upon it after a long ascent through mist that grows warmer as you climb, which is wrong, and wetter, which is more wrong still. The air thickens. It begins to feel less like air and more like the memory of water — not quite breathable, not quite swimmable, but something between the two that your lungs learn to accept in stages.

And then the land opens, and you understand.

The highlands are flooded. Not recently — this is not the aftermath of a storm or a river's tantrum. The water has been here long enough to become the ground itself. Vast shallow seas fill every depression between ridgelines of dark, young stone — stone so new it still holds the glassy sheen of its cooling, its edges sharp and unretouched by time. The rock is basaltic, mostly, threaded with veins of something faintly iridescent that catches the normal, diffused light and throws it back in muted coppers. The ridges rise just enough to break the water's surface, forming archipelagos of black stone no wider than a person could jump across, stretching in braided chains toward a horizon that is more water-sky than land-sky.

But the water is not empty. It is not even close to empty.

Growth saturates everything. Immense fronds of something between fern and kelp rise from the shallow floors, their structures so dense and layered that from above, the water looks less like water and more like a forest canopy seen from a great height — deep greens, blue-greens, the occasional shock of rust-orange where a different organism establishes itself. The vegetation grows above the waterline too: thick, waxy-leafed stalks erupt directly from the wet rock, their roots threading into hairline fractures in the stone. Mosses — if that's what they are — coat every exposed surface in velvet layers so thick they soften the ridges' sharp geometry into something organic and rounded. Everything is wet. Everything is growing. The biomass per cubic meter here must be extraordinary; you can feel it in the heaviness of the air, the resistance when you push through any passage between growth, the way every surface is already occupied and something else is trying to occupy that occupier.

The warmth comes from below. Not dramatically — there are no hot springs, no visible vents — but the water is warm to a depth you cannot measure by hand, and the stone itself holds a gentle, persistent heat. The highland sits, it seems, on a thin crust over something still molten, still settling. This explains the youth of the rock, the water's temperature, the strange mineral veins. It also explains the quiet: this is a place of patient geologic process. Nothing here happens fast. The water rose slowly. The stone cooled slowly. The vegetation claimed it slowly. You are standing in the middle of something that has been arriving for millennia and has not yet decided what it is.

The quiet is the first thing you truly notice after the visual overwhelming. For a place so dense with life, it is profoundly still. No birdsong — or almost none; a single, flutelike call repeats at long intervals from somewhere you cannot locate, and you cannot identify the creature that makes it. No insects in any familiar pattern. The water moves, but it moves like breathing: a slow tide that rises and falls imperceptibly across the stone shelves, sheeting thin and silver over the dark rock before withdrawing again. The vegetation rustles, but the leaves are thick and heavy; they produce more of a sound like fabric rubbing against fabric, a soft, arrhythmic shushing. Underneath it all, if you stand still long enough and stop trying to listen with your ears and start listening with your body, you feel the subsonic hum of the heat below. It is not a sound. It is the absence of silence, a vibration so low it lives in your sternum.

There are no structures. No artifacts. No sign that anything with hands or tools has ever been here, or if they were, they left nothing the water and the growth did not reclaim within a season. This is not abandoned; it was never claimed. The place does not invite habitation. It is too saturated, too warm, too alive in a way that is indifferent to any visitor's needs. You can walk the ridgelines, but the ridgelines are narrow and the growth encroaches, and the water between them is just deep enough — chest-deep in places, over-your-head in others — that crossing is a negotiation rather than a decision.

The strangeness — and there is strangeness, a 1.8 out of 3, which means it hides well — lives in the details. The iridescent mineral veins sometimes seem to shift pattern when you're not looking directly at them, as though the stone's interior is slowly rearranging itself. The water, which should be turbid given the density of life, is in places preternaturally clear, offering views straight down to the dark floor twenty meters below where things move — slow shapes, possibly geological, possibly not. The vegetation on the newest stone, the sharpest-edged ridgelines that must have surfaced most recently, is already as dense as the vegetation on the oldest formations, as though growth here does not need time, only surface. And the single birdcall — if it is a bird — never seems to come from the same direction twice.

The scale of the place is regional. You can walk for hours along the ridge chains and see nothing but the same pattern repeated: dark stone, green water, suffocating growth, mist. But "the same" is not "the identical." Each basin has its own character. One is shallow and orange with a different kind of growth that smells faintly of something sweet and chemical. Another is deep and black and still, the water undisturbed, the vegetation pulling back from its edges as though in respect or fear. Another is choked so thick with growth that the water is invisible and you walk on what feels like solid ground until it gives slightly, wetly, under your weight, and you realize you are walking on a mat of living things floating on water you cannot see.

It does not change quickly. The tides are slow. The growth is patient. The stone is still becoming. You have the persistent sense that this landscape is not a finished thing — not a place, but a process, one that is happening at a speed designed to be invisible to anything that lives and dies as fast as you do. If you came back in a hundred years, the ridges would be a little lower, the water a little deeper or a little shallower, the growth a little thicker. If you came back in a thousand, you might not recognize it. If you came back in a million, it might be an ocean with no highlands at all, or the water might have retreated and left a mountain range sharp as broken glass and already green.

For now, it breathes. The warm water sheets over the dark stone. The fronds sway with a motion that is almost imperceptible. The low hum lives in your chest. The single call sounds from wherever you are not looking.

You are standing in a place that is becoming something else, and it is in no hurry, and it does not know you are here.
