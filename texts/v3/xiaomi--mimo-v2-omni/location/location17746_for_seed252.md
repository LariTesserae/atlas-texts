# The Weeping Bright

You are underground. How far underground is difficult to say — the air is thick and hot and luminous, and depth has lost its meaning.

The cavity is small. Not a cavern in the traditional sense, but a pocket, a blister in the rock — perhaps the size of a modest room, perhaps smaller, perhaps larger, depending on when you measure it. The walls are sharp. Geologically speaking, this place is an infant. The stone has only recently fractured open, and its faces are fresh as broken glass, all angles and cleavage planes catching light that has no obvious source. Everything here is bright. Not daylight-bright. Brighter. The kind of brightness that exists without shadow, that seems to emanate from the substance of the air itself, a white-gold saturation that makes the geometry of the space difficult to read. You squint and the walls are still there. You look away and the afterimage is the same color as everything else.

The rock is dark — basite or something older — and it is wet. Not flooded. Wet the way a forehead is wet. Water beads on every surface, draws together in thin threads along the sharp edges of fractures, gathers in trembling globules that fall almost reluctantly, as though the water here does not fully obey gravity. It lands on the floor — the floor of broken planes and angular debris — and doesn't pool so much as *adhere*, spreading in thin films that shimmer. The heat is considerable. You can feel it press against your skin like a palm. And yet the water does not boil. It should, by your understanding of temperature and pressure, but it doesn't. It weeps, and it clings, and it shimmers, and it stays liquid.

There is almost nothing alive here. Almost. The minerals themselves may constitute a kind of life, or may have, once — there are patterns in the stone that suggest crystal growth responsive to something other than simple chemistry, arrangements too deliberate for geology, too patient for engineering. Filaments of pale mineral thread run through the rock like capillaries. They pulse faintly. Whether this is a trick of the light — and there is so much light — or something else, you cannot be sure. These are not organisms as you know them. They may not be organisms at all. They may be the residue of an intelligence that worked at scales below the atomic, that built itself into the substrate of stone so long ago that the stone has only now fractured open to admit you, to admit this brutal bright heat, to admit the water that condenses from the dense and luminous air.

Occasionally — rarely — something moves. A flicker at the edge of perception. Not an animal. Not a mechanism. A distortion. A place where the light bends slightly wrong, where the water on a wall flows uphill for the length of a finger-width before correcting. These are the fauna of this place: presences that are less than organisms and more than phenomena, that occupy the gap between physics and intention. They have no sound. They have no name. They occur.

The sound, when it comes, is geological. The faintest crack of cooling or thermal stress — a hairline fracture propagating through stone that is, despite the heat, still finding its equilibrium. The tiny percussion of a water droplet striking a mineral shelf. A low, below-hearing vibration that you feel in your teeth and in the bones of your wrists. The place hums. Not mechanically. Not with any tune. It hums the way a body hums — blood and electricity and the memory of processes too old to recall their own origin.

Everything here is young and impossibly ancient at once. The rock faces are sharp because they were never weathered. The mineral filaments are old because they predate the fracture that revealed them. The technology — if that is what it is — is beyond anything your era can name, but it is not visible. It is subsumed. It became the rock, became the water cycle, became the light. Somewhere, in a scale of engineering smaller than your instruments can resolve, something is still running. The brightness is its exhaust. The heat is its breath. The water is a byproduct of whatever it is still doing down here, in this small and secret pocket of the world's underbody.

You could reach out and touch the wall. The water on your fingertips would feel warm and faintly electric. The sharp edge of the stone would not cut you, though it looks like it should. Everything here looks like it should be dangerous and isn't. It simply *is* — active, bright, weeping, hot, and profoundly indifferent to observation.

The place will be here when you leave. It has been here for longer than the concept of *here* has existed. The fractures will propagate. The water will weep. The light will continue to have no source. And the small, impossible processes embedded in the mineral will continue their work — whatever that work is — in silence and in brightness, beneath everything you have ever known.
