The city climbs. That is its first and last truth. It is not built *on* a mountain, but *is* the mountain—a terraced, buckled, and cascading mass of constructed geology rising from a base of misty foothills to a summit perpetually gnawed by low clouds. The scale is overwhelming; from any given ledge or balcony, the eye can trace the city’s ascent for miles, only to lose it in the steam or the curve of the earth. It is a regional fact, the only fact.

The materials are uniform in their variety: a family of grey composites, poured and molded, that mimic the forms of natural stone—basalt columns, limestone shelves, sandstone drifts—but with a precision and repetition that betrays their origin. Every surface is this *made-stone*. There are no raw earth patches, no native soil. The ground underfoot is ribbed, grooved, or faceted, engineered for drainage and grip. The entire landscape is settled into its own state, a century-old equation of form and function balanced against the relentless pull of water and gravity.

And the water. It is everywhere, a weeping of the world. The air itself is a condensing breath, beading on metal handrails and the frilled leaves of the engineered flora. It gathers in the countless seams and micro-fissules of the made-stone, then travels, tracing dark, capillary lines down every vertical face. It drips from the underbellies of elevated transport ways, patters into collection grates that hum with subterranean pumps, and pools in the shallow, moss-furred basins that serve as public squares. The sound of the city is the sound of this perpetual, soft leakage—a hush of falling droplets, the distant gurgle of a thousand internal channels, and beneath it, the low, polyphonic hum of industry.

For this place is packed, saturated to the limit of its own engineering. There is no room between presences. Every terrace, every ledge, every cantilevered platform is occupied. Residential pods cluster like barnacles on the sides of larger infrastructure ribs. Workshops, with their glowing furnace-mouths, are sunk into the foundations of water treatment spirals. Vertical farms, heavy with luminous, fast-growing greens, hang in the voids between districts, their root systems tangled in a mesh of nutrient pipes. The vegetation is lush, aggressively so—not wild, but cultivated with a ferocious, utilitarian density. Broad-leafed climbers choke the less-frequented maintenance shafts. Velvety mosses, bred for rapid coverage, soften the acoustic profile of the plazas and form a spongy, damp carpet in the few open parks. Orchards of nut-and-fruit trees, stunted and hardy, line the windward terraces, their roots drinking from the perpetual seepage.

The light is dim, a diffuse silver-grey filtered through perpetual mist and the layered overhangs of the city itself. Sunlight is a rumor, a memory for the elder generations. True darkness never arrives, banished by the amber glow of workshop windows, the blue-white strips of transit tunnels, and the soft, phosphorescent pads that mark pedestrian ways. This is an industrial light, utilitarian and constant.

The turbulence is not in storms, but in the city’s own metabolism. The change is constant, granular, and chaotic. In one sector, a section of facade is being jackhammered away, the debris cascading down chutes into a grinder, while meters away, a new panel of pre-grown moss-concrete is being hoisted into place by a whining crane. Drones, like metallic insects, buzz through the air currents between terraces, carrying tools and components. The distant, rhythmic thud of pile drivers echoes from a far-off construction ledge, a heartbeat against the quiet drip. It is the anthill-energy of a place perpetually rebuilding itself, where erosion is not defeated but harnessed; the slow weathering of the made-stone is anticipated, measured, and fed back into the cycle of demolition and renewal.

Fauna is rare. There are no birds, no mammals of note. The ecosystem is one of engineered plants, fungi, and bacteria, all designed to process the air and water within parameters. The few animal presences are the scuttling, multi-legged maintenance drones that clean the grates, and the occasional, silent flight of a surveillance unit. The quiet, in its way, is profound—a damp, busy, mechanical quiet, where the dominant sounds are water, the thrum of engines, and the echo of your own footsteps on the grooved stone.

This is a world that has forgotten the natural substrate. It is a complete, self-consuming, and self-renewing artifact. The rules are simple: everything is made, everything is wet, everything is dense, and the only constant is the turbulent, quiet effort to remain in balance against the patient, dripping pull of its own existence.
