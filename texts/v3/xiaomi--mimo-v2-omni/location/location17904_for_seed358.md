The world here is a vast, sunken basin, a wound in the earth still weeping. The ground is not solid but a saturated sponge of black loam, mineral-veined clay, and gritty sand, all compacted so densely there is no space between—a solid, waterlogged mass that gurgles and sighs underfoot. It is a landscape of perpetual post-rain, not from the sky, but from the land itself. Water weeps from every pore, from the fractured faces of low, emerging bluffs, from the tangled roots of the sparse, salt-tolerant grasses that shiver in the humid air. The atmosphere is thick, warm, and condensing, heavy with the smell of wet stone, iron, and deep, anaerobic decay.

The scale is immense, a regional basin several miles across, but the density of its features makes it feel claustrophobic. There is no horizon, only a continuous, wrinkled plain of geologic infancy. Low ridges of shale and mudstone, their layers still soft and unconsolidated, twist and buckle across the terrain like the petrified sinews of some giant. They are not mountains, not yet; they are the raw bones of mountains, just coming into being through slow, tectonic groans and the patient work of water. Between them, shallow, brackish tarns pool in depressions, their surfaces scummed with yellow bacterial mats and reflecting the dim, diffuse light that filters through the perpetual steam-haze. The light is that of a permanent, overcast twilight, sourceless and grey, making the wet surfaces gleam dully.

The deafening sound is the landscape’s constant voice. It is not the roar of a river, but the unified, crushing noise of a million simultaneous processes: the high-pitched trickle and gurgle of water seeping from stone; the deep, resonant *thrum* of the earth itself, a vibration felt in the bones more than heard; the metallic groan and shudder of the scattered industrial remnants that stud the basin like rusted iron mushrooms. These structures—corroded pumping stations, skeletal frameworks of forgotten refineries, lengths of pipe that jut from the ground like broken ribs—are fused into the geology. They are not abandoned, but *absorbed*. Condensation beads on their rivets, and mosses trace the paths of old lubricant leaks. Their machinery is silent, but their presence amplifies the basin’s acoustic signature, acting as strange, echoing chambers for the land’s wet pulse.

Life, though sparse in its flora, is abundant in its fauna. Things squelch and skitter in the murk. Pale, eyeless crustaceans, their carapaces glistening with slime, pick their way across the mud. Small, heat-loving amphibians with vestigial limbs cling to the weeping rock faces, their skin patterns mimicking the mineral stains. In the distance, herds of larger, hoofed creatures move slowly, not grazing, but sifting the bacterial films from the water’s edge with broad, flat muzzles. They are silent, their movements part of the slow, patient dynamic of the place. Change here is measured in geological strata, in the imperceptible rise of the basin floor, in the gradual mineralization of a rusted bolt. The world is not dying, nor is it being born. It is in a state of prolonged, wet, and noisy gestation, a place where industry and geology have become indistinguishable, locked together in a dense, warm, and deafening embrace.
