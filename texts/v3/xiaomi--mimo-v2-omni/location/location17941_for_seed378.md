The basin is a kilometer-wide bowl of rust and obsidian, rimmed by bluffs of vesicular basalt that sweat a constant, hot dew. The air is thick with moisture that doesn't quite form clouds—it hangs as a suspended, shimmering haze, refracting the white-hot glare of a sun that seems too close. There is no green. The ground is a mosaic of thermal features: black silica flats that crackle underfoot, bubbling springs of iron-oxide red that leave scalding, rainbow-sheened deposits, and pale, chalky terraces of mineral precipitate that climb the slopes like frozen waterfalls.

The silence is the first thing you notice. It's not an absence of sound, but a compression of it into a narrow, sub-audible band. The hiss of steam vents, the *plink* of superheated droplets falling onto hot stone, the deep, grinding shudder of tectonic plates adjusting far below—it all blends into a profound, vibrational hum that you feel in your molten teeth. Above this, the only sharp sounds are the skittering of fauna and the distant, rhythmic *clang-hiss* of the Works.

The Works are the urban skeleton here. They are not buildings for habitation, but for processing. Great, lattice-like smelters and condenser towers climb the basin walls, their iron and steel skeletons stained with mineral bloom. They are active, but not manned. Automated hammers pound geodes into aggregate with earth-shaking regularity; sluices channel boiling, acidic water into vast evaporation pans where crystals grow in geometric perfection. Power is drawn from the planet's own fever—turbines sunk into hydrothermal vents spin with a subsonic whine, their energy pulsing through thick, insulated cables that root into the ground like metallic taproots. This is industry divorced from biology, a city built for and by machines, slowly becoming a geological feature itself.

Life, of a sort, abounds. It is not animal life as understood, but a mineral ecology. Geodes, hollow and resonant, are cracked open by six-legged creatures of glassy chitin, their interiors revealing delicate, singing crystal formations that the creatures farm and consume. Herds of lithovores, like amorphous blobs of living quicksilver, flow across the silica flats, absorbing metallic compounds and leaving behind trails of purified stone. In the perpetually damp shadows of the smelters, fungal-like colonies of piezoelectric filaments pulse with stored energy, giving off faint, blue light and a scent of ozone. They are preyed upon by sharp, ambulatory shards that move with the sound of grinding mica.

The dynamic here is one of relentless, slow transformation. The machines process the planet's geothermal outflow, and the fauna process the machines' waste and the new minerals they create. The water cycle is frantic: vapor rises, condenses on the cooler metal of the structures, runs down grooves cut for this purpose, and is channeled back into the thermal vents to begin again. It is a balanced, self-sustaining system of extraction and adaptation, where the line between natural and constructed has been dissolved by time, heat, and a patient, silent logic. The strangeness isn't in any one element, but in the perfect, quiet integration of it all—a world that is simultaneously a factory, a geothermal spring, and a thriving, mineral-based ecosystem, humming under a bright, wet sky.
