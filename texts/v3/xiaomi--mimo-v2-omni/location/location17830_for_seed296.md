The world here is a high, ancient plateau—a vast shelf scoured flat by eons of wind and ice, now settled into a profound and patient stability. The elevation is extreme, a tableland lifted into the thin, cool air where clouds snag and dissolve against its edges. The air itself is a permanent damp, a mist that condenses on every surface, beading on leaves and slicking the stone. It is not a rain, but a constant weeping of moisture from the sky, collecting in shallow, wandering runnels and dark, peaty pools that reflect a dim, pearlescent light.

Vegetation thrives in this persistent wetness, a dense, layered tapestry of green so saturated it feels almost solid. Great ferns with fronds like ribbed umbrellas crowd the margins of the watercourses. Mosses, in a hundred shades from jade to charcoal, furs every vertical surface and swallows the foundations of things. Climbing vines with pale, throbbing veins lace through the canopy of low, wind-shaped trees—gnarled, resinous conifers and broad-leafed giants whose roots grip the fractured rock. The growth is close, intimate, often brushing against you as you move. It smells of wet earth, crushed needles, and the faint, sweet decay of fungal blooms.

Into this primordial lushness, something else has been inserted. At intervals, the mossy floor is broken by the stark geometry of human endeavor: dark, iron-rail tracks half-buried in loam, leading to the skeletal remains of industrial trestles that span deep, eroded fissures. Factories or processing plants, built from soot-stained brick and corroded steel, huddle in the lee of rock outcrops. Their windows are shattered or clouded with grime, but from their interiors comes a low, rhythmic thrum—the sound of great, slow engines still turning, driven by geothermal vents or the endless drip of water through ancient turbines. The urban scale is one of abandoned district, a settlement built to extract and refine, now slowly being digested by the landscape. Power lines, heavy with moisture and draped in creeper vines, sag between leaning poles, their purpose forgotten.

The ecology is quiet. Birds are rare, their calls short and sharp in the muffled air. The fauna consists of small, furred things that dart through the underbrush, and a strange, slow-moving insect life that clings to the underside of leaves. The dominant motion is not of animals, but of the place itself: the constant shift of mist, the patient drip of water, the almost imperceptible sway of the highest trees in the ceaseless wind, and the deep, geological pulse of the machinery within the hills.

This is a place where two timescales exist at once. One is the frantic, mechanical heartbeat of the industrial past, still twitching in its mossy grave. The other is the deep, slow time of the plateau itself, where erosion is not a force of destruction but a form of sculpting, and where life doesn't conquer the ruins but incorporates them, making the iron part of the soil, the brick a new kind of stone for lichen to colonize. It is strangely peaceful, eerily active, a landscape in the final, stable stage of becoming something entirely new, born from the marriage of the ancient and the abandoned. The dim light filters through the mist and canopy, casting no sharp shadows, and in that grey, even time seems to pool and stagnate, like the water in the dark, silent basins scattered across the land.
