The land breathes water. Not in rivers or lakes, but from the stone itself. The ground is a mosaic of dark, vitreous basalt and paler, chalky limestone, worn so anciently that all edges have surrendered to a profound, undulating smoothness. It is a landscape of soft contours, as if the bones of the earth have been filed down over eons by a patient, humid hand.

The water is ubiquitous, a film over everything. It beads on the basalt, turning the black stone into a mirror for the vast, hazy sky. It seeps from fissures in the limestone, not in gushes, but in a constant, weeping exudation that makes the rock face shimmer. The air itself is a warm, wet cloth, thick with the smell of wet stone, of ozone from distant storms, and the faint, vegetal tang of decay that never quite overwhelms the mineral scent. Condensation drips from every low-hanging vapor-cloud, a soft, irregular percussion that forms the landscape’s constant, gentle rhythm.

Vegetation is a rare jewel here. Sphagnum moss, in shocking chartreuse and deep burgundy, cushions the dampest hollows, a living sponge holding a miniature world. Elsewhere, stiff, reed-like grasses grow in isolated tufts from cracks in the stone, their roots clinging to the meager sediment. A few, gnarled trees—perhaps ancestral to mangroves or cypress—stand like sentinels in the wider basins, their buttressed roots gripping the wet rock, their canopies sparse but wide-spreading, casting a lattice of dappled shade.

Yet life, in the faunal sense, is loud and present. The air hums and chitters. Insects in iridescent clouds rise from the mossy patches when disturbed, their wingbeats a faint buzz against the drip of water. Birds—strange, long-legged waders with plumage the color of wet slate—pick their way across the stone flats, their calls echoing in the open expanse: sharp, piping notes that cut through the humid air. From the deeper pools, hidden in eroded basins, comes the occasional *ploop* of a surface-breaking fish or amphibian, and a chorus of croaks and peeps begins as the warm day light begins to soften into a warmer dusk. There is the skitter of small, many-legged creatures in the crevices, and the distant, low moan of wind moving over the ancient, near-flattened topography—a sound that feels like the landscape’s own breath.

This is a place in its final, stable form. Erosion has done its ultimate work, leaving a continent of smoothed stone and shallow water, a world that feels both primordial and post-apocalyptic. Change is measured in the millimeter-deepening of a watercourse over a century, in the slow expansion of a moss colony. The sun, a diffuse disc through the perpetual steam, traces a warm arc across the sky, its heat driving the cycle of condensation and drip that is the lifeblood of this place. There is no horizon in the traditional sense, only a gradual blending of wet stone, hazy air, and the pale sky, extending in all directions in a scale that feels regional, a valley without walls. It is a quiet kingdom of water and stone, where life has learned to thrive not in abundance of form, but in the patient, loud, and scattered persistence of being.
