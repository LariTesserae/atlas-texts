The plateau is a city of stone that no hand has built, or that the hands have been ground back to stone. It stretches under a bright, diffuse sky—a light that seems less to fall than to reside in the air itself, a luminous haze with no visible source. The ground is not earth. It is a tightly packed, seamless mosaic of dark basalt and pale, chalky mineral, fitted together with the precision of a master stonemason, yet with the flow and logic of a crystalline growth. Every surface is *made*. There are no soils, no sands, only interlocking plates and facets.

And everything is wet.

A constant, gentle weeping saturates the world. It beads on the sharp, young edges of the stone forms, gathers in thin, silent sheets across the polished floors, and drips with a soft, metronomic irregularity from low-hanging protrusions. The air itself is heavy with moisture, a cool breath that condenses on every surface, blurring the hard lines of the architecture. There are no rivers, no lakes—the water simply emerges from the stone, or from the air, as if the entire plateau is exhaling a slow, eternal sigh.

The architecture—if it can be called that—is low and dense, packed without a single gap. It is a forest of hexagonal and pentagonal pillars, no taller than a person, rising in clustered, uneven ranks. They are not uniform; some are smooth as glass, others are rough with a kind of mineral barnacle, all of them glistening. Between them, the ground is a complex network of shallow channels and shallow depressions, all holding still, clear water. It is a labyrinth without doors or roofs, a city of pedestals and plinths, their purpose long since eroded away, if they ever had one. The scale is vast, extending in all directions until the luminous haze swallows the detail, suggesting a territory the size of a town or a small valley.

The coolness is absolute. It is not the biting chill of a winter wind, but the deep, stable cold of a stone cellar, or of water drawn from a deep well. This cold is preserved by the slowness of everything. The water moves, but it does not rush; it seeps and drips and pools with immense patience. Change here is measured in the slow thickening of mineral crusts, in the almost imperceptible deepening of a channel over a century. The quiet is profound. The only sounds are the drip of water, a soft, liquid *plink* that is swallowed instantly by the damp air, and a faint, almost sub-audible hum—the vibration of the plateau itself, perhaps, or the sound of moisture moving through microscopic pores in the stone.

There is no life. Not a lichen, not a spore, not a microbe recognizable as such. The "biology" here is mineral. In places, the stone flowers into delicate, frost-like structures of pure silica, growing in intricate, slow-motion blooms. Other surfaces are coated in a slick, vitreous film that is not algae, but a self-replicating mineral accretion, a kind of non-organic patina. These are the only ecologies: the competition of crystal forms, the slow conquest of one mineral species by another in the perpetual wet.

It is a world of aftermath. The sharpness of the features—the un-eroded edges of the pillars, the crisp facets of the ground mosaic—speaks of youth, of a landscape recently emerged or recently finished. Yet its processes are primordial, pre-biotic. It feels like the planet’s first draft of a world: all structure and chemistry, no messy biology. A constructed environment not by a mind, but by a set of physical laws that, in the absence of heat or frantic energy, have settled into a state of bright, wet, quiet, and endless becoming.
