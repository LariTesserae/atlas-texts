# The Keening Flats

The wind makes no sound here. That's the first wrongness.

You stand on a high plain — not a plateau exactly, but a broad upland basin ringed by eroded ridgelines that must once have been mountains and are now merely large hills, their slopes planed smooth by epochs of ice and weather until they look like the sleeping backs of animals. The elevation is enough that your breath should cloud, and it does, but the cold doesn't bite. It settles. It lives in the ground and rises through the soles of your feet as a kind of patient, mineral cold — the cold of stone that has never once known warmth, even in summer.

And then there is the green.

There is *only* the green.

---

Everything that is not stone is plant. The ground beneath you is not soil — it is root. Dense, interwoven, a mat of fibrous tissue so tightly packed that walking on it feels like walking on the taut skin of something alive, which it is. The root-mat extends in every direction to the horizon, broken only where shallow ponds have collected in depressions — grey, still, edged with ice that is more air than crystal, translucent and fracturing under its own weight. These ponds are not rivers or streams. They are condensation. The plain sweats. Water beads on the surfaces of leaves that are themselves the size of dinner plates, low-growing, waxy, so thickly laid that they overlap like scales. The water pools between them, gathers, slides into the hollows, and sits. Some of it sinks back into the root-mat, and you can almost feel the uptake — a slow, capillary pull downward, as if the ground is drinking through a thousand thousand straws.

But the green is not just on the ground. It climbs. It builds.

In every direction, the vegetation has structured itself upward into forms that look — in their scale and repetition and silent, grinding purpose — like a factory. Not a building. Not a *made* thing. But an *arrangement* of staggering complexity, all of it living, all of it plant. Columns of tightly wound stem tissue rise from the root-mat at intervals, spiraling, branching, and re-branching until they form lattice-towers fifteen or twenty meters high. The lattices support broad horizontal platforms of interlocking leaf-matter, dense and dark, and from these platforms hang curtains of pale rootlets that descend to the ground like the threads of a loom. The effect is architectural. You are standing inside a structure that was not built but *grown into being* by the simple, relentless logic of phototropism and gravity and the need to compete for light on a plain where every square centimeter is already claimed.

Between the towers, the ground-level vegetation surges. It moves. Not *fast* — not in any way your eye can track in real time — but when you look away and look back, the topography has changed. A mound of mossy growth that was knee-high is now at your hip. A shallow trough you could have stepped across has closed, sealed over with new tissue, as if it never existed. The plain is *growing* — not in the gentle, pastoral sense of a garden, but with the industrial throughput of a system operating at capacity, converting light and cold mineral water and the recycled matter of its own dead layers into new biomass at a rate that feels, standing in the middle of it, like a kind of silent violence.

The dead matter is everywhere, too. Beneath your feet, if you were to dig, you would find layer upon layer of compressed plant tissue — brown, black, peat-like, some of it already mineralite, turning to something that will one day be stone. The decomposition is slow in the cold, so the layers accumulate. The plain is building itself *upward*, centimeter by century, on its own dead body. The erosion works against this — the wind, when it comes, scours the exposed ridgelines, carries fine particulate of ancient plant-stuff in invisible currents, deposits it in the basin. The plain is a sink. It catches its own dust and grows on it.

And it is turbulent. That's the second wrongness, the one that makes the silence so disorienting.

Because the turbulence is *visible*. The lattice-towers sway — not in wind, because there is almost no wind, but in response to some internal pressure, some hydraulic force within their tissues that causes them to expand and contract in slow, rhythmic pulses. The leaf-platforms overhead ripple. The root-mat beneath you contracts, subtly, like a muscle flexing. New shoots erupt from the edges of the ponds, visible now, *actually visible*, pushing up through the water in real time, unfurling with the slow inevitability of something that has no choice. Old structures collapse — one of the lattice-towers at the edge of your vision simply *fails*, its base tissue giving way, and it topples in near-perfect silence, the impact absorbed by the dense mat below, the crash reduced to a muted, organic thud that you feel in your sternum more than you hear. Within minutes — actual minutes — new growth is climbing the fallen column, re-colonizing it, incorporating it back into the system.

It is an anthill. It is a bazaar. It is a factory floor. It is all of these things and it makes no sound.

You listen for insects, for birds, for any fauna at all, and there is almost nothing. A faint, occasional rustle in the deeper leaf-litter that might be a small mammal or might be the plant itself rearranging. A dark, darting shape at the edge of one of the ponds — too fast to identify, gone before you could point. The fauna here is rare not because the ecosystem is inhospitable but because the plant-matter has *colonized every niche*. There is no room. Every calorie of light, every molecule of water, every scrap of mineral nutrient is claimed by the green. Animals that once lived here have either left or been absorbed — and in a place like this, the distinction is hard to parse.

The cold preserves everything. The dead layers don't rot so much as *mummify*, becoming denser, harder, more structural. Some of the lattice-tower columns are not living tissue at all but this ancient, petrified plant-matter, serving as the load-bearing cores around which new growth spirals upward. The living wraps itself around the dead and uses it as bone. The whole plain is like this — a palimpsest of growth and preservation, each layer informing the next, the present literally standing on the compressed bodies of its ancestors.

And the light. The light is ordinary — a flat, white-grey sky, high and featureless, the sun diffuse behind it. No drama. No golden hour. Just sufficient illumination for the machinery of photosynthesis to continue its enormous, silent, world-building work. It is the light of a place that does not need beauty, only throughput.

You realize, standing there, that you are witnessing a system at its own equilibrium — or as close to equilibrium as something this alive can get. The erosion planing the ridges. The condensation pooling in the hollows. The growth surging upward and the death compressing downward and the whole plain breathing in its slow, turbulent, silent rhythm. It has been doing this for longer than anything you would recognize as a civilization. It will continue. It does not need you. It barely acknowledges that you are a different kind of thing from the cold and the water and the light.

You take a step, and the root-math shifts beneath your foot, accommodating you, already beginning to grow into the impression you've left.

You take another step.

The silence follows.
