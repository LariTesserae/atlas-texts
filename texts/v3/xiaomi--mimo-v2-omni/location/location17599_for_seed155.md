The Aerie Plateau stretches under a sky the colour of faded zinc, a high, wind-scoured tableland that has been sinking into itself for eons. The ground is not stone, nor earth, but a deep, springy mattress of root-mass and humus, metres thick, woven from a million generations of plants that now form the very substrate of life. This is the Green Deep, and it breathes.

Every surface is claimed. What were once cliffs are now vertical meadows, mantled in cascading mosses and arterial lichens that glow with a faint, phosphorescent blue in the perpetual damp. The air, cool and heavy with mist, condenses on every leaf and filament, sending silver trickles down the stems of towering, bone-white fungi-trees that form the skeletal architecture of this world. The forest canopy is a solid, undulating roof of interlocking leaves and bladder-like fronds that collect and store the moisture, dripping incessantly onto the sponge below. The sound is never still: a vast, wet hiss of transpiration, a percussive *plink* of drops, and beneath it, a low, groaning creak—the sound of the entire forest slowly, imperceptibly, settling under its own weight.

Here and there, the organic mat breaks, revealing not soil, but the fused, vitrified remnants of a prior age. These are the Bones of the First City. Slender spires of what was once glass and alloy jut from the green like bleached ribs, now strangled by epiphytic vines that pulse with a slow, rhythmic light. This is the urban layer: not a city built, but a city consumed and repurposed. The infrastructure remains—collapsed transit tubes, shattered plazas—but it is all scaffold for the true architecture. Dwellings are grown from cultivated, fast-hardening resin, shaped around the old girders, their windows mere gaps in a wall of living leaves. Paths are not paved; they are worn, spongy trails through the moss, lined with nodding, bell-shaped flowers that chime softly when brushed against, their tones discordant and haunting.

The industry is silent yet pervasive. Along the edges of what might have been a great boulevard, colossal, rust-red machines lie half-swallowed, their gears and pistons permanently seized. Yet, from their carcasses, new forms of activity emerge. Fungal hyphae thread through their gears, and from these hyphae hang clusters of glassy pods. Inside each pod, a viscous, amber fluid churns, heated by a geothermal tap drilled deep into the plateau's bedrock. This is the sap-sweetening process, the "factory" work of the plateau. The sweet, resinous exudate is harvested by climbing mammals with slick, prehensile fur—creatures so rare and furtive they are more felt than seen—whose flickering presence at the edge of vision is the only sign of fauna beyond the insects and the blind, burrowing things in the Deep.

The true weirdness lies in the light and the echoes. The normal light of the sky is fractured by the moisture and the crystal-spined plants, casting prismatic bands that slide across the ground. But sometimes, in your peripheral vision, those bands hold still, forming geometric shapes—doors, archways—that vanish when you turn to look. Sound behaves strangely. A wind through a canyon of old steel might carry the distant, garbled echo of a shout, not from the past, but from a place that is *laterally adjacent*. The plateau is old, and its reality is thin, worn smooth. Time does not so much flow here as it pools and eddies. A drop of water may fall from a frond, hit the moss, and then fall again from the same frond. You walk a path you have not yet walked.

The air smells of damp soil, of ozone from the crackling discharges that occasionally arc between the highest spires, and of something faintly metallic, like blood. The dominant sound, however, is a deep, resonant *hum*. It comes from the roots of the great fungi-trees, a subsonic vibration felt in the bones—the sound of the Green Deep itself, drawing water and sustenance from the ancient bedrock, a lullaby for a world that has already ended and is patiently, verdantly, becoming something else.
