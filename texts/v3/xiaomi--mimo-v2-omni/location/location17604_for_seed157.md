# The Still Basin

You would not find it by looking. You would find it by falling — by following some game trail or drainage line up into high country where the air thins and cools, then stepping wrong, and slipping through a gap in the rock into a place that does not invite arrival.

It is a basin. Perhaps a hundred paces across, perhaps less — hard to judge, because the scale of things resists measurement. The walls rise close on all sides, dark stone worn smooth and round by uncountable ages of water seeping from within the rock itself. The walls *weep*. Not from rain, not from snowmelt — from some deep saturation in the stone, as though the mountain here is a sponge and this is where it wrings itself out. Every surface is slick. Every surface gleams faintly in what little light reaches the floor, and the little light that reaches the floor is almost none at all.

Darkness is the medium. Not the darkness of a sealed cave — there is sky above, a rough oval of it, but the basin sits deep enough and the overgrowth is thick enough that what filters down is the darkness of a forest floor at midnight, permanent and complete. Your eyes adjust and find almost nothing to adjust *to*. There is phosphorescence — a faint green-blue — growing in patches on the wet stone and on the bark of the trees, and this is how you see. This is how anything sees here. The light is alive. It is fungal, or it is bacterial, or it is something that has no name because nothing has ever needed to name it.

The trees are not tall. They cannot be tall — there is not enough sky-reach for that. But they are *thick*, gnarled, dense-grained, their trunks swollen with water uptake, their bark dark and ridged and furred with moss. Every surface hosts something. The mosses here are extraordinary — deep green, black-green, blue-green, luminous green — layered three and four and five deep, over every root, every stone, every fallen trunk that has not yet finished decomposing and may never finish decomposing, because the rates of decay and growth here have reached some ancient, locked equilibrium. Nothing outpaces anything else. The fallen are composted at exactly the rate the living produce new dead. The system is *tight*. It is saturated with itself. There is no room for anything additional, and nothing new has arrived in a very long time.

Between the trees, the undergrowth is a wall. Ferns with fronds wide as your arm span. broad-leafed plants whose surfaces are wet not from rain but from the same condensation that beads on every surface here — the air itself is heavy with moisture, cool and thick, hovering just above the dew point at all times, always about to drip, always dripping. You cannot touch anything here that is dry. The ground is a floor of roots and moss and black soil and standing water — not pooled exactly, but *present*, the water table inches below the surface at most, sometimes breaking through into miniature seeps that travel nowhere because everywhere is already wet.

And then: the fauna.

It is *everywhere*. It fills the volume of the basin the way water fills a glass. The air is thick with insects — not biting insects, not swarming insects, just *present* insects, a density of small flying things that moves in slow clouds and settles on the luminous bark and the wet leaves and the still air itself. Moths with wings like wet paper. Beetles in the moss, so many that the moss *shifts* — you realize after a moment that what you took for the gentle sway of vegetation in a breeze is actually the collective movement of ten thousand small bodies crawling through it. The ground is alive with things you cannot clearly see in the dark — small, many-legged, soft-bodied things moving with patient deliberation over the roots and the stones.

Larger things exist here too. There are amphibians — or things like amphibians — clinging to the wet walls of the basin, round-bodied and smooth-skinned, the size of your palm or larger, their skin faintly luminescent in the same green-blue as the fungi. They do not move when you look at them. They are not startled. Nothing in this place seems startled by anything, because nothing here has changed in so long that surprise has been selected out of the behavioral repertoire. They breathe slowly. Their throats pulse. The largest of them might be the size of a cat, hunkered in a cleft of rock, breathing water and air equally, its eyes clouded, seeing nothing, needing to see nothing.

There are small mammals — or mammal-like things — in the root tangles. You hear them sometimes: a faint rustling, a click. They are nocturnal in the way that everything here is nocturnal, which is to say the distinction does not apply. They are always active, always foraging, always present in the undergrowth, filling every available niche with a body shaped precisely to fill it.

What is most striking is the *sound*. For all this density of life, the basin is almost silent. Not silent — never silent — but held at a volume just above the threshold of hearing. The insects create a hum that is less a sound than a pressure, felt in the sinuses and the bones. Water drips — not rhythmically but constantly, from leaf to leaf, from stone to stone, a thousand tiny impacts that blur into a single whisper. Something breathes in the dark. Something shifts its weight on moss-covered roots. But there is no birdsong, no wind (the basin is too deep, too sheltered, too still), no weather, no change. The dynamic parameter of this place reads as *zero*, and you feel it — not as calm, not as peace, but as a kind of permanence so total it borders on the suffocating. This basin has been exactly like this for longer than you can reasonably contemplate. The water has wept from these stones at the same rate. The moss has grown at the same rate. The insects have hummed at the same frequency. The equilibrium is not fragile. It is *iron*. It has persisted because nothing has been able to disrupt it, and nothing wants to. The place does not want to change. It does not want anything. It simply *is*, and will continue to be, until the mountain it sits in erodes to nothing, which it will not, because erosion here is slow, and the stone is old, and the balance is established.

You feel, standing in this place, that you are the only thing that has ever been here that did not belong. And even that feeling passes, because the place does not react to you. It does not acknowledge your presence. You are a brief anomaly in a system that is complete, and the dark water beads on your skin, and the phosphorescence glows on the stone behind you as you climb back out, and the basin returns to what it was: full, still, luminous, breathing, saturated with its own ancient, unchanging life.
