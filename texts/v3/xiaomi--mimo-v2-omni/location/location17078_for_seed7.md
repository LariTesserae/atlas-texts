The air hangs thick and hot, a breath of geothermal warmth that clings to the skin. It is a subterranean space, a hollowed-out chamber of irregular dimensions, its boundaries lost in a gloom that drinks the eye. The scale is intimate, almost architectural—a vaulted cavern perhaps, or a vast, abandoned excavation, its ceiling high enough to gather echoes but close enough to feel oppressive. The light is minimal, a residual dusk that seems to seep from the stone itself, or perhaps from the faint, sickly phosphorescence of certain growths. True darkness lurks in the far corners and in the deep crevices of the walls.

The stone is young, its edges sharp and freshly cleaved, showing none of the soft rounding of ancient water-carved caverns. It is a dark, fine-grained basalt or slate, slick with condensation that beads and drips in a steady, silent rhythm. Here and there, the rock faces are scarred with geometric patterns—long, straight grooves and bolt-holes—faint marks of some past industry, now overgrown. Amongst these scars, metal artifacts lie half-buried: a rusted drum split open, a coil of corroded conduit, a single, massive gear fused to the floor by time and damp. They are scattered sparsely, like relics of a forgotten ritual.

Water is present but not abundant. It gathers in shallow, still pools on the uneven floor, black and reflective, and beads on every surface. The air is saturated with a dampness that is almost palpable, a mist that hangs in the still air, yet it never quite coalesces into rain. It is an intermittent moisture, a constant weeping from the walls and ceiling.

From this dampness, life erupts. The vegetation is shockingly lush, a verdant tapestry that defies the gloom. Great ferns with fronds like prehistoric hands unfurl in the weak light. Mosses of a deep, velvety green carpet the stone in patches, glowing with a faint, internal luminescence of their own—a pale, blue-green cold-light that does little to illuminate, but paints the cavern with pools of spectral color. Fleshy, leafless vines with translucent stems climb the walls, tapping directly into the weeping rock. Strange, bulbous fungi cluster in the warmer seams, exhaling spores in slow, visible puffs that drift and settle with glacial patience. Yet for all its lushness, the growth is sparse in distribution; wide swathes of bare, sharp stone separate these islands of green, creating a balance between life and empty, dark mineral.

There are no animal sounds. No insectile chirp, no rustle of unseen things. The silence is profound, broken only by the tiny, percussive *plink* of water droplets hitting a pool, or the almost inaudible, low-frequency hum that seems to emanate from the rock itself—a vibration felt in the bones more than heard. This hum is the only hint of the industrial past: perhaps a generator, impossibly old, still turning somewhere in the labyrinthine depths, its motion so slow as to be eternal.

The heat is relentless, a dry, stone-baked heat that belies the dampness, making the air shimmer slightly near the floor. It rises from below, from the planet’s inner workings, and seems to pulse in time with the slow dynamic of the place. Change here is measured in centuries: the gradual advance of moss over metal, the patient enlargement of a crack by root and water, the imperceptible settling of dust on a forgotten lever.

This is an unusual place, a wound in the earth that has been partially healed by a strange, dark ecology. It is a silent, hot, damp grotto where industry has been consumed by a slow, verdant rot, and the only watch kept is by the blind, dripping stone and the softly glowing plants.
