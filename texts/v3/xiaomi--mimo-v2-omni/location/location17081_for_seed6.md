# The Upperworks

You arrive at elevation. You know this because the air is thinner, because the light has that particular clarity of height, because there is wind — though it makes almost no sound. The place is small. You could walk its extent in minutes. And yet it is *complete* in a way that makes the word "small" feel insufficient.

Everything here is made.

There is no ground. There is no bedrock, no soil, no original surface of the earth. What you stand on — what you *are surrounded by* — is a terraced infrastructure of poured concrete, riveted steel plating, copper piping, and heavy ceramic tile, all of it arranged in a dense, interlocking matrix that steps upward and inward like the interior of some industrial cathedral that has been turned inside out. The geometry is industrial but not geometric. It *fits itself*. Every surface seems to have been placed with awareness of what would rest against it, what would grow from it, where the water would need to go.

And the water.

The water is everywhere and it is not falling. It rises. It seeps. It *exudes*. From the seams between ceramic tiles, from the mouths of copper pipes that jut from walls at irregular intervals, from the joints of riveted plates — water emerges in a constant, patient saturation. Not a flood. Not a deluge. Something slower and more total. Every surface glistens. Every channel, every carved trough, every shallow basin and angled gutter carries a slow film of movement, the water finding paths through the infrastructure with the unhurried certainty of something that has been doing this for a very long time. There are pools — warm, clear, faintly mineral-scented — gathered in basins of hammered copper and grey concrete. There are cascades, but they are hand-sized, sheeting over lips of metal and stone in thin, glassy curtains. The whole structure breathes water. It is a machine for water. Or it is water's idea of a machine.

And the vegetation.

This is where the strangeness begins to settle in your chest.

It is *abundant*. Every surface that is not actively channeling water is colonized — but not by the mosses and ferns you might expect. The growth here is architectural. Great curtains of pale, broad-leafed plants drape from upper terraces, their roots gripping steel beams and concrete parapets, their leaves the size of open hands, waxy and green-dark and slightly translucent when the light catches them. Vine-things with segmented stems coil through pipe-runs and emerge from ventilation gaps, flowering occasionally in tight clusters of cream and ochre. In the basins where warm water gathers, dense rafts of a sedge-like growth crowd the edges, their tips curling inward as if in thought. From vertical surfaces — walls, the faces of steps, the insides of arches — a creeping groundcover spreads in fractal patterns, its tiny leaves alternating between green and a deep, bruised red. The vegetation is not wild. It is not manicured. It exists in a state of *occupation*, as if it and the infrastructure reached an agreement long ago about who would hold what, and where the boundaries lay, and that those boundaries were not to be revisited.

The warmth is ambient. Not tropical, not oppressive — just *present*, the way warmth is present in a room where many people have been sitting, even though the room is now empty. It rises from the water. It seems to rise from the infrastructure itself, as if the concrete and copper are exhaling. You are aware, standing in this place, that it is *maintaining* something — a temperature, a saturation, a condition. The slowness of the dynamic, the patience of the water's emergence, suggests cycles measured in decades or centuries. This place is not in a hurry. It has never been in a hurry.

And the silence.

It is not empty silence. It is *full* silence. The kind that has texture and weight. You can hear water — you are always hearing water — but it is the most intimate scale of sound: the lip of a trickle over a ceramic edge, the barely-audible plink of a droplet into a pool, the faint hiss of seepage through a porous joint. These sounds do not accumulate into noise. Each one remains distinct, separate, identifiable, as if the silence between them is a substance that refuses to let them merge. There is no wind-sound. No mechanical sound. No hum of industry, despite the industrial nature of everything you can see. Whatever engines or systems operate here — and something must operate, something must *tend* — they are beyond your hearing. Or they have finished their work for now.

You look for fauna. The lushness demands it — insects, at least, or the small wet sounds of something moving through the undergrowth. There is nothing. No buzz. No chirp. No rustle. The leaves are undisturbed. The pools are unbroken. The vegetation grows and the water flows and the warmth holds and there is *no animal presence whatsoever*. This is not a ruin. This is not abandoned. The place is clearly functioning, clearly alive in its vegetal and hydraulic way. But it was not built for creatures. Or it was built for creatures that have not yet arrived. Or it was built for something that is not a creature in any sense you recognize.

The strangeness sits in the geometry. In the way certain clusters of pipes terminate in shapes that suggest joints, hinges, orifices — but go nowhere, serving no hydraulic function you can identify. In the way the vegetation, if you look at it from a distance, seems to follow the infrastructure *too precisely*, as if it is not growing from it but is *part of it* — leaf and vine as biological extension of steel and concrete, the green circuitry of a system whose purpose you cannot read. In the way the water, always the water, seems to be *going somewhere* — not to a drain, not to an outlet, not to the sea, but *inward*, deeper into the structure, through passages you cannot see, toward something at the center or the bottom or the top that you cannot reach.

You are standing on a constructed peak, high above whatever lies below. The structure ends at its edges — you can see where it ends, where the last terrace drops away into sky and distant ground. It is finite. It is bounded. You can see the whole of it from a single vantage, a dense, wet, green-lit knot of humanmaking and growing-things and endlessly patient water, holding its warmth in the high air, saying nothing, keeping whatever it keeps.

It is not waiting. Waiting implies uncertainty. This place has already decided. You simply have not arrived at the part where you understand what it decided, or why, or for whom.

The water seeps. The leaves turn slowly toward a light source that does not move.

You listen to the silence, and the silence listens back, and neither of you finds what you were looking for.
