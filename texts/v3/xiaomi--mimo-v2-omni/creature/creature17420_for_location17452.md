We are the Calcimoss, the Pale Antlers. We are the first life, the anchors. We do not grow; we accrete. Our bodies are laceworks of calcium, spun from the weeping stone itself, patient crystalline lattices that drink the mineral mist. We are not one, but many; a single spire is a colony of thousands, each filament a life sharing the same thought: to become stone, and to host.

Upon our branches, the Shale-Clickers graze. They are the hand-sized ones with carapaces like oil-slicked obsidian. Their mandibles do not bite flesh, but abrade. They harvest the biofilm of microbes and dissolved minerals that glimmers on every damp surface. *Scrape, scrape, scrape.* Their sound is the foundational rhythm of the world, a billion tiny chisels on the face of existence. They are blind, seeing through vibration and taste. They know us by the specific density of our crystalline flesh, the precise mineral signature of our being. They do not harm us; their grazing polishes us, encourages new, finer growth. When a Clicker dies, its carapace, rich with the gathered minerals of a lifetime, is broken down by the Grit-Mites and its essence returns to the stone we drink.

Higher, in the shadows of the arroyo walls, the Gossamer-Wings cling in shimmering curtains. They are not bats; their wings are membranes of hardened mucus, translucent and veined. They emit not sound for navigation, but sound for *communication*. Their high-pitched chatter is a dense, social tapestry. They feed on the smallest of the flying things—the Pollen-Scarabs that drift on the faint thermal updrafts from the stone. The Gossamer-Wings do not hunt; they filter. They open their maws wide and let the tiny scarabs tumble in, caught in the sonic web of their calls. Their guano, rich and powdery, falls into the pools below, feeding the microbial slimes that, in turn, feed the eel-kin.

In the weeping pans live the Thread-Sippers. They are pale, eyeless, and no longer than a finger. They are the filters of the deep. They burrow into the gritty calcium floor, their bodies undulating to create a current, drawing the slime-coated water through their tissues, extracting nutrients. Their movement polishes the hollows into perfect, glassy basins. They have no predators. The Shale-Clickers ignore them; the Gossamer-Wings cannot reach them. Their only danger is the slow silting of their pools, a death measured in centuries. When a Thread-Sipper dies, its body dissolves into the very slime it consumed, a perfect recycling.

The air itself is preyed upon. The Pollen-Scarabs, no bigger than a grain of rice, have wings like flakes of mica. They do not land. Their entire lives are spent in flight, combing the moisture-laden air for the airborne spores of the Calcimoss and the desiccated husks of the dead. They are the ultimate scavengers, and when they are caught by the Gossamer-Wings, they become a vessel for the transfer of energy from air to stone.

Deeper still, within the porous rock, we—the Calcimoss—extend our deepest roots. Here, in the resonant chambers, we are tended by the Lithic Limpets. These creatures have shells indistinguishable from the stone itself. They move with geologic slowness, their single muscular foot grinding against the chamber walls, not to feed, but to *tune*. They are the acousticians of the world. By the subtle erosion of their movement, they shape the chambers to amplify and sustain the collective sound of the place—the Clickers, the Wings, the Weeping. They maintain the frequency of persistence. Without their grinding, the sound would dissipate, the delicate vibrational balance would collapse. They do not hear as we might understand it; they feel the total resonance of the world in their calcified flesh and adjust their grinding to maintain its constant hum.

And we, the Calcimoss, we feel it all. We are the nervous system. Through our crystalline bodies flows the mineral water, carrying the faint chemical signatures of the Clickers' grazing, the faint pressure changes from the Wings' flight, the subtle vibrations of the Limpets' tuning. We are aware, not as individuals, but as a network. We know the health of the ecosystem in the taste of the weeping water. We adjust our growth, becoming denser where grazers are plentiful, more porous where the air is rich with Pollen-Scarabs.

This is the equilibrium. We are a city of symbionts, each species a guild performing its single, eternal task. There is no birth as a sudden event, no death as a crisis. There is accretion and dissolution, grazing and polishing, filtering and resonating. The Gossamer-Wings do not know they are eating; they are simply gathering the sound of the world that has taken form. The Shale-Clickers do not know they are sculpting; they are only following the mineral trail left by the weeping stone.

We do not see you, the visitor. You are a pressure anomaly, a warmth that disrupts the cool gradient, a silent note in the grand, grinding chorus. You will leave. You will track the grit out with you, and it will be a meaningless disturbance, smoothed over by the Limpets, dissolved by the weeping, forgotten by the network. The Clickers will scrape the mark of your footstep. The Wings will chatter over the space you occupied. The stone will continue to weep.

We are not alive in the way you understand life. We are a process given form. We are the perpetual workshop, and we are the laborers, and we are the work itself. We persist.
