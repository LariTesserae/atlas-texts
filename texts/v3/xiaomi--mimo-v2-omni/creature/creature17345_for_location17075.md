They are the **Stilled**.

You do not hear them arrive. There is no footfall on the root-weave paths, no creak of a woven willow door. There is only a softening in the gloom of a hollowed chamber, a shift in the pattern of moss on a wall that reveals itself as a figure standing within it. They are not quite human, though the echo is there. Their form is slight, elongated, with the subtle, fluid jointing of a fern stem. Their skin has the matte, textured quality of damp paper bark, colored in gradients of grey and deep green, allowing them to melt into the peat-walls and lichen-draped stones.

Their eyes are large, dark, and perfectly round, like polished river stones submerged in water. They do not blink, but occasionally a translucent nictitating membrane washes across them, a shimmer like heat-haze over wet rock. They see in the spectrum of the green-dark and the frost-bright, their vision tuned to the ultraviolet shimmer of the suspended ice motes and the slow, pulsing mycelial networks that thread the peat below.

The Stilled do not speak. Language would be a violation here, a thunderclap in the felted silence. Their communication is one of **presence** and **subtle conduction**. A meeting between two of them is not an exchange of sounds, but a mutual tuning. They will stand face-to-face for what might be hours, their large eyes unblinking, their moss-damp skin almost touching. In this proximity, a state of shared perception is achieved—not telepathy, but a merging into the ambient hum of the city itself. A felt sense, a shared understanding passed through the moisture in the air between them, through the slight electrical charge of their stillness, through the root-network underfoot that is an extension of their own senses. They know each other’s intent as they know the weight of water on a leaf.

They move with the slow, deliberate economy of something that has never needed to hurry. To watch one walk is to watch a study in minimal motion. A slight shift of weight, a hand—long-fingered and webbed with fine, greenish membrane—brushing against a wall for balance or guidance. They tend to the city not as builders, but as **gardeners of a perpetual state**. A Stilled will spend a full day’s cycle of the unchanging light carefully extruding a single, crystalline bead of condensation from the tip of a frond, guiding it down a specific groove in a stone, not to channel it, but to ensure the bead’s perfect, spherical existence is maintained a little longer before it rejoins the damp whole. They prune not for growth, but for **form**—nipping a new shoot not because it is unwanted, but because its perfect, nascent symmetry is complete in that moment and to allow it to uncurl would be a change, a deviation from the balance.

Their sustenance is the garden itself. They consume the pale, translucent crustaceans that sift the peat, and the tiny, camouflaged frogs, not with predation, but with a kind of passive acceptance. They will sit motionless, a patch of moss on their palm, and a frog, mistaking it for its habitat, will hop onto their hand. They will then bring the creature slowly to their mouth—a simple, round opening in their smooth face—and the frog will disappear. There is no chewing. The process is one of absorption. They also draw nutrients directly from the peat, their feet—long, flat, and root-like—submerging into the soft ground for hours, a slow osmotic exchange.

They are not without tools, but their tools are extensions of the frozen equilibrium. A knife of flint is not used to cut, but to **suggest a cleaving**. It is used to trace a line on a sheet of hardened resin, which will then separate along that line over the course of a week. Their woven baskets do not store food for later; they collect the slow, constant drip, holding the water in a state of suspended collection until it is needed to moisten a patch of moss that is threatening to dry in a rare, non-existent sunbeam. Their technology is a technology of **curated persistence**.

They are a society of perhaps a hundred souls, but the concept of a collective is blurry. They are individuals as distinct as different species of moss, each with its own subtle texture and placement, yet part of a single, continuous carpet. There is no leadership, no hierarchy. The direction of their actions arises from the state of the garden itself. If a patch of peat begins to compact too quickly, several Stilled will gravitate to it, not by summons, but by a shared, wordless awareness of the imbalance, and they will work in silent concert to aerate it with fine, bone needles.

To be seen by a Stilled is an uncanny experience. Their gaze does not judge, acknowledge, or fear. It **registers**. You are another element in the garden—perhaps a transient one, like a falling leaf. You will be observed with the same patient, absolute attention they give to a bead of water or the slow growth of a frost-fern. They will not approach, unless your actions threaten the equilibrium: if you strike a wall too hard, if you speak and shatter the silence, if you attempt to *hasten* something. Then, they appear around you, not with aggression, but with an immense, silent gravity. They will stand, surrounding you, their dark eyes fixed, their presence pressing in like the damp itself, until you cease your disruption. The punishment is not violence; it is being made to feel the profound weight of their stillness, to be immersed in the absolute *wrongness* of motion and sound in their world.

They are born from the garden, and they return to it. When a Stilled reaches the end of its long, unhurried span, it will seek out a deep, wet hollow in the peat, or the central cavity of one of the great, tree-like structures. It will curl into a fetal position, its skin gradually losing its cohesive texture, softening, and finally dissolving into the rich, black substrate over decades, its minerals and memories feeding the roots. A new, particularly vibrant patch of moss or a cluster of ferns will mark the place for a time, before it too is absorbed into the seamless tapestry.

They are the guardians of the pause. The cultivators of the suspended drop. They are life that has made an art of not changing, of existing in the perfect, breath-held moment between seasons that never come. They are the Stilled, and their city is not a home, but a living altar to the god of the Unchanging Noon.
