They are not born, and they do not die. They are the **Stone-Sitters**, and they have always been here, condensing from the same damp air that feeds the terraces.

Their forms are human in the way a riverbed is human-shaped by the water that flows through it—suggestive, but not definitive. They are tall and lean, as if drawn upward by the mountain’s yearning, with skin the pale grey of wet limestone. It is cool to the touch and faintly porous, often hosting a fine lacework of the same lichen that crusts the walls. Their eyes are wide and dark, the colour of deep cistern water, adapted to the flat, silver-grey light; they do not squint, and their gaze holds the patient, unfocused quality of the mist itself.

They move with a slow, fluid economy, their limbs seeming to glide through the thick air. Their clothing is not woven but grown—shaped from the broad, waxy leaves of the terrace plants, layered and stitched with their own saliva into supple, waterproof garments that cling to their bodies like a second skin. It rustles softly, a sound indistinguishable from the rustle of the gardens.

Their society is a silent choreography. They communicate in subtle shifts of posture, in the almost imperceptible tapping of fingernail against knuckle, in the collective direction of their stillness. A gathering in a plaza is not a crowd, but a formation, like crystals aligning in a solution. They understand each other as the cisterns understand the trickling channels—a passive, complete knowing.

Their work is ritual, not production. A **Stone-Sitter** at a loom is not making cloth; they are *performing* weaving. The shuttle passes, the thread is drawn, the pattern emerges with glacial slowness—a geometric echo of the terrace walls. When the pattern reaches a certain complexity, the weaver will patiently unravel it from the beginning, the thread returning to its blank state. The act is the point. It is a meditation that harmonizes with the sub-audible gnawing of the stone-borers, a physical prayer to the equilibrium.

They cultivate the terraces not for harvest, but for communion. They do not eat the fungi or the moss-grain in a way one would recognize as consumption. They will sometimes sit for days beside a broad-leafed plant, their hands resting on the soil, their pores and the plant’s stomata exchanging breaths and minute exudations of mineral-rich dew. They are nourished by the process, by being a conduit in the water cycle. The true nourishment is the damp air itself, which they inhale in long, slow draughts, their chests rising and falling with the rhythm of the mist.

The creatures of the terraces are their kin and their charge. They do not hunt the six-legged, iridescent scuttlers. Instead, they may gently guide them from the cracks in the dwellings, their fingers tracing paths for the tiny creatures to follow back into the moss. The jade-skinned amphibians in the reed-beds are oracles; a Stone-Sitter will sit by a cistern for an age, listening to the liquid bursts, interpreting the patterns of the calls as one reads weather in the clouds. The high-soaring *kethra* are their eyes, their distant, silent thoughts given form. And the deep, bone-vibrating gnawing of the *stone-borers* is the mountain’s heartbeat, a sound they feel in their own marrow, a reminder of the deep, mineral patience that underlies all.

They have no names for themselves as individuals. They are facets of the same consciousness, a consciousness that is the mountain’s own quiet self-awareness. A new one does not arrive with birth, but occasionally, in a damp alcove or on a lonely terrace, the mist will thicken, coalesce, and over days, a form will slowly precipitate from the grey—another limb of the same being, rising with the same slow grace, ready to join the endless, unchanging dance.

They are the memory of a beginning that never ends. They are the keepers of the perfect, frozen moment. They sit, they weave, they tend, they breathe. They are the living stillness at the heart of the high, cold terracescape, and they will be here when the sharp peaks have softened to hills, and when they are sharp again, for time, here, is a circle drawn in the condensation on a slate roof, wiped clean and drawn again, forever.
