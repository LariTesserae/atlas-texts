The life that endures here has learned to sing in harmony with the hum. It does not fight the heat or the mineral brine; it metabolizes them, builds its very flesh from the factory's leavings and the planet's seepings. To understand them, you must first understand their world's pulse—the slow, deep tectonic inhale and exhale, the rapid, frantic cycle of water, the rhythmic, metallic heartbeat of the Works.

In the shadow of the great condenser towers, where the air is thick enough to taste as wet, iron-rich grit, live the **Glasscrackers**. These are the six-legged artisans, their bodies a lattice of transparent, vitreous chitin that acts as both armor and solar lens, focusing the oppressive light into internal heat. They move in slow, deliberate herds, their feet making no sound on the silica flats. Their target is the geode—the planet's stone fruit. They do not break them open with brute force. Instead, they cluster around one, their bodies aligning until the focused light from their carapaces converges into a single, intense point. The geode heats unevenly, stress fractures singing through its crystal heart until, with a sub-audible *pop*, it cleaves perfectly along its natural geometry. Inside, the Glasscrackers do not feast immediately. They tend the delicate, singing crystals—the "Heart-Lattice"—using specialized mouthparts to deposit trace minerals and vibrate their limbs at specific frequencies to encourage growth. These crystals are their crop, their shelter, and, it is theorized, their means of communication—a silent, resonant language of tuned stone.

Flowing like mercury across the vast, cracked plains are the **Lithovore Congregations**. They are not individuals but fluid, democratic collectives. A single Congregation might appear as a shimmering, quicksilver puddle ten meters wide, following subterranean thermal gradients. When they encounter a vein of unprocessed ore left by the automated hammers, or a trail of metalite dust from the sluices, they engulf it. Their amorphous bodies secrete a solvent that dissolves the target material at a molecular level, absorbing the metal ions and leaving behind a path of stunningly pure, sterilized stone—a blank slate. The purified stone is their waste and their legacy, a canvas upon which the next generation of mineral life may take hold. They are the silent, essential recyclers, turning the Works' chaotic output into uniformity.

In the perpetual dusk beneath the overhanging smelters, where condensation drips in a steady, scalding rain, the **Capacitor Fleece** thrives. It is not one organism, but a colonial filament, a living web of piezoelectric tendrils that cling to iron struts and warm stone. It glows with a cold, blue phosphorescence, a captured bioluminescence from a reaction between the minerals it consumes and the electrical charge it generates. The Fleece pulses in slow waves, absorbing the kinetic energy of dripping water and the ambient vibrational hum, converting it into chemical energy stored in crystalline nodules. It smells, sharply, of ozone and wet metal. It is the base layer of the smelter's shadow-ecology. Feeding upon it are the **Grinders**—sharp, ambulatory shards of composite mineral, no larger than a human hand. They look like fragments of broken machinery or fractured geode, but they move with a skittering, purposeful gait, their edges honed by a constant process of self-abrasion. They harvest the Capacitor Fleece by shearing it with their edges, their grinding motion producing the distinct sound of mica on slate that punctuates the basin's hum. Their waste is a fine, conductive dust that nourishes new Fleece growth.

Higher up, on the chalky terraces where the mineral precipitate forms its frozen waterfalls, live the **Terrace-Knitters**. These are the slowest of all. They resemble nothing so much as clusters of rust-colored stalagmites, barely distinguishable from their surroundings. They are sessile filter-feeders, their porous bodies drawing in the superheated, mineral-laden mist that constantly wets the slopes. They extract silicates and trace metals, building their own bodies into intricate, fractal patterns that strengthen the terrace walls against erosion. Over decades, they are the architects of the landscape itself, knitting the precipitate into stable, honeycombed structures that become habitats for smaller, microscopic lithophiles.

The silent logic of this world dictates that nothing is wasted, no energy is static. The Glasscrackers leave geode husks that become shelters for Grinders. The Lithovores' purified stone plains are colonized by new fungal-like growths that the Capacitor Fleece can later spread upon. The Works' mechanical vibrations are a constant energy source for the Fleece and a navigational cue for the Congregations. The system is a closed loop of astonishing resilience. It is life not as a conquest, but as a patient, inevitable accretion—a living patina upon the face of a world that is, itself, both the factory and the raw material. Under the bright, wet sky, they persist: a mineral bestiary, each creature a functional component of the geothermal engine, moving, growing, and transforming in a slow, silent ballet of survival and synthesis.
