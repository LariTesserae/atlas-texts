The creatures of the Underloom are not visitors; they are expressions of it. To know one is to understand the place itself, for they are built of its rules.

The great grazer is the **Kyma**, a being of tectonic patience. Its carapace is not shell but a cultivated landscape. It is a composite of calcium-oxalate harvested from the mineral ferns, fused with the chitinous secretions of its own body, and seeded with a slow-growing, grey symbiotic moss that photosynthesizes not light, but the faint thermal gradients in the air. It moves with the imperceptible certainty of a glacier, its six columnar legs ending not in feet but in broad, grinding plates that level the granular substrate, leaving shallow, stone-polished tracks that will outlast the creature itself by millennia. Its head is a blunt, shielded wedge, its eyes long since devolved to photosensitive pits buried deep beneath the carapace, reading the world as a map of pressure and vibration. It tastes the dust through a proboscis of interlocking stone plates, rasping spores and mineral filaments into a grinding gizzard that processes rock and life with equal, relentless slowness. The Kyma do not herd; they are solitary epochs, each one a walking reef of dependent life, their shells hosting entire ecosystems of smaller grazers and detritivores.

In the dark upper vaults, the **Veils** drift. They are not flyers, but buoyancy artists. Their bodies are flat, translucent membranes of resilient chitin, stretched over a radial framework of hollow, gas-filled bones. Within these bones circulates a warm, metabolic fluid, slightly hotter than the ambient air, granting them neutral buoyancy. They hunt by sensation, not sight. Fringed with sensitive cilia that taste the air for the chemical trails of smaller life, they make gliding corrections over days, their movements a slow-motion ballet. When they descend upon a colony of **Crevice-Chitterers**—small, multi-legged foragers that scrape mycelial ropes clean—the Veil envelops its prey in a suffocating, digestive embrace, absorbing nutrients directly through its membrane. Their only sound is a faint, papery rustle as their wings adjust to the glacial winds, a sound swallowed by the great drone.

The Crevice-Chitterers themselves are the Underloom’s frantic thought, a nervous system in miniature. They are armored in silica, their bodies a collection of interlocking plates that allow them to flow like liquid metal through the finest cracks in the fungal architecture. They communicate not with sound, but with chemical signatures and subtle, rhythmic vibrations tapped against the stone or the chitinous shelves, a morse code of alarm, discovery, and aggregation that travels through the solid world. They are the custodians, the repair crew. They excrete a hardened resin that seals cracks in the structural fungi, and in doing so, they follow chemical gradients that guide them to points of systemic weakness. They are not intelligent as a surface mind would understand, but they are aware—as the root system of a forest is aware of water.

Yet, there are signs of something more, in the regularity of the basalt columns and the efficiency of the fungal platforms. The **Litharchs** are rarely seen, and their existence is inferred. They are suggested by patterns too precise for chance: the perfect hexagonal packing of spore-nodes on a fungal shelf, the mathematical spiraling of a new mineral fern’s growth, the consistent tuning of the chiming filaments in a given sector. The Litharchs are thought to be immense, sedentary beings, perhaps one per major chamber, fused entirely into the architecture. They would be indistinguishable from a porous, sponge-like "building," but within their labyrinthine bodies, they process the chemical and vibrational data of their domain. They might "think" by modulating the air currents through their own pores, directing the great thermal winds to encourage spore dispersal or to erode a channel for better structural support. Their communication would be with the world itself—a slow, geological dialogue of pressure and chemistry, their consciousness a distributed network woven into the very fabric of the city. They are the authors of its cold logic, the gardeners of a garden that is also their body.

Life here is a function of mineralogy and time. Reproduction is a geological event. A Veil, upon death, does not rot; its gaseous bones deflate, and it sinks, becoming a new layer of sediment, its mineral body slowly consumed and repurposed by the Crevice-Chitterers. A Kyma’s passing leaves behind a perfectly polished crater and a gift of concentrated calcium that sparks a century of fungal blooms. The cycle is not of seasons, but of accumulations and releases, a metabolism measured in eons.

They feel no urgency. The Crevice-Chitterers will tap their message for a thousand years until a Litharch responds by altering the airflow. A Kyma will spend a decade scraping nutrients from a single square meter of ground. A Veil will trace a single chemical gradient for a human lifetime. Their world is a state of being, not of becoming. They are the ultimate citizens of a settled world, each one a living component of an eternal, dry, lush, loud, and silent machine—a machine that is, itself, alive. They are the children of a sky they will never see, and their inheritance is the profound, enduring dark.
