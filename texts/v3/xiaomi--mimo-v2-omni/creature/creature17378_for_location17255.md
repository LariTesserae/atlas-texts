It is not a place of inhabitants. It is a place of **emergences**.

Life here is an afterthought, a persistent, puzzling froth on the surface of a vast and indifferent engine. Yet it persists. The things that live in the Young Reaches are not *of* the mountain; they are its **accidents**, its first, tentative experiments with biology.

They are small. They are fast. They are efficient to the point of being spare.

**The Vent-Clingers** are the most conspicuous. They are not true insects, but chitinous, multi-legged grazers that scrape invisible microbial films from the warm, mineral-slimed rocks surrounding the hydrothermal chimneys. Their bodies are translucent, showing the digested minerals within as faint, shifting pastel stains. They move in synchronized herds, a slow-motion crustacean tide, and when the mountain groans, they flatten themselves into crevices with a synchronicity that suggests they feel the vibrations not through ears, but through every point of contact with the living rock. They have no eyes. They navigate by taste and thermal gradient. Their life cycle is brutally compressed: hatch, graze, mate, lay eggs in a warm crack, and die, all within a span of weeks. Their carcasses, rich in concentrated sulfur and metals, dissolve back into the runoff, becoming food for the next microbial bloom.

**The Sky-Pickers** are the birds, but calling them birds gives a wrong impression. They are kinetic sculptures of bone and feather, all angles and abrupt motions. Their wings are long and rigid, built for holding position in the thin, turbulent air above the thermal columns. They do not sing. They communicate in short, sharp clicks and scrapes, the sound of stone on stone. They hunt the swarms of **True-Midges**, delicate flies whose larvae live in the near-freezing pooled water. The Sky-Pickers dive with such velocity that they seem to *vanish* from one point in the sky and *re-appear* at another, their beaks snapping shut on clouds of prey with an audible *tick*. They nest on sheer, wet cliff faces, their nests being little more than depressions packed with the sharp, sterile gravel. There is no parental affection, only a programmed regurgitation of food into the open, begging maws of chicks that look like smaller, hungrier versions of themselves.

**The Stone-Burrowers** are the mammal-like creatures, though they are more mineral than meat. Their fur is the dull grey of the basalt, and it is perpetually damp, not with water, but with a thin, oily biofilm that helps regulate their temperature in the fluctuating cold. They dig not for soil—which does not exist—but for *veins*. They follow hairline fractures in the rock where specific minerals collect, gnawing at them with incisors that are reinforced with metallic crystalline deposits. Their digestion is a slow, geothermal process; they huddle in deep burrows near pockets of residual warmth, their metabolisms working in tandem with the mountain’s own cooling heat. They do not hibernate; they *mineralize*, entering a state of torpor where their heartbeats slow to one per minute, their bodies becoming, for all intents, another stone until a vibration or a chemical change in the rock rouses them.

And then, there are **The Pale Ones**.

They are not animals. They are not plants. They are the logic of the place given form.

You see them sometimes at the edges of the largest, warmest pools, where the mineral concentration is so high it forms delicate, crystalline shelves. They are not moss, though they grow in cushion-like colonies. They are a lattice, a living architecture. Each filament is a silica tube, housing a symbiotic chain of microbes: some that harvest the chemical energy from the vent gases, some that fix carbon from the thin air, some that secrete the hard, glassy structure itself. They grow with geological patience, millimeters per year, building intricate, fractal patterns that mimic the larger drainage channels of the mountain. They are cool to the touch, even when surrounded by warmth. They do not spread by seed or spore, but by *extension*—a new tube budding from an old one, a new colony beginning where a fragment has broken off and been carried by water.

They are the first architects. Their structures trap sediment, the first true accumulation of *dust* and *grit* and *dead midge parts* in the Young Reaches. In their hollows, a crude, mineral-rich paste begins to form. It is not soil. It is a **promise** of soil.

They take the raw, industrial output of the mountain—the heat, the minerals, the water—and begin, with infinite slowness, to translate it into the terms of life. They are the factory’s first, self-designed product: a mechanism for turning stone into a substrate.

To live in the Young Reaches is to be an aspect of the process. The vent-clingers are the harvesters of the machine’s heat. The sky-pickers are its aerial sensors. The stone-burrowers are its subterranean drills. And the Pale Ones are its nascent, self-replicating wiring.

They have no history. They have no culture. They have only function, and the relentless, unthinking imperative of the new: to persist, to process, to become. They are life as a geological phase, as inevitable and as impersonal as the cooling of the crust. They are not waiting for anything. They *are* the waiting, made flesh and chitin and silica, in a world that is still, patiently, deciding what it will be.
