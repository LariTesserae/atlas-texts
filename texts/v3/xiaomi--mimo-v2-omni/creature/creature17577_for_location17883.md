The people of the Understone are like the stone itself: soft in some places, hard in others, and always, quietly, settling.

They are not tall. The low, arched ceilings and narrow passages have shaped them over generations, or perhaps they were shaped for it—a genetic memory of a life lived close to the earth. Their bones are dense, their movements deliberate and economical, conserving energy in the cool, oxygen-thin air. Their skin holds a permanent pallor from the diffuse light, but their eyes are keen in the low gloom, adapted to a world of subtle shades of white and grey. In the dust, their footprints are the only loud things—faint, sharp impressions that are smoothed away by the next passing foot or slow drift of mineral breath.

They are the *Stone-Speakers*, though not because the stone talks in words. They speak *to* it, and *through* it. Their language is soft, a series of murmurs, clicks, and hums that carry in the damp air without echo. The loudest sound in the settlement is often the rhythmic *tap-tap-tap* of a chisel—a sound that is more felt than heard, a vibration that travels through the walls and becomes part of the place’s quiet pulse. They listen to the stone more than they speak to each other. A hand laid flat against a wall can feel the slow percolation of water, the deep, distant shudder of a new fracture miles below. They know the difference between the cool of a stable chamber and the clinging chill of a damp seam that is not yet ready to be opened.

Their tools are extensions of the earth they shape. Chisels of the hardest, darkest bone from creatures that once roamed the surface long ago, their origins now myth. Hammers of dense, fine-grained stone polished to a mirror sheen. Wooden mallets that give a softer blow, for persuading rather than forcing. Wedges of pale, straight-grained wood that they soak in collected rainwater until they swell and split the rock along its own desire lines. They do not conquer the stone; they court it.

Their bodies are adorned with what the earth gives. Necklaces of polished pebbles, smooth and cool. Bracelets of braided fungal thread, surprisingly strong. Hair is often bound with strips of pale, cured hide or woven with tiny, polished bone beads. They wear simple tunics and wraps of woven grass-fiber, dyed with the faint pigments of deep-earth minerals: a rusty ochre, a ghostly blue from copper traces, a soft yellow from sulphur. The colours are never bright; they are whispers of colour, stained by the dust.

Their lives are structured around the rhythms of extraction and settlement, of water and breath. The *Digging-Song* is their most sacred act—not a song of melody, but a coordinated rhythm of taps and breaths that guides a group working a new seam. It is a communal listening, a way to harmonize their intent with the stone’s willingness. They believe the settlement is a living joint venture between their will and the earth’s slow, generative pressure. To break stone without rhythm is to create a wound. To carve with patience is to help the place be born.

In the central chambers, where the light is most generous, the *Gatherers* tend to the sparse ecology. They cultivate the mosses and lichens, not just for material, but because they are indicators of the settlement’s health—a thriving moss bank means good air, good moisture flow. They guard the delicate, thumb-sized ferns with a near-reverence. Their social structure is a reflection of the geology: layered, interdependent, and slowly shifting. The *Shapers*—the master carvers—are the most respected, but their authority is quiet, earned by the smoothness of an arch or the perfect gradient of a drain. The *Water-Keepers* hold a critical role, reading the weep of the shafts, directing the flow, knowing which cisterns will taste of limestone and which of clay. The *Listeners* are those most attuned to the deep vibrations of the place; they are the early warning system for shifts and collapses, spending hours in silent meditation in the newest, rawest chambers, feeling the earth settle.

They do not fear the dark, but they respect it. The deep places are not for the unprepared. The blind, transparent salamander they call the *Stone-Tear* is considered a spirit of those depths—a creature of pure patience, having given its eyes to the darkness to gain a deeper sight. Children are taught not to fear it, but to emulate its stillness.

Their art is the settlement itself. The most beautiful chamber is not the largest, but the one where the acoustic of a drip into a basin creates a perfect, repeating note, or where the angle of a shaft catches the sun at the solstice and paints a single, brilliant stripe across a smoothed wall for a few precious minutes. They carve small, intimate alcoves into the walls of sleeping rooms, not for idols, but for a single perfect stone or a treasured bone tool—their form of devotion is to utility and quiet beauty.

In the end, they are a people in a state of becoming. They do not speak of a past, only of the next cut, the next chamber, the next season’s seepage. Their history is written in the expanding map of their home, in the tool marks that smooth into obscurity as new layers are carved over old. They are the soft, persistent force that is giving form to the young stone, and in turn, the young stone, with its patient pressure and yielding grain, is giving form to them. They are not inhabitants. They are a feature of the geology.
