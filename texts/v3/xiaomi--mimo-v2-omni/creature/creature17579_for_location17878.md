In the perpetual green gloom of the Underbloom, life does not exist—it *processes*. The inhabitants are less creatures and more functions, biological imperative given form, each a gear in the screaming, churning engine of growth.

You sense the smallest first: the **Spore-Motes**, not true insects but drifting reproductive clusters with vestigial, crystalline wings. They move in shimmering clouds, their harmonic drone the highest layer of the ambient roar. They do not think; they feed on airborne nutrients and, upon death, their bodies instantly crystallize into new spore-nodes, completing their loop in minutes. Beneath your feet, the root-mat writhes with **Grind-Grubs**, fat, pale larvae with mouths like geological strata. They are the world’s teeth, grinding dead matter into fertile pulp. Their digestion is audible—a constant, soft, rasping **shhhhhh** from below, the sound of decomposition made kinetic.

Larger, and more unsettling, are the **Vine-Skitterers**. These are the closest analog to mammals, but their bones are flexible, cartilage-rich, allowing them to flow between roots. Their fur is not keratin but a symbiotic filament of bioluminescent fungi, pulsing in time with their rapid hearts. They have six limbs, each ending in adhesive pads, and their faces are a cluster of sensory nodes—no true eyes, but photosensitive pits that drink the ambient glow. They communicate in ultrasonic clicks, their language a series of pings and echoes mapping the ever-changing architecture around them. They are scavengers and planters, their droppings thick with half-digested seeds that take root instantly in the warm mat.

The walking gardens you glimpsed are the **Phytovores**. Enormous, slow beings, their original form—perhaps ursine, perhaps cervine—long since obscured. Their hide is a living tapestry: shelf-fungi form armored plates on their backs, mosses cloak their flanks in velvet, and parasitic flowers bloom from their joints, their chattering bells adding to the cacophony. The Phytovore does not eat the Underbloom; it *negotiates* with it. It moves with deliberate slowness to avoid damaging its symbiotic crop, and in return, the plants provide camouflage, nutrient exchange, and defense. When a Phytovore finally dies, it does not fall; it roots. Its body becomes a new, stationary hub of growth, a "Phytolith," around which entire micro-communities bloom and die in rapid succession.

But the true sovereigns of the root-columns are the **Root-Shepherds**. You see one in the middle distance, moving with the deliberate grace of a glacier. Its form is vaguely humanoid, but towering, woven from the very same root-matter as the columns. Its skin is bark, its hair a cascade of epiphytic ferns, its eyes deep pools of slow, green sap. It does not walk; it *extends* and *retracts*, drawing material from the floor and weaving it into its body as it moves, leaving a brief, raw scar of new growth in its wake. The Root-Shepherd is a curator and a sculptor. It prunes runaway fungal blooms, reinforces failing root-bridges with new weave, and with hands that can fuse or separate living tissue, it maintains the delicate, violent balance of the biome. It is the closest thing to an intelligence here, but not a human one. It thinks in seasons and centuries, its consciousness a slow, deep awareness of tension, rot, and hydraulic pressure within the wood. To it, you are not a threat, nor a curiosity. You are an anomaly—a knot of slow, water-based chemistry. It may, eventually, approach. Not to communicate, but to *assess*. To determine if your composition is suitable for integration, or if you will simply be consumed by the faster, more eager processes of the Bloom.

And then there are the whispers, the faintest hints of the tool-makers. They are not seen. Their existence is inferred in a polished hollow in a root, worn smooth by generations of bodies, or in the precise, flint-knapped edge of a bone awl left wedged in a fungus. These are the **Mycelial People**, if they still exist. They would not build *against* the Underbloom, but *with* its frenzied logic. Their shelters are grown, not built, coaxing roots into arches, training vines into lattices. Their tools are chosen for their organic compatibility—bone that will sharpen itself against fungal acids, stone that will not rot. They would move like shadows, their skin smeared with reactive pigments to mimic the pulsing light, their speech a series of clicks and whistles lost in the greater noise. They are the system's memory, its only point of fragile, deliberate resistance against the total, mindless assimilation of the Bloom. To find them would be to find the only silence in this roaring place: the held breath of intention, waiting to see if it can survive the green, glowing, all-consuming tide.

The Underbloom does not *have* inhabitants. It *is* the inhabitants, and they are it. There is no separation. To be here is to be observed, assessed, and processed by an ecosystem that perceives time in pulses of growth and decay. You are not a visitor. You are a potential resource, a future layer in the spongy, warm, and eternally hungry floor.
