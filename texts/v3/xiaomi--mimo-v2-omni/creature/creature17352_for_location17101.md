They are the **Stone-Weavers**, though that is a translation of sound and gesture, not of a name they possess in any fixed way. They are a people shaped by verticality and warm, rising stone. Their limbs are long, ropy with tendon, and their fingers and toes are broadened, the pads textured like the grip of a gecko, allowing them to cling to wet rock and woven root-ropes with an ease that seems to defy gravity. Their skin has a faint, opalescent sheen, not from any creature of the deep, but from a lifetime of immersion in the mineral-rich water, which leaves a thin, luminous deposit on the skin, catching the algae-light from below.

They move with a slow, deliberate economy. On the hand-carved steps and ledges, they flow like water, their balance innate. Their voices are soft, pitched to carry in the damp, muffling air—a language of hums, clicks, and the striking of stone on stone, which they use to converse across the chasms. A tap on a specific vein of flint can mean “food,” “danger,” or “come see,” the meaning deciphered by location and rhythm. The loudest sound they ever make is during the **Tide-Turn**, when the breathing sea reaches its apogee or nadir; then, from every balcony and carved threshold, they strike tuned stones with mallets, creating a resonant, harmonic thrum that seems to thank the mountain for holding steady.

Their tools are extensions of the world. Knives of obsidian, so sharp they part not just flesh but water. Chisels forged from the rare, hard nodules found in the deepest hot spring vents. They weave the thick vines and aerial roots into everything: nets for catching the blind, pale crustaceans; baskets that breathe; the very frameworks of their overhanging balconies, which they train and graft over generations into a single, living lattice. Their clothing is minimal, often just a kilt or sash of woven fern-fiber, dyed with the rust-red and electric cyan of the mosses, marking lineage and tidal-cycle.

Their dwellings, the honeycombed caves, are both home and history. The deepest, warmest chambers are for nurseries and the elderly, warmed by the seeping heat. The outermost, wind-sung chambers are for meditation and the observation of the sky-glow. Every surface is carved. Not with decoration as an afterthought, but as a living record. The slow fanning of the limestone strata is their calendar; they carve new glyphs alongside the newly exposed rock layers, tracking generations not in years, but in the growth of travertine ledges—a foot per lifetime, perhaps. Reliefs on the walls tell not stories of war or kings, but of the mountain’s own story: the great breaths of the tide, the pattern of a flowering vine, the shape of a particularly large crustacean seen decades ago.

They are governed by the **Rhythm**. Their entire society is organized around the two great cycles: the daily breath of the sea, and the slower, grander breath of the land’s emergence. There are those who are **Cyclemasters**, who watch the monoliths where the tide-heights are etched, their authority absolute in matters of when to harvest the deep-water algae or when to retreat to higher chambers. There are the **Growers**, who coax the vines and mosses, not cultivating them for food—though many are edible—but to guide their clinging, to strengthen the living architecture. And there are the **Listeners**, who spend days in silent communion with the singing ridges, interpreting the mountain’s vibrations to divine where new springs might break forth or where a section of cliff has grown unstable. They believe the mountain is not dead stone, but a slumbering, dreaming consciousness, and their carvings are a way of singing it into a pleasant dream.

Their diet is a reflection of their world: the flesh of the pale, blind crustaceans, boiled in the hot spring water; the tender hearts of the giant waxy vines; the honeyed-metal nectar of the night-blooming flowers, fermented into a heady, luminescent wine; and a paste ground from the luminescent algae itself, which they spread on flat stones to dry into chewy, glowing cakes that sustain them through long nights of carving.

They are not conquerors of this place, but its conscious expression. The land emerges, and they emerge with it, their culture an extension of the geology. They do not fear the dim light; they find vision in the play of shadows and the subtle glow from below. They do not struggle against the verticality; they are its masters. When a new fissure cracks a cliff-face or a new vent breathes scalding water into a chamber, they do not see disaster, but a new text to read, a new chapter in the mountain’s slow, loud autobiography—a story they are tasked with recording, one patient tap of the chisel at a time.
