They are a people of the vertical world, born to the weight of thin air and the patience of stone. Their lungs are broad and deep, their blood rich to coax warmth from the minimal oxygen. Skin and eyes bear the permanent etch of wind and the pale, reflective light off the rock; complexions are weathered to the hue of the slate itself, and squinting is a permanent cast of the face. They move with a deliberate economy, for a misstep on a scree slope or a wasted motion in the chill is a debt paid in vital warmth.

Their lives are dictated by the mist. They call it the *Breath*, and its gathering is their clock. When it condenses in the hollows, they collect it with cloths of woven scrub-fiber, wringing the precious moisture into clay jars. It feeds the moss-gardens in protected ledges—cultivated cushions of grey-green that are both food, insulation, and spiritual symbol. The moss is their soil, their blanket, their currency.

Their tools are extensions of the plateau. Knives and scrapers are of flint or obsidian, chipped with agonizing slowness on stone anvils. Bone, from the grazers, is worked into needles, fishhooks for the deeper, ice-locked tarns, and intricate combs. The hardest wood, from the stunted, wind-twisted trees in the highest, most sheltered crevices, is reserved for the frames of their shelters, the levers to pry new living space from the mountain, and the resonant-log bells that mark the passing of the shifts.

They are called the **Kyma**, a word that means both "to breathe" and "to endure." Their social unit is the *Cleft*, a lineage that owns a specific fissure or series of ledges in the city-sprawl. A Cleft’s prestige is measured by the depth of its storerooms, the health of its moss-gardens, and the number of its working ropes—the thick, braided lifelines that bind the vertical community together. Leadership falls not to the strongest, but to the most patient and observant—the one who can read the slow patterns of the grazer herds, predict where the mist will linger longest, or see a flaw in the rock that will yield a new chamber.

Their days are a rhythm of maintenance and harvest. One shift is spent tending the six-legged **Olgren**, shearing their shaggy, insulating fleece. Another shift scrapes lichen for medicinal teas and dye. Another chips incrementally at the central massif, expanding the honeycomb. The immense birds, the **Teth**, are revered and feared. Their feathers are the only true insulation for their best garments, and their down is woven into the innermost layers of burial shrouds. To take a Teth is a sacred, perilous hunt, performed only by volunteers from a Cleft in need of honor.

Their spirituality is geologic. They believe the plateau itself is the petrified body of a primordial being, and to carve into it is to commune with its dream. The hollow-log bells don't just mark time; they resonate with the mountain’s heart, a sympathetic vibration that they believe strengthens the stone and calms the winds. Rituals are quiet, communal affairs: the sharing of a rare, sweet-root found deep in a crevice; the binding of a new rope with songs that mimic the creak of its fiber; the silent, collective gaze as the mist rises from the ravines at dawn, a slow, grey exhalation they see as the mountain's soul made visible.

They have no written history, only a tapestry of knotted cords and carved symbols on storeroom doors, recording lineages, harvest tallies, and the rare, catastrophic event—a rockfall that claimed a Cleft, a season of unbroken wind that froze the mist. Their art is in the fit of a stone, the braided pattern of a rope, the subtle, shifting mosaic of their moss-gardens.

In this immense, silent world, the Kyma are a dense, humming cluster of intention. They are not conquerors of the plateau, but its most intricate tenants. They live in a state of perpetual, active negotiation with the stone, the cold, and the sparse gifts of life. Their existence is a testament to the fact that even in a place of apparent emptiness and stasis, life, given a crack, will not only persist but will weave itself into the very fabric of the world, becoming as enduring and essential as the mist, the lichen, and the unyielding, ancient rock.
