In the perpetual grey wash of the plateau, where time pools like the water in its dark basins, there are the Root-Singers.

They are not creatures of flesh and bone as understood below, in the frantic, sun-drenched lowlands. They are a consciousness of damp and patience, born from the marriage the land itself has arranged. Their form is fluid, a convergence rather than a birth. One might see a torso in the slow twist of a vine-laced conifer, its skin the textured bark of a broad-leaf giant, its face a subtle, weathered knot in the wood where two pale, lichen-veined branches meet. Their limbs are not distinct, but are suggested—a trailing moss that thickens and lifts, a root system that shifts with a grating whisper across the loam. They are covered in the land: a mantle of jade and charcoal mosses, a drizzle of ferns sprouting from shoulders, a crown of tiny, phosphorescent fungi that glow with a dim, pearlescent light in the deepest twilight.

They move, when they move, with the rhythm of the plateau. A step is a century, a gesture the slow tilt of a stone. They do not walk, but *accrete*, one point of contact becoming another, as if the ground itself is accepting and presenting them anew. Their primary interaction with the world is through listening and vibrating.

The Root-Singers do not speak. They hum.

This hum is the sub-audible thrum of the geothermal engines deep in the earth’s bones, translated through their mossy bodies into a resonance that the land understands. It is a low, bone-deep vibration that can cause beads of condensation to tremble on a fern frond, that coaxes shy, slow-moving insects from their leafy shelters. It is a call and a mapping. When they hum, they are listening to the echoes of their own song bouncing back through root systems, through the iron rails buried in the soil, through the corroded steel bones of the factories. They feel the shape of the ruin, the density of the rot, the path of the water. The landscape becomes a singing bowl, and they are the strike upon its rim.

Their society is not social, but symphonic. They exist in solitary resonance with their chosen anchor: one might be permanently entwined with a leaning power pole, its body a conduit for the forgotten electricity now replaced by geothermal song; another might be seated within the shattered brick of a furnace, its hum amplified by the chamber, a focal point for the area’s vibrations. They are the organs of the land’s body, each performing a function of perception and subtle influence. They do not tend the ruins; they *listen* to them, interpreting the slow collapse as a form of music. The frantic, mechanical heartbeat of the past has been slowed, digested, and integrated into their own tempo. A spinning turbine blade, driven by a relentless drip, is not a machine to them, but a percussion instrument of immense and beautiful slowness.

Their sustenance is the mist and the hum. They draw moisture directly through their mossy integument, and the vibrations they crave are both their food and their breath. They have no eyes as such, but perceive the world as a tapestry of textures, densities, and resonant frequencies. A sharp rock, a soft patch of decay, a vein of cooling iron—all are distinct "notes" in their sensory field.

If a traveler were to stumble into this realm, they would not be met. They would be *absorbed* into the soundscape. The Root-Singers would adjust their hum, incorporating the intruder’s erratic heartbeat, the crunch of their footsteps, their breath of surprise, into the vast, patient song. They would feel the traveler’s haste as a dissonance, a sharp, brittle chord in the composition. And they would wait, vibrating with infinite patience, for that dissonance to slow, to soften, to align with the drip, the sway, the deep and geologic pulse. The traveler would not be threatened, but invited—through the sheer, overwhelming peace of the plateau’s timescale—to become, like everything else, a note in the eternal, weeping hymn of the land.
