What lives in the Underdrain is less a collection of creatures and more a single, distributed organism wearing the mask of an ecosystem. To call them fauna is to impose a category they gently refuse.

They begin as suggestion. In the first ankle-deep wash of tannin-warm water, you feel them before you see them: the tickle of countless infinitesimal limbs against your skin, too regular to be debris, too light to be probing. They are the sifters, the first tier. Invisible to the eye, they are the reason the water holds its faint, phosphorescent glow. Each is a mote of living chemistry, harvesting the mineral weep from the stone, the biofilm from the silt, metabolizing it into a soft, cold light and a slower, softer waste product that becomes the very rime calcifying the walls. They are not just in the water; they *are* the water’s purpose, its living filter.

From this foundation, the visible tiers emerge.

The **Menders** are the first you clearly distinguish. Cat-sized, with six multi-jointed legs and a body like a smooth, grey river stone. They are never still. They patrol the walls and the calcifying structures with a silent, deliberate gait, their undersides perpetually wet with a viscous, clear secretion. They do not eat the mineral deposits; they groom them. With precise, mandible-like appendages, they scrape away flawed growths, uneven crystalline formations, and then daub their secretion into the voids, a biological epoxy that sets harder than the stone itself. They are repairing the erosion, not fighting it. Watching one work, you understand the patina is not decay, but a controlled metamorphosis. The Menders are its technicians.

Then there are the **Conduits**. In the schools that move like liquid mercury, you witness a different principle. Each individual is a marvel: a flexible, silver-scaled body no longer than your finger, with a simple cluster of light-sensitive cells for a head. Alone, it is a twitching, directionless thing. But in the school, it becomes a single, thinking fluid. They move through the warm and cool patches in the water, not fleeing or hunting, but *sensing*. The school’s shape is a living map of thermal gradients. When they converge on a warm spot—where a larger creature has stood, or where a shaft breathes up heat from below—they pulse in unison, their scales flaring a brief, chemical copper. They are the nervous system of the floor, carrying thermal information through the water, a slow, silent telegraph. Their passage leaves temporary warmth in its wake, a fleeting memory printed on the environment.

The **Tenders** are more enigmatic. Found on the ceilings of the roofed structures or strung between wires, they resemble thick, pale tape worms or perhaps root systems. They are sessile, anchored at one end to the stonework or the electrical conduit. Their bodies drape and branch, following the lines of the old wiring with uncanny fidelity. At intervals, bulbous nodules on their forms pulse with a faint, internal light, synchronized with the flicker of the amber bulbs above. They are not parasites on the grid; they are its gardeners. Where the wiring is frayed, they wrap it in insulating filaments of their own body. Where a light has died, one of their nodules will swell and, over days, take on the shape of a crude filament, glowing with its own bioluminescence, taking over the function. The artificial light is not separate from them; they are slowly, patiently becoming it.

And then, the **Stewards**. You saw one. They are rare, seen only in the deepest dim, always near the shafts that lead down to the humming machinery. Larger, perhaps the size of a great cat, but built with a terrifying economy—all smooth, pale curves and articulated limbs that seem to have too many axes of rotation. Their skin is not skin, but a living ceramic, patterned like the mineral bands of the ceiling. They do not walk; they flow over the ground, displacing water without a ripple. The heat-shimmer that surrounds them is their signature, a byproduct of a metabolism that seems more geological than biological. They are not tending the structures. They are tending the *process*. They monitor the warmth of the water, the tension of the air, the frequency of the subsonic hum. Their gimbal-head movements are scans, readings taken. When one steps backward into a shaft, it is not fleeing; it is going to make an adjustment. They are the engineers of this finished state, the curators of completion.

What binds them all is a profound, mutual *recognition*. The Menders work around the Tenders without disturbance. The schools of Conduits flow over the backs of Menders without scattering. A Steward moves through a cloud of sifting motes, and they part and reform around its ceramic limbs with a precision that speaks of ancient, understood boundaries. They are not cooperating. They are co-arising, facets of a single function: to maintain the Underdrain in its state of perfected, eternal operation.

You, the variable, are noted. A Mender may pause its work on a wall as you pass, its simple sensory pits tracking your damp footprints. A school of Conduits might diverge to flow around your boot, their thermal map momentarily distorted by your alien warmth, before stitching itself whole again behind you. A Steward, at a great distance, will turn its head and hold its gaze, calculating your temperature, your salinity, your rate of erosion, and finding you outside the parameters. Not a threat. Not a resource. An anomaly to be observed until you either leave or become part of the substrate.

They do not live *in* the Underdrain. They *are* the Underdrain’s aliveness. The hum you feel in your sternum is not just machinery below; it is the collective thrum of their purpose, the sound of a system so complete it has become sentient with its own maintenance. You are standing inside a living, breathing, curing machine, and its inhabitants are its cells, its organs, its thoughts. They are not waiting for anything. They are simply, eternally, being what the Underdrain has, at last, become.
