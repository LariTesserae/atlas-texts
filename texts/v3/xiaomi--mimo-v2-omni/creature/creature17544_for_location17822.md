They are not seen, not directly. Not at first.

You infer them from the evidence: the tended gardens with tools just beyond your grip. The patched foundations, fresh concrete still damp in the morning fog. The machinery, running. Always running, but never wearing out, never producing so much as a changed light or a different pitch in its hum. The silence is not an absence of life, but the sound of a life that has perfected a different kind of attention.

Their presence is a pressure, a warmth against the cold clay. Not from below this time, but from within the walls. You feel it when you lean against the sheet metal of a longhouse—not the bite of the cold, but a faint, steady thermal bloom behind it, like a body turned away. The condensation doesn't form there. That is how you learn to find their paths: by the dry trails on the skin of the buildings, where the heat of their passing keeps the dampness at bay.

You hear the work. A rhythmic *shhh-shhh* of a broom on concrete, always just around the next corner, vanishing into the fog-choked gap between two structures. The *clink* of metal on stone, from a ventilation shaft three stories up, when you look there is only a grey-green bird, watching. At night, when the larger thing moves in the bamboo, its passage is sometimes accompanied by a softer sound: the drag of something heavy, but rounded, smoothing the earth. Not a footstep. A purpose.

They are the gardeners. The maintainers. The keepers of the pulse.

They move in the spaces between spaces. In the utility corridors choked with pipes and vines. In the maintenance tunnels that honeycomb the ground, where the air is warm and smells of damp metal and turned soil. You see their handiwork most clearly where the building and the geology meet: a concrete buttress, poured fresh against a new knuckle of stone pushing up through the clay; a rebar lattice woven through the heart of a thicket of bamboo, not to restrain it, but to guide its growth, a collaboration of steel and sap.

Their scale is the greatest tell. The doorways are standard, the corridors human-width, but the deeper you go, the more you find the accommodations: handholds placed a meter too high, levers with a throw length that would require an arm of impossible reach, stairs with a rise just slightly too great for a comfortable human gait. These are not relics of a taller species. They are adaptations. They have changed their tools, or themselves, to meet the needs of the work. Or the work has changed them.

There is no malice in it. No hostility. Only an immense, quiet industry. The muffled voices you hear are not whispers hiding secrets. They are the sound of communication optimized for efficiency in a dense, damp medium—low, resonant frequencies that travel well through metal and earth, carrying only the essential information: *Here. Patch needed. Growth vector adjustment. Water flow diverted.* A laugh, high and brief, might be the sonic signature of a seal testing true, or a correct pressure reading.

They do not build for you. You are a weather event, a brief turbulence in their atmosphere. They observe with the patience of moss, with the deliberateness of the birds. If you stand still for long enough in a forgotten courtyard, you may feel the air change beside you. A warmth, a slight displacement of fog. The subtle scent of ozone and wet clay, rich and organic. You will not see a shape. You will see a fern uncurl a new frond toward a patch of sky where the fog has thinned, and know that the thinning was not accidental.

They are the nervous system of the Steaming Rows. The settlement is not their creation; it is their body. The engines are its heartbeat, the cisterns its circulatory system, the gardens its metabolism. The shifting ground is its slow, dreaming thought. They tend to it not as builders, but as cells in a vast, intelligent organism, performing their functions without need for a master's voice. They are the warmth in the soil, the dry path on the metal, the silent movement in the bamboo.

To leave the Steaming Rows is to forget the exact weight of their attention. You remember it only as a feeling, a wrongness that was not wrong but merely different: the sensation of being in a place that was already full. Occupied. Alive with a purpose so deep and so integrated it had no need to announce itself. It simply was, as the clay was, as the water was. And you, a brief, cold anomaly, passed through it like fog, leaving no mark they would not patiently, silently, smooth away.
