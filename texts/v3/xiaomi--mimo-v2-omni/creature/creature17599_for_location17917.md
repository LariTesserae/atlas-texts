The people of the High Tephraves are a study in adaptation and attunement. They are not conquerors of the mountain; they are its careful listeners, its symbiotic partners. Their lives are shaped by the same forces that carve the basalt and coax the spectral orchids from the air.

**Bodies in the Mist:**
Physically, they are lean and wiry, built for navigating steep, crumbling terraces. Their skin has a cool, greyish undertone, like the stone they shape, and their movements are deliberate and quiet, a choreography of efficiency that minimizes disturbance. Their most striking feature is their eyes—large and dark, with pupils adapted to the perpetual twilight under the great trees. They possess an exceptional low-light vision and a peripheral sensitivity to movement, able to track the slow drift of a luminous beetle or the subtle shift of fungal light across a slope. Their hands are strong and calloused, but preternaturally dexterous, their fingers adept at the twin tasks of grinding basalt into fine tools and weaving the most delicate filaments of aerial root into cloth.

**Sustenance and Shelter:**
They are not farmers in a traditional sense, but cultivators of equilibrium. Their food comes from a careful, seasonal foraging that feels more like a respectful harvest. They collect the starchy, tuberous roots of certain silver-leafed shrubs, harvest the protein-rich grubs that live within the heart of the great trees (taking only what the colony can spare), and skim the luminous, probiotic films that form on the surface of the stiller condensation pools. The six-legged tortoises are sacred; they are never harmed, but their shed moss-gardens are occasionally gently pruned, a mutual grooming.

Their dwellings, as described, grow from the mountain. But they are more than shelters; they are instruments of living. The sod roofs are meticulously tended gardens of the purple succulents, which provide insulation, drink the mist, and bloom as living lanterns. The interior walls, shaped and smoothed over generations, are often inlaid with patterns of polished different-colored stone—veins of pale tuff or iron-red jasper—that map the water channels of the slope or the migration paths of the click-beetles. Inside, there is little furniture. Soft, woven mats from tree bark cover the stone floors, and niches in the walls hold their few crafted possessions: basalt blades, collection pots of fired clay glowing with fungal light, and bundles of dried, aromatic mosses used to scent the air and repel tiny, biting insects.

**The Language of Silence:**
Their most profound technology is social and linguistic. They communicate in a layered system that exists *within* the silence. Hand signs form a quick, practical vocabulary for work and warning. But deeper communication happens through intentional sound and attentive perception. A specific tap of a stone tool against a bowl is an invitation. A particular rhythm of breathing, audible only in close proximity, can convey calm or concern. They "read" the landscape and its inhabitants as one would read a vast, slow text. A sudden cessation of the fungal pulses in one sector might indicate a predator's passage. The collective movement of the translucent beetles can foretell a shift in the wind and incoming heavy mist.

**Relationship with the Fauna:**
They see themselves as the youngest and most precarious branch of the ecosystem. The fauna is not wildlife to be managed, but the senior, more permanent citizens. They have names and stories for the great, slow-moving tortoises, seeing each as an individual, a mossy elder. The water-eels are regarded as spirits of the pools, and leaving a small, shiny chip of basalt by a basin is considered a good gesture of respect. They understand the click-beetles' swarm patterns as a form of aerial debate. They do not feel "above" this web; they feel *within* it, and their survival depends on accurately interpreting its threads.

**Tempo of Existence:**
Their sense of time is cyclical and environmental, not linear. A day is marked by the intensity of the mist, the bloom-cycle of the rooftop succulents (opening at dusk, closing at dawn), and the pulsing rhythm of the fungal blooms. A season is measured by the growth of a particular moss or the migratory return of a certain cloud of luminous insects. Their history is not kept in records, but is etched into the smoothed curves of their stone homes and passed down in the silent stories told by the patterns on their walls. Change is accepted as the only constant—the slow grind of erosion, the quick reclaiming of a path by moss, the quiet landslide that redefines a terrace. They do not fight this; they accommodate it, sometimes moving a dwelling a few paces over a generation, letting the mountain reclaim what it will.

**Their Inner World:**
They are a people of profound inner stillness, cultivated not for spirituality alone, but for practical survival. To be mentally noisy—to harbor anger, or despair, or frantic ambition—is to be deaf to the world's necessary quiet signals. Their emotions are deep but expressed through the subtle language of their craft: the extra care in smoothing a stone bowl, the choice of a particular moss for a woven mat, the shared, silent gaze over a basin of pulsing light as two people sit together. They find community not in loud gatherings, but in the shared, focused act of listening to the mountain breathe.

In essence, the people of the Tephraves are the mountain's consciousness turned inward. They are the basalt given form and attention, the condensation given patience and dexterity. They live in the crack between geology and biology, a small, enduring testament to the possibility of a life lived not against the overwhelming wild, but as its most attentive and subtle guest.
