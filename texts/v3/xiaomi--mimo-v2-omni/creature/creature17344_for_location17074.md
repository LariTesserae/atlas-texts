They call themselves the Rhizome, though that is a name given by others, long ago. They have no word for themselves as a people; there is only the breathing of the grove-halls, the drip of the ceiling, and the collective tasks of the day. Their language is a soft, wet series of clicks and hums, designed to carry in saturated air without startling the chitinous neighbors.

They are few. Perhaps three dozen in the entire basin, scattered across five or six cultivated groves. To be many would be to upset the balance, to demand too much from the root-floor, to draw the attention of the great, silent hunters above. Each grove-house is a family, a lineage of grafters and listeners, their histories recorded not in books, but in the very shapes of the walls they have coaxed—specific bends in a willow-shoot denoting a flood year, a particular pattern of fungal glow on a ceiling arch commemorating a first successful chitin-harvest.

Their bodies bear the marks of this world. They are lean, with a wiry strength for climbing knuckled roots and navigating the springy substrate. Their skin has a permanent cool, damp translucence, and their eyes are large, adapted to a spectrum of dim, internal light. They wear garments woven from shed arthropod carapaces, pounded and softened into flexible, pale plates, and from the papery bark of their own wall-shoots. Their tools are simple: chitin blades set in wooden hafts, hooks of polished beetle-horn for climbing, shallow baskets woven from mycelial strands to carry glowing fungal spores.

Their life is a ritual of maintenance and observation. A dawn, such as it is, is marked by a slow, communal journey through the grove's chambers, fingers trailing over walls and floors, feeling for new growth, for sickness in the wood, for the subtle shift in humidity that signals a need for new ventilation shoots. They listen. To the drip-patterns, which tell of water flow above. To the chitinous rustle in the under-lattice, which tells of centipede migrations or beetle herds. To the faint, high-frequency hum of the winged hunters, which tells of small-fly swarms and thus, safe foraging zones.

They do not hunt the great insects, but they understand them with an intimacy that borders on reverence. The slow, multi-legged passage of a **Glimmer-Beetle** through the outer groves is a sign of fertile decay; they will later harvest the brilliant, cool-blue fungal colonies that bloom in its wake. The **Long-Crawlers**, the dog-sized centipedes, are their silent partners. The settlers have learned to place specific, aromatic mosses along certain root-ways, pheromone-signals that encourage the creatures to prune away dead growth and keep the root-floor aerated. In return, they meticulously collect the Crawlers’ shed exoskeletons, enormous segmented tubes of pearlescent armor. A child’s first task is to scrape and polish a fragment of a Crawler-shell into a mirror-like surface, the only reflective thing in their world.

Above, in the vaults, hang the **Veil-Wings**, leathery, silent creatures that fold into the gloom like discarded cloaks. The settlers mark their roosts with tags of glowing fungus, not as a warning, but as a boundary. To disturb a Veil-Wing is to risk a silent, taloned descent. But the creatures are part of the rhythm. When their high-pitched, echolocating cries echo in the upper dark, the settlers know the buzzing **Cloud-Swarms** are active, and they stay within the root-pathways, harvesting only the sap-pockets the swarm has already warmed and fermented.

Their deepest symbiosis is with the light. The spectral pulses from within living things—the soft, green glow of the wall-moss when brushed, the urgent red pulse of a stress-reactive fungus, the guiding blue of the Glimmer-Beetle’s trail—are their constellations. A skilled *Grafter* can splice together different bioluminescent strands, creating slow-burning scripts in the walls: a record of a spore-bloom, a map to a new water-weep, a warning that feels like a gentle, pulsing unease in the periphery of vision.

They are not conquerors. They are a specialized organ within the ecosystem, a thin layer of consciousness that performs a function: tending the architecture, curating the light, and bearing witness. They fear not the creatures, but the **Overgrowth**, the terrifying, unchecked surge of life that can swallow a grove in a season. Their greatest act of cultivation is, paradoxically, restraint—constant, gentle pruning of the world’s own exuberance.

In the deepest stillness, when even the drip seems to pause, the settlers of the Rhizome can feel the true scale of their home. The basin is a single, breathing organism, and they are the fleeting, thoughtful thoughts passing through it. They are born from its wetness, sustained by its slow decay, and when they die, their bodies are returned to the root-floor, broken down into the sap and the fiber that will feed the very walls that once sheltered them. They do not dream of the sun. Here, in the profound dark, under the blanket of endless rain, they have cultivated a different kind of light: a fragile, persistent, and deeply rooted life, woven into the very sinew of a world that never wakes.
