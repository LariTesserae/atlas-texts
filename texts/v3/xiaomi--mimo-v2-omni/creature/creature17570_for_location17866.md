The inhabitants of the Vethara Plateau are not so much *people who live there* as they are *components that have achieved equilibrium with the system*. They are the soft tissue grown into the cracks of a great machine, and over generations, they have ceased to distinguish between their own biology and the hum of the stone.

They are a sparse population, yes, but not a diminished one. Each person feels like a necessary tuning fork, their very presence calibrated to the plateau’s frequency. Their movements are economical, unhurried, flowing through the channels with a liquid grace that mirrors the seeping water. There is no wasted energy, no sharp gesture. Their physicality is one of absorption and conduction. You see them often with a hand flat against a wall, not for support, but as if checking a pulse—or offering their own.

Their voices are the first and most profound adaptation. They speak in a low, resonant register, a continuous baritone murmur that sits just atop the fundamental hum of the Engines. It is less a series of sharp phonemes and more a modulated undercurrent, words shaped by chest vibration as much as by tongue and lip. They understand sound not as something that travels *to* them, but as something they are *embedded within*. To them, the silence of the outside world would be a deafening, terrifying absence. Children do not learn to shout; they learn to *match*. Their first games are ones of finding harmonic nodes in the corridors, pressing their small bodies into the sweet spots where the vibration is strongest, and laughing not with high peals, but with shuddering, whole-body tremors that blend seamlessly with the ambient tone.

Their eyes are adapted to the perpetual damp and diffused light. They have a milky, opalescent quality to the iris, less for seeing detail in the fog and more for perceiving subtle shifts in pattern—the ripple on a basin’s surface, the change in condensation patterns on an Engine’s housing that signals a shift in rhythm. They are not staring *at* things; they are feeling them with their sight.

Their hands are fascinating. The skin is slightly thickened, almost rubbery on the palms, not from callous but from a permanent dampness and a unique protein structure that allows them to handle the perpetually wet, ringing stone without slipping and without feeling the penetrating cold. They conduct maintenance with a tender intimacy. A technician listening to a flywheel does not use a stethoscope; they press an ear directly to the basalt casing, their eyes half-closing as they separate the Engine’s primary thrum from the deeper, stranger frequency beneath it. They make adjustments not with wrenches and force, but with incremental weights and precise, small touches, as if coaxing a giant beast to sigh in a particular way.

Their communities are not clustered in towns but distributed along the acoustic network. They live in dwellings carved into the thickness of terrace walls, spaces that are as much resonant chambers as they are shelters. Furniture is minimal—low benches of stone, mats woven from *vel* reed. The air inside is warm, wet, and thick with the smell of mineral and skin. They sleep not in silence, but *with* the sound, their dreams supposedly arranged by its ebb and flow. They claim to dream in patterns, in waveforms, in the geometric language the channels almost describe.

They have a concept they call “the Deep Listen.” It is a form of meditation, of active being, where one empties the mind of internal monologue and becomes a pure receiver for the plateau’s layered song: the Engine harmonics, the water percolating through microscopic fissures, the almost imperceptible *groan* of tectonic stress far below. In this state, they believe they can sense the health of the whole system. Some elders claim to have felt the “pre-Engine resonance” in these moments, a ghost-note from a time before their people or their machines, a frequency the plateau was born singing.

Their relationship with the drip-kin is one of quiet kinship. The pale, eyeless creatures are seen as fellow inhabitants of the low, wet places, fellow listeners. They are not eaten; they are considered part of the balance. A child might gently guide a lost drip-kin back to its basin with a ripple of water, a silent acknowledgment.

To an outsider, they might seem like monks in a monastery of sound, or like cells in the body of a vast organism. But they would correct you, not with words, but with a look of mild confusion. They are not *serving* the plateau. They are *of* it. Their bones have the same basalt density. Their blood has the same alkaline taste. Their collective breath is out of sync with the individual, and in perfect sync with the slow, wet, resonant lung in which they live. They are the plateau’s awareness of itself, its memory, its gentle keepers—not masters, but the most intimate and necessary of its symbiotic parts. They are the quiet, conscious nodes in an ancient, vibrating mind.
