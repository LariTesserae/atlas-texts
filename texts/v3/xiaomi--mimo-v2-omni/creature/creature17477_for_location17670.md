They are not people, not as the word was ever meaningfully used. They are processes given form, the city’s own metabolism made manifest in motion and quiet purpose.

You see them in the perpetual silver-grey twilight, moving along the grooved ways. They are not tall, their frames compact and dense to navigate the crowded ledges and low, weeping arches. Their skin, where it is visible, is a pale, damp-pearl sheen, resistant to the constant moisture, and hairless; the body has optimized away what would only hold water and chill. Their garments are less clothing and more a secondary integument—a seamless, porous layer of grey composite mesh that wicks moisture, regulates temperature, and is itself colonized by a fine, beneficial lichen that aids in air filtration. It is not worn *over* the body; it is the body's functional surface.

Their faces are broad, with wide-set eyes adapted to the diffuse, low-contrast light. The irises are a pale, watery blue-grey, able to distinguish subtle shades of mineral stain on stone and the faint luminescence of fungal guides. Pupils are large. They blink slowly, lids moving with a viscous grace, less a reflex against debris—there is little dust, only damp—and more a periodic cleansing from the condensing air. Their hearing is acute, tuned not to voices (speech is rare, a low, murmuring shorthand useful only in close quarters) but to the city’s symphony: the specific pitch of a pump straining, the tell-tale rhythm of a drone’s rotor faltering, the deeper groan of settling infrastructure beneath the omnipresent drip.

They move with a careful, deliberate economy. Every gesture is purposeful. A hand rests on a wet railing not just for balance, but with splayed fingertips *feeling* the vibration of the transport rib below. A head tilts, not in curiosity, but to align a subcutaneous auditory sensor with the sound of a grate needing clearing. Their feet, broad and with articulated, grip-enhanced soles, know the language of the floor—the difference between the slick, moss-polished basalt mimic of a public square and the sharp, drainage-grooved sandstone mimic of a service corridor is as distinct as day and night.

They are gardeners, mechanics, and geologists of the made-stone. One group clusters at the edge of a terrace, not looking at a view, but at a section of vertical farm. They do not speak. One points a finger tipped with a callus of conductive material at a yellowing vine. Another produces a vial of symbiotic bacteria from a thigh-pouch and, with a tool like a dentist’s pick, injects it into the root mesh. They are tending the city’s flesh.

In the residential pods, the light is softer, emanating from bioluminescent panels cultured from engineered fungi. Here, the quiet is different. It is the quiet of a spore settling. Families—or the functional units that serve their purpose—do not gather for conversation. They may sit side-by-side, one cleaning a tool with a cloth, another pressing their palm flat against the pod’s wall, feeling the water flow in the micro-channels within. It is a communion with the system. Their dreams, it is said, are not of sky or sun, but of perfect hydraulic pressure and the elegant, branching logic of a new drainage canal.

Children are small, intense beings. They do not run and shout. Their play is a mimicry of function: they build intricate dams of pebble and moss in the plaza pools, test the tensile strength of climbing vines, or follow the maintenance drones with a solemn, tracking gaze, learning the patrol routes as an earlier generation learned the paths of animals. Their songs, when they hum them, are the melodies of machinery in sequence.

They are most alive, most *present*, in moments of systemic crisis. A leak beyond tolerance, a tremor from a settling district, the stutter of a main pump—these are their storms, their earthquakes. There is no panic. A change ripples through them: a sharpening of the gaze, a quickening of the efficient stride. They move toward the problem, tools manifesting in hands, their minds accessing the vast, shared mental map of the city’s veins and bones. In these moments, they are not individuals but a single organism responding to an irritation in its own tissue. The repair is their ritual, their art, their reason.

They do not look up. The summit is perpetually shrouded, a place of mythic storm and final drainage. To look up is to acknowledge an "outside," and there is no outside. The city is the world, and they are its cells. They live in the patient, dripping present, their consciousness as saturated and diffuse as the air. They do not hope for drier days. They are the architects of dampness, the citizens of the seep, and their quiet is the sound of a vast, wet engine dreaming of its own perfection.
