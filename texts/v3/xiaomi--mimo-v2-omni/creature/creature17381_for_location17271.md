The people of the Upper Tanneries are **the Grown**.

They do not think of themselves as separate from the city. To them, the distinction between inhabitant and habitat is a foreign notion, like trying to separate the sap from the tree. Their bodies tell the story of the place.

**Physiology & Adaptation**
Their skin carries a permanent dampness, not from sweat, but from a constant osmotic exchange with the humid air. It is pale, sometimes with a faint, greenish undertone near the veins, as if chlorophyll runs close to the surface. Their hands are their most remarkable feature: the fingers are long, the joints slightly hyper-extensible, and the fingertips are broad, smooth, and insensitive to cold or wet. They are living tools—perfect for pressing into soft bark, scoring guide-lines with a fingernail, or feeling the faintest pulse of growth in a young shoot. Their eyes are large, adapted to the perpetual grey twilight of the canopy, and they blink slowly, with a translucent nictitating membrane that flicks across to wash away constant mist.

They are not tall. The architecture, grown for them, is intimate, and so are they. Their bones are light, their musculature lean and wiry, built for climbing and balancing on swaying root-bridges, not for heavy labor. They are quiet movers, their bare feet making no sound on the root-mat floors. The constant roar of water has given them a different relationship with sound. They do not shout. They have developed a form of sub-vocal communication—a humming, resonant language carried more by bone conduction through the living walls than through open air. When they speak aloud, the vowel-rich tongue you hear is a simplified version for outsiders or for carrying across the great gaps between structures. Their true talk is a hum felt in the wood.

**Culture & Time**
Patience is not a virtue here; it is a law of physics. A person’s life is measured in growth-rings. An apprentice learns to whisper to a shoot; a master has shaped a doorway that has since thickened into an arch; an elder has seen a wall they guided in their youth become the load-bearing heart of a home for their grandchildren. They do not have historians; they have **listeners**, who press their ears to the ancient heartwood and interpret the faint vibrations of deep growth as memory.

Their society is organized not by family, but by **grove**—those who tend a particular cluster of structures. A grove is responsible for the health, shaping, and feeding of its trees. Disputes are rare and settled by mutual consultation with the wood itself. If two groves disagree, they will both place their hands on the disputed boundary and listen. Whichever side the sap-flow subtly warms toward is considered to have the more righteous claim. The trees, it seems, have opinions.

**Sustenance & Ritual**
Their diet shapes them. The cambium paste is their staple, filling them with a slow-release energy that matches the city’s pace. It makes their blood iron-rich and slightly sweet, a fact known to the mosquitos, which are their most persistent companion and pest. The frogs, too, are more than food; they are **bellows**. The Grown use the large, stupid *Pool-Drummers* in a ritual of breath. A keeper will gently hold a frog to their chest, mimicking its throbbing hum until their own lungs synchronize with its rhythm. This, they say, helps them breathe with the city, to not fight the weight of the water-filled air.

**The Unspoken Bond**
What is most profound is their **symbiotic unease**. They are cared for by the city, housed and warmed within its living body. In return, they are its nervous system, its hands, its memory. But there is an understanding, never voiced because it is too fundamental, that the tolerance is conditional. The city grows *around* them, not *for* them. They are useful parasites, beloved tumors. The slow shifts in doorways and walls are not hostility, but the city’s impersonal metabolism. The Grown adapt their sleeping positions, their storage nooks, their gathering spots, in a continuous, unconscious dance with the growth.

The strangest and most sacred of them are the **Deep-Listeners**. These individuals, often the very old or the strangely born, spend most of their time in the lower levels, in the warm, black dark where the roots become geological. They do not speak at all. Their skin has lost all pigment, their eyes have receded, and their fingertips have developed sensitive, hair-like cilia. They listen to the deepest hum of the city—the sound of the foundational roots splitting bedrock, centimeter by century. They are the ones who interpret the city’s long moods, its slow desires. When a new structure must be begun, or an old one is being abandoned to become fruiting body, it is the Deep-Listeners who sense the need first. They emerge, blinking in the grey light, and guide the groves with simple gestures, their instructions as slow and certain as the growth they command.

They are not masters. They are the city’s **consciousness**, the fleeting thoughts in a mind made of wood and water. And they know, with a calm that would terrify an outsider, that they live inside a great, slow dream. One day, perhaps in a thousand years, the dream will move on, or change its shape in a way that no longer includes hollows for flesh. Until then, they tend, they listen, and they grow where they are placed, their own lives a brief, bright moss on the skin of the living world.
