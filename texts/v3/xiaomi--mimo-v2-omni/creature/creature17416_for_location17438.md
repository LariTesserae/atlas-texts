You do not walk upon the World-Root. You walk *within* it, a mote in its vast, slow-breathing architecture. To understand what lives here is to understand that everything—every creature, every tribe, every fleeting sound—is a transient symptom of the host’s own biological imperative. The World-Root is not a biome; it is a **body**, and what we call life here is simply the activity of its cells, parasites, and symbionts.

**The Host Itself:**
First and foremost, the living entity is the primary inhabitant. It has a consciousness, but not a mind—more a distributed, chemical awareness. It senses the chemical composition of the thin atmosphere, the weight of condensation on its spires, the seismic tremors of its own growth. Its “thoughts” are plumes of spores released on calculated winds, the directional thickening of a root-wall toward a nutrient-rich aquifer. It does not think *about* the creatures upon it; it incorporates them. A dead grafer is not a tragedy; it is a localized fertilizer packet. A tribe’s abandoned fire-nest is a carbon deposit to be absorbed. Its primary drives are expansion, consolidation, and efficient resource allocation, expressed as the tectonic groans and the silent, hollow conquest of territory.

**Symbiotic Residents:**
*   **The Lichen-Mantas:** These are the closest thing to permanent animal residents. Vast, flat, leathery creatures that cling to the high-canopy ice where the faint sun touches. They are photosynthetic, but also feed by filtering the nutrient-mist transpired by the World-Root itself. Their undersides are a felted mat of rootlets, forming a symbiotic exchange with the host. They drift slowly, like living continents of flesh, and their migration patterns are the true calendars of the tribes below.
*   **Spore-Sifters:** In the damp, hollow interiors, entire ecosystems exist in the dripping dark. Blind, translucent arthropods with feathery gills walk the walls, harvesting the constant fall of fungal spores and particulate organic matter. They are the cleaners, the decomposers re-consumed. Their lifecycle is the interior weather of the World-Root: a cycle of dampness, decay, and quiet harvesting.
*   **The Resin-Borers:** Small, beetle-like creatures that drill into the living “wood” to feed on sap. They seal their tunnels behind them with a secretion that hardens into a clear, amber-like plug. These plugs, over centuries, become the only “fossil” record in a world that consumes its dead. A tribe might find a vein of this amber and prize it for its clarity and its story—a map of a creature’s life, sealed in the body of the god.

**Parasitic & Opportunistic Life:**
*   **The Hollow-Eels:** Elongated, limbless vertebrates that swim through the condensation pools and the water-filled xylem channels deep within the structure. They are ambush predators, sensing vibrations through the living walls. Their presence is a sign of a deep, interconnected hydraulic system, and the tribes know the pools where they hunt are also sources of precious water—if you are quick and lucky.
*   **Carrion-Crickets:** Large, flightless insects that converge with terrifying speed on any significant death—a fallen grazer, a tribal casualty. They do not eat flesh, but the fast-growing fungal necromass that blooms on it within hours. They are the vanguard of the World-Root’s recycling system, converting solid matter back into the substrate of the living world.

**The Tribes: “The Weave-Walkers,” “Spire-Tappers,” “Ridge-Singers”**
The scattered tribes are not masters here. They are **adaptations**. They are the World-Root’s externalized nervous system, its mobile sensory organs for the macro-scale.
*   **Their Bodies:** Lean, long-limbed, with wide, gripping feet and hands calloused to a bark-like toughness. Their skin is pale, adapted to the gloom, often marked with ritual scars that mimic the patterns of vascular scarring on the root-surface. Their vision is acute in low light, but they are partially color-blind, seeing the world in gradients of bioluminescent blue, gray, and deep violet.
*   **Their Minds:** They think in **connections**, not objects. A tool is not “a spear”; it is “a-thorn-from-the-growing-edge, hardened-by-fire, bound-with-vine-from-the-sagging-plains.” They do not have a word for “territory,” but they have twelve words for different types of hollow ground and their acoustic properties. Their maps are songs, encoding the resonant frequencies of safe ridges, the groaning zones to avoid, and the migratory calls of the lichen-mantas.
*   **Their Spirituality:** They do not worship the World-Root; that would be like a blood cell worshipping the body. Instead, they practice **Symbiotic Animism**. Every significant structure has a spirit: the Wind-Flute Spires, the Heartwood Abyss, the First-Shedding Ground. Their rituals are practical negotiations with these aspects. A “Spire-Tapper” doesn’t just harvest sap; he sings a specific, harmonic plea to the “vein-spirit” to release its moisture, a practice that seems to influence capillary action through subtle vibration. To kill a grazer is to thank the “Spine-Ridge Spirit” for providing, and to quickly disperse the remains so the World-Root’s own scavengers may reclaim them without attracting the attention of larger, hollow-dwelling predators.
*   **Their Technology:** Their most advanced tool is **knowledge-in-context**. A woven net is not just mesh; it is a understanding of which fibers flex in the cold, which knots hold on smooth, living bark. Their greatest engineering is the **listening-platform**: a suspended structure of bone and fiber, hung in a known acoustic hollow, which amplifies distant sounds—the crack of a shedding limb, the migration call, the tell-tale groan of an impending growth-shift—miles away. They build nothing permanent, because to do so is to become an obstacle, and the World-Root removes obstacles. They are, in essence, a conscious, roving leukocyte in the body of their world, identifying points of need, conflict, or opportunity and migrating to address them, their very survival a homeostatic function for the giant they inhabit.

Thus, life here is a symphony of adaptation to a single, overwhelming biological fact. The tribes are not its pinnacle, but its most fragile and perceptive note. They live in the cradle of a living god, a cradle that is also a tomb, and their entire existence is a lullaby sung to keep it, and themselves, in a balanced, precarious sleep.
