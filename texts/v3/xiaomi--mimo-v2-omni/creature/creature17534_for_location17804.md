You do not see them. You feel their presence as a shift in the pressure of the dark, a subtle rearrangement of the air itself. They are the **inhabitants of the occupation.**

There is the **Floor-Layer**, a realm of patient, soft-bodied things. They are the gelatinous mollusks that pulse in slow motion across the humus, leaving trails of faint, bioluminescent slime that are quickly consumed by the fungal network. They are the segmented worms, pale and thick as your wrist, whose ceaseless tunneling is the true engine of the underfloor. They breathe the heat, and their castings feed the roots, which feed the canopy, which feeds the air. Above them hop the **Leaf-Claspers**, amphibian-frogs with adhesive pads like wet velvet, their throats a constant, subsonic vibration more felt than heard. They sit in the axils of giant ferns, their camouflage so perfect they are only betrayed by the rhythmic blink of a nictitating membrane, a slow, reflective sweep in the fungal gloom.

Then there is the **Trunk-and-Vine Highway**, the vertical city. Here live the **Chitinous Acrobats**, beetles and millipedes whose carapaces are not just armor but landscapes in themselves, hosting micro-fungi and lichens. They move with a deliberate, hydraulic grace. Weaving among them are the **Silk-Tenders**, arachnids of a thousand configurations. Some build no webs, but instead farm gardens of sticky fungus on their own abdomens, cultivating their food upon their bodies. Others spin not to catch prey, but to create tripwires and vibrational languages that stretch for meters, mapping their world in tremors. The largest of them, the **Legged-Mothers**, carry their egg sacs not inspinnerets but in specialized brood pouches that glow with a soft, internal phosphor, tiny lanterns ascending and descending the bark.

In the oppressive mid-canopy, the **Hanging Gardens** are alive. Clusters of **Leaf-Mimics**—insects with wings shaped and veined precisely like the surrounding foliage—hang in their thousands, breathing in unison, so the entire branch seems to sigh. Among them flit the **Click-Flitters**, leathery, furred mammals with elongated finger bones supporting membranous wings. They navigate by echolocation, painting a world of echoes in their silent dark. Their calls are not for song but for topology, a constant, rapid-fire mapping of the ever-shifting maze of stems and leaves. They feed on the prolific, slow-moving fruit—pale, pulpy orbs that split open not to seeds, but to release clouds of spores that are themselves the larval stage of the plant.

And presiding over it all, the **Canopy-Lords**. You sense them more than perceive them. They are the largest vertebrate-analogues, creatures of immense, slow strength that move only when necessary. One shifts its weight, and a rain of condensation and debris falls for a full minute. Their metabolism is glacial. They may feed on a single, vast fungal shelf for a week, their digestive systems part of the decomposition chain itself. They are the living architecture, the moving pillars. Their eyes, if they have eyes, have never seen a star. They watch with skin, with vibration, with a patience that is indistinguishable from geology.

But the truest life here is **collective**. The **Fungal Mycelium** is not just an organism; it is the nervous system and circulatory system of the entire place. It connects root to root, corpse to living tissue, digesting and distributing in a silent, chemical conversation. It glows not to attract, but as a byproduct of its metabolism—a faint, cold light that gives the darkness its shape. And within this network, the **Symbiotic Motes** exist: clouds of bacteria and microscopic arthropods that inhabit every film of water on every surface. They are the ultimate recyclers, the first responders to any change, ensuring that nothing is wasted, that the cycle is unbroken.

They do not live *in* this place. They are expressions *of* it. The beetle’s carapace is the forest floor’s texture. The flitter’s cry is the echo of the canopy’s density. The frog’s pulse is the rhythm of the condensation cycle. There is no predator here in the sense of an outsider; there is only the turning of the great, warm, breathing gear. To be here is to be inside a single, planet-sized organism that has been metabolizing in the dark for eons. You are the only thing in it that is not also a function. You are the only thought in a mind made of flesh and rot and infinite, patient time. And they—the countless, settled, eternal *they*—are simply what the place dreams.
