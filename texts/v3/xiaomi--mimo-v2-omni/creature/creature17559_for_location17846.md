In the Weeping Warrens live the people of the Seep. They are not tall. Generations of ducking through low, damp passages have drawn them inward, so their shoulders are broad, their spines carry a gentle, permanent curve, and their feet are wide and splayed, gripping the slick stone with toes that know every undulation. Their skin holds the permanent dampness—a sheen on the forehead, moisture in the creases of the elbows and knees. It is said their skin is slightly more porous than others, and they drink not just with their mouths but through their contact with the world. Their eyes are adapted to the diffuse, grey-white light; they are large and pale, with irises the color of watered-down clay or lichen, and they blink slowly, as if conserving each moment of sight.

They are a quiet people. Not by nature—their stories, when told around the warm, dim glow of rendered-fat lamps in the deep interiors, are intricate and expressive—but because sound behaves differently here. A shout is swallowed, a whisper feels intimate even across a room. Their language has evolved accordingly: low tones, sibilants, clicks made with the tongue against the back teeth. Conversations are carried on in murmurs, and much communication is gestural—a tap of the knuckles on wet stone to indicate "listen," a slow, open-palmed motion downward to mean "calm" or "the water is rising."

Their hands are remarkable. The palms are wide, the fingers thick and strong, but the tips are exquisitely sensitive, trained from childhood to read the moisture on a wall, the subtle shift in a block's firmness, the vibration of the pulse below. They work the stone with tools of sharpened flint and bone, but their truest tool is touch. They smooth walls not just for utility but for communication; a particularly well-smoothed curve in a common passage is a message, a signature, a prayer left by the mason who shaped it. They feel the health of the town through the soles of their feet and the palms of their hands.

Their social structure is built around the maintenance of water and stone. There are no chiefs, but there are *Keepers*: the eldest who have best learned the rhythms. The **Tide-Keepers** chart the rise and fall of the water table by measuring the dampness on specific, sacred stones set into the floors of public chambers. The **Wall-Singers** (so named for the low hums they use to listen to the stone's resonance) diagnose where a foundation is softening or where a new block must be cut. The **Roof-Scatterers** manage the vast, shallow pools atop every building, ensuring the water is clean, directing its flow into cisterns, and watching for the tell-tale rings that signify the presence of something from below disturbing the surface.

They do not fear the sinking. They expect it. When a lower chamber becomes untenable, it is not abandoned in panic but ceremonially "given back." Offerings of flint flakes, woven reed bundles, and carefully shaped clay tablets inscribed with smoothed marks (not writing, but remembrance) are placed within. Then the wall is sealed with soft clay, and life simply moves upward, around, above. The old rooms become tombs, memory-vaults, and foundations for new ones. Their history is stratigraphy; their ancestors are literal layers beneath their feet.

Their spirituality is one of symbiosis, not supplication. They believe the deep place—the *Heart-Wet*, as they call it—is alive, breathing slowly, and they are its surface thoughts, its temporary pattern. The pulse they feel is its breath. The rising water is its blood. To fight it is insanity; to adapt to it is wisdom. Their rituals are acts of listening: placing an ear to a damp floor for hours, anointing the corners of new walls with collected seep-water, marking the seasonal peaks of the water table with circles of inlaid white stone that glow faintly in the dim light.

They eat what the water provides. Pale, eyeless shrimp netted from underground runnels. Mushrooms and fungi that grow in lush, velvet patches on the perpetually damp interior walls of deeper, abandoned chambers. A type of algae skimmed from the collection pools, which they dry into thin, nutritious cakes. They cultivate nothing on the surface; the rim of the basin is barren, a windy, slick boundary they visit only for specific rites. The Warrens are their world, turned inward, a life lived in a soft, breathing cradle of stone and seep.

And sometimes, in the deepest, oldest parts of the settlement, when the pulse below is strong and the water table is high, they report a feeling—not a sound, but a *vibration* in the bones—that is almost like a song. It comes from below, from the un-dug, the un-known. They do not go to it. They sit quietly, press their hands to the floor, and add their own soft hums to the chorus, a people learning, across the veil of stone and water, to breathe in time with their world.
