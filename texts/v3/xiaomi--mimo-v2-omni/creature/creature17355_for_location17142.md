You come to know them not by their faces—for in the dim amber, all features are softened to smudges and silhouettes—but by their *sounds*. The soft, gritty shuffle of bare feet on ceramic clay. The specific, rhythmic *scrape-scrape-tap* of a woman maintaining the growths along a walkway arch, her curved blade removing the papery bulbs with a precision that speaks of a pattern memorized in muscle. The low, breathy chorus of greeting when two meet in a corridor, a phrase of three sibilants and a long, open vowel that hangs in the hot air.

They are a people of small, continuous gestures. A man kneels at the base of a great column, his palms flat against the spiraling grooves. He is not reading, not in the way you understand it. He is *listening* with his skin. His eyes are closed. You see the muscles in his forearms tense and relax in a slow sequence as his hands shift position, following the thermal contours of the data. Another comes, places her hands over his. For a long moment, they are still, sharing the information through skin and stone. Then they part with a soft exhalation. An archive updated. A message left in the language of warmth.

Their days are not marked by sun or moon, but by the cycles of their herds—the tended growths of the vegetation. There is the "Scraping Hour," when the colony moves as one, a quiet procession of figures carrying tools of sharpened basalt, to clear the encroaching filaments from the vital walkways and the thresholds of their layered homes. The air thickens with spores then, a coppery dust that settles on your shoulders and makes you want to sneeze, though they do not. Their bodies are reconciled to it. The dust is part of their respiration, their very skin.

The very old ones, those with the faintly luminous skin, seem to exist on a different rhythm. They move less, often stationed near the great pillars, their hands resting on the stone for hours. They are the primary readers, the living conduits for the thermal library. Sometimes, one will speak—not to another person, but to the column itself—a string of the aspirated phrases, as if in conversation. You learn to watch them. When they begin to speak, others gather, not crowding, but finding perches on worn stone benches or the low roofs of walkways, listening. It is a history lesson, a legal decree, a genealogy—all vibrating up from the bedrock into their bones.

Their nourishment is a mystery of transformation. They harvest the most abundant filament grasses and the dry, pithy centers of the larger lichen-tongues. These are not eaten directly. Instead, they are taken to communal grinding chambers—shallow, circular rooms where generations of grinding have worn the ceramic floor into a smooth basin. Here, the plant matter is crushed with heavy stones into a fine, rust-colored powder. This powder is their staple. Mixed with water (a carefully conserved commodity, dripped from the thickest resin deposits and stored in lined basalt cisterns), it forms a dense paste. This paste is pressed into molds, or layered onto slabs of warm stone near the thermal vents to dry into a hard, brittle cake. It tastes of dust, of minerals, of the faintest acrid whisper of the resin-light. It sustains. It does not delight.

They are not a people who build outward. They build *inward*. Expansion means delving deeper into the network of natural fissures behind the main atrium, sealing new chambers with resin, and patiently waiting for the vegetation to colonize the walls, integrating the new space into the living whole. A young couple is given a new doorway, cut into a wall that was solid a season before. Their first task is not to furnish it, but to cultivate the specific orange moss that is prized for sleeping mats, coaxing it across the threshold with offerings of powderedite and whispered, aspirated encouragement.

And they do something else, something you only understand by watching the elders. At the end of their "day"—a cycle of activity determined not by light, but by a subtle shift in the pillar-harmonic they feel in their bones—they gather. Not in a central square, but at the bases of the four oldest, largest columns, those whose spiral-grooves are deepest and most complex. They stand, or sit, and for a time, they are utterly silent. They press hands and foreheads to the warm stone. They *feel*. The great, slow pulse of the atrium’s heat engine, the drip of the resin, the hiss of the filaments, the pop of the spores—it all continues. But in this ritual silence, they are not *filling* the sound; they are *joining* it. It is a moment of communion, not with a deity, but with the fact of the place itself. The acknowledgment that the seal is real, that the sky is a myth, that this heat, this dimness, this crackling life, is not a condition they endure, but the *substance* of their world. A world they chose, and keep choosing, with every breath of the hot, dry, loud air.

When they look at you, their dark eyes hold no pity, no curiosity. They hold a kind of quiet, settled knowledge. They see the salt crust on your skin, the confusion in your eyes, the way you start at the sudden pop of a wall-fruit. They see that you are listening with your ears, not your skin. They understand that you are *outside* the column’s story. And they welcome you to listen, to watch, to feel what you can. But they do not expect you to understand. The spiral-warmth under their palms says enough. It says they are home.
