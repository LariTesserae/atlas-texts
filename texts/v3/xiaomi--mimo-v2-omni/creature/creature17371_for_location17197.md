The people of the Weeping Plateau are called the **Lithans**, a name others might give them, for they are one with the stone. They are not tall, for the land offers no vertical encouragement. Their frames are compact and dense, built low to the ground for stability on the slick terraces. Their skin has a slightly waxy, polished quality, not from oil, but from a lifetime of perpetual dampness that has conditioned it to neither crack nor chafe. Their eyes are large, with wide, dark irises adapted to the flat, shadowless silver light that never harshens or dims.

Their most striking feature is their **stillness**. They move with a geologic economy, a slow, deliberate grace born of conserving energy in a land of scarce sustenance. A Lithan walking across the plaza does not hurry; each step is placed with the precision of a stone settling into place. They are utterly silent walkers, their soft-soled footwear—woven from the fibrous roots of the moss-shrubs—making no sound on the wet grit or basalt.

Their entire society is a ritual of **attention**. The constant, soft patter of condensation is their clock, their calendar, and their scripture. They do not mark time by sun or moon, but by the accumulation of water: the filling of a cistern, the slow drip from a specific carved channel onto a sacred receiving stone. Their primary craft, and highest art, is **water-sculpting**. From childhood, they learn to carve the intricate networks of channels on rooftops and in communal plazas. A master carver is revered not for speed, but for the perfect, silent efficiency of her work—guiding each bead of moisture with minimal loss, creating patterns that are both functional and, in their own stark way, beautiful: spirals that mimic the unfurling of a moss-frond, straight lines that echo the clean fracture of a new basalt column.

They speak little, and when they do, their voices are low and soft, pitched to carry in the damp, dense air without echoing. Their language is rich in words for different kinds of wetness, for the texture of stone, and for subtle shades of grey and green. Much communication is non-verbal: a slight tilt of the head toward a particular cistern, a hand tracing the path of a new channel in the air, a shared, prolonged gaze at a thickening bead of water on a leaf. Silence is not an absence, but a shared medium of understanding.

Their dwellings are extensions of their bodies. Inside the low, basalt homes, the air is cool and humid. There are no soft furnishings. They sit and sleep on woven mats of dried moss-core, elevated slightly on stone platforms. Their possessions are few and stone-based: bowls polished to a sheen, carving tools of harder, imported flint or obsidian (their most precious trade goods, for nothing on the plateau can hold an edge), and small, intricate water-clocks—vessels designed to fill and tip at a specific interval, marking the phases of the drip.

They have no livestock, no beasts of burden. All toil is human. They cultivate the moss-shrubs not for food—they sustain themselves on a sparse, gelatinous lichen scraped from shaded rock faces and on the mineral-rich water itself—but for their roots and fibers. They prune the shrubs with stone blades, encouraging them to grow dense and low, their roots knitting the thin soil to the rock. This is their agriculture: a patient gardening of the cracks in the world.

Their social structure is as layered as the terraces. At the lowest, most foundational level are the **Gatherers**, who wander the fractures in the constant mist, collecting lichen, fiber, and rare, fragile crystals. Above them are the **Carvers**, the shapers of their world. And at the heart of the town, in the most sheltered, quietest dwelling, lives the **Drip-Listener**, an elder, often of indeterminate gender, whose sole purpose is to listen. They place their ear against the great central cistern, against the carved sacred stones, against the bare rock itself. They interpret the subtle changes in the rhythm of the plateau—the slight increase in drip from a certain fissure, the faint, deep groan of settling stone—as oracles. They are the keepers of the equilibrium, the interpreters of the land’s quiet, constant murmur.

The Lithans feel no sense of poverty. To them, the world is perfectly full: it is full of water, full of stone, and full of the profound, necessary silence between drips. They have no concept of “sky” as an open expanse to be yearned for; the sky is merely the upper surface of the weeping ceiling that feeds them. They are waiting, but not for a change. They are waiting *within* the eternal moment, their entire existence an act of resonant attention to the slow, patient pulse of their wet, stone world. They are the consciousness of the plateau, and the plateau is, in turn, the architecture of their souls.
