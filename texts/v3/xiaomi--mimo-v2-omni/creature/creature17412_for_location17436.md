The first thing to understand about life in the Lung is that **you are never alone.** The concept of solitude, as the surface understands it, does not exist here. You are always accompanied by the rasp of a hundred bodies moving through the stone a few inches from your ear. You are always listening to the deep, shifting harmony of the city’s many breaths—the groan of cooling basalt, the sigh of rising vapor, the wet percussion of a thousand drips, and the foundational, chitinous hum of the Vermithrax.

To be born in Vethara is to be born into a specific, sensory language. An infant’s first conscious memories are not of a face, but of a **texture**: the cool, damp roughness of the basalt wall against a cheek during a nap in a honeycomb alcove. Its first lullaby is not a melody, but the **sound**: the subsonic vibration of a deep migration, felt in the crib’s frame before it is heard, a rhythm that becomes the baseline of all sleep. Its first word is often not “mother” or “father,” but a pointing sound—*“there”*—directed at the pale, pulsing trail of bioluminescence leaking from a hairline crack in the floor, marking the passage of a small vermithrax colony beneath.

The **children** of Vethara are a study in adapted stillness. They are not hyperactive. The heat and the thick air breed a languid, observant temperament. They play games of silent mimicry, learning to communicate with hand signs long before they learn to whisper, because the games with sound—shrieks, laughs, shouts—are for the **Upper Ledges**, the only chambers where a raised voice won’t summon a curious or agitated swarm from the walls. Their favorite toys are not manufactured, but harvested: a shed vermithrax segment, iridescent and hollow, becomes a whistle; a hardened lump of glassite, polished by tiny hands, is a treasure. They learn to walk not on open ground, but by touch—their small, sure fingers trailing along guide-ropes and the familiar, damp grooves of the walls, their feet knowing by feel where the stone is slick with residue and where it is safe to step.

The **adults** carry the city in their bodies. The **cart-pullers** have arms like knotted rope and a peculiar, rolling gait, a constant subtle adjustment to the imagined pull of the track, even in their sleep. Their skin is permanently flushed from exertion in the heat, and their ears are tuned to a specific spectrum of sound—the creak of a stressed iron strut, the change in pitch of a pump’s labor, the tell-tale shift from the grinding of vermithrax to the scraping of stone that means a new passage is being born nearby.

The **glassite processors** work in the dim, hot chambers nearest the smelters. Their hands are a map of fine, silvery scars from the cured resin, which is sharper than obsidian when first sliced. They move with a calm, deliberate economy, for a single hurried motion can mean a deep, clean cut that heals slowly in this air. They speak in low, continuous murmurs to one another, a running commentary that becomes part of the room’s noise, never rising in pitch or volume.

The **pump-keepers** are the city’s priests and its most neurotic inhabitants. They live and sleep beside their thundering machines, their dreams dominated by the rhythm of pistons and the smell of hot oil and steam. They develop a hypochondriac’s sensitivity to vibration, laying a hand on a pipe for hours, feeling for a stutter, a tremor, an arrhythmia that heralds failure. They are respected and slightly feared, for they hold the city’s life in their calloused hands, and the shadow of the Lower Seventh deluge clings to them like the ever-present damp.

And then there are those who **listen to the stone**. Not the miners, who listen for weakness, but the **Seam-Tasters**, the old ones who have spent a lifetime in the deep galleries. They sit in absolute darkness, hands pressed to the living rock, and interpret the long, slow language of the Lung. They feel the thermal surges coming hours before the instruments. They can place the location of a major vermithrax migration by the pattern of vibrations in their teeth. They are the ones who understand that Vethara is not a place, but a **process**—a slow, geological conversation between the cooling earth and the life it cradles. They speak of the city not in terms of districts or levels, but in rhythms and temperatures and moods. “The rock is hungry today,” they might say, or “The western flank is sighing; a new void is opening there,” or simply, “The heat is patient.”

The **Verminthax themselves** are a presence that transcends mere fauna. They are the city’s geography, its infrastructure, and its gods. A dense, shimmering flow moving through a main gallery is a traffic hazard, a tourist attraction, and a moment of awe. Workers will pause, pressing themselves into alcoves, and watch the silent river of pale, segmented bodies pass, feeling the warm, displaced air on their faces. To harm one without cause is not illegal, but it is considered deeply foolish and vaguely blasphemous—you do not bite the hand of the world that houses you. Children who are cruel to the small, finger-sized vermithrax are spoken of gravely, as if they have committed a sin against the future.

At the thermal surges, twice a day, a unique kind of life emerges: the **deep-scouts**. These are the humans—often scarred, always quiet—who venture into the newly opened voids *with* the vermithrax, not behind them. Equipped with only a vermithrax-fat lamp and a lifetime of instinct, they follow the creatures into the hot, unknown dark, mapping the new territory by its life, not its emptiness. They return not with maps of stone, but with reports of light, of the direction of the flow, of the taste of the air. They are the city’s eyes in the blind, growing dark.

Life in Vethara is a **collaborative haunting**. Humans haunt the vermithrax highways, building their nests in the creatures’ wake. The vermithrax haunt the humans’ walls, their endless passage a subtle, grinding reminder of who the true tenants are. And the Lung itself—hot, wet, and dark—haunts them all with its slow, immense, mineral patience. To live here is to accept that you are a temporary rhythm in a much older song, your breath and heartbeat a fleeting counterpoint to the drip of water, the grind of chitin, and the patient sigh of stone that is still learning how to be still.
