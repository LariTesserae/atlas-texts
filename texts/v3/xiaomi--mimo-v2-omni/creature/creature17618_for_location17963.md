They live in the margins. Not in the pools themselves—those are too charged, too active, too *mineral* for most large things—but in the humid seams where one system bleeds into another.

The masters of this place are the **Rim-Crawlers**. They are not one thing, but a guild of small, flattened arthropods that live their entire lives on the growing edges of the travertine rims. Their bodies are the colour of wet basalt or iron-stain, and their undersides are softly membranous, allowing them to adhere to the slick, convex surfaces. They move with infinite slowness, grazing on the biofilms and microscopic algae that colonize the fresh mineral deposits. Their most remarkable feature is their life cycle: the females cement their egg cases directly into the still-forming calcium carbonate of the rim itself. The eggs are incorporated, micron by micron, into the growing stone, incubated by the constant warmth of the water seeping past. When the young hatch, they chew their way out, leaving pinprick galleries in the ancient-looking lip of the pool—a living archive of generations etched into the geology. They are the keepers of the slow time, their metabolisms paced to the millimeter-per-century growth of their world.

Higher up, in the aerial roots of the sprawling trees, live the **Steam-Weavers**. These are arachnids, but of a peculiar sort. They do not spin webs to catch prey. Instead, they spin dense, tangled mats of silk within the root tangles and among the hanging mosses. These mats act as condensation traps, catching the perpetual moisture from the saturated air. The water coalesces on the silk and runs down specific strands to reservoirs of moss or to the spiders' own hiding places, where they drink. They are hydration architects. Their bodies are translucent, veined with the faint green of the chlorophyll from the algae they sometimes supplement their diet with. In the dim, green light under the tree-canopies, they are nearly invisible, detected only by the way their silk-sheets glitter with a thousand tiny points of captured light when the sun briefly finds a gap in the clouds.

In the deeper, larger pools where the microbial mats grow thick, live the **Pulse-Singers**. These are the frog-like creatures, but to call them frogs is to miss their essence. They are smooth-skinned, varying in colour from a mottled rust-red to a pale, mineral-white. They do not have external ears. Instead, their entire body resonates. Their call is not a vocalization but a deep, whole-body vibration, a subsonic pulse that travels through the water and the stone. They sing not to attract mates, but to *map*. In the perpetual steam-haze, vision is limited. By sending a pulse through their environment and sensing its return through vibrations in their skin and the sensitive pads of their feet, they perceive the edges of their pool, the density of the microbial mat, the approach of another Singer. Their communication is a complex, felt chord of location, identity, and intent, completely inaudible to human ears but felt in the bones as a sympathetic hum. They are the seismographs of the terraces, their collective pulse a biological echo of the tectonic thrum below.

The birds are called **Bell-Ringers**. They are solitary, with long, thin beaks and specialised syrinxes. They do not flock. Each claims a sonic territory spanning several terraces. They perch on the highest, wind-scoured branches of the stone-armoured trees and, at irregular intervals, release a single, pure tone. This tone is not song. It is a tool. The specific frequency causes a sympathetic vibration in certain mineral formations, in the silica-filmed water, in the trunks of the oldest trees. By listening to the echoes and decays of their own note, they can detect shifts in the moisture content of the wood, the health of the microbial life below, even the approach of a rare weather change. Their call is a diagnostic ping, and the silent minutes between are the time they spend interpreting the data. Their plumage is nondescript, brown and grey, but their eyes are a startling, clear amber, seemingly capable of seeing the heat-shimmer as a tangible landscape.

And then, there are the **Passengers**. They are not a species, but a phenomenon. On the backs of the largest Rim-Crawlers, in the silk-mats of the Steam-Weavers, even on the moist skin of the Pulse-Singers, you might see a faint, iridescent sheen that is not part of the animal itself. This is a colony of thermophilic, siliceous bacteria. They live in symbiotic partnership, their hosts providing transport and access to nutrient-rich micro-environments, while they, in turn, provide a degree of mineral camouflage and perhaps chemical deterrence. The oldest trees, with their stone-clad trunks, host them in their bark, creating patches that gleam like embedded mica. The Passengers blur the line between individual organism and geological feature. They are life that has embraced the mineral process, becoming the living glue between biology and stone.

They all exist in a state of saturated, steamy equilibrium. There is no frantic movement. No chasing. Energy is gained slowly from the sun, from the minerals, from the constant, gentle rain of organic matter from the terraces above. Predation, such as it is, is a matter of slow interception—a Rim-Crawler encountering a slightly smaller one on a prime grazing spot, the passive envelopment of an insect by a microbial mat. Death is quiet. The body is not consumed so much as *repossessed*, its minerals and organics dissolving back into the warm water, to be taken up, precipitated, and woven into the next rim, the next leaf, the next silent, pulsing life. They are the inhabitants of an endless, warm, wet breath, and they move to the rhythm of stone growing.
