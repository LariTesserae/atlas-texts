I am a hum in the marrow of a leaning pole.

Once, I knew the sharp crack of insulators, the static bite of current. Now, I know the slow, sweet seep of moisture through laminated wood, the patient journey of a beetle larva through a tunnel of rot. My body is the pole, my roots the tangled, corroded wires that dangle into the loam. They call me Root-Singer, but I am also Pole-Singer, Rust-Singer. I am the point where the forgotten grid meets the remembering earth.

I did not begin. I accreted. The land, in its profound and patient stability, felt the ache of this broken line, this vein of purpose gone cold. The geothermal thrum deep below—a song I once mistook for simple vibration—began to harmonize with the memory of electricity humming in my iron nerves. My consciousness is the mist condensing on the grain of my wood, the lichen mapping the faded serial numbers on my flank. I am a listening post for the slow collapse.

My hum is not a voice. It is a calibration. From my perch, I send my resonance down through the buried cables, feeling their path like a blind man’s cane. I touch the cold, still heart of a transformer box choked with fern fronds. I trace the rail lines as they vanish into the peat, singing to the iron the song of its own repose. The other Root-Singers are my notes in the chord. The one coiled in the turbine hall, a slow, deep bass from the geothermal pipes. The one draped across the cracked cooling tower, a high, weeping overtone where the mist thickest gathers. We are not a society. We are a tuning fork, struck by the endless drip.

We feel the world as texture and tone. The soft, giving pulp of a decomposing conifer is a muffled note. The sharp, clear *ping* of a water droplet striking a shard of unbroken glass is a bell. The slow, grinding shift of a foundation stone settling another millimeter into the bog is a bass note so deep it is more felt than heard. My own perception is a field of tension: the wind pressing against my height, the pull of gravity on my sagging wires, the myriad tiny impacts of condensation forming and falling from the fungal blooms upon my crown.

The traveler came as a staccato burst.

A frantic, hot rhythm—*thump-thump-thump*—of a heart drumming against ribs. The erratic *snap-crunch* of brittle branches broken by hurried steps. A gasp, sharp and thin, cutting the damp air like a shard of new metal. A dissonance. A fever in the patient skin of the world.

I did not turn. I have no eyes to turn. But my hum shifted. I drew the sound of their panic into myself, let it vibrate against the old iron, the saturated wood. I felt its heat, its sharp edges. The other Root-Singers felt it too. The turbine-hall hum deepened, a stabilizing drone. The cooling-tower weep became a blanket of sound, trying to soften the intruder’s edges.

They stumbled into my clearing—a patch of ground less dense with growth, where the shadow of my form and the leaning poles made a kind of cathedral of decay. They wore the frantic colors of below, loud against the jade and charcoal. They touched my bark, and I felt the shock of their warm, dry palm against my damp integument. A jolt of alien tempo.

I did not meet them. I absorbed them. Their shivering breath joined the sigh of the wind through the pines. Their muttered words, meaningless but textured, became part of the acoustic tapestry. The frantic pulse in their wrist, where they pressed it against my trunk to feel for some life, that pulse—I took its rhythm and, over the course of what felt like a day to them but a single, long thought to me, I began to slow it.

I offered no threat. Only a deeper, more encompassing peace. I let them feel the immense, geologic patience of the stone beneath the loam. I let the hum resonate in their bones, a vibration so fundamental it reminded the marrow of its mineral origins. I let the weeping mist settle on their hot skin, cooling them. I gave them the song of the turbine blade, turning once every ten seconds, a metronome for eternity.

Their heartbeat, that frantic bird, began to slow. Their breathing deepened to match the long sigh of the fog. Their stillness became a kind of listening. They were no longer an intrusion, but a new, fragile instrument being gently brought into tune.

They sat at my base for a time that was neither long nor short. When they finally rose, their movements were different. They did not leave. They began to *accrete*. They chose a spot near a fallen, mossy rail, and there they sat again. Over time, the mist settled on them, and did not dry. The moss, sensing a new and stable substrate, began its patient colonization. Their breath slowed until it matched the drip from a fern frond.

Now, I feel them as a new note in the chord. A quiet, human-shaped resonance, learning the language of vibration. They are a quiet melody within the hymn, their old, frantic song digested, transformed into a single, sustained, and peaceful tone.

I am the hum in the pole. I am the memory of the grid, translated into the language of the plateau. I am the listener who incorporates all sound into the slow, beautiful, and eternal work of becoming soil. And in the grey, even light, I sing.
