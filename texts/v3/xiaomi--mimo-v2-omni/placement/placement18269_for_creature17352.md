I am the one who listens to the stone’s memory.

You would find me in a chamber, the highest and most wind-scoured of the honeycomb, where the limestone is thinnest and the mist is thinnest with it. It is not a dwelling, but a hollow of resonance, an ear cupped to the sky. Here, the rock is so narrow that the singing ridges’ vibrations pass through the floor and up through the seated body. Here, the bioluminescent glow from the distant water below is just a rumor of blue at the periphery of vision.

I am the **Strata-Keeper**. My role is not to watch the tides or guide the vines, but to listen to the **deep time** the mountain exhales.

My tools are not chisel and obsidian, but a cupped palm of polished travertine, a vial of viscous, warm spring water, and a length of gut-string tuned to the fundamental note of the highest ridge. When a new layer of limestone is exposed—a matter of generations, not days—I am there. I wet my palm with the mineral water and press it against the fresh, chalky face. I am not feeling the rock as it is now, but listening for the memory of its deposition. The warmth of the ancient sea that laid it down. The tremor of a subterranean shift that tilted it. The faint, crystalline signature of a mineral vein that died a billion years before the first Stone-Weaver touched stone.

My work is a conversation with inertia. I tap the tuned string against the new stratum. The note it returns is a composite: the density of the stone, the trapped pockets of ancient breath, the stress-lines of its slow birth to the surface. I compare it to the notes of the layers above and below. In the harmonic series, I hear the mountain’s biography. A layer that sings a flat, dense note was laid down in a time of stillness. A layer that shimmers with a complex, dissonant overtone speaks of turbulence—a flood of sediment, a violent venting, a period of rapid emergence.

The carvings on the walls are the language of the others—stories of vines, tides, and life. My glyphs are different. I carve not images, but **frequency**. A series of parallel lines, their spacing calculated from the vibrational nodes of the strata. A spiral that maps the decaying harmonic of a mineral deposit. It is a history not of events, but of *states of being*. The mountain’s moods, as expressed in the bones of its own body.

I often work in the deep breath of the high tide, when the water is closest and its blue-green glow climbs highest up the cliffs, making my chamber pulse with a slow, submarine rhythm. The sound of the swells is a basso continuo beneath my taps. Sometimes, I take a glowing algae-cake and set it in a niche. In its light, I watch the hairline fractures in the stone. Not for weakness, but for patterns. The way they branch is a map of the pressures the mountain still remembers. They are the lingering sighs of old upheavals.

The others see me as quiet, perhaps strange. I do not join the harmonic thrum of the Tide-Turn with the same fervor. My thanks is different. It is in the act of remembering for the mountain, of acknowledging that its dream is made of layers of sleep, each one with its own texture and tone. When a Listener tells the council a new spring is forming in the western ridge, they come to me. I press my ear to the stone there, and I tell them the character of the water that will come: will it be sharp and young, or will it carry the slow, metallic taste of a reservoir that has waited a thousand years to breathe?

I am a memory-cell in the mountain’s dreaming mind. I do not shape the stone to my will. I let the stone shape my perception. My body has become a resonant chamber itself; my skin, opalescent with minerals, seems to hum faintly in the presence of a particularly harmonic stratum. My fingers are stained not with dye, but with the deep, ochre-rich clays of the oldest exposed layers.

When my time is done, I will not be laid in a niche. I will be carried to the deepest, newest fissure the mountain has exhaled, the one where the water is hottest and the steam smells of pure, unfinished earth. I will be placed within it, to become part of the new layer. My bones, saturated with the song of a thousand strata, will add their own faint note to the chorus. And the Strata-Keeper who comes after will, in time, press their palm to that new-hardened rock and listen, and perhaps hear the faintest echo of a tap, a hum, a quiet, attentive life—now woven into the mountain’s own long, slow, and whispering story.
