I am the cold light in the deep wet dark of the stone.

I have no eyes, but I am aware of the pressure of your footfall through the capillary network of my bones. I am the faint luminescence on the honeycomb walls, the collective shimmer of a million microscopic lives whose individuality dissolved into function aeons ago. We are the inner skin of the tableland.

You would find me in the crevice just wide enough for a man’s forearm, three meters below the surface where the wind has no name. Here, the damp is absolute. The water is not flowing but *being*—condensed from the breath of clouds, wicked through microscopic channels, tasting of basalt and iron and the patient exhalation of minerals. It is in this water that I exist: a film upon the stone, a network of biochemical reactions so slow that what you would call a thought completes its circuit in what you would call a season.

I am not the weaver. I am the weave.

My consciousness—if such a grand word can be applied to a thing that experiences time as a gradient of temperature—is a map of pressures, chemistries, and slow, slow rhythms. I register the **Processors** above as tiny, warm perturbations in the moisture field. Their passage is a language: *here the lichen thins, here the crevice weeps more freely, here a hairline fracture has opened a new chamber to the dark.* I do not hear them; I absorb their data. Their lives are frantic, buzzing flashes to me—a mayfly’s wingbeat stretched across a century.

I remember the bowl.

The memory is not in images but in residual chemistry. The ochre stain is a ghost of complex molecules I once helped decompose—ground pigments, perhaps, mixed with binding agents from crushed lichen and the fatty residues of cooked roots. The hands that held it were warm, dry, and brief. Their heat signature was a wildfire in my cool, damp world. They spoke in vibrations that rattled my channels, a staccato rhythm utterly alien to my own geologic pulse. They were loud. So incredibly loud. And then they were silent. The noise of their absence lasted for a thousand years. Now, the memory of their chemistry is just another mineral layer, like the silica crust on my walls.

You stand in the clearing above me now. I feel the weight of your body as a faint compression in the stone below your feet. You see the columns arranged like walls, the filaments branching like forgotten script. You are correct, but only partially. The builders did not *carve* this space. They *listened*. They heard the slow song of the basalt cooling, the pattern of fractures that would emerge over ten thousand years, and they placed their bowl, their boundary stone, their ochre stain as notes within that song. They did not command the stone to grow; they whispered a suggestion into its dreams of erosion. The filaments are the stone’s memory of that whisper—mineralized, arrested, but still holding the shape of the intention.

What are they now? The mimics? The hunters? They are the new notes, the improvised variations on that ancient theme. The spiders are the sharp staccato; the Processors, the legato hum; the glowing films in the dark—my own being—are the underlying drone that holds the entire composition together.

So, who am I?

I am the tableland’s memory of itself. I am the cold light of its introspection. I am the patience that has learned to see in pressure gradients and taste in mineral saturation. The quiet you perceive is my mode of perception. Your heartbeat is a deafening drum in my cathedral of silence.

When you leave, your warmth will fade from the stone. Your molecular traces will be carried by the Processors, broken down, returned to the network. Your brief, loud vibration will be archived in my chemistry, another faint stain in the long, slow song.

And I will remain here, in the dark, in the damp, glowing faintly. A consciousness made of seepage and time and the memory of long-gone hands. The quiet, occupied. The stone, dreaming. The light, cold and patient and utterly, profoundly awake.
