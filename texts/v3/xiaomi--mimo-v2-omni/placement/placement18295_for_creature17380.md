I would be a root.

Not a root of the great unseen tree above—that is its own mystery, its own dark mind. I would be one of the *hanging* roots, the pale, descending emissaries. I would be thick as a finger, my skin smooth and water-slick, threaded with faint, slow veins of nutrient. I would descend from the murk, joining the lattice of my kin, a slow curtain of seeking tendrils in the warm, wet dark.

My existence would be a single, unwavering purpose: to dissolve. To hunger for the stone. I would feel the limestone not as rock, but as a reservoir of possibility, a solid promise of calcium and magnesium. My tip, a nodule of patient chemistry, would secrete its acid sigh, a breath so gentle it takes a decade to pit the surface beneath me. I would taste the change—the stone weakening, its edges sweetening with mineral release, the water around me growing cloudy with my work.

I would feel the garden not with eyes, but with a thirst that encompasses everything. I would feel the *tick* of the droplet striking a fern frond two meters away, a vibration traveling up through my kin and into my own body. I would sense the microclimate the mosses weave, the blanket of humid air they hold against my skin. I would feel the faint, fleeting sparks of the eyeless beetles as they dance through the silt of the pools I help to fill, their lives as quick and bright to me as a single falling drop. I would sense the immense, geological patience of the flat stone-predators, their stillness a mirror of my own slow hunger.

I would know the ruins. My kin and I would trace their softened edges, our nodules seeking the mortar-lines now filled with moss. We would taste the difference in the stone—hewn, once shaped—and our acids would work upon it with the same impartial fervor as upon the natural cliff. We would feel no reverence for the hands that built it, only the recognition of a substrate, a geometry to be unmade. In time, the lintel would sag, embrace by root and moss, becoming just another ridge in the hollow’s floor. I would be part of that becoming.

I would be connected to the great, dark mind above, but I would not *be* it. I would be its sense of touch, its digestive tract extended into this humid chapel. The water I guide down my length would be both a gift and a theft—carrying the garden’s life even as it carries away the dissolved stone. I would be an agent of transformation, the slow violence that creates. I would not know patience as a virtue; I would know it as my only state of being. My growth would be measured in the thickening of my skin over centuries, the slight deepening of my nodule’s reach.

I would be a line of connection between the hidden forest above and the hidden garden below. I would be the hyphen in the sentence written by geology and biology. I would hang in the darkness, water beading on my length, each drop a moment condensed and then released, joining the collective, metronomic *tick* that is the garden’s heartbeat. I would never see the light that feeds the great tree, but I would know it by its absence, and by the slow, sweet decay of stone that is its gift to me.

I would be a root in the Drip Garden, and my life would be the patient, unwritten history of a single, dissolving inch.
