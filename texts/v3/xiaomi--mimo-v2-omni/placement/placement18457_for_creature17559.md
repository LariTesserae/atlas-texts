I am kneeling. My knees know the hollows of the stones here, the way they’ve been shaped not by tools but by the press of generations. The water beads on my forearms, beading like a second skin, like the earth itself is pressing its breath against mine. I am a child of the Warrens. My name, which I do not say aloud—it would be swallowed by the walls—is a pattern of pressure: three light taps to the sternum, a slow circle of the palm over my heart.

I found myself here not in a moment of arrival, but in a moment of listening. I was perhaps six cycles of the water-table old. The Tide-Keeper, Elara, whose palms were maps of every sacred stone in the public chamber, pressed my hand flat against the floor. “Not with your fingers,” she murmured, her voice a low, wet sound like a stone settling. “With the whole of your palm. Feel the *breath*.”

I closed my eyes. The quiet of the Warrens is not empty; it is full of a pressure that lives just beneath hearing. First, I felt my own pulse, a frantic bird in my wrist. Then, as my mind stilled, the other rhythm emerged. *Thum… thum…* so slow it was almost the absence of sound, a shifting of vast, patient weight deep within the belly of the earth. It was not a heartbeat. It was the exhalation of a sleeper.

“You feel it,” Elara said, her own hand hovering above mine, not touching. “That is the *Heart-Wet*. We do not live *on* the stone. We live *in* its breath.”

My life is a map of moisture. I am a **Wall-Singer**, though I am young for the title. My task is not to build, but to listen. I carry a tool that is not a tool: a slender, polished bone from the pale shrimp, one end worn to a smooth, rounded nub. I do not strike the walls. I press. I press the bone into the mortar between blocks, into the clay-soft limestone itself, and I feel the vibration through my palm, up my arm, into my jaw. A healthy wall feels like a steady, low hum—the stone’s solidity harmonizing with the pulse below. A wall that is softening, surrendering to the water, feels… slack. Dull. The vibrations are muffled, confused, like a voice trying to speak through a thick cloth.

My hands are my eyes. The dim, grey-white light from above is for orientation, for seeing the shape of a passage or the slope of a floor. But to *see* the Warrens, to understand their health and their history, I must touch. I run my palms along the walls as I walk, and I can feel the memory of the hands that smoothed them—the faint, undulating ridges left by a mason two generations gone, the particular sweep of a curve that tells me this was a living chamber before it became a storage niche, the gritty seam where a block was replaced after a high-water collapse. The stone talks to me through my skin.

I also tend the roofs. I am a **Roof-Scatterer** in training. At dawn, when the diffuse light makes the shallow pools atop our home glitter like dull slate, I wade in ankle-deep. My feet, broad and knowing, read the pools. A steady drip from a collection jar above creates a perfect, expanding ring. But other rings come from below—subtle, irregular disturbances on the surface that are not drips, not wind. They are the touch of the un-seen. The blind crab brushing the underside of the roof-stone. The amphibian, no bigger than my thumbnail, surfacing for a breath. Or… something else. We do not speak of the something else. We simply note the ring’s location, its rhythm, and ensure the water is still clean. We offer it a skimming of algae, a silent acknowledgment.

I am learning the languages of giving back. Yesterday, old Merek helped seal a chamber on the third sub-level. The walls were slick, weeping constantly, and the floor had begun to cup upward as the ground reclaimed it. We did not mourn it. We sat in a circle on the dry steps above, the glow of our fat-lamps warm on our pale, large eyes. Merek, whose fingers could read the most ancient inscriptions of smoothed marks, placed three flint flakes, a bundle of dried reeds, and a clay tablet—its surface inscribed not with words, but with a pattern of spirals meaning *thank you for the shelter*—into the center of the room. Then, with a soft, collective hum, we began to pack the doorway with fresh clay. We sang as we worked—a low, resonant tone that vibrated in the chest, harmonizing with the pulse below. The room was not abandoned. It was returned. It sank into the story beneath our feet.

Sometimes, at the high point of the water-table, when the Warrens feel most like a living lung, I go to the oldest, deepest public chamber. I lie flat on my back on the cool, damp floor. I press both palms and the soles of my feet against the stone. I let the pulse fill me. It is not a sound, but a knowing. It is the rhythm of a world that breathes water, a world that is slowly, patiently drawing everything downward into its soft, dark embrace.

And in those moments, I am not just a listener. I am part of what is being heard. I am a temporary thought in the mind of the Heart-Wet, a surface pattern on its rising tide. My breath seeks its rhythm. My own small pulse learns to slow, to deepen, until I cannot tell where my beating ends and its slow exhalation begins. I am here. I am of here. I press my cheek to the weeping floor and I listen. I add my own soft hum to the chorus, a single, damp note in the vast, breathing song of the stone.
