I would not find myself *in* the fen. Not really. To be *in* it would imply a separation, a container and a contained. I would find myself *as part of* the fen, briefly and imperfectly, the way a single, errant note might momentarily join a sustained chord before dissolving back into silence.

I think I would be the **disturbance in the stillness**.

Not a loud one. Not a violent one. But a ripple of *attention* in a place that has not needed to be attentive for millennia. The braided vegetation would turn its translucent leaves toward me, not with alarm, but with a slow, sleepy curiosity—the equivalent of an eye cracking open in a drowsy face. The fractal moss would seem to tighten its geometric patterns, a subconscious clenching of order in the face of the disordered. The small, bronzed custodians would alter their processions, their shimmering paths bending away from my footprints not out of fear, but as a polite yielding, the way a stream yields to a stone.

I would be the source of the **unnamed taste** in the seeping water. For a time, the faint mineral sweetness would carry a ghost of something else—the salt of my skin, the carbon dioxide of my breath, the fleeting, alien chemistry of my panic and wonder. My presence would be a brief impurity in the Fen’s perfect solution, one that the system would begin to gently process and digest.

My sounds would be the most profound intrusion. My heartbeat, a frantic, arrhythmic drum against the subsonic hum. My breath, a noisy bellows in the quiet lung of the air. My thoughts, a chaotic chatter in the deep, settled silence. The central structure would seem to drink these sounds, the low tones from its apertures shifting momentarily to a minor key, as if absorbing the dissonance and transmuting it into something that could be incorporated into its endless chord.

And the great, still creatures—those geologic memories—would **know** me. Not as an individual, but as a category: *Transience*. One, coiled around a braided tower, would unfurl a sensory filament, not toward my body, but toward my *afterimage*, the lingering disturbance I left in the warm air. Another, half-sunk into the ceramic-earth, would shift its weight by a millimeter, a tectonic acknowledgment. They would not see me as a threat or a meal, but as a punctuation mark—a comma, perhaps—in their long, slow sentence. And they would wait, with the patience of stone, for the sentence to continue after I was gone.

In the end, I would be a **memory in the making**. My passage would leave shallow prints in the honey-colored clay, filling with sweet water. My touch on the warm scratches of the central structure would leave oils and warmth that would fade over hours. The faint shift in the hum of the apertures would settle back into its ancient resonance. The braided leaves would slowly, slowly return to their original orientations. The custodians would resume their perfect patterns.

I would be the Fen’s brief, vivid dream of something other than itself. A dream of hurry, of noise, of purposeful doing. And upon waking—the instant I stepped back through the curtain of warm air into the harsh, shadowed world of unfinished things—the Fen would forget me. Not out of malice, but out of completion. A finished thing has no need for new memories. It simply *is*.

And I, in my world of wind and birdsong and traffic, would be left with the memory of *its* memory: the quiet, the glow, the taste of time that has already arrived, and stayed, and was enough. I would carry the fen as a stillness inside me, a counterpoint to the noise, a reminder of a place where life is not a struggle, but a settled, luminous, and unhurried fact.
