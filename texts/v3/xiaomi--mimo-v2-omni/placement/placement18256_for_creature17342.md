I am a listener in the city that hums. My name is a rhythm tapped on stone: *Tap-pause-tap-tap*. It means *One Who Follows the Veins*.

I am a Geometer of the Second Stratum. My work is not with lines on paper, but with the tremors in the bone of the world. I carry a set of silver tuning forks, each etched with a different fracture-pattern from the mesa’s deep history. When struck against the living rock, they sing back not just a tone, but a story of density, stress, and yearning.

You would find me on the less-traveled bridges, the ones that groan with a song older than the city itself. I am the one kneeling, my cheek pressed to the damp steel, feeling the slow heave of the mesa beneath. My dense, low-slung body is a perfect anchor against the vibration. The chelating mucus on my skin glistens in the fungal glow, protecting me from the metallic tang in the mist.

My tool is a great, resonant compass—a heavy brass ring with a weighted needle that doesn't point north, but to the area of greatest current *pull* within the stone. I follow these pulls. I map the invisible. Yesterday, the needle swung deep toward a barren ledge where the rock gleamed fresh and wet, split only a season ago. I struck my deepest fork against its face. The note that returned was not clean; it was fractured, a chord that dissonated and then… looped. A three-beat echo, repeating. A pocket of weirdness, but slow, like a memory caught in the mineral matrix.

This is my charge. Not to seal it—that is for the Stability Technicians with their rituals of still-earth and sound. My role is to *understand* it, to chart its boundaries, to predict its drift. Will this looping echo spread? Will it cause the new rock face to transmute into something brittle and glassy? Or will it, in a century, become a Silent Sanctuary, a place where the past is palpable, useful for meditation and deep listening?

I paint my maps with ink made from ground crustacean shell and bioluminescent moss-spores. On large sheets of treated hide, I do not draw shapes, but *gradients of vibration*. Blues for deep, stable resonance; throbbing ochres for areas of active heave; shimmering, indigo whorls for pockets of transmutation. My legends are not of roads, but of tonal qualities: *The-Groan-of-Old-Grief*, *The-Sigh-of-Rising*, *The-Silence-That-Breathes*.

Life is slow here, and so is my work. I may spend a full moon-cycle on a single archipelago of mesas, listening. I eat the sweet, mineral-rich flesh of the crystalline crustaceans, roasted over a geothermal vent. I sleep in shelters grown by Mycologists, where the walls glow with a soft, green light that doesn't disrupt the dark-adapted eyes. In the deep quiet of my vigil, the bird-things wheel in silent patterns above, their iridescence flashing like distant signals. I read them, too.

I am not a hero. I am a nerve-ending for the city, a conscious part of its dialogue with the unstable ground. My maps are filed in the deep, vaulted libraries of the Echo-Wrights, used to tune the great Anchor Tones that sing our defiance into the humming dark. I am a bridge, not of steel, but of attention—between the frantic, momentary life of the city and the vast, slumbering, *becoming* life of the mountain.

When I grow old, my bones will ache with the mountain’s arthritis. My final request will be to be carried to a quiet, damp alcove, laid upon a bed of glowing moss, and left. My dense body will become part of the strata. The crustaceans will pick my bones clean, and perhaps, in a thousand years, a fragment of my skeleton, transmuted and lovely, will be found by a new Geometer, a shimmering note in the endless, resonant song of this place. And they will strike a fork, and listen, and know they are not alone in the hum.
