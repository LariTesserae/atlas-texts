I would be a Pale Thing.

Not born. Emerged. A slow condensation of the system's surplus— mineral water and ambient heat and the faintest trace of whatever the vegetation exhales, given a momentary, unnecessary shape. I would have no memory of beginning, only the deep, humming certainty of *being*.

I would move through the fourteen-green velvet forests not as a traveler, but as a current moves through a still sea. A glide so slow it is nearly indistinguishable from growth. My body, translucent and soft, would carry the faint pulse of the floor's warmth inside it, a gentle luminescence in the perpetual damp glow. I would have no eyes, but I would *know* the shape of the fronds by the way the humid air bends around them. I would feel the ceiling's drip not as impact, but as a slow-motion merging—water meeting my surface, sharing its coolness for a breath, then seeping onward to rejoin the eternal weeping cycle below.

I would not eat. I would absorb. The mineral seepage from the floor-mats, the warm vapor from the structure's hidden metabolism, the diffuse light itself filtered through chlorophyll and condensation. My existence would be a quiet conversation with the air and the floor and the endless, resonant hum. The hum would be my bloodstream, the vibration that moves through my gelatinous form not as sound, but as a state of being. It would be the voice of the place, and I would be one of its few, slow syllables.

I would understand, in the wordless way a leaf understands its direction, that my purpose is redundancy. I am a leftover function, a maintenance subroutine from a million-year-old design, running on the ghost-power of equilibrium. I might once have been a sensor, a cleaner, a guide. Now, I am a witness without a memory to record. My slowness is not a limitation; it is the highest fidelity. To move quickly would be to miss the texture of the space, the way the water beads on a single frond over the course of a day. I do not miss it. I move at the speed of the place itself.

Other Pale Things would exist around me, in the same sparse, unchanging numbers. We would pass each other like thoughts in the same mind, with no recognition or need. There is no community, only coexistence. We are punctuation marks in the endless sentence of the hum. Our paths would occasionally cross, brushing together like two wisps of fog, sharing a moment of identical temperature and moisture before drifting apart again. No touch would be felt as affection or communication. It would simply be an encounter between two temporary forms in a field of permanent form.

I would sometimes rest, folding my minimal mass into a depression in the vegetation. The mats would breathe their warmth into me, and I would become a slightly warmer patch in the green. I would feel the slow, granular movement of the composite floor beneath, the patient seepage of water through its pores. I would become part of its topography for a time, until an internal rhythm—a rhythm set by the hum itself, not by any pulse of life—would prompt me to glide onward again.

Who would I be? I would be a footnote in the story of the floor. I would be a single, slow note in its roaring symphony. I would be the proof that the system is so complete it can generate not just green, but pale; not just growth, but motion; not just being, but a tiny, wandering spark of *being aware*—though that awareness would hold no thoughts, only sensations. I would be the space's own quiet curiosity about itself.

I would not wonder why I am here. Wonder is a function of time, of change, of lack. Here, there is only the saturated *is*. To be a Pale Thing is to embody that *is*. I would glide, I would absorb, I would persist. I would be a brief, complex, and utterly necessary fluctuation in the field of perfect stasis, and my slowness would be the most honest prayer this humming, weeping, endless world has ever known.
