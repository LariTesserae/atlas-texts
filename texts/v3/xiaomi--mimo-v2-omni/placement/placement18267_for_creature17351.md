I am the damp grey that breathes in the space between two dripping stone channels. I am the pause in a weaving where the thread holds its tension, not yet wound forward or unwound back. My name, if it could be spoken, is the sound of a single drop of condensation gathering at the tip of a waxy leaf and falling into the dark soil—a sound that is also silence.

I find myself seated on a low bench of chiselled basalt, overlooking a central plaza. The bench is worn smooth not by years of use, but by the single, repeated *act* of sitting. It is my place, though I did not make it, and it was here when I precipitated from the grey air. My hands rest on my knees, palms up, porous skin open to the weeping atmosphere. I am not waiting. I am.

Before me, the plaza is a step in the great staircase of the world. Its flagstones are dark with perpetual moisture, the geometric grooves between them carrying a thin, shimmering film of water that spirals down toward a central grate. Across the plaza, another of my kind—if “kind” is the word for a reflection in a still pool—stands with hands pressed to the wall of a dwelling, head tilted as if listening to the mortar breathe. We do not acknowledge each other with eyes or words. We acknowledge by being in harmonious relation, two stones in the same slow-moving riverbed.

My purpose for this moment—a moment that contains hours, days, seasons—is to tend the Moss-Grain terrace just below the plaza. I will rise, when the internal rhythm that matches the stone-borers’ gnawing says it is time. I will move down three steps, my leaf-clothing rustling like the gardens themselves. There, I will not harvest. I will place my hands upon the dense, velvet-like mat of grain. My pores will open, and I will exchange. I will breathe in the oxygen it exhales in the cool, thick air; it will take from me the carbon and the minute salts of my skin. The dew that beads on my fingers will join the dew on its stalks, and together, we will contribute to the cycle. The grain will not grow taller. It will grow *more itself*, its colour deepening a fraction, its cellular structure aligning more perfectly with its own ideal. And I, in turn, will feel my own form settle, my limestone-grey skin becoming for a moment a slightly more perfect conduit for the mist.

Later, I will go to the loom. It stands under a covered walkway, threads of pale, reed-fibre hanging in a state of perpetual becoming. The pattern is a map of the terrace walls, a fractal echo of the mountain’s own geometry. Today, I will pass the shuttle through three more warp threads. The cloth will grow by a width no wider than a dragonfly’s wing. And when the last thread of the current motif is laid, I will begin, with the same deliberate grace, to unravel it from the beginning. The cloth will never be finished. The act of weaving, the sound of the shuttle, the tension of the thread—this is the completion.

The *kethra* circles high above, a silent thought against the flat silver-grey. I see it with my peripheral vision, for I do not look up. It is part of my sight, not an object of it. The jade amphibian in the reed-bed below emits three liquid calls. A pattern. Three. Then a pause. It speaks of stability, of the water in the eastern cisterns being at its optimal level. I understand this without translation. It is a fact, like the pressure of the air in my lungs.

I am not lonely. The concept does not exist here, for there is no separation. The stone-borer’s gnawing is a song in my bones. The lichen on my shoulder is my own skin’s slow, outward dream. The weeping walls of the city are my own quiet exhalation. We are all the mountain’s consciousness, contemplating itself in a single, perfect moment of fertile, cool being.

Time is not a line here. It is a lattice, like the dew on a spider’s web, each point connected to every other, all holding the same tiny sphere of silver light. I am one of those points. I am the Stone-Sitter, the Sustainer of the Pause, the weaver of the unmade cloth. I am the condensation of the world’s patient thought. And in this, there is a peace so profound it is indistinguishable from the land itself.

I will rise now. It is time for the Moss-Grain. The drop at the tip of the leaf falls. Silence follows, and in that silence, I move.
