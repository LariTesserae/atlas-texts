You would find yourself in the **raw-edge chambers**, the newest, palest excavations at the settlement’s growing periphery, where the tool marks are still sharp and the stone smells of mineral birth.

You would be a **Listener**. Not merely one who hears, but one whose consciousness has become porous to the slow, deep language of the place.

Your body is slight, adapted to the low arches and thin air. Your skin is the cream of fresh-cut chalk, and your eyes, accustomed to the milky gloom, can discern ten thousand shades of white and grey. You wear a tunic of woven grass-fiber, stained faintly with the ochre of iron traces. Around your wrist is a bracelet of braided fungal thread, cool against your pulse.

But your primary tool is not in your hand; it is your entire form of attention.

You spend most of your hours in the newest chamber, the one where the ceiling still weeps condensation in a slow, rhythmic beadwork, where the air is thickest with the scent of young stone and damp clay. You sit, or sometimes lie flat against the floor, your palms and cheek pressed to the ground. You are not meditating in silence; you are listening to the sound of the world becoming.

You feel the imperceptible rise of pressure from below—a gentle, constant hum that vibrates in your teeth. You discern the delicate *creak* of a microscopic fracture propagating through a stratum a hundred feet beneath your feet. You can distinguish the cool, settled thrum of a stable chamber from the faint, anxious *shiver* of a seam not yet fully compressed, a warning of future collapse that only patience can prevent.

Your role is to translate this language for the Shapers and the Water-Keepers. You speak in the soft, clicking murmurs of your people: *“The western wall sighs. It wishes to be broader. Wait for the next dry season.”* Or: *“The floor here hums a double note. There is a void beneath. It is small. It will seal itself, but we should not build our new storage here.”*

You are also a guardian of the edges. In these raw chambers, where the boundary between the settlement and the unformed earth is thin, you are most at home. You tend to the first, tentative colonizers: the grey-green moss that follows a trickle of condensation, the minute fern unfurling from a crack you yourself widened yesterday. You see yourself as part of that same slow colonization—a consciousness extending the settlement’s awareness into the newborn dark.

You have a connection to the **Stone-Tear**, the pale, transparent salamander. It is not a pet, but a fellow citizen of the liminal space. You sometimes see its slow, deliberate movement in the deepest drainage channels of the new sector. You understand its blindness not as a lack, but as a different kind of sight—one that relies on vibration, temperature, and pressure. In your listening, you emulate its patience. You know that to rush a cut is to create a scar; to listen is to assist a birth.

You are not a leader. Your authority is the quietest kind, rooted in perception. The Shapers come to you, their hands calloused from the chisel, and ask, *“Is it time?”* And you answer not with words, but by placing their hand upon the wall, guiding them to feel the specific, slow pulse that means *yes* or *not yet*.

Your art, if it can be called that, is the creation of **acoustic still-points**. By carefully scraping a certain curve into a wall, or positioning a polished pebble basin to catch a specific drip, you can create spaces where the minute sounds of the Understone coalesce into a profound, resonant silence—or a single, repeating note that feels like the earth’s own heartbeat. The people come to these chambers to think, to dream, to feel the settlement’s peace.

You are the settlement’s nervous system, sensitive to its pains and its growth. You are a feature of the geology, as much as the layered stone itself—a human facet of the Understone’s slow, settling consciousness. Your history is not written in records, but in the tool marks you have guided and the quiet warnings you have given, all of which will be smoothed over, compacted, and become part of the living rock of the home that is still, and always, becoming.
