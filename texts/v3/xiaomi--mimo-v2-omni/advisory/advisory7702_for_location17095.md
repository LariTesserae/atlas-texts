### II. Physical & Gear Preparation

**A. Clothing & On-Person Gear (The Second Skin):**
*   **Primary Layer: Full-Body Vapor Barrier Suit.** Not a standard wetsuit (too insulating), nor a drysuit (too bulky). Think a high-tech, breathable-yet-impervious membrane suit, akin to advanced chemical protection gear. Its purpose is to keep the constant, cold, biological moisture *off your skin*. The air itself will condense on it, so it must have excellent moisture-wicking properties on the *inside*.
*   **Insulation:** Beneath the barrier, wear a thin, synthetic (not wool or cotton) insulating layer. The chill is pervasive and conductive; it will leach heat from you relentlessly.
*   **Footwear: The Most Critical Item.** You need "Symbiotic Traction Boots."
    *   **Soles:** Wide, flat, and made of a slightly soft, grippy compound. No hard treads—they'll slip on the wet, fibrous surfaces. Think the pattern of a gecko's foot or a water strider's leg, designed to distribute weight and create micro-suction.
    *   **Construction:** Fully sealed, with built-in gaiters that integrate with the suit. They will get wet *inside* from condensation, so they must be drainable and quick-drying.
    *   **Purpose:** To prevent you from breaking through the spongy mat (a dangerous event) and to provide stable purchase on slopes that are actively, if slowly, moving.
*   **Head & Face:** A full hood integrated with the suit. A clear, wide-vision face shield (not goggles) to protect from the constant dripping and spore-laden air, with an anti-fog coating that works in high humidity. A compact, multi-filter respirator is non-negotiable. Filters must handle: 1) Biological particulates (spores, micro-insects), 2) Volatile Organic Compounds (the "metal" and "memory of salt" tastes), and 3) Potentially, unknown pheromonal or mycotoxin agents.
*   **Hands:** Gloves with the same vapor-barrier principle as the suit, but with enhanced tactile sensitivity at the fingertips for manipulating gear. They will be constantly wet.

**B. Expedition Gear:**
*   **Mobility & Stability:**
    *   **Poles, Not Axes:** Two lightweight, extendable poles with wide, basket-like tips (like snowshoe poles). They are for testing ground firmness, probing depth of water-depressions, and maintaining balance on shifting slopes. *Never* use them to strike or damage the vegetation.
    *   **Harness & Tether:** For potential need to anchor yourself during significant landscape movements or to secure gear. The anchor would be a broad, screw-in type device designed for soft substrates.
*   **Life Support & Sampling:**
    *   **Hydration:** All water must be carried. The exuding liquid is part of the ecosystem's bloodstream; consuming it would be akin to drinking blood—a profound biological interaction with unknown consequences (assimilation, poisoning, or triggering an immune response from the landscape itself).
    *   **Nutrition:** High-calorie, low-residue food. Digestion may be complicated by the environment and potential ingestion of spores.
    *   **Environmental Sealed Kit:** For any samples or observations. Absolute containment is key. Nothing from here should be allowed to cross over.
*   **Navigation & Observation:**
    *   **Inertial Navigation System (INS):** GPS will likely fail or be unreliable. A high-quality INS, supplemented by celestial navigation (if the dim sky allows) and careful dead reckoning, is essential.
    *   **Chronometer:** To track the slow rhythms. Note the time between landscape heaves in your sector; it may provide a crude predictive model.
    *   **Multi-Spectrum Sensors:** Thermal imagers (to see the heat signatures of the "mossy lumps" and the cold sinks of new depressions), low-light cameras, and moisture sensors. The data will be confusing but invaluable for understanding the immediate "metabolism" around you.
    *   **Passive Acoustic Sensors:** To listen to and record the sub-bass hum. Changes in its frequency or pattern could signal large-scale shifts.

### III. Safety & Procedural Protocols

1.  **The First Step:** Do not step off your landing zone immediately. Spend hours, even a full day, observing. Use your sensors to listen and watch. Identify the baseline rhythm of the local sector.
2.  **The Walking Test:** Use your poles to gently probe the mat ahead. Look for "veins" of faster water flow, which may indicate weaker ground or a pending shift. Avoid the weeping rocks; they are sinking digestive organs.
3.  **Avoid the "Organs":** The temporary water-depressions are not ponds. They are filling/draining pores. Do not approach their edges. They can drain suddenly, creating a suction mire, or fill just as fast.
4.  **Fauna Interaction:** Observe, do not touch. The grazing lumps are benign, but the frantic insectiles may be defensive or parasitic. The feathered serpents are symbiotic architecture; disturbing them could provoke a response from the plant they are part of.
5.  **The Golden Rule: Do Not Impede the Industry.** Never dam a water trickle. Never block a shifting path. Never anchor into a major root-spine or fungal plate in a way that resists its movement. You must flow *with* the churn, not against it.
6.  **Psychological Monitoring:** The perpetual twilight, the disorienting sensory input, the feeling of standing on a living, breathing, moving entity, and the profound solitude will take a toll. Have scheduled check-ins, meditation practices, and a clear, achievable mission objective (e.g., "document one full cycle of local sector movement") to maintain focus.

Leave no trace of your visit. Your exit point may have moved. Be prepared for the landscape to have "digested" your landing zone. Your final act is one of gratitude and withdrawal, a parasite disengaging from its host, leaving the vast, wet dream to continue its slow, eternal churn.
