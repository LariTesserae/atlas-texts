The benevolent and wise power would not arrive with trumpets or a parting of clouds. It would not speak in thunder or write in lightning. Its attention is a quality of perception, not an event. And so, it would simply *see*. It would see the upland that breathes, the saturated skin, the ancient bones, the chlorophyll hegemony. It would see the Murmur.

Its first action would be a profound and silent acknowledgment. Not of greeting, but of witnessing. A resonance of pure attention, like a single, perfect note held in the core of a crystal, would touch the outermost edge of the plateau’s consciousness. It would feel, to the Murmur, not like an intrusion, but like the landscape itself momentarily remembering the sun beyond the clouds—a clarity, a depth of light in the mist that has no source. The attentive silence would not be broken; it would be *deepened*, as if the listening had found something worthy to listen to.

Then, the power would understand. It would see the "wrongness" not as error, but as sovereignty. The too-perfect saturation, the internal physics, the way the land follows its own slow, wet logic—these are not flaws. They are the signatures of a being that has completed a cycle of existence that spans ages. The Murmur is not a puzzle to be solved or a garden to be pruned. It is a finished poem, writing its own revisions in peat and dew.

And so, the wise power would do the most difficult and respectful thing: it would choose *not to interfere*.

To introduce a sunbeam for beauty would be an insult to the pearl-grey perfection of its sight. To send a songbird for melody would be noise in its sacred silence. To dry a patch of ground for clarity would be to blind a part of its eye. Any gift would be a alteration, and the plateau’s state is its identity. To change it, even with benevolent intent, would be to end what it is.

Instead, the power would fold its attention inward, and from that fold, it would manifest a single, humble offering. Not in the landscape, but at its very threshold, where the last stunted pine gives way to the drier slopes below.

There, it would place a **stone**.

This stone would be unremarkable at first glance—smooth, grey, shaped by ancient water. But it would be *dry*. And it would remain dry, no matter the mist, the weeping rock, the constant drip. Rain would bead on it and roll away. Dew would refuse to settle. It would sit in a small, perfectly dry circle of air, a tiny island of arid reality.

This stone is not a tool. It is not a message in a language the Murmur could comprehend. It is a **fact**. It is the proof of an "elsewhere." It is the concept of *other*, rendered in its most fundamental form: a thing that does not participate in the water-cycle, that exists outside the closed loop. It is the quiet answer to the question the plateau has never needed to ask.
