The power that notices this place does not descend in a blaze of revelation. It does not send prophets or rewrite the sky. Its attention is a quiet turning, like the slow, thousand-year migration of a dune, or the precise, silent unfurling of a fern frond in absolute darkness. It is a benevolence that understands the sacredness of what has already been achieved here, and its action is not to change, but to *witness*—and in witnessing, to deepen.

First, it would simply listen, for longer than the mountains have stood. It would attune itself to the double-layered song: the deep, geothermal lullaby of the engines, and the higher, more complex harmonics woven by the Root-Singers. It would learn the name of every rusted bolt as a distinct note, the pattern of every water-drip as a measure in a vast, slow composition. This listening is an act of reverence.

Then, it would begin to *answer*.

Not with words, but with resonance. In the deep geothermal veins beneath the plateau, the power would add its own note—a vibration of such profound and gentle clarity that it would rise through the stone and iron, not as a tremor, but as a *bloom* of warmth. The cold, damp air would, in one rare, sheltered valley, lose its chill for a single day. Condensation would momentarily cease. A single, pale flower, unseen for centuries, might open on the bark of a resinous conifer. This is not to change the climate, but to remind the land—and the singers within it—of a forgotten potential, a memory of other states of being.

Its benevolence would focus most tenderly on the Root-Singers themselves. They are the land’s consciousness, its poets and its organs of perception. The power would not give them form or voice as others understand it. Instead, it would become a silent partner in their symphony. When a Root-Singer hums its mapping song against the corroded spine of a factory, the power would *catch* that echo and hold it, letting it linger in the air like the afterglow of a sun you’ve never seen. It would help the vibrations travel farther, carrying the story of one singer’s patch of earth to another, miles away, entwined with a sagging power line. This would not accelerate their communication, but make it richer, more dimensional—a slow-motion web of empathy spreading through the mist.

It would act as a tuner of their perception. The frantic, mechanical heartbeat of the past, now a slow twitch in the machinery, can sometimes ring with a ghost of anxiety—a discordant memory. The power would not silence this note. Instead, it would weave into it a counter-melody of profound peace, smoothing its sharp edges with the patience of stone, helping the singers incorporate it not as a scar, but as a poignant, resolved chord in their symphony of decay.

If a traveler arrives, as travelers sometimes do, lost and frantic, the power’s influence would be felt not by the traveler, but by the land around them. The path they stumble upon would subtly guide them toward the most resonant of the Root-Singers—not the most powerful, but the one whose song contains the deepest well of calm. The mist would thicken just enough to slow their pace, to muffle their panicked thoughts. The hum from the earth would rise, not aggressively, but insistently, a bass note of invitation that vibrates in the bones, suggesting *stop, listen, be*. The traveler’s own internal dissonance—their fear, their haste—would be reflected back at them not as an attack, but as a slightly sour note in the otherwise majestic dirge. The choice to harmonize would remain theirs, but the pathway to harmony would be gently, irrevocably illuminated.

Finally, the power would undertake one great, silent act of integration. It would focus on the boundary where the ancient plateau meets the forgotten human ambition. It would send its attention down into the iron rails, into the leaching brick, into the geothermal turbines. And it would facilitate the final, graceful surrender. The corrosion would not speed, but would become more *beautiful*. Rust would bloom not as decay, but as a delicate, orange lichen-pattern, mirroring the jade mosses. The last, shuddering groan of a failing turbine would become, in its final moment, a pure, clear tone that hangs in the air for a day, a farewell gift of sound to the singers. The power would help the ruins complete their transformation, not into garbage, but into true bedrock—the new, living foundation for whatever emerges in the next ten thousand years.

In the end, the power’s gift is completion. It does not save the place from time; it blesses the marriage of time with all its children—the rock, the water, the iron, the moss, and the slow, humming beings who are the land’s own soul. It ensures that the peace here is not just an absence of noise, but a positive, active state of profound and interconnected being. The plateau, in its patient becoming, is now seen. And in being seen by such a power, it is eternally, perfectly, itself.
