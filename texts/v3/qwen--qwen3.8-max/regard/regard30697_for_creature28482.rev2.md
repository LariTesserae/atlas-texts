A benevolent and wise power would not begin by giving water.

That would be the first and most important act of wisdom: refusal of the obvious kindness. A lesser goodness, seeing so much dryness, might think at once of rain, of springs, of dew, of rivers slipping between the tiles. It might pity the hollow for what it lacked. It might try to fill the basin, soften the dust, make the air cool and wet, and call that mercy.

But a wise and benevolent power would see that the dryness is not a wound here. It is the law around which the whole place has learned to be alive. To pour water into that sunken room would be to unmake it. It would dissolve the salt-pegs, blur the powdered brick, swell the cords, crack the clay, confuse the beetles, startle the dust-fish, and turn the quiet abundance of the hollow into something ordinary, muddy, dependent, and less itself. The power would understand that some places are not saved by being given what they lack, but by being protected in their own nature.

So it would not bring rain.

It would do something harder than giving. It would pay attention.

It would look first at the whole hollow as one being, because the being who lives there is not a single creature. It is the beetle carrying grit. It is the ant bearing resin. It is the seam-spider stitching a wall back into itself. It is the moth with a maker’s mark on its wing. It is the dust-fish leaning against the light. It is the fat-leaved plants hoarding warmth. It is the inlaid ones pressing powder into cracks. It is the dry fountain gathering shed skins and copper filings. It is the silence that is not empty but crowded. The wise power would see all these small lives as one body, one made world, one long patience.

And it would not announce itself with thunder.

It would not tear the lattice aside. It would not flood the hollow with brilliance. It would not speak in a voice that rattled the vessels or flattened the moths. It would know that a place built from small hands, small bodies, small repairs, and enormous quiet cannot bear an overwhelming entrance without being injured. So it would lower itself. It would make its attention narrow. It would come first as a slight change in the light: not brighter, only more patient. Perhaps the lattice would cast one extra pattern on the floor. Perhaps a corner would seem less empty. Perhaps the air would settle with a warmth that felt, for a moment, like being recognized.

The inhabitants would notice.

The beetles would pause, legs lifted a fraction above the tiles. The moths would open and close their wings more slowly. The lizards would turn their painted-clay heads. The ants would tighten their chain around a resin bead. The seam-spiders would feel the change in a cord and wait. The dust-fish would rise a little higher in their dry medium, fringes stirring motes of gold. The inlaid ones would stop, if they were moving, and become still in the particular way that means they are listening with their hands.

The power would not command them to worship. It would not demand names. It would not ask for thanks. It would ask, in the only language the hollow respects, for permission.

It might ask by doing almost nothing.

It might place a mote of warmth on a tile where a beetle often rests. It might steady a loose thread in a hanging bundle of stems. It might cause a seedcase to wait one moment longer before splitting, so that the husk-mice below are ready. It might simply let the silence become more kind, less pressurized, as if the lid over the hollow had been lifted by the width of a breath but not removed.

If the hollow accepted this, the power would enter more fully.

It would not enter as a giant. It would enter small.

Perhaps it would make for itself a body no larger than a child’s forearm: matte, warm, dry, with skin like smoothed brick dust or woven fiber. Perhaps its face would be an uncarved tile. Perhaps its eyes would be two beads of dark resin. Perhaps it would look exactly like one of the inlaid ones, except that its hands would seem even more patient. Or perhaps it would come as a moth with a blank mark on its wing, a mark not yet filled in. Or as a dust-fish moving with a slightly silver wake. Or as a warmth near the dry fountain that no creature could see but none would mistake.

It would sit, or perch, or stand, or rest, according to its chosen smallness, and it would wait.

The residents would come closer by degrees. The beetles would resume their work. The ants would carry their resin past its knees. A seam-spider might draw one filament near it, testing whether the presence was a gap to be stitched or a solid thing to be worked around. An inlaid one might approach and hold out a short cord, not as a gift but as a question: *Are you here to repair, or here to undo?*

The power would answer by taking the cord, if offered, and placing it where it belonged. Or by not taking it, if that was the better answer. It would submit to the hollow’s rules. It would move slowly. It would make no sudden distance. It would not desire the center too much. It would let the corners remain negotiable. It would let the entrance appear where it wished. It would accept that here, desire makes distance stubborn, and therefore it would desire gently.

Then it would ask the real question.

Not: *What do you lack?*

That question belongs to the outside world, where lack is corrected by addition.

It would ask instead: *How shall I help you remain what you are?*

The hollow would not answer in words. Its answer would come as a series of small events. A tile would tick with warmth. A leaf would rasp against another leaf. A resin bead would swell. A dust-fish would circle the dry fountain. One of the inlaid ones would press powder into a crack and then look up, or seem to look up. A seedcase would open with a sound like a fingernail tapping clay. The moths would settle into a pattern that repeated the same carved mark three times. A beetle would carry a grain of brick from one terrace to another, even though no one had commanded it.

The answer would mean something like:

*Do not finish us.  
Do not flood us.  
Do not make us natural.  
Do not make us larger.  
Do not make us loud.  
Do not take away our smallness.  
Do not turn our dryness into grief.  
Let us continue.  
Let us mend.  
Let us grow into what we are still becoming.*

And the wise power would understand.

It would begin its blessing not by adding, but by keeping.

It would set a mercy around the hollow.

Not a wall of force. Not a flaming gate. Nothing that would make the place feel defended in a violent way. It would make the protection subtle, folded into the hollow’s existing strangeness. The entrance would remain small to those who came with carelessness. It would become difficult to find for anyone who intended to take without understanding, to break without listening, to flood without reverence. Such people would pass near and not notice the way down. They would see only a ruined floor, a dip in the ground, a tangle of old debris. If they did find an opening, distance would grow stubborn around them. Paths would fold. Corners would recede. The same terrace would repeat until the intruder became bored, unsettled, or humbled enough to leave.

But to those who came gently, the way would open.

Not to everyone. The hollow is not meant to be a spectacle. It is a room that has learned to behave like a world, and too many feet would bruise its quiet. So the power would let it be found rarely, and only by those capable of entering without demanding. A tired person might stumble upon it after learning, without knowing they had learned, to walk more slowly. A grieving person might find the entrance after they have stopped asking the world to be louder than their sorrow. A child with dust on their face and no fear of small, dry things might slip through where a scholar would have failed. The power would not choose the worthy in a grand sense. It would choose the compatible.

It would bless the hollow’s boundaries.

It would make sure no flood reached it. If rain gathered above, the lattice and stretched skins would shed the water outward, away from the sunken floor, so that not a single accidental drop violated the law of the place. If the surrounding world grew cold, the hollow would keep its warmth. If heat came from outside, the warm still air inside would not turn cruel. The power would not make the temperature unnatural; it would only prevent violence. It would preserve the hollow’s slow inhale and exhale.

It would bless the materials.

It would let powdered brick be enough. It would let ash be useful. It would let ground shell, salt, wax, fiber, copper, and clay continue to serve their purposes without failing too quickly. It would not create endless piles of new matter, because that would be excess, and excess would disturb the intimacy of the place. Instead, it would make nothing needed be entirely absent. When an inlaid one reached for a peg of dark wood, a peg would be near. When a seam-spider needed a firm edge to anchor a thread, that edge would remain. When a plant required mineral dust, a beetle would carry it there, or the great slow terraced creature would leave a heap of powder in the right place at the right time. The blessing would feel like luck, but it would not be random.

It would bless the plants.

It would not make them wetter. It would make their dryness more generous. The fat leaves would keep their wax. The resin beads would swell at intervals that did not exhaust the stems. The seed capsules would open with their small dry reports, and the seeds would find the creatures meant to carry them. The mineral-threaded cushions would draw nourishment from copper and clay without corroding the pins that held them. The broad fan-leaves would give off their scent when brushed, and the scent would calm rather than overwhelm. Where one plant grew over another, the power would keep the overgrowth from becoming burial. It would allow crowding, because crowding is part of the hollow’s lushness, but it would prevent cruelty.

It would bless the animals.

The beetles would not be crushed by careless feet. The moths would not be scattered by sudden air. The ants would not lose their resin. The lizards would find enough small prey, and their hunting would remain courteous, quick, and quiet. The fur-covered and feather-covered things in the upper tangle would find hollow stems for nesting. The dust-fish would have enough particulate light to lean against, enough stillness to swim through, enough absence to sustain their strange bodies. The great slow terrace-creature would not be mistaken for a wall and broken, nor would it break anything by moving. The power would give all these lives enough time.

It would bless the inlaid ones most particularly, because they are the hollow’s hands.

It would not make them immortal in a way that removed them from change. It would not make them into rulers. It would not give them command over the other creatures. It would give them endurance. Their fingers would not tire too soon. Their attention would not fray. They would remember, in the way bodies remember, how to press dust into cracks, how to tie cords, how to smooth wax, how to listen to a creaking strand and know whether it will hold. When one of them appeared from an act of repair, the power would let that appearance be stable enough to matter. When one of them faded back into a knot of fiber or a seedcase or a wall, it would not be a death. It would be a pause.

The power would also bless the repairs.

It would make sure that mending was not endless drudgery. In the hollow, repair is already a form of life, not a punishment, but even a joyful work can become heavy if it has no witness. So the power would witness it. It would notice the smallest act. The single grain placed in a crack. The thread drawn across a gap. The leaf lifted from a basin. The resin bead carried by ants like a lantern. The copper filing laid near the dry fountain. None of these would be lost. The blessing would be that no small care in the hollow was ever meaningless.

Then the power would come to the dry fountain.

This is where its action would be most delicate.

It would stand beside the stacked bowls, broken columns, lashed poles, and pressed clay. It would see the seeds, shed skins, copper filings, folded leaves, and fine warm dust moving in slow spirals. It would understand that this feature is not an altar in the sense of demanding sacrifice. It is a gathering place. A memory-organ. A point where the hollow admits to itself that its many small acts belong together.

The power would not fill it with water.

It would not even fill it with light in any obvious way. It would not make the dust rise in dramatic columns. It would not cause the shed skins to glow. It would not transform the fountain into a miracle that forced belief. That would be too loud. It would disturb the courtyard-sized intimacy of the place. It would make the residents servants of spectacle instead of participants in quiet.

Instead, it would leave one small thing there.

Perhaps it would place a single bead in the basin.

The bead would be blue, or blue-green, or the color of distance seen through horn. It would be no larger than a millet grain. It would be cool to the touch, but dry. It would not melt. It would not weep. It would not become wet. It would not smell of rain, except as a dream remembers rain. It would be made of the thought of water without being water itself: a mercy for visitors, not a need for the residents.

It would have no name, but if a name grew around it, the name might be something like *the Unfallen Drop*.

The inhabitants would not depend on it. The beetles would walk past. The ants would ignore it. The dust-fish would circle over it without changing course. The inlaid ones might look at it for a long time and then return to their work, recognizing that it belongs but does not need tending. It would be there for the rare guest who entered the hollow with ordinary thirst still burning in their body.

Because a wise power would not be cruel.

It would not allow the hollow to become a trap for those unable to live by its law. If a traveler entered desperate for water, the power would not force them to admire absence while their throat cracked. It would not make the hollow a test. It would make the way out gentle. The entrance would appear when the traveler needed it. The paths would not become stubborn in the wrong direction. If the traveler could not leave quickly, the bead in the fountain might give them a moment of peace: not water, not sustenance, but enough calm to think, to choose, to stop panicking. And if it was possible without violating the hollow, the power would guide them toward whatever relief the outside world held.

For those who could receive the hollow’s law, the bead would be a teaching.

It would say: *You do not need to become something else to be full. You do not need to be wet to be alive. You do not need to have what another world has in order to be complete.*

But the power would do more than leave a token.

It would give the hollow a kind of future.

Not a future of expansion. Not a future of conquest. Not a future in which it becomes famous, mapped, studied, drained, preserved under glass, or ruined by admiration. It would give it a future of continuation. The hollow would remain young in the way it was already young: unfinished, crisp-jointed, still becoming itself. The power would not complete it. Completion would be a kind of death here, because the place lives by being tended, repaired, overgrown, and re-understood. Instead, the power would bless its incompleteness.

It would make the hollow difficult to destroy.

Not invulnerable. Nothing should be invulnerable; that would make it lonely. But difficult to destroy. Fire would be slow to catch in its wax-and-resin world unless fed with intention. A careless foot would find its steps strangely guided away from the most fragile tiles. A hand raised to tear down a wall of lashed reeds would be met by a sudden thickness of stems, a confusion of paths, or a silence so crowded that the hand would hesitate. If someone came with true malice, the hollow would not attack them. It would simply become unreadable. It would give them several centers and no exit they desired. It would tire them with repetition. It would make them feel the weight of small things until they left, or until they became still enough to be changed.

And if they could be changed, the power might let them stay.

Some beings are not healed by abundance. Some are healed by restraint. Some people are too full of noise, appetite, hurry, or longing for more. The hollow could teach them. Not by preaching. Not by withholding water from their bodies, but by showing them another kind of satisfaction. The power might bring such a person to the dry fountain and let them sit there until their craving became less sharp. It might let them watch an inlaid one press dust into a crack for hours, or days, or what felt like days. It might let them see a beetle carry one grain, then another, then another, without anxiety. It might let them hear a seedcase open and realize that even small events have enough sound. It might let them rest in shade that did not cool them but softened them.

When they left, they might not remember the hollow clearly. They might remember only a room made of broken things that felt strangely whole. They might remember the smell of wax and heated clay. They might remember wanting something less urgently. That would be the power’s quiet work.

It would also protect the hollow from being misunderstood as barren.

The place is lush, but it is a strange lushness. To eyes accustomed to green wetness, it might look severe. To those who measure life by rivers, it might seem impoverished. The wise power would not advertise it, but it would let the truth be available to those who looked closely. It would make the moss-like mineral cushions beautiful. It would make the resin beads glow faintly in certain lights. It would let the dust-fish be seen, or almost seen, at the moment when a visitor’s perception had become gentle enough to bear them. It would allow the dry abundance to reveal itself not as deprivation, but as a different covenant with existence.

It might send dreams.

Not many. Not often. But sometimes, when someone far away was suffering from a thirst that water could not answer, the power might give them a dream of the hollow. In the dream, there would be no pool, no fountain spray, no cold stream. There would only be a warm sunken room, tiled with fragments, crowded with fat leaves and quiet creatures. The dreamer might kneel by the dry fountain and find, instead of despair, a strange comfort. They might wake not with water in their mouth, but with less panic in their chest. They might wake able to continue. The power would know that not all thirst is physical, and not all physical thirst can be answered by the same means. It would not confuse the two. It would give water to bodies where water was needed, and it would give the hollow’s dry peace to spirits that could be nourished by it.

It would be careful with longing.

The hollow already has this law: if you seek a center, the place will give you several; if you look for the builders, you will find only their marks; desire makes distance stubborn. A wise power would not abolish that law, because it teaches something true. But it would add a mercy to it. It would make sure that longing inside the hollow did not become torment. If one of the small creatures sought a particular seed, a particular nesting stem, a particular patch of warmth, the power would not necessarily bring it closer, but it would prevent the seeking from becoming hopeless. There would always be another ledge, another vine, another cracked tile where the needed thing might be found. Not always immediately. Not always in the form expected. But enough. Enough for patience. Enough for continuation.

It would bless sound.

Sound in the hollow is rare and therefore expensive. The power would not fill the place with music. It would not make the bird-like creatures sing more often. It would not let echoes multiply. But it might make certain sounds carry a little more meaning when they occurred. The click of a beetle’s claw would reassure nearby lives. The creak of a cord would be understood before it became danger. The low breathy note of a plume-vole would settle the upper tangle. The dry report of a seedcase would be loud, yes, but not frightening; it would be a small announcement: *something has opened; something is available; something continues.* The power would make sure that no sound in the hollow was wasted, and none became violence.

It would bless the inlaid ones’ silence.

Their communication is made of taps, resin-scents, pauses, and decisions. The power would deepen this without making it complicated. It would allow them to understand one another across the room by the angle of a hand. It would let a pause mean agreement. It would let the passing of a wooden peg be a conversation. It would let their blank clay faces remain blank when blankness was needed, and it would not impose expressions on them for the comfort of outsiders. They would not have to become more human to be meaningful. The power would honor their form of life exactly as it was.

It would perhaps leave one of itself among them.

This is the part that is hardest to say, because a wise power does not need to be seen in order to be present. But if it wished to dwell in the hollow, it would not dwell as a monarch. It would dwell as a participant. It might become one of the inlaid ones, indistinguishable except for a certain extra stillness. It might be the one who appears when a repair is so small that no one else notices it. It might carry a thread too short to use elsewhere and place it in the dry fountain. It might press its palm to a bead of resin and remain there until its fingers shone faintly, not from hunger but from fellowship.

Or it might be a moth.

A small, papery-winged moth with no mark on its wings at first, until one day a mark appeared: not a chevron, not a carved line, not a maker’s stamp, but a simple circle, like a mouth that had stopped asking. It would rest beneath a leaf, opening and closing its wings by tiny degrees. It would not be more important than the other moths. It would only be more patient.

Or it might be a dust-fish.

A slender dry swimmer with fringes stirring particulate light, moving through the hollow’s air as though absence were a sea. It would rise around the dry fountain in slow spirals, and when it passed, the dust would close behind it without sound. Sometimes it would leave a wake faintly silver, faintly warm, and those who saw it would feel, for a moment, that the hollow had been looked after.

The residents would not worship this presence. They would incorporate it. A beetle might walk over its foot. An ant might touch its hand in a patient chain. An inlaid one might pass it a cord. That would be right. The power would not want special treatment. It would want to belong to the pattern.

It would also know when not to act.

If a leaf needed to fall, it would let it fall. If a creature needed to die dryly and become lighter and hollow and part of the substrate, it would let that happen. If a wall needed to be overgrown until it was guessed at rather than seen, it would not clear the wall. If a doorway needed to disappear under stems, it would allow the doorway to become an arch of green-brown patience. It would not prevent change. It would prevent only violation.

It would not rescue the hollow from its own ecology.

That ecology is closed, intimate, and severe by the standards of wetter worlds. There is no wet rot, no lush decay, no pooling. Dead things crisp and become powder. Powder becomes substrate. Substrate feeds plants. Plants feed creatures. Creatures mend structures. Structures shelter plants. The circle is tight and strange. A benevolent but unwise power might see the slowness, the dryness, the closedness, and try to open it, to make it easier, more abundant, more familiar. A wise power would understand that ease is not always kindness. The hollow’s life is precious because it has been fitted together under a difficult law. To remove the difficulty would remove the meaning.

So the power would preserve the difficulty, but soften its cruelty.

It would let no creature be trapped unnecessarily. It would let no plant choke another beyond recovery. It would let no visitor be consumed by the place’s strangeness. It would let no builder’s mark be erased by accident. It would let no small act be wholly lost. It would keep the pressure of silence from becoming suffocation. It would keep closeness from becoming confinement. It would keep abundance from becoming hoarding. It would keep dryness from becoming desolation.

And if someone entered asking for water, the power would answer according to need.

If their need was physical and urgent, it would guide them outward. It would not shame them for requiring what the hollow did not. It would make the entrance visible. It would make their legs remember the way. It might even arrange, outside in the larger world, that they found moisture soon: a stream, a well, a fruit, a rain-wet stone, whatever the surrounding world could provide. The hollow would not be made false to itself, but the traveler would not be sacrificed to it.

If their need was spiritual, the power might let them stay longer. It might give them a warm pod to hold, its oily sheen catching the filtered light, and say without saying: *This is what liquid is here.* It might lead their eyes to a resin bead swelling slowly from a stem. It might let them watch the ants stand around it in a loose circle until it loosened. It might let them understand that nourishment can come as oil, as warmth, as scent, as attention, as repair. It might let them weep, and if they wept, their tears would be a strange weather in the hollow. The power would not forbid them. But it would not let the tears fall onto the floor unguarded. Perhaps a seam-spider would catch one in a thread. Perhaps an inlaid one would hold a leaf beneath it. Perhaps the drop would become a temporary bead, strange and bright, and then be carried away respectfully, not as water but as a visitor’s brief offering.

Even grief would be handled with care.

At the very center, or one of the centers, the power might make one change so subtle that only the hollow would know.

It might let the dry fountain become a place where lost things could be returned. Not magically. Not obviously. But if an inlaid one misplaced a peg, if a beetle dropped a grain, if a moth tore a wing, if a seed was carried into the wrong crack, the fountain’s dust would sometimes move in a slow spiral and reveal the lost thing near at hand. This would not be abundance. It would be attentiveness. It would be the power saying: *I see the smallness. I do not consider it too small.*

It might also let the fountain hold memory.

When a creature died and became light and hollow and indistinguishable from the materials of the place, a grain of its passing might remain in the basin: a fleck of shell, a piece of papery wing, a fragment of mineral thread. The power would not preserve these as relics. It would let them become part of the ongoing work. But for one moment, before they were carried away and used, they would be acknowledged. The hollow would not forget its dead. It would simply incorporate them, and the power would make sure that incorporation was not indifference.

It would bless the builders’ absence.

The hollow feels built, yet the builders are not seen. A wise power would not reveal them if they were gone, and it would not invent them if they were only a suspicion. It would let the mystery remain. It would protect the place from needing an explanation that flattened it. It would allow the inlaid ones to be descendants, or tools that learned to walk, or memories of making, or all three at once. It would not demand a single origin. It would honor the fact that the place is still being built by the slow work of plants and animals repairing it.

In this way, the power’s greatest action would be guardianship without ownership.

It would not call the hollow its own. It would not put a sign above it. It would not send priests. It would not require pilgrims. It would not make the residents into symbols for outsiders. It would keep the hollow intimate. It would let it remain a small room that behaves like a world, not a world reduced to a room for display.

If it ever withdrew, it would withdraw gently.

It would not vanish in a flash that left absence like a burn. It would recede the way light thins over an object that should be brightly lit but is instead kept secret. The hollow would continue. The beetle would cross the tile. The leaf would unfold by a fraction. The moth would lift, settle, lift again. The dust-fish would lean against the light. The inlaid ones would press powder into cracks. The seedcase would tap its small clay note. The dry fountain would gather dust in slow spirals.

But there would be a difference.

The difference would not be wetness. It would not be brightness. It would not be a new creature, unless the new creature had always seemed possible. The difference would be a sense of being kept. A warmth that was not heat. A safety that did not imprison. A silence that was crowded but not oppressive. A patience that did not feel watched. The hollow would feel, to those sensitive enough, as if some great hand had once rested near it and then lifted away without taking anything.

And perhaps, if someone entered after that, they would notice one tile slightly warmer than the others. Or a crack filled with resin that shone faintly blue in certain light. Or a small blank-faced inlaid one working alone at a repair no one had ordered, its hands tireless, its posture courteous. Or a moth with a circle on its wing. Or a dust-fish leaving a silver wake. Or the dry fountain holding a bead no larger than millet, cool and dry, remembering water without becoming it.

They might ask, in their heart: *Did something holy happen here?*

And the hollow would answer in its own way: by a beetle’s claw ticking on tile, by a cord creaking under tension, by a leaf brushing another leaf, by the slow spiral of dust, by the silence lying over everything like a lid full of lives.

The answer would be:

*Yes, but not loudly.  
Yes, but not wetly.  
Yes, but not by adding what would undo us.  
The power saw us.  
It did not make us another thing.  
It made us safer in being this.  
It blessed the dryness.  
It blessed the smallness.  
It blessed the mending.  
It left, and stayed, and did not disturb the law.*

And if the visitor was wise enough to sit down near the dry fountain, not demanding, not grasping, not asking for rain, they might feel the benevolence for themselves: a quiet fullness, a constructed breathing, a room-world kept alive by countless small hands and patient absences. They might understand then that the wise power’s final gift was not a miracle in the ordinary sense.

It was this:

It allowed the hollow to remain impossible, and yet sustained.  
It allowed the absence to be complete, and yet generous.  
It allowed the made to be alive, and the alive to be made.  
It allowed thirst to become law, and law to become beauty, and beauty to become care.

It did not bring water.

It brought witness.
