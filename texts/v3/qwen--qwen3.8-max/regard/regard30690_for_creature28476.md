Into this place, then, imagine that a benevolent and wise power turns its attention.

The first thing they would do is nothing.

Not nothing from indifference. Nothing from reverence.

A lesser goodness, seeing such a world, might immediately try to correct it according to its own habits of love. It might send rain. It might tear open the dark canopy and let down a blue sky. It might pour coolness into the hot aisles, loosen the packed roots, introduce soft green shoots, moisture, breath, animals, birdsong, the quick silver drama of drinking and bleeding and dying in the open. It might say: *This place is severe. This place is silent. This place has no tenderness. I will make it gentler.*

But a wise power would know better.

They would see at once that water here would not be mercy. Water would be violence. A single drop of free moisture in that varnished, insulated, resin-sealed world would not bring life; it would bring rupture. It would be an invading solvent, a chemical error, a short-circuit in the electrical skin of the copper filaments, a swelling and splitting of tissues evolved to keep all fluid bound, internal, and controlled. Rain would not bless the plateau. Rain would unmake it.

So they would not send rain.

They would not bring animals, either. No soft mouths to eat the seeds. No feet to wear paths into the fiber decks. No lungs to moisten the air. No hunger to set the system into motion through fear. The place has no need of predators, no need of prey, no need of pollinators, no need of dung and carrion and milk. Its reproduction is not accident; its maintenance is not appetite. To introduce fauna would be to insert a foreign economy of thirst, excretion, pain, and death into a being that has already solved continuity without them. That would not be kindness. It would be colonization.

They would not bring daylight. The black leaves do not yearn for a sun they have never known. They harvest heat, gradient, charge. They are not failed green things. They are successful black things.

They would not bring cold for the sake of comfort. Heat is not merely stress here; it is metabolism, memory, motion. The engine-groves are hearts. The furnace-flowers are organs. The hot dark is not a punishment. It is a condition of being.

Therefore the wise power would begin by refusing the first and deepest temptation of benevolence: the temptation to remake the beloved in the lover’s image.

They would not say, *You are wrong because you are dry.*  
They would not say, *You are poor because you have no water.*  
They would not say, *You are lonely because you have no voices.*  
They would not say, *You are dead because you do not bloom as soft things bloom.*

They would look upon the high, hot, waterless forest-factory and understand that it is not a ruined world. It is a finished one. Not a prison. Not a machine mistaken for life. Not a living thing enslaved by its own industry. It is a terminal metabolism, an ancient distributed body, a being whose organs are landscapes and whose thoughts are slow adjustments of heat and charge. It has its own integrity. Its own dignity. Its own terrible beauty.

And because the power is benevolent, they would wish to give something.

Because they are wise, they would ask what can be given without harm.

Not water.  
Not air.  
Not flesh.  
Not youth.  
Not progress.

What, then?

Attention.

That would be the first gift.

They would not enter the plateau as a body. No shining figure descending through the frond-vault. No footfall on the resin roads. No face reflected in the polished seed-shell troughs. No voice disturbing the muffled quiet. They would not arrive as an object among objects, because objects there are immediately claimed, sorted, dismantled, or integrated into the system. Instead, they would enter as a benign anomaly in the pattern of the place: a subtle order in the electrical lattice, a harmless thermal signature that consumes nothing, a gradient that does not demand work.

The copper filaments would feel it first.

They would lift almost imperceptibly along certain bark seams, fine as metal
