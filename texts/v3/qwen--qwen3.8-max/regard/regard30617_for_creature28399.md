If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention toward that hidden room, the first thing it would do would be almost nothing.

It would not arrive as light. Light would be a trespass. It would not arrive as voice, because voice would fill the chamber with a shape of intention too large for the small, damp dark. It would not arrive as warmth, or triumph, or miracle, or any of the bright forms in which power usually announces itself. A truly wise power would understand, immediately, that this place had survived by being unannounced. Its life was not a spectacle waiting for witness. It was a balance maintained in secret, a negotiation among cold, water, stone, leaf, and time. To reveal it too plainly would be to wound it.

So the first act of such a power would be restraint.

It would make its attention small. It would draw close without pressing. It would listen without filling the silence. It would look without brightening the dark. It would become present in the way that care is present around a sleeping child: close enough to protect, quiet enough not to wake.

And it would observe.

It would see the lushness without sun. It would see the water without rivers. It would see the cold held at the edge of victory. It would see the artifacts half-swallowed by root and moss, no longer tools, no longer machines, no longer explanations, but bones in a body made of patience. It would see the absence of animal life and recognize that this absence was not poverty. It was part of the room’s architecture, one of the materials from which its stillness had been made. It would see the Undergreen not as a collection of separate plants, but as one enclosed existence: a living system folded into itself so completely that it had become its own world, its own body, its own memory.

A lesser benevolence might look upon such a place and want to improve it.

It might want to warm the air. It might want to widen the entrance, bring in light, invite creatures, make the garden flourish in a way that surface life would recognize as abundance. It might want to rescue the Undergreen from darkness. It might want to give it eyes, speech, motion, joy, companionship, purpose. It might want to make it known.

A wise power would know that these desires were forms of violence.

It would understand that the room was not suffering because it lacked the world above. It was not waiting to be completed by animals, or sunlight, or human recognition. Its completeness was narrow, cold, and specific. It was the completeness of a seed held in a closed fist. To open the fist would not free the seed. It would change the nature of the thing held there. It might even destroy it.

So the power would ask, in whatever way powers ask without speaking:

Are you being kept against your nature?

The room would answer in the only language it had.

A droplet would form on the ceiling. It would hang for a long moment. It would fall. The pool would dimple. The black water would accept the pulse without changing level. A leaf near the edge would lift by less than a breath. A root would remain in the water, neither drinking nor retreating. The moss would hold its moisture. The cold would descend from the ceiling and be met by the faintest warmth from below. The capillaries in the stone would continue their imperceptible labor. The dead leaf beneath the living mat would continue becoming floor.

The answer would not be yes. It would not be no.

It would be continuation.

And a wise power would recognize continuation, when freely maintained, as a form of consent.

Then it would act.

But its action would be subtle. It would not enter the chamber and rearrange it. It would not repair the artifacts in a way that restored them to their ancient purpose, because their ancient purpose had already become soil, root-path, and memory. It would not awaken the hidden machinery, if machinery it was, because whatever remained operative had earned its place inside the room’s slow life. To make it suddenly visible would be to reverse the long settlement of centuries. The power would not undo what time had quieted.

Instead, it would work at the edges.

It would become a guardian of probabilities.

Above the slab, it would see that the weathered stone remained ordinary. It would not make the entrance beautiful. It would not make it curious. It would not make it famous. It would let it remain one among many cracks in a cold and dry land, too modest to attract ambition. If a map were made of that region, the place would not appear on it except as unremarkable ground.
