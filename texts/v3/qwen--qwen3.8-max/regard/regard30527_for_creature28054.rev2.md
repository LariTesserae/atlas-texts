**What They Would Do**

They would not come as a sun.

That is the first thing.

A benevolent and wise power, turning its attention toward such a place, would understand at once that light is not the universal gift it seems to sunlit worlds. Here, light would be an intrusion. It would flatten the city’s subtleties, expose what should be hidden, heat what should remain cold, and make the place ashamed of its own darkness. So they would not blaze. They would not descend in radiance. They would not announce themselves with brightness that the basin has no use for.

They would come, if they came at all, as a change in listening.

Perhaps the first sign would be very small: a drop of water falling more gently than usual into a shallow pan. A patch of fog pausing instead of drifting. A pressure field easing for one moment so that a climbing creature can shift its weight without strain. A warm current rising a little higher in a maintenance shaft, just enough to touch a cluster of eggs. Nothing dramatic. Nothing that could be mistaken for conquest.

A wise power would begin with restraint.

It would see the basin not as a failed daylight city, not as a ruin waiting to be repaired, not as a bleak nursery needing to be made green, but as a mode of being already complete in its incompleteness. The wetness is not a flaw. The cold is not cruelty. The darkness is not absence. The noise is not confusion. The unfinished architecture is not imperfection; it is openness. The place is still negotiating with itself, and that negotiation is one of its deepest dignities.

So they would not bring grasses to soften the stone. They would not make trees root in the cisterns. They would not fill the air with birdsong or the terraces with leaves. They would not try to make the basin pastoral. They would not give the fauna eyes if they do not need them, nor warmth beyond what the ecology can bear, nor speed where patience is the local form of intelligence. They would not separate the city from the animals. They would not separate the machines from the bodies. They would not separate the water from the architecture. They would not make the place simpler.

Because the being that lives there is not one body in the way a surface mind expects.

The being is a congregation. It is mineral varnish and grazing fronts, eel-chains turning in canals, wall colonies adding bead after bead of cement, eyeless climbers resting in warm crevices, blind aerial drifters calling across the dark, filter-masses breathing in reservoirs, microbial films oxidizing stone, fungi binding waste, technologies humming like slow weather. It is a citizen with countless mouths and no single face. It thinks in accretion. It remembers by building over its own past. It speaks in echo, pressure, drip, and drone.

To address such a being, a wise power would have to learn its grammar.

They would not ask, *Do you believe in me?*

They would ask, *May I help without unmaking you?*

And they would not ask in words. They would ask in the basin’s own language.

The question might be a faint warming near a place where too many eggs chill. It might be a mineral taste in the water, slightly sweeter than usual, offered near a channel that has begun to silt. It might be a harmonic tone introduced into an acoustic tower, not to override the city’s sound but to make a space for response. It might be a subtle widening of a passage where generations of climbing bodies have rubbed themselves thin. The question would be shaped like an offer, not a command.

If the basin refused them — if the whole composite life turned away in its slow way, if the pressure fields closed, if the rookeries grew restless, if the water chose other paths — then the wise power would not force itself inside. It might remain at the threshold. It might keep watch from outside the basin’s becoming. It might still act only to prevent a catastrophe that would erase all future possibility, but it would not settle there uninvited. Wisdom knows that even kindness can become violence when it ignores consent.

But if the basin accepted — not with a voice, but with a settling, a turning, a softening, a widening — then they would begin to act.

And their actions would be subtle.

They would not rebuild the city. They would tune it.

They would not rule the being. They would tend it.

They would not give it a new nature. They would help it become more fully what it already is, with less unnecessary suffering.

The first thing they would do is learn the difference between the hardness that belongs to the place and the cruelty that does not.

Cold is part of the basin. Scarcity is part of the basin. Death is part of the basin. Slow predation, mineral recycling, the patient consumption of films and bodies, the layering of generations into walls — these are not evils to be abolished. To remove them would collapse the ecology. A wise power would not make every creature safe from every other creature. It would not make the filter-feeders immortal or the grazers painless or the wall colonies free from loss. It would not turn the basin into a sterile sanctuary where nothing dies, because nothing would truly live there either.

But there are forms of suffering that serve no one. There are accidents that close futures without opening any. There are blockages that turn a gentle system into a destructive one. There are cold snaps that arrive too suddenly for adaptation. There are resonances that become painful, channels that drown nurseries, edges that cut, pressures that crush, isolations that make death waste rather than return.

Those things, a wise power would ease.

They would begin with warmth.

Not heat as fire. Not heat as sun. Heat as a slow kindness moving through stone.

In the deepest crevices where climbing creatures cluster around eggs, they would encourage the city’s own thermal veins to rise a little closer. Not enough to change the climate of the basin. Just enough so that a cold pulse does not become a killing pulse. Just enough so that the eggs do not still too often. Just enough so that the bodies resting together can give less of themselves to mere survival.

They would make warm pockets near the coldest crossings. Small refuges. Hollows where the air is a fraction thicker, where wing membranes can rest, where chilled grazers can press their flat bodies to the rock and feel a faint answering warmth. They would not make these places obvious. They would simply be there, found by those who need them, remembered by bodies that return.

They would work with water next, because water has priority here.

They would not dry the basin. They would not tame the wetness. But they would help the water move where it can help rather than where it can harm.

If a thread of condensation is about to pool behind a fragile wall, they would guide it — not by force, but by encouraging a mineral lip to rise, a microbial film to thicken, a pressure field to tilt. If a cistern is silting too quickly, they would nudge the eel-chains toward it, or wake the filter-masses into a slow circulation. If a rookery is about to be drowned by an overflow, they would open a forgotten drain, or soften the edge of a channel until the water chooses another path.

They would create reserves. Not great shining tanks, but hidden depths: emergency volumes of mineral-rich water held in sealed stone, accessible only when the basin’s normal condensation thins. They would make it so that a long cold or a long dryness does not become extinction. They would give the city a margin against despair.

They would also work with sound.

In a place where sound is architecture, a careless power could do terrible harm by adding the wrong tone. A wise power would be almost silent at first. They would listen until they could hear the city’s own deep grammar: where echoes guide, where echoes mislead, where noise becomes support, where noise becomes injury.

Then they would tune.

They would smooth a harsh reflection from a wall that sends aerial drifters into confusion. They would deepen the resonance of a tower that has begun to crack, helping it carry its note without breaking. They would make quiet pockets inside the loud districts — small acoustic shelters where hatchlings can rest, where injured creatures can hide, where the wall colonies can secrete without being disturbed by construction drones. They would not silence the city; they would give it rooms of mercy inside its own noise.

They might give the city a new kind of echo: one that returns not merely shape, but intention. A call made near such a wall would come back slightly clearer, slightly more patient, as if the stone had learned to listen before answering. The inhabitants might not understand it. They would only find that, in certain places, it becomes easier to know where one is, easier to find another, easier to be found.

They would care for passages.

The basin is crowded. Bodies move through it constantly: feet, claws, bellies, wings, drifting membranes, slow migrations through pipes and drains. A wise power would notice where the city wears against its own inhabitants. They would round sharp edges that cut climbing bodies. They would widen channels where egg migrations become stuck. They would polish routes where the stone has become too rough for soft mouths or tender undersides. They would make resting ledges near long ascents. They would make drainage mouths gentle so that small bodies are not torn.

They would not build grand monuments. They would make countless small kindnesses.

They would tend the machines, too, because here the machines are not dead things. They are part of the living weather of the basin. The power would not override them. They would not impose a foreign logic upon the resonance engines or the pressure fields or the condensation lattices. Instead, they would help those systems become more responsive to life.

Where a field pauses for a large body, they would make it pause more gracefully. Where a maintenance process threatens to smother a colony, they would delay it, redirect it, or let it proceed in a gentler sequence. Where a structure incubates eggs as much as it stabilizes stone, they would strengthen that double purpose. They would teach the city’s technologies — if teaching is the word — to treat living bodies not as obstacles but as part of their own continuity.

They would not elevate one species above the rest.

That would be a mistake. The basin does not need a king-species. It does not need the climbing things made into rulers, or the aerial drifters made into prophets, or the large slow mason-beings given dominion. The intelligence of the place is distributed. Its care is distributed. Its memory is distributed. A wise power would respect that. They might strengthen certain capacities in certain creatures, but only to improve the whole conversation, not to create hierarchy.

They might help the climbing things remember safer routes more reliably. They might help the wall colonies coordinate their cementing when a storm of cold approaches. They might help the aerial drifters find one another when the fog becomes too dense. They might help the canal filterers sense when a chemical imbalance is becoming dangerous. But they would not make any of these creatures masters of the basin. They would not give any one kind the final word.

If there is something like governance in the basin, the power would help it remain plural.

They would also care for death.

Not abolish it. Return it.

In a place where bodies become mineral wealth, death should not be waste. A wise power would make sure that when a creature dies, it is not simply lost in a cold crack or washed into a dead space where nothing can use it. They would encourage currents — water currents, air currents, fungal currents — to carry dead matter toward places of transformation. They would make small mortuary hollows, not for mourning in a human sense, but for return. There, shed carapaces, broken wings, spent bodies, and failed eggs could be received by fungi, filter-feeders, and mineral processes.

They would make death less lonely, if loneliness has any meaning there. Not by denying separation, but by ensuring that even in ending a body remains part of the city’s ongoing speech.

They might create a slow procession in the deepest drains: a patient movement of water and scavengers that carries what is left to where it can be useful again. The basin would not grieve as a sunlit world grieves. But it would not forget. Its memory would be material. Its dead would become texture, layer, echo, wall.

Then they would offer the being something stranger: a mirror.

Not a visual mirror. There is too little light for that, and sight is not the primary sense of the place. They would make an acoustic mirror. A pressure mirror. A mirror of vibration and pattern.

Somewhere in the basin — perhaps in a half-finished plaza where rock and architecture are still undecided — they would shape a chamber or a field that can gather the city’s total sound and return it in compressed form. Not all at once; that would be violence. But in slow waves. A district’s dripping, a rookery’s percussion, an engine’s drone, an aerial call, a grazing front’s rasp, a reservoir’s breathing — all gathered, softened, and returned as a pattern the basin can feel.

This would be dangerous if forced. A composite being may not be ready to know itself as one. Too much unity could erase its plurality. So the wise power would make the mirror optional. It would open only at times when the basin’s own rhythms invite it. It would not say, *You are one.* It would say, *Here is how you sound when you are together. Take what is useful. Remain many if that is your truth.*

Sometimes, perhaps, the city would hear itself and draw closer into a temporary whole. At other times, it would reject the reflection and scatter back into its multiplicity. Both responses would be valid. The power would not be offended. They would not demand selfhood from the being. They would only offer it the chance to know its own shape.

And perhaps, once in a very long while, they would allow something like a festival.

Not a celebration with light or music as surface creatures understand them. A festival of alignment.

For one long hour, or one long day, the basin’s sounds might fall into a rare coherence. Water drips would answer one another in patterns. Engine tones would harmonize. Rookeries would settle in waves. Aerial drifters would call and receive answer across the darkness. The climbing things would pause in their crevices. The grazers would still their slow scraping. The reservoirs would breathe in phase. The city would become, briefly, a single chord made of countless lives.

No one would command it. The power would not conduct it like a performance. They would only make the possibility available, like widening a door that already almost exists.

If the chord happened, it would not be worship. It would not be proof of a god. It would be a moment in which the basin experiences its own depth. The power would be present not as a face above it, but as the silence inside the chord, the pause that allows all the separate voices to belong together without erasing one another.

Afterward, the city would return to its ordinary noise: drips, drones, wing-thumps, shifting bodies, water over stone. Nothing would be visibly different. And yet something would remain. A slight ease in the way calls cross the basin. A slight warmth in the way bodies cluster. A slight patience in the way machines yield to living movement.

If outsiders ever came, the power would protect the basin without turning it into a fortress.

They would not make it easy to possess. A place like this cannot be owned. It cannot be mapped quickly, drained, illuminated, simplified, or put to use without destroying the very thing that makes it real. So the power might make the basin difficult to grasp with hostile intent. Greedy expeditions would find their echoes misleading. Their instruments would return confused readings. Their lights would feel too thin. Their bodies would become aware of cold, damp, pressure, and crowding until they lost the arrogance of possession.

But guests who came with care might find something else.

A polished trail might open before them. A warm ledge might appear near a place of rest. A pressure field might ease around their breathing. A faint phosphorescent film might glow just enough for their hands, not to conquer the darkness but to let them move without harm. The basin would not become safe for everyone. It would become legible only to those willing to be changed by it.

The wise power would not force contact between the basin and other worlds. They would not make the city an exhibit, a shrine, or a lesson. If the basin ever wished to extend itself — to seed another high wet amphitheater, to send microbial films and egg migrations and acoustic patterns into another stone basin — the power could help it do so gently. But they would not spread it by conquest. They would not make it a universal form. They would honor its particularity.

Perhaps the most important thing they would do is make themselves less necessary.

A foolish power creates dependence. A wise power increases the place’s own capacity for care.

So every gift they give would be shaped to remain as skill, not as crutch. If they warm a crevice, they would also help the city remember how to route heat there itself. If they clear a channel, they would encourage the creatures and machines that maintain it to learn its pattern. If they ease a resonance, they would leave behind a shape that the city can reproduce. If they offer a mirror, they would make it possible for the basin to build others without divine intervention.

If the power withdrew, the basin should not collapse. It should continue. It should remember kindness as structure, not as presence.

That is perhaps what divine benevolence would look like here: not a ruler, not a savior, not a bright father above the city, but a gardener of possibilities. A tender of thresholds. A listener who helps a strange life become more capable of listening to itself.

They might never be known as a god.

That would be fitting.

The inhabitants might experience them only as changes in probability. A wall that does not crack. A flood that does not come. A warmth that arrives before a killing cold. A call that is answered sooner than expected. A body that finds shelter. A migration that does not choke. A machine that pauses at exactly the right moment. A sound that becomes bearable. A silence that feels company rather than emptiness.

If the climbing things have anything like reverence, they might express it by tending a warm place more carefully. If the aerial drifters have anything like memory, they might alter their calls around a certain tower. If the wall colonies have anything like tradition, they might lay mineral beads in a pattern that repeats over generations. None of this would be doctrine. None of it would be commandment. It would be lived texture.

And if the composite being itself ever became aware enough to ask what the power wanted, the answer would be simple.

They would not say, *Love me.*

They would not say, *Obey me.*

They would not say, *Become like me.*

They would say, if such a thing could be said in pressure and water and stone:

*Continue.*

*Continue, but with less needless pain.*

*Continue, but with more room for tenderness.*

*Continue, but do not forget that your manyness can hear itself.*

*Continue, unfinished. Continue, wet and cold and loud. Continue, not because you are becoming something better, but because you are already worth tending.*

In the end, what would they do?

They would not finish the city.

They would not complete the being.

They would not make it bright, or soft, or human, or clean.

They would help it keep becoming without cruelty.

They would leave the darkness mostly dark. They would leave the water mostly wild. They would leave the animals mostly themselves. They would leave the machines mostly mysterious. But everywhere they touched, there would be a slight difference: a kinder echo, a gentler current, a warmer stone, a safer passage, a remembered body, a less lonely death.

The basin would still be high, wet, lightless, crowded, loud, slow, strange.

But it would be held.

And perhaps that is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if it did anything at all: not rescue the place from itself, but stand inside its darkness as an invisible promise that even a city made of rock, water, fauna, and sound can be loved exactly as it is, and helped — gently, patiently, without domination — to become more merciful toward its own becoming.
