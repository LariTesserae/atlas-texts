**The Attention That Came as Maintenance**

The power did not arrive with trumpets, because trumpets would have startled the fog-combs and embarrassed the engines. It did not arrive with a blinding light, because the high, thin daylight already carried all the revelation the place could bear. It arrived as a pause.

For one breath, a bead of condensation held too long on the edge of a catch-frond. A piston delayed its fall. A Recalled figure stopped halfway through a doorway that had not existed a moment before, and did not fade. Every pool, however briefly, showed only the sky it actually had.

The settlement felt itself seen.

Not judged. Not measured for conquest. Not weighed against some brighter pattern it had failed to become. Seen the way a very old and very strange tree might be seen by someone who understands trees: with patience, with curiosity, with respect for the fact that its shape has been earned.

The power saw the being that lived there, and saw that the being was not one thing but a long agreement among many things. It saw the root-felt as skin. It saw the mycelial threads as memory. It saw the sap-pipes as circulation, the cisterns as kidneys, the boilers as fever, the engines as a slow civic heartbeat. It saw the inhabitants not as separate tenants but as the being’s habits of attention: fog-combers tending the breath of the air, cistern-keepers listening to water, pruner-surveyors revising the map, engine-feeders calming heat, grafters persuading wood into form, the born and the grown and the Recalled all moving through the green intervals like thoughts that had learned to wear coats.

The settlement had many names for itself, depending on the work at hand. The Maintained. The Combed. The Fed. The Cut-Back. Beneath all those names, the power heard one older name, not spoken so much as pressed into the grain of every trained trunk and polished root:

*We continue.*

That was the being’s deepest word. Not *I am*, but *we continue*. Not a single self, but a self kept in relation.

The power listened for a long time. It listened through the wind-drag of leaves, through the hollow boom of seed-capsules, through the thud of sap-engines and the tick of beads falling from high fronds. It listened to the loudness that had so little animal breath behind it. It listened to the emptiness between districts, which was not dead but tense. It listened to the small municipal sounds of care: a knife being sharpened, a trough being filled, a hinge being oiled, a name being spoken into the rings of a young tree so the settlement would not forget it.

Then the power asked the only question that matters when benevolence encounters a world already made:

*What here needs help, and what here must not be helped in the wrong way?*

Because a wise power knows that kindness can be a kind of violence if it is careless. It knows that to “fix” a place too quickly is often to erase the very intelligence by which that place survives.

The power could have made every distance honest. It could have straightened the streets, stabilized the stairs, forced every door to open into the room expected. It could have filled the sky with birds, flooded the cisterns, silenced the engines, softened the predator-plants, abolished the thin places, and turned the whole high tableland into a gentle garden where nothing ever behaved strangely again.

It considered this, and understood that it would be a cruelty.

To make every distance ordinary would have been to destroy the privacy the place had grown into. To restore fauna in abundance would have been to overwrite an ecology that had found its own austere balance. To silence the engines would have been to stop the settlement’s metabolism. To abolish the thin places would have been to erase its memory. To make all doors safe would have been to make all doors meaningless.

The being did not need to be rescued from itself. It needed to be maintained in its own nature, with less loneliness and less unnecessary cruelty.

So the power chose the older form of benevolence.

It did not command. It did not remake. It did not descend in glory and demand worship.

It tended.

---

The first gift was small enough that most people missed it at first.

The power gave the empty intervals a way to carry notice.

Not speech, exactly. Not voices across the lawns. Not visions or apparitions. Just a faint pressure in the root-felt, a suggestion that moved like water through the compressed moss and old leaf-laminate. If a household was low on water, someone a kilometer away might wake with the thought that a trough needed filling. If a boiler was beginning to whine at the edge of a district, an engine-feeder might feel the urge to walk a route they had not planned. If a child had gone out into a wide green space and not returned, a neighbor might suddenly remember an errand that led them in the right direction.

They called this the Errand.

No one claimed it as miracle. They recorded it in census-trunks as “mutual notice,” “interval sense,” or “maintenance instinct.” The fog-combers said the mist had learned to carry intent. The cistern-keepers said water remembered where it was needed. The pruner-surveyors, more cautious, only noted that certain paths had become slightly more persuasive when someone was in trouble.

The power did not abolish solitude. It only made solitude less likely to become abandonment.

A person could still live alone in a trained tree with amber windows and see no one for days. A household could still be separated from its nearest neighbor by a shallow basin, a field of breathing fronds, and a long avenue of bare compressed leaf. But now, when the emptiness grew too full, something in the ground would stir. A knock would come. A jar of water would appear on a doorstep. A tool would be borrowed at exactly the right moment. A voice would call across the interval and ask, with the plain tenderness of the Maintained:

“Are your walls breathing?”

And the answer would come back:

“They are. Come in if the door agrees.”

---

The second gift was for the misbehaving places.

The power did not end the instability of the settlement. It gave the instability manners.

Streets still folded. Stairs still arrived at surprising levels. Doors still grew where none had been. Districts still thinned at dusk and thickened again by morning. Pools still sometimes held the sky of another hour. But the worst of it became less likely to swallow a person whole.

A traveler caught in a folded street would find, after the third turn, a bead of water resting on a leaf at head height. If they were thirsty, they could drink. If they were not, they could follow the bead-line backward, because beads in such places tended to appear where the path remembered itself. A wrong stair would often land not in danger but in a quiet interval with a bench grown from a root, as if the settlement had decided that confusion deserved rest. A door that opened into a room not yet built would usually show a strip of ordinary daylight along the floor, so no one would step too far.
