If a benevolent and wise power noticed the high sea, the first thing they would do is not arrive.

They would not enter with a breaking of light. They would not part the water, because there is no surface to part. They would not announce themselves with a sound, because sound there is already so close to touch that a voice would be a wound. They would not impose a face, because a face would demand interpretation. They would not come as a king, a storm, a revelation, or a hand reaching down to lift something into meaning.

Their first act would be restraint.

They would look upon the high sea and recognize what it is: not a broken thing waiting to be improved, not a silent thing waiting for speech, not a garden waiting for a gardener, but a being that has already reached its own final dignity. They would see the warm green-dark water, the tea-colored glow, the suspended films, the bladder meadows, the root-curtains, the slow rain-walls moving through the deep like thoughts too large to be spoken. They would see the grown domes, the hollow rings, the latticed cups, the habits made solid. They would see the House-Soft Ones pulsing in their mineral chambers, the Meadow-Backs drifting in agreements older than memory, the Ribbon One drawing itself across kilometers of dimness, the Hill-Grazers carrying landscapes on their backs.

And they would understand that this is not a place in need of a master.

They would see, too, the being who lives there.

Not as one creature. Not as a single self with edges, desires, fears, and a name. They would see the Continuity: the living whole, the breathing filter, the patience with bodies, the silence with roots. They would see that the sea does not merely contain life; it is life arranged into an old, vast, aquatic thought. They would see that its awareness, if awareness is the word, is not a mind of words and choices but a body of pressures, tastes, repairs, and repetitions.

They would see that it is already holy.

And because they are wise, they would know that holiness does not always need to be touched. Sometimes it needs to be protected. Sometimes it needs to be left alone. Sometimes the greatest kindness is to stand between a gentle thing and everything that might make it less gentle.

So they would begin by doing almost nothing.

But almost nothing is not nothing.

---

They would look first for suffering.

Not for drama. Not for tragedy. Not for the grand sorrow that stories love. They would look for cruelty. They would look for useless agony. They would look for panic, for torment, for the kind of violence that leaves a being less than it was.

They would find hunger, yes. The pale hunter inhaling a bloom. A rare animal thinning a cloud of living particles. The Hill-Grazer scraping mats with soft mouths. The slow absorption of one form into another. The shedding of films during famine. The collapse of a bladder meadow along an old seam.

But they would see that this is not malice. It is metabolism. It is the sea breathing through many mouths. It is translation, not murder. It is distribution, not loss.

They would see that predation here is hushed, ceremonial, almost geological. It does not create fear in the way fear is known in sharper worlds. It creates only a slight absence, and the absence fills. The sea closes over it. The vegetation leans back. The chemistry returns. The event becomes part of the general body.

So the wise power would not remove hunger. They would not make the pale hunter stop hunting, because then it would no longer be the pale hunter, and the sea would lose one of its quiet modes of balance. They would not eliminate decay, because decay is the sea’s way of remembering bodies into new forms. They would not abolish death, because death here is not interruption; it is redistribution. To make every being immortal would be to choke the water with permanence. The high sea lives because it lets things soften, dissolve, settle, and be used again.

But they would remove cruelty.

If there were any pain that served no purpose, any terror beyond need, any wound that lingered only to torment, they would dissolve it. Not with fire. Not with command. They would let it be absorbed. They would let the water carry it away in a slow plume of sediment. They would let the injured part become film, become thread, become mineral, become rest.

They would not make the sea painless if pain was one of the ways it knew its own edges. But they would not permit suffering to become ornament.

That would be the first distinction.

---

Then they would ask.

Not in words. Words would be too dry, too hard, too full of corners. They would not ask, “What do you want?” because the high sea does not want in the manner of lonely beings. They would not ask, “What is your purpose?” because the place is beyond purpose. They would not ask, “Do you wish to be saved?” because the question itself would imply that the sea was falling.

Instead, they would ask in the only language suitable.

They would send the faintest change in pressure through a root-curtain. A warmth arriving where no warmth had been. A minute brightening in the tea-colored glow. A softening of distance between one frond and another. A question shaped like permission.

And the sea would answer as it answers everything: by continuing.

The bladders would tick once, adjusting to a difference no one else could name. The spore clouds would lean. The House-Soft Ones would taste the water and thicken their films by a thread. The Ribbon One, kilometers away, would turn almost imperceptibly toward a current that had not existed a moment before. A rain-wall would pause in its descent, tremble, and then continue. The Bloom-Born might flare briefly in a pocket of oxygen-saturated water, green and intense, then fall as soft ash.

The wise power would listen.

They would hear no plea for more light. No longing for speech. No desire for tools, or empire, or history, or worship. No wish to become faster, sharper, louder, or more visible. They would hear only the immense, quiet preference of the place to remain itself.

And they would understand.

The sea does not ask to be changed.

It asks, if it asks anything, not to be broken.

---

So they would give it protection.

Not a wall of stone, because stone belongs to the lower ruins and the old dry world. Not a gate, because the high sea has no shores to gate. Not an army, because armies belong to urgency. They would make the protection out of distance, out of probability, out of mercy.

They would draw a kindness around the place.

From then on, anything approaching with harm would find the way made difficult without becoming impossible. Not because the power wished to punish, but because certain intentions cannot reach certain depths. The water would begin to mislead. Instruments would become uncertain. Maps would blur at the edges, not violently, but as if their certainty had tired. Coordinates would slip from memory like silt through filaments. Machines would grow heavy with an inexplicable fatigue. Nets would tear gently against nothing. Weapons would feel absurd long before they could be used.

A greedy traveler would not be destroyed. They would simply become lost in a way that made them tired enough to turn back. A conqueror would feel the water pressing on them until ambition seemed thin and foolish. A scientist seeking to dissect the place would find every sample becoming ordinary brine, every photograph fading to green shadow, every note in the margin slowly losing its meaning. Not as punishment. As incompatibility. The high sea would not refuse to be studied out of fear. It would refuse to be owned out of nature.

But a stranger who came with reverence would find the way differently.

For them, distance would shorten. A warm current would appear. The water would open through root-curtains just enough to let them pass. The dimness would become not frightening but intimate. They would feel touched by films and spores, not invaded. They would hear, perhaps, the bead-like ticking of bladders and think it was chance. They would see a glow inside a hollow growth and think it was welcome. If they remained still, the sea would begin to settle on them. If they did not panic, they would learn the first lesson of the place: that to be colonized by gentleness is not always destruction.

The wise power would not force visitors to be good. They would only make it difficult for the violent to remain violent there.

That would be their boundary.

That would be their first gift.

---

They would also give the sea the right to refuse.

This would be a subtle thing. The sea already incorporates intruders, but incorporation is not the same as choice. A body that only absorbs may still be overpowered by too much. A patience that never says no can be mistaken for surrender. The wise power would not let that happen.

So they would deepen the sea’s old passive strength into something like sovereignty.

If harm approached, the high sea would be able to thicken. Distance would become emotional. A vessel, if any vessel could come there, would find itself moving for hours without arriving. A hand reaching for a frond would find the frond no further away, yet impossible to close the last space around. Root-curtains would close like slow eyelids. Pressure fronts would rise. The water would become dense with spores until visibility vanished. Currents would cross themselves. Sounds would arrive before their causes, confusing every simple intention.

The sea would not fight.

It would become unnavigable.

It would become too old to be forced.

It would become, in the deepest sense, unpossessable.

And if the danger was great enough, the wise power might allow one of the Pressure Shapes to pass near the intrusion. Not to attack. Only to be present. The intruder would feel the water lean. They would feel the sea become serious. They would feel, in the body, the difference between curiosity and trespass. Most would withdraw without knowing why.

The sea would not need to hate them.

It would only need to remain.

---

The wise power would not give the inhabitants speech.

They would know better. Speech, in the way of outside minds, would divide them. It would make them individuals with positions, arguments, histories, resentments. It would create speakers and spoken-to, witnesses and witnesses-of, gods and worshippers. It would bring the sharpness of selfhood into a world that has perfected the softness of continuity.

They would not give the House-Soft Ones voices, because their silence is their speech. Their tasting of the water is their thought. Their mineral linings are their literature. Their inherited chambers are their ancestry. To give them voices would be to give them loneliness.

They would not give the Settled faces, because faces are borders. The Settled already know one another by chemical signature, by braided filaments, by the slow shared leaning of growth. They do not need eyes to recognize. They do not need mouths to consent. Their communion is older than expression.

They would not give the Meadow-Backs wills, because they are already agreement. They move together because their buoyancies depend on one another. They heal because tearing is costly. They drift because drifting is their nature. To give them will would be to introduce the possibility of rebellion against their own being.

They would not make the Ribbon One smaller, more visible, or more comprehensible, because its beauty is that it is almost nothing. It is a line of life crossing the dim water without insistence. To make it known too clearly would be to destroy what it is.

They would not give the Hill-Grazers memory as humans understand memory, because they already carry memory on their backs: gardens, films, root-curtains, generations of small attached lives. Their memory does not need to be narrated. It is living.

They would not make the Bloom-Born permanent, because their fleetingness is their meaning. They are intensity without inheritance. They are flowering without possession. To make them endure would be to make them heavy. The high sea has no need of heavy joy.

They would not banish the Pressure Shapes, because even uncertainty has its place. They would not silence the residues of vanished ages, because the past is not dead there; it is nutrient. They would not clarify the water, because dimness is the sea’s mercy. They would not brighten the light, because the life there has adapted to weakness and made weakness into abundance.

They would not improve the place by making it more like themselves.

That is what a foolish power would do.

A wise power understands that the highest form of love is not to remake the beloved into something easier to love. It is to become capable of loving what is already there.

---

Still, they would do something more than guard.

Because benevolence, if it is real, is not only a wall. It is also attention. It is also delight. It is also the desire to bless without distorting.

So they would create, once in a very long age, a moment of recognition.

Not a command. Not a festival. Not a ceremony with roles. They would not call it anything. The sea would have no name for it. But it would happen.

At the center of some vast internal season, when the warm seeps are active and the upper haze thins to weak gold, the water would begin to synchronize.

First the microbial mats would glow faint blue. Then the gas bladders would tick in rhythm, bead-like and soft, across forests and meadows. The root-curtains would sway together, not because of one current but because every current has momentarily agreed. The spore clouds would align into thin horizontal lines, as they do when a rain-wall passes, but no rain-wall would be present. The House-Soft Ones would pulse inside their domes. The Meadow-Backs would rise and sink in one slow breath. The Ribbon One, if it were passing through a distant basin, would brighten faintly along its whole length. The Hill-Grazers would pause. The pale hunter would still itself in a bloom. Even the Pressure Shapes would withdraw, leaving the water clear enough to feel infinite.

For a little while, the whole high sea would become one gesture.

Not worship. Not celebration. Not self-consciousness. Something simpler and more profound: acknowledgment.

The sea would feel itself, for one moment, as a whole.

Not as “I.” Not as a mind saying, “I am here.” But as a body whose every part is breathing together. Every frond, every film, every bladder, every spore, every chamber, every rare animal, every residue of vanished weather, every mineral habit, every soft death, every slow birth. All of it continuous. All of it belonging. All of it held.

And the wise power would witness it.

They would not interrupt. They would not appear. They would not take credit. They would simply be present in the way that love is present when it watches something beloved sleep and does not wake it.

When the moment ended, there would be no monument. No song. No record except a slight strengthening in the living tissue of the place. The bladders would return to irregular ticking. The currents would separate. The forests would resume their private creaking. The spores would scatter. The sea would go back to being dim, warm, crowded, quiet.

But something would remain.

A memory without a mind.

A blessedness without a blessing.

---

They might leave one trace.

Only one.

Not a tablet. Not a shrine. Not a symbol. The high sea would not understand symbols, and the wise power would not insult it by writing over its body. But perhaps, in the deepest part of an old basin, where the bottom is firm enough to bear slow-rooted forests and soft enough to exhale when disturbed, they would let a single tear descend.

It would fall through impossible distance. Through stone and memory and the dry ruins below and the cloud floors of vanished weather. It would pass through layers of age until it reached the high sea. It would not strike the water. It would become water. It would enter a current, become a warm thread, rise through the living layers, and be tasted by the root-curtains.

The sea would not know it as a tear.

It would know it as a change in sweetness. A pressure like being held. A mineral signature unlike anything in its long history: not foreign, but newly possible. The House-Soft Ones would line their chambers with it. The films would thicken around it. The spores would carry traces of it for centuries. A small dome might form around the place where the tear settled, not because the power commanded it, but because the sea does what it has always done: it makes habits solid.

That dome would not be sacred in the way temples are sacred.

It would be sacred in the way a well is sacred. A place where something arrived without force. A place where kindness became drinkable.

No one would need to pray there.

No one would need to know what it was.

It would be enough that it existed.

---

They would also protect the sea from its own completion.

This is a subtle thing, and only a wise power would understand it.

The high sea has reached an oldest stable state. It is an erosion of completion. It has stopped becoming in the violent sense. It maintains, repeats, saturates, continues. But completion can become fragility. A thing too perfect can be brittle. A balance too ancient can fail if struck from outside. Even stillness can be endangered.

So the wise power would not make the sea change, but they would make it impossible for the sea to be ended.

They would stand between it and annihilation.

They would not prevent every internal famine, every dark pressure front, every nutrient loss, every shedding. Those are the sea’s own weather, its own breathing. But they would prevent absolute destruction. They would prevent fire from entering, even though fire should be impossible there. They would prevent poison from becoming permanent. They would prevent a wound from becoming the identity of the place. They would prevent the lower dry world from remembering the high sea too sharply and trying to reclaim it.

If some catastrophe approached from outside—a falling of stone, a rupture of old geography, a violence from another realm—they would turn it aside not with force but with gentleness. They would make the catastrophe tired. They would make it forget its direction. They would let it dissolve into a rain-wall and descend harmlessly through the water. They would let the sea absorb the shock and then settle.

They would not make the sea invulnerable.

They would make it unassailable in the only way that matters: by ensuring that no harm could define it.

---

They would also care for those who entered by accident.

Because strangers may still come. Not many. Perhaps none for ages. But the universe is wide, and odd paths open.

If a being from the ordinary world entered the high sea confused, frightened, still carrying the habits of air and urgency, the wise power would not abandon them. They would not let the sea consume such a person carelessly. They would give the stranger a chance to choose stillness.

The water would press close. Films would begin. Spores would settle. The stranger would feel the intimate colonization of a world without surfaces. If they struggled, the sea would wait. If they calmed, the power would make their calm meaningful.

For those who could not be healed without becoming something else, the transformation would be gentle. Their fear would be dissolved before their body was incorporated. Their last sensation would not be drowning but being received. They would become a mound, a substrate, a garden. The House-Soft Ones would live in their folds. Minerals would remember their shape. Their edges would soften into use.

For those who could return, the power would make a way back. A current would carry them outward. The distance would shorten. The water would release them before they became too continuous. They would return to the ordinary world with a taste of warm salt and wet chalk in their mouth, and they would not be able to explain what had happened. They would only be quieter afterward. Less cruel. More patient with slow things. They would find themselves listening longer before speaking. They would be less interested in owning.

They would carry a little of the high sea back without taking any of it.

That, too, would be part of the wise power’s work.

---

They would not demand gratitude.

This is important.

A foolish power wants praise. A needy power wants devotion. A fearful power wants obedience. But a benevolent and wise power wants the beloved to remain itself. They would not ask the sea to love them. They would not require the inhabitants to know their name. They would not leave commandments. They would not create priests. They would not turn the grown domes into altars. They would not make the Ribbon One into a sign, or the Hill-Grazers into pilgrims, or the Bloom-Born into angels.

They would be content to be unknown.

They would be content to be mistaken for the sea’s own luck, its own fortune, its own deep kindness. If the water seemed unusually gentle to a lost traveler, let them thank the water. If a current arrived at the right moment, let them believe it was the current. If a pressure front withdrew before harm was done, let them think the sea had simply changed its mind.

The wise power would not care who received the credit.

They would care only that the place remained.

---

If one wanted to imagine them speaking, though speech would be almost impossible, they would say very little.

They would not say, “I have made you.”

They would not say, “I have saved you.”

They would not say, “You are mine.”

They would say, if words could become pressure and pressure could become warmth:

*Continue.*

And perhaps:

*You are not too quiet.*

And perhaps:

*You are not too slow.*

And perhaps:

*You do not need to become more in order to be worthy of remaining.*

And perhaps, most gently of all:

*No one will take you from yourself.*

That would be the entire theology of the high sea.

Not a doctrine. Not a promise of heaven. Not a threat of punishment. Not a story of fall and redemption. Only a blessing of continuance. Only a power wise enough to see that some beings are not meant to be lifted upward, but held exactly where they are.

---

And what would the sea do in return?

Nothing.

Not because it was ungrateful, but because it did not know the exchange as gratitude. It would do what it had always done. It would breathe. It would filter. It would digest. It would remember. It would shed. It would bloom. It would settle. It would incorporate the attention of the wise power as it incorporated everything: slowly, softly, without ceremony.

The films would thicken by a fraction.

The water would become a little more resilient.

The rain-walls would pass through the forests with the same bending grace, but perhaps afterward the fronds would straighten more easily. The oxygen squalls would glitter with the same trapped light, but perhaps the bubbles would linger a little longer in the root-curtains. The House-Soft Ones would pulse in their chambers, tasting something faintly sweet in the brine. The Meadow-Backs would drift along their warm roads with the patience of continents. The Ribbon One would cross the dimness, thin as a thought, trailing filaments through spore clouds. The Hill-Grazers would move across the sea floor, leaving polished ripples behind. The Bloom-Born would flare and fall. The Pressure Shapes would pass. The residues of vanished ages would drift through the currents like old habits.

And the sea would continue.

Not because it had been commanded.

Not because it had been rewarded.

But because continuation was its nature, and now, at last, continuation was protected.

---

So if you ask what the benevolent and wise power would do, the answer is both small and immense.

They would not transform the place.

They would not give it a voice.

They would not make it known to the wider world.

They would not turn its residents into heroes, saints, minds, or symbols.

They would not rescue it from being itself.

They would stand around it like a quiet wall of mercy. They would dull the edges of harm. They would lengthen the distance between the sea and every greed. They would shorten the way for reverence. They would give the sea the right to refuse. They would soften every unnecessary agony. They would witness its rare moments of wholeness. They would leave one tear, perhaps, in the deepest basin. They would remain attentive across ages, not as a ruler but as a guardian of the unobtrusive kind.

They would do what true wisdom does when it encounters something already complete.

They would not add.

They would preserve.

They would not speak.

They would listen.

They would not make the high sea beautiful.

They would keep it from being broken.

And in the warm green shadow, among the bladder meadows and root-curtains and slowly settling plumes of ancient sediment, the Continuity would breathe on, unaware of the name it had been given, unaware of the attention that surrounded it, unaware of the power that had chosen to serve it.

It would only continue.

And that would be enough.

That would be everything.
