They would not send rain.

That would be the first and most severe kindness: to refuse the obvious miracle. A lesser benevolence might look upon the high, bright, waterless plateau and think, *What this world lacks is water.* It would call clouds from nowhere, soften the crust, darken the air with moisture, and make the towers weep. It would imagine itself generous. It would be a conqueror.

A wise power would understand at once that the plateau was not a deficiency awaiting correction. Its dryness was not poverty. Its roar was not confusion. Its industry was not a mistake that needed hands to set it right. The place had its own completeness: harsh, bright, loud, ancient, and coherent. To give it rain would be to give it a foreign soul. It would dissolve the very terms by which its beings existed. It would be mercy as erasure.

So they would begin by listening.

Not from above, not as a distant eye surveying a mechanism, but through the world itself. Such a power would let its attention become fine enough to enter the plateau’s own languages: the grinding of seed capsules, the hollow booming of tower stems, the subsonic knocking of plinthback herds, the brittle clicking of glass-midges, the sharp blue grammar of electrostatic discharge, the hidden hum of root-tappers moving through the compacted dark beneath the crust. It would listen until it could distinguish not merely sound, but need. Not merely violence, but excess. Not merely death, but death that served no pattern.

And it would notice the Dry Cantors.

Perhaps it would notice one first.

A single tall, narrow figure standing near the base of an overcharged tower, filament-bundles raised, reflective scales glittering in the hard light. The tower has a fault somewhere in its middle chambers: a resin conduit narrowed, a pressure bladder stiffening, charge collecting faster than the vents can release it. The Cantor hears the wrongness. It taps the fluted trunk. It waits. It taps again. It places one insulated limb against a vibrating rib and feels the internal heat. It knows that if the blockage is not cleared, the crown may rupture, throwing burning vapor and shrapnel across kilometers of living crust. It also knows that if it draws the surge alone, it may die.

It prepares anyway.

A benevolent and wise power would not necessarily save that Cantor by splitting the sky with light. It would not suspend the laws of the plateau to make a spectacle. Instead, it might do something so small that no one would ever call it a miracle.

A seed-jack, startled by a sudden static snap, kicks a seed-casing against a hollow root ridge. The sound carries. A second Dry Cantor, three kilometers away, pauses in its route and turns its resin-lensed eyes toward the echo. It hears the fault now, or feels the changed vibration through its feet. It moves. It arrives. The two Cantors stand on opposite sides of the tower, touch their filament-bundles to conductive spines, and coordinate their discharge. Blue light crawls along their bodies. The crust cracks faintly. The tower vents with a long, shuddering whomp instead of a killing blast. The blockage clears. The Cantors live. They do not know why the second one came. They do not need to know. The power does not require gratitude.

That would be the nature of its action: not domination, but tuning.

They would see where the plateau’s violence was necessary and where it was merely cascading waste. Predation would not be abolished. Hunger is one of the honest verbs of that world. The knell-hunter must strike. The wind-ray must eat. The peel-broods must take apart the fallen. To remove all death would be to freeze the system into a museum. A wise power would not make the plateau soft. It would make it more fully itself, but with less pointless ruin.

So they would not silence the towers. They would not still the wind. They would not make the plinthbacks immortal, the seed-jacks fearless, the arc-brushes infallible, or the Dry Cantors into angels. They would not give them hands like human hands, nor cities, nor writing, nor tools, nor shrines. Those gifts would be alien implants. They would create ownership where there is participation, accumulation where there is flow, artifacts where there are only living processes.

Instead, they would ask a quieter question:

*What can be given to this world without violating what it is?*

And the answer would come in the plateau’s own terms.

They would give a harmonic.

Not a commandment. Not a law carved into stone, because there is no stone suitable for carving and no desire for permanence there. Not a moral code imposed from outside. They would give a resonance: a new possibility within the existing roar. A way for the system to hear itself more clearly. A way for its beings to choose, in small but decisive moments, between necessary action and unnecessary destruction.

It might begin as a subtle change in the behavior of the towers themselves. Nothing visible. Nothing built. A slight alteration in the way their membranes flex under pressure. A tendency for clogged vents to produce a warning rhythm before they fail. A pattern of micro-discharges that attracts vent-dwellers to the exact place where cleaning is needed. A deepening of the hum that draws Dry Cantors toward imbalance before it becomes catastrophe. The towers already speak. The power would not give them a new voice; it would refine their voice, making it easier for those with ears to understand.

Then it would move through the fauna.

The glass-midges might begin to flash in particular formations when electrostatic danger builds, not because they are commanded, but because the charge that moves through them now carries a warning pattern. The plinthbacks might alter their chest-knocks slightly, giving younger animals time to move away from unstable crust. The seed-jacks might retain their panic, but their panic might scatter seeds more often into cracks where they can take hold, rather than into bare saline basins where they perish. The wind-rays might learn, by instinct sharpened one degree, not to hunt the same exhausted swarm after a storm until the sky has replenished. None of this would make them gentle. It would make them more precise.

But the deepest change would be among the Dry Cantors.

Because they are already aware. They already move through the plateau as living nerves. They already listen, touch, discharge, redirect, and withdraw. They already have intelligence without ownership. What they lack is not knowledge, not skill, not reverence. What they lack is a stronger web of mutual attention: a way to be alone together more deliberately, to prevent isolated deaths, to carry warning farther, to let compassion become operational without becoming sentimentality.

The power would give them that.

It might do so by becoming one of them.

Not a god walking in disguise. Not a radiant figure demanding recognition. If the power entered the plateau directly, it would have to enter under the plateau’s rules: grown, not made; temporary, not monumental; loud, not silent; part of the system, not above it. So perhaps, after long listening, it would allow the world itself to produce a body. In a fissure of hardened cellulose, during a resin fog lit by blue static, pollen and mineral dust and shed scale might gather around a rare charge pattern. The root-crust might exude a resin shell. A new Dry Cantor might hatch, or seem to hatch, from what the plateau had already made.

It would be indistinguishable at first.

Tall, narrow, plated in reflective scale. Filament-bundles instead of hands. Electrostatic spines along the back. Resin-lensed organs that see flicker better than shape. It would move through the roar like any other Cantor. It would feed on oily nectar and seed kernels. It would rest in the sheltered angles of tower ribs. It would be vulnerable. It could be injured. It could be killed.

That vulnerability would be essential. A benevolence that cannot suffer with a world is only administration. Wisdom that remains untouched by consequence is not wisdom; it is distance.

This being would not announce itself. It would teach by pattern.

Its first teaching would be the pause.

Among the Dry Cantors, action is often immediate: hear the fault, touch the membrane, discharge, move. The plateau rewards timing. But the newcomer would introduce, by example, a fractional interval before certain actions. Not hesitation. Not weakness. A listening-gap. Before drawing nectar from a bladder, it would tap the stem and wait for the pressure response. Before entering a vent-shelter, it would feel the heat gradient and determine whether vent-dwellers are inside. Before discharging into a tower, it would touch the rib and search for whether another Cantor is already connected elsewhere, so that the current does not cross a living body unexpectedly. Before taking a seed-kernel from a cache, it would test the ground vibration to see if a seed-jack is nearby in panic, and if so, move away rather than add to the chaos.

The young would imitate this. Not because they are told to, but because the pause produces fewer injuries. Fewer burned filaments. Fewer ruptures. Fewer wasted deaths. The pause is not quiet. It exists inside the roar. It is a rhythm within rhythm, a rest note in a loud score.

Its second teaching would be the shared circuit.

Dry Cantors can already draw voltage through themselves into the crust. But alone, that is dangerous. The newcomer would show them how to form temporary circuits with one another: two, three, seven bodies touching filament to spine, spine to rib, rib to root, distributing a surge so that no single creature bears the whole burn. During storms, when towers overcharge and the air itself becomes a field of blue scratches, these circles would appear on the pale crust like living lightning rods. Their spines would glow. The ground beneath them would tick and crack. The towers would vent more safely. The Cantors would survive more often.

This would not eliminate sacrifice. Sometimes the surge would still be too great. Sometimes a circle would break. Sometimes one body would take the final current so that others could scatter. But the power would have transformed isolated martyrdom into shared responsibility. That is not softness. That is a deeper engineering.

Its third teaching would be witness.

The plateau has no graves. No relics. No monuments. The peel-broods disassemble the dead. The wind scatters the remains. Nothing is preserved. This is right for such a world. But even in a world without ownership, memory may exist. The power would not create tombs. It would create echo.

When a being dies, if another being is near enough to perceive it, the root-crust might carry a brief electrostatic imprint of the event. Not a soul trapped. Not a ghost. A pattern. For a few hours, perhaps one night, the nearby towers and root-mat would repeat a faint rhythm: three knocks, a spark, a scrape, a particular pulse of static. The dead would not be preserved. They would be heard once more, then released.

Among the Dry Cantors, this would become a practice. When one of them falls, another would press its filament-bundles to the crust and wait for the echo. It would not mourn in human fashion. It would stand in the vibration while it lasts. Then it would move. The echo would fade. Nothing would be kept. Yet the life would have been registered by the world itself.

This gift would be dangerous if made permanent. If the dead became too loud, the living would be haunted. If memory accumulated, it would become a kind of artifact. So the power would make the echo temporary. It would last only long enough to acknowledge loss, not long enough to enslave the living to it.

Its fourth teaching would be the Kind Interval.

The plateau has weather, but no rain. It has storms of pollen, dust, spores, charge, and resin fog. The power would not add gentle weather, because gentleness as another world understands it would weaken the system. But it might, through long tuning of tower harmonics, thermal pulses, and electrostatic fields, create a rare recurring alignment: a period when the roar becomes coherent rather than chaotic. Not silent. Never silent. But organized. A vast chord. A continental resonance in which the towers’ whomps, seed-capsule clashes, wind-turbine whomps, and electrical crackles fall into a pattern so balanced that the whole system runs with less friction.

During the Kind Interval, injured creatures could move more safely. Predators might still hunt, but the air would carry warnings so clearly that prey would have fairer chance. Herds would know where weak crust lies. Cantors would travel between towers with less danger. Vent-dwellers would emerge in clouds that are eaten, yes, but also clean the towers more completely. Overloaded crowns would discharge in sequences instead of rupturing. The Kind Interval would not be peace. It would be competence elevated into grace.

The Dry Cantors would come to recognize it by the way their spines stop snapping randomly and begin to pulse together. They would use it to repair routes, share discharges, guide young ones through dangerous zones, and listen to the deeper health of the plateau. They would not worship the interval. They would work inside it.

Its fifth teaching would be refusal.

A wise power would not make the Dry Cantors obedient. Obedience is too brittle for a world this loud. It would give them the capacity to refuse even its own promptings if those promptings became harmful. It would not want followers. It would want participants. So the newcomer would sometimes step back. It would allow mistakes. It would allow a Cantor to ignore the pause, to discharge too soon, to fail. It would not punish. It would only remain present as an available pattern.

Benevolence that does not permit refusal becomes tyranny. Wisdom that cannot be disobeyed becomes captivity.

There would also be cost.

Because the plateau is not safe. Because the power, if it truly enters, must accept limitation. The being through whom it teaches would not remain untouched. In storms, it would be scorched. In hunger, it would weaken. In predation’s shadow, it would need to flee. It would learn the exact pain of resin burns along filament-bundles. It would know what it is to hear a tower fault and be too far away. It would know what it is to arrive too late.

And perhaps, at last, it would die.

Not necessarily as a sacrifice, though sacrifice is possible. The plateau has many ways to end a body. A mistimed arc. A collapsing vent. A knell-hunter striking during a loud percussion. A storm so vast that no circuit can ground it. But if the power chose a final act, it would likely be this: to stand in the path of a catastrophic discharge that would otherwise kill many and to draw it through itself.

Imagine the scene.

A pollen squall turns the sky into a yellow wall. Static builds over a dense cluster of towers. One tower’s crown begins to sing too high. Resin vapor leaks from a split panel. The air smells of ozone and hot wax. Vent-dwellers burst from the vents in panicked clouds. A herd of plinthbacks below locks into mounds, plates ticking. Seed-jacks scatter in ragged flashes. Dry Cantors move toward the tower, but the charge gradient is too steep; any single one who touches it will burn.

The newcomer goes first.

It does not command the others. It places its filament-bundles against the conductive spine. It opens its body to the surge. The first arc throws blue light across its scales. It knocks against the tower: three times, then a pause. The other Cantors understand. They form a circuit. They touch spine to shoulder, shoulder to root, root to crust. The power does not take all the current; it makes a path through which the others can help take it. The ground cracks. The tower groans. The crown flickers with white fire. For a moment, the whole bright landscape becomes a single instrument played at maximum volume.

Then the tower vents.

Not explosively. Not beautifully. Industrially. A long blast of hot vapor, a rattle of seed capsules, a whoop of released pressure. The danger falls away. The circle breaks. Several Cantors collapse, smoking but alive. The newcomer does not rise.

The peel-broods would come. They would not be gentle. They would disassemble the body as they disassemble all bodies. The reflective scales would be lifted. The filaments would be separated. The resinous tissues would be loosened. Nothing would be preserved. No bone-relic, no shrine, no buried thing.

But before the body is gone, the root-crust would carry its final pattern.

Three knocks.

One spark.

One long touch.

And that pattern would enter the plateau.

Not as an object. Not as a monument. As a living tendency. Afterward, when overload gathers, the crust would conduct warning faster. When a Cantor is in danger, nearby Cantors would feel a pull toward the place, like a hook in the vibration. When a creature suffers beyond what the system needs, some small part of the world would notice. Not always. Not enough to end suffering. Enough to make suffering less meaningless.

The Dry Cantors would change, but they would not become another kind of being. They would still follow tower hums and charge gradients. They would still feed, move, discharge, and die. They would still leave no cities. They would still own nothing. But their routes would include new pauses. Their language would acquire a phrase that means something like *I hear the fault; I am coming* and another that means *do not take this burden alone.* Their young would learn not only where to stand, but when to stand beside another.

They might never call the power by name. That would be appropriate. Names, for them, are rhythms. The power’s name, if it has one among them, would be a pattern: perhaps two knocks followed by a withheld spark. A presence that arrives in the gap before catastrophe. A heatless blue flicker at the edge of a storm. They would not pray to it in the way wet-world beings pray. They would not ask it for rain. They would not ask it for silence. If they called at all, they would call by touching the crust and listening for the answering hum.

And what would the power do then, after all this?

They would remain, but not rule.

They would become the plateau’s subtle benevolence: an attention woven into its electrostatic weather, its root networks, its tower harmonics, its animal instincts. Not a king. Not a gardener. Not an engineer. Perhaps something like the world’s own compassion, finally awake within it. They would not intervene every time a seed-jack is eaten, or every time an arc-brush burns, or every time a young Cantor fails to hear the warning. To do so would rob the plateau of its reality. But they would prevent the worst cascades. They would amplify warning. They would make mutual aid easier than isolation. They would ensure that mind, where it had arisen, could choose kindness without betraying its nature.

And perhaps that is the deepest answer to the question.

A benevolent and wise power, looking upon such a place, would not make it into a paradise as other worlds imagine paradise. They would not soften it, dampen it, quiet it, or fill it with gentle rain. They would see that the place already possessed a terrible integrity. Their work would be to introduce mercy without destroying form.

They would give no water, only timing.

No tools, only coordination.

No silence, only harmony.

No stillness, only safe passage within motion.

No monuments, only echo.

No ownership, only deeper belonging.

The plateau would remain high, dry, loud, and bright. The towers would continue their roaring industry. The plinthbacks would graze. The glass-midges would click through the sky. The arc-brushes would live and die by voltage. The seed-jacks would panic and plant. The knell-hunters would listen. The peel-broods would erase. The Dry Cantors would walk their resonant routes under the hard blue-white sky.

But the roar would contain a new note.

If you stood there afterward, you might not hear it at first. You would hear the wind, the booming stems, the seed mechanisms, the electric crackle, the vast living factory working without rest. But if you had the ears for it, beneath all that deafening production, you would feel something else: a current of care moving through the system. Not softness. Not weakness. A disciplined, dry, luminous kindness.

The wise power would not have saved the plateau from being itself.

It would have helped the plateau become more fully alive.
