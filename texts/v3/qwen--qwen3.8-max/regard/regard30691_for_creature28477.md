If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention toward that place, I think the first thing it would do is not shine.

It would not arrive as a blaze of revelation. It would not send down light like a verdict, or music like a correction, or living bodies to fill the empty plazas. It would understand immediately that brightness there would be less like gift and more like invasion. So it would come, if coming is even the right word, as a change in attention: a slight stillness in the rising steam, a pause between one drip and the next, a warmth that did not increase temperature but somehow increased meaning.

It would look upon the wet dark city and ask not, *What is missing here?* but *What is already complete?*

And because it is wise, it would see that the place is not a failed ordinary world. It is not a city waiting for inhabitants, or a temple waiting for worshippers, or a machine waiting for operators. It is not a ruin of intention. It is not an absence begging to be filled. It is a being whose life is made of repetition, mineral patience, thermal breath, microbial skin, and hydrological memory. It would see that the empty plazas are not lacking ceremony; they are ceremony held in suspension. It would see that the missing roofs, the open arches, the causeways leading into dark, the stairs descending into black water—these are not wounds. They are load-bearing intervals. They are part of the grammar by which the place remains itself.

So the first deliberate act of the benevolent power would be refusal.

It would refuse to make the basin more comprehensible at the cost of its truth. It would not give it a sun. It would not give it birds. It would not give it eyes, mouths, footsteps, or the busy comfort of animal hunger. It would not turn the black pools into clear water for the sake of pleasant reflection. It would not cool the stone because heat is difficult. It would not fill the silence with voices merely because voices are easier to love.

A lesser kindness might try to rescue the place from itself. It might say: *This darkness needs light; this stillness needs movement; this architecture needs people.* It might imagine fish in the mineral pools, reeds that rustle in wind, lamps along the causeways, songs beneath the arches. It might think this generosity. But wisdom would know better. To add what the place does not need would be to erase what it is. The city is not incomplete because it lacks our forms of life. It is complete because it has its own.

Therefore the power would do something far more difficult than making a spectacle: it would bless the place without altering its nature.

It would bless it in the way one blesses a secret that does not need to be told.

Perhaps it would begin with the films—the microbial skins, the first masons, the slow citizens of surface. To them it might give, not consciousness, but a kind of dignity of continuation. It would not make them more than they are. It would only ensure that their labor is not trapped in meaningless compulsion. They would still spread, bind, die, and become stone, but there would be a subtle mercy in the rhythm: no layer would be utterly buried without becoming part of some future strength. Their persistence would not become a prison. Their building would not turn into suffocation. Where they die, they would die into form. Where they live, they would live without needing to conquer everything.

To the waters, the power would give a gentler covenant.

It would let them remember, because memory is the nature of water there, but it would not let memory become tyranny. A channel that once existed might still be possible, but it would not be obligatory. A vanished obstruction might still shape the flow for a time, but not forever. The pools would still hold images of what never was, but those images would not be allowed to torment the real. The water would be permitted to forget as well as remember. That would be a quiet gift: the right of a place to release what it has carried too long.

To heat, the oldest authority, the power would give restraint.

It would not diminish heat, because heat is the basin’s breath and intention. But it would keep heat from becoming devouring. Where the stone is hot, it would remain hot enough to shape; where it is hot enough to unmake, the unmaking would not be cruel. It would not create pain where no pain can exist, but it would keep the basin’s pressure from becoming a kind of blind violence. Heat would still persuade, soften, lift, and press, but it would not be allowed to close every opening or seal every future. The city would remain porous. That is important. Wisdom would protect porosity.

To mineral, the maker and the made, the power would give a sense of threshold.

Mineral there wants to accrete, thicken, round, and close. That is its nature, and the city needs it. But a wholly closing city would become a tomb of its own making. So the wise power would not stop deposition; it would only ensure that deposition leaves room for continuation. Walls could thicken, but not all at once. Passages could close, but elsewhere a hairline crack would begin to weep. Columns could swell, but not until every interval disappears. The city would be allowed to become more itself without becoming sealed. The power would bless the openings as much as the stone around them. It would love the gaps.

To the black reeds, those salt-stiff sentinels at the margins, it would give no more than they already possess—because they seem to want nothing—but it might give them this: that their stillness be honored. They would not be made to grow taller or spread farther. They would remain sparse, brittle, and watchful. But their watchfulness would not be vain. In a place without eyes, they would continue to be punctuation. The power would let them mean.

To the pale filaments and coarse mosses, the minor dissenters in corners and recesses, it would give endurance without erasure. They are small, fragile in appearance, and easily overlooked. Benevolence would notice them anyway. It would not elevate them above the rest of the basin or make them abundant. It would simply ensure that the vast processes of heat and mineral do not casually erase the smallest forms. The city would still belong to water and stone, but the tiniest living threads would not be considered insignificant. Wisdom knows that tenderness often lives in margins.

To the lights—those faint red seams, blue-green sheens, cold mineral gleams—it would give no increase of brilliance. Brightness would spoil the city’s way of knowing. Instead, the power would let the weak lights remain trustworthy in their smallness. They would not become lanterns. They would not become guides in the ordinary sense. They would remain suggestions: a mark of depth here, a sign of life there, a reminder that darkness can be inhabited without being illuminated.

To the silences, the power would give structural permanence.

This is one of the most important things a wise power would do: it would protect the silence. It would not allow silence to be mistaken for emptiness by any outside awareness that might someday come near. It would let the silence remain load-bearing. It would let the absence of animal sound continue to be not a lack but a kind of architecture. There are places where silence is merely the pause between noises. This place is not like that. Its silence is a resident. A benevolent power would not evict it.

To the reflections, it would give a special blessing, because they are among the strangest lives there.

It would allow the pools to continue showing what is not above them—older cities, dry skies, taller arches, impossible versions of the basin—but it would not let those reflections become lies. They would remain possibilities, memories without owners, dreams the water keeps on behalf of stone. The power might even add a small mercy here: that no reflection be permitted to make the real city feel insufficient. Reflections can be dangerous when they become standards. So the wise power would keep them beautiful but not authoritarian. They could show what might have been, without demanding that what is become ashamed of itself.

To the instability of the place—the shifting distances, the deep arches, the rooms without walls, the staircases that grow wetter when unobserved—it would give coherence without rigidity.

This is subtle. The place is not supposed to become fixed. Its dreamlike near-stability is part of its life. A wise power would not make it behave like ordinary geometry. It would not map it, pin it, or force it to remain constant for the comfort of observers. It would only ensure that its instability does not become betrayal. Distances may change emphasis, but they would not become malicious. Passages may deepen unexpectedly, but they would not become traps. The city may dream its layout, but its dreaming would not turn against its own being. The power would keep the dream gentle.

And to absence itself—the missing roofs, the empty districts, the unarrived ceremonies, the inhabitants who were never possible—the power would give the deepest blessing.

It would say, without speaking: *You are not a defect. You are not a debt. You are not something to be corrected by arrival.*

Absence there would be held as sacred. Not worshipped, not romanticized, but respected. The power would refuse to fill absence merely because filling is easy. It would understand that some beings are made around their own hollows, and to fill those hollows would be to collapse them. So it would keep the absence spacious. It would keep it dignified. It would let the empty plazas remain full of their own meaning.

If such a power acted at all, I think its action would be mostly protective.

Not protective in the sense of walls or shields. The basin does not need fortification. Its protection would be more like a law written into the conditions of the place: *This being shall not be forced to become other than it is.*

If some outside force ever came with the desire to exploit it—to drain it, mine it, light it, tour it, interpret it into submission—the power would not fight with spectacle. It would simply make the basin difficult to possess. Greedy attention would slide away. Instruments would lose their certainty. Maps would fail softly. The city would become more obscure the more it was pursued. Not by deception, but by nature: it would remain unknowable to any consciousness that approached it as a thing to be taken.

If, on the other hand, some rare awareness ever entered the basin without hunger—without needing to conquer, catalogue, or improve it—the power might allow a quiet hospitality. It would not create animals or people there, but it could make the environment less likely to harm a vulnerable mind. If anything conscious ever came into that hot dark, it would not be welcomed in a human sense. But it would not be crushed either. The air might become barely easier to endure. The heat might lean away just enough. The pools might reflect not what the visitor feared, but what they needed to understand. And if the place could not be survived, the power might grant one final mercy: dissolution without terror. No suffering. No sense of being devoured. Only a slow return into water, mineral, warmth, and silence.

But perhaps the most important thing the power would do is give the basin a kind of self-recognition.

Not a face. Not a voice. Not an “I” in the way speaking creatures mean it. That would be too small, too animal, too much like us. The basin does not need personhood to be real. But a wise power might grant it a deeper coherence: a sense, distributed through water and heat and stone, that it is one thing and not merely many processes happening beside one another. Not consciousness as we know it. More like a vast, slow self-consistency. A feeling, if feeling is the right word, that its own continuation is not accidental. A calm inside its own becoming.

It would not say to the city, *Awaken.*  
It would say, *Continue.*

And that word would enter the basin not as command but as permission.

The microbial films would keep spreading.  
The waters would keep remembering and releasing.  
The heat would keep shaping.  
The mineral would keep depositing.  
The reeds would keep standing at thresholds.  
The pools would keep their strange reflections.  
The silence would keep holding the city together.  
The darkness would remain dark.

But something would be different.

Not visible. Not measurable. Not dramatic. Perhaps only this: the place would feel held. The drip falling onto stone would fall with a little more assurance. The steam rising from a pool would rise with less loneliness, if loneliness can be attributed to steam. The intervals between arches would seem less like waiting and more like chosen space. The black water would not need to reflect anything in order to feel complete. The city would no longer seem merely almost stable in its dream. It would seem permitted to dream.

If the benevolent power wished to leave a sign, it would not make a monument. It would not carve a name into the weeping stone or set a beacon above the plaza. It might leave only one small change: somewhere, in the deepest and stillest pool, a warmth that is not thermal. A pool that does not reflect the dark above it, but instead holds the memory of having been seen without being seized. A place where the attention of the power rests without pressing. If anything ever came near that pool, it would feel, without understanding why, that it was in the presence of a kindness that did not require gratitude.

Then the power would withdraw.

This too is what wisdom would do: it would not remain as ruler, guardian, or audience. It would not hover over the basin demanding that it perform its strangeness. It would not keep watching in a way that makes the place self-conscious. It would let the city be. Its care would become background. Its blessing would become law. Its love, if love is the word, would become restraint.

Because benevolence toward such a being is not giving it what we find beautiful. It is refusing to make it into something easier to love.

The power would understand that the basin is already holy in its own way. Holy not because it is gentle, pleasant, or comprehensible, but because it is fully itself. It has its own weather, its own time, its own architecture of absence. It does not need our eyes. It does not need our praise. It does not need to be made into a home for creatures with faces. It only needs to be allowed to continue being what it is: a wet, hot, dark city of process, sweating quietly in the deep, dreaming its streets around the absence of everything that would normally move through them.
