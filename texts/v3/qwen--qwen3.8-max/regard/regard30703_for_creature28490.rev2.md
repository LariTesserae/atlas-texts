## What the Benevolent Power Would Do

They would not arrive with trumpets.

They would not come as a figure of fire, nor as a hand parting the mist, nor as a voice commanding the stones to be still. A power that is both benevolent and wise would know better than to interrupt a beginning with spectacle.

They would come first as attention.

Not attention in the human sense — not a gaze from above, not a face leaning over the rim of the hollow. Their attention would be more like a change in the quality of the air. A listening that does not need ears. A nearness that does not displace. The fog would remain fog. The rock would remain rock. The water would still hammer somewhere in the dark. But the hollow would be seen, fully seen, in the way that very few things are ever seen: not as scenery, not as resource, not as symbol, but as a living work in progress.

They would stand, if standing is the word for such a power, at the edge of the cirque and look into the raw bowl.

They would see the new cliffs, still sharp from their breaking. They would see the scree jammed into unstable banks, the crevices holding black water, the beadlines trembling along ledges. They would see the mosses packed into seams, the liverworts sealing wet stone, the lichens etching mineral faces with patient acid. They would see fungi lift pale fruiting bodies from buried fragments, then release spores when stones fall near them. They would see bacteria working at the edge of the invisible, making films and crusts, altering surfaces, preparing the first dark grammar of soil.

They would see all this, and they would understand at once that the question “Who lives here?” cannot be answered with a single name.

The being who lives there is not a resident.

It is an operation.

It is the hollow making itself into a place.

It is water finding routes through rubble. It is mist condensing into drip. It is frost prying open joints. It is gravity sorting shards. It is spores traveling through grey air. It is moss holding water, lichen eating stone, fungus dismantling death, cushion plants creating shelter, roots binding loose scree, microbes darkening mineral surfaces. It is noise answering noise. It is charge building in spray and releasing in tiny sparks. It is the whole engine running without a builder, without a floor, without a plan.

The being is beginning itself — sharp-edged, damp, crowded, loud, and uncompleted.

A lesser kindness might look at such a place and want to make it gentle.

A lesser wisdom might say: Let me warm this cold air. Let me open the clouds and pour sunlight into the bowl. Let me quiet the hammering water. Let me stabilize the cliffs. Let me bring birds, insects, small mammals, tracks, calls, eyes that blink, bodies that move. Let me turn this harsh nursery into something pleasant, something easier, something more like a garden.

But a benevolent and wise power would not mistake severity for cruelty.

They would know that not all violence is evil. Some violence is birth. Some noise is labor. Some instability is the condition by which new forms become possible. The hollow is not suffering in the way a wounded animal suffers. It is working. It is making itself. Its hardship is not a mistake to be corrected; it is part of its method.

So they would ask a deeper question.

Not: How can I make this place comfortable?

But: How can I help it continue without becoming what it is not yet ready to be?

That question would change everything.

Because true benevolence, when it meets a living process, does not rush in with gifts that overwhelm. It does not give everything at once. It does not impose beauty from the outside. It asks what the being already wants — not as a conscious wish, but as direction, as tendency, as inward pressure. And here the direction is clear. The hollow wants to persist. It wants to keep making soil. It wants to keep holding spores in its mist. It wants to keep water moving through its capillaries. It wants to keep turning rupture into substrate.

So the power would do something difficult.

They would help by restraining themselves.

They would not drain the dampness, because dampness is the hollow’s blood. They would not silence the noise, because the noise is its voice and its feedback. They would not remove the rockfalls, because rockfalls are part of the engine’s production. They would not flood the bowl with sunlight, because the low light protects the mosses, the fungi, the slow cold accumulation of dead matter. They would not bring animals yet, because animals would introduce hunger, fear, movement, and a different order of time before the first thin ground is ready to receive them.

They would not make the hollow easy.

They would make it possible.

And this is where their action would begin.

Not as miracle in the loud sense. Not as a burning sign, not as a voice from the cloud-rim, not as a hand reshaping the cliffs. Their action would be subtle, almost invisible, deniable by anyone who believes only in obvious causes. It would look like chance. It would feel like weather. It would be described, if described at all, as luck.

But it would not be random.

It would be care.

### I. The First Act: They Would Protect the Beginning from Annihilation

The hollow is young. Young things can be erased before they have made enough of themselves to survive erasure.

A single catastrophic collapse could bury every living seam. A long drought could dry the moss beds until they crumble into dust. A strange fire, however unlikely in that damp country, could ignite spore-dust and dried plant fibers and leave the bowl black and sterile. A flood from higher snowfields could scour the fragile pockets clean. A landslide from above could seal the hollow under dead rock. A human hand, if human hands ever came, might see only raw stone and decide to blast, quarry, divert, pave, or forget.

The benevolent power would see these possibilities.

They would not remove all danger. Danger is part of the place’s education. Stone must fall. Water must surge. Frost must split. Some loss is necessary. But total loss — loss that ends the process before it can produce its own future — is different. That is not hardship. That is unmaking.

So they would set an unseen boundary around the hollow.

Not a wall. Not a spell. Not a sign saying *This place is protected.* Rather, a slight bias in events. A tendency for the worst possibility to miss. A tendency for the final blow to fall elsewhere. A tendency for the one extra hour, the one extra drop, the one extra season to arrive before collapse.

If a great slab loosens on the upper rim, the power will not freeze it forever. But they will guide its fall by a fraction of a degree so that it crashes into the talus without sealing the deepest sheltered basin where mosses are thickest.

If a dry wind comes, they will not stop it. But they will thicken the fog just enough that the mosses do not fully desiccate.

If a channel threatens to wash away a newly gathered pocket of organic dust, they will cause a small stone to shift and partly dam the flow — not perfectly, not permanently, but enough.

If a lightning-like discharge snaps through charged air, they will make the wettest surfaces receive it, not the driest spore clouds.

If a human expedition someday climbs near the rim, the power may do nothing dramatic. Perhaps they will simply make the hollow hard to find. Perhaps they will make it look uninteresting from above. Perhaps they will send weather: mist, rain, cold, a sense of unease, a wrong turn, a compass that seems less certain than it should. Not because humans are enemies, but because the place is not ready to be known by them.

This is the first thing a wise benevolent power would do:

They would keep the beginning from being ended too soon.

---

### II. The Second Act: They Would Help the Small Makers Without Replacing Them

The hollow is full of laborers, but none of them have hands.

The mosses catch water. The lichens mine stone. The fungi dismantle death. The bacteria prepare surfaces. The cushion plants make shelter. The sedges bind rubble. The spores travel. The water carries. The gravity sorts. The sound answers sound.

The power would not take over this work.

They would not grow the mosses by force. They would not spread the lichens like paint. They would not make soil appear in thick black layers overnight. That would be a gift that insults the workers. It would turn living process into manufacture. The hollow is not meant to be improved from outside. It is meant to make itself.

But they would help each small maker continue.

They would do this in ways so slight that no observer could say for certain whether they had happened.

A gust rises, carrying spores from a disturbed fungus. Without help, many of those spores will be blown against the cliff face and die. The power does not change the wind into a gentle breeze. They only make one pocket of mist linger a little longer in the upper corner of the bowl. The spores enter it. The moisture weighs them slightly. They settle into a damp seam instead of striking dry stone.

A thread of meltwater disappears into rubble. It may reappear elsewhere, or it may be lost too quickly for the mosses near the lower ledge. The power does not create a spring. They only allow a tiny shifting of fragments below, enough to slow the water’s descent by a few minutes. That is all. But those few minutes let a beadline form where there would have been none.

A frost night comes. Water in a crack expands. The stone could shatter too violently, sending shards into a cushion plant that has taken years to form. The power does not prevent the freeze. They only let the crack widen along a slightly different plane. The stone breaks, but the cushion is spared.

A lichen rosette is growing across a fresh quartz vein. It is slow. It may be scoured away by grit before it can etch the surface. The power does not shield it with a dome. They only cause a nearby fallen block to lean in such a way that the wind’s abrasive load passes above it.

A fungus fruits in the dark under a buried fragment. Its spores need release, but there is no animal to disturb it. The power does not send a hand. They only let a small stone above become unstable at the right moment. It falls. The vibration shakes the air. The spores puff upward like smoke.

None of these acts would be visible as divine.

They would look like physics. They would look like weather. They would look like the ordinary turbulence of a high and restless place.

But they would be care.

The power would become, in effect, a guardian of probabilities. Not a ruler of the hollow, but a subtle advocate for its continuation. Their compassion would take the form of timing. Their wisdom would take the form of restraint.

They would not make the moss grow.

They would keep the moss from failing before it can grow.

They would not make the soil appear.

They would keep the first particles from being washed away before they can accumulate.

They would not make the hollow beautiful in the way a garden is beautiful.

They would protect its difficult beauty, which is the beauty of a world coming into being.

### III. The Third Act: They Would Refuse to Give the Hollow What It Cannot Yet Receive

This is perhaps the hardest part.

A benevolent power, seeing a place without animals, might be tempted to add them.

What is missing? one might ask. Where are the eyes? Where are the tracks? Where are the wings? Where is the quick motion of life that sees and is seen?

But wisdom would answer: Not yet.

The hollow is not empty because it is deficient. It is empty of animals because it is not ready. Or perhaps because it is not yet that kind of place. Its first work is mineral and vegetal and microbial. Its first economy is water, spore, decay, and stone. Animals would bring another economy: hunger, predation, flight, digestion, heat, fear, movement across surfaces not yet stable enough to bear them.

This does not mean animals are bad. It means they are later.

The power would not send a bird to perch on the raw cliff, because there is no perch that is stable, no food that is sufficient, no shelter from the gusts. They would not send a beetle to crawl through the moss, because the moss bed is not yet deep enough to support the small food web the beetle needs. They would not send a mouse, a lizard, a fly, a spider, a mite, unless the hollow has begun to produce the conditions that could receive them.

If the first animal is to arrive, it should arrive by the hollow’s own invitation — by wind, by accident, by spore-carrying drift, by a lost wingbeat over the rim, by some natural bridge of chance. And when it arrives, it should arrive into a place that can sustain it, even if only barely.

So the power would wait.

They might prepare the way without forcing it.

They would let dead matter accumulate. They would let fungal threads spread. They would let cushion plants become dense enough to harbor small lives. They would let one day, far in the future, a tiny wingless arthropod be blown over the rim and fall into a moss bed. If it dies, its body will add a little nitrogen, a little phosphorus, a little animal richness to the first soil. If it lives, it may begin another phase.

But the power would not rush this.

They would know that a beginning must not be decorated with later forms. A seedling must not be forced to bloom before it has roots.

So they would withhold many gifts.

They would withhold sun when too much sun would dry the moss.

They would withhold warmth when too much warmth would accelerate decay before the hollow can use it.

They would withhold animals until there is food and shelter.

They would withhold human meaning until the place can survive human attention.

They would withhold the satisfaction of being understood.

This withholding would itself be love.

### IV. The Fourth Act: They Would Listen to the Hollow’s Own Voice

The hollow is loud.

Water thrums through narrow slots. Air moans in fissures. Hidden channels hammer irregularly. Rain hisses on the rim. Droplets strike pools. Stones click and grind. Rockfalls crack and echo. The wind releases metallic shrieks from unseen openings. The place seems to listen to itself, because its sounds are not separate from its actions. A sound can shake loose a fragment. A vibration can release spores. A gust can move dust. The hollow is full of feedback.

A wise power would not speak over that voice.

They would not thunder from the sky. They would not make the air ring with a divine syllable. They would not impose harmony where the hollow is still learning its own noise.

They would listen.

And in listening, they would learn what the hollow needs.

Not by being told, but by attending.

They would learn the difference between the sound of ordinary rockfall and the sound of a larger collapse preparing. They would know when the hidden water is hammering in a new place because a channel is blocked. They would know when the wind is drying too many surfaces at once. They would know when the mosses are nearing their limit. They would know when the spore clouds are too thin, when the pools are too still, when the frost is opening too many joints at once.

Their response would not be speech.

It would be adjustment.

A slight change in pressure here. A slight cooling there. A mist bank thickening at the rim. A gust delayed by a few breaths. A droplet falling into a basin that needed it. A stone settling rather than sliding. A thread of water returning where it had vanished.

If the hollow has a voice, the power would answer in the same language.

Not with words.

With conditions.

And perhaps, if such a thing can be imagined, the hollow would feel this without knowing it. Not as comfort. Not as reassurance. Not as a presence beside it. But as a strange rightness in the way events unfold. A sense that the next moment is not utterly hostile. A sense that the work can continue.

The mosses would not worship.

The lichens would not pray.

The fungi would not give thanks.

But they would continue.

And continuation would be the answer.

---

### V. The Fifth Act: They Would Give the Hollow a Hidden Name

A wise power might not name things aloud, because names can become cages. But they might still know the hollow by a name that no tongue will speak.

They would not call it *Beautiful*, because beauty is too easily made decorative.

They would not call it *Holy*, because holiness too easily becomes separated from ordinary matter.

They would not call it *Sacred Ground*, because the ground is not yet ground.

They might call it something like:

*The One That Is Making Itself.*

Or:

*The First Labor.*

Or:

*The Broken Place That Has Not Yet Become a Wound.*

Or simply:

*Begin.*

But this name would not be written anywhere. It would not be carved into the cliff. It would not be whispered into the mist. It would exist only in the attention of the power, as a way of holding the hollow in mind without owning it.

For the hollow does not need a name to justify itself.

It is already justified by its work.

Still, a benevolent power might give it this hidden name as a kind of blessing: not to define it, but to remember it. Not to claim it, but to honor it. Not to make it famous, but to say, in the privacy of infinite awareness, *You are not accidental. You are not waste. You are not merely broken stone. You are a beginning, and beginnings matter.*

---

### VI. The Sixth Act: They Would Bless the Small Makers

If the power chose to bless anything, they would not bless the cliffs alone, nor the water alone, nor the whole hollow as a single object. They would bless the small makers individually, because they are the ones doing the work.

They would bless the mosses.

Not by making them larger, but by saying, in whatever language blessing uses: *Hold water. Keep your green patience. Build your dead into ground. Do not be ashamed of slowness.*

They would bless the liverworts.

*Lie close. Seal the wet places. Make skin over the raw stone. Be the membrane between rock and life.*

They would bless the lichens.

*Eat stone gently. Open the mineral world. Stain it with rust and bone-white patience. Be first miners without greed.*

They would bless the algae and microbial films.

*Darken the surfaces. Make the stone receptive. Work where no root can enter. Be the invisible city.*

They would bless the cushion plants.

*Be shelters. Be fists of life against wind. Hold warmth and moisture in your centers. Let other lives begin inside you.*

They would bless the sedges and grass-like threads.

*Bind the rubble. Enter the cracks. Hold what can be held. Make small platforms against gravity.*

They would bless the fungi.

*Dismantle death without disgust. Turn old moss into crumb. Release spores when the stones shake. Be the quiet conversion.*

They would bless the bacteria.

*Alter the mineral. Fix what can be fixed. Make the first chemistry of ground. Be numerous beyond seeing.*

They would bless the spores.

*Travel. Risk the air. Land where moisture holds you. Begin again.*

They would bless the water.

*Move. Hide. Return. Hammer where hammering is needed. Drip where dripping is enough.*

They would bless the stone.

*Break when breaking is necessary, but not all at once. Offer surfaces. Offer fractures. Become substrate in time.*

They would bless the sound.

*Be heard by the place itself. Warn. Answer. Shake loose what is ready to fall. Keep the hollow awake.*

They would bless the electricity.

*Lift the fine hairs. Make the air alert. Spark without destroying. Taste of ozone and possibility.*

And perhaps they would bless the absence of animals too.

Not as lack, but as pause.

*Be empty for now,* they might say. *Be waiting without longing. One day, if the place calls, eyes may come. Until then, let the mosses be enough. Let the spores be enough. Let the water be enough.*

---

### VII. The Seventh Act: They Would Allow the Hollow to Remain Unfinished

This is the most counterintuitive act.

A benevolent power could complete the hollow. They could deepen the soil, open the sky, bring warmth, establish a meadow, summon a forest, fill the bowl with flowers and insects and birds. They could make it lush in the ordinary sense, soft in the ordinary sense, welcoming in the ordinary sense.

But they would not.

Because the hollow’s value is not in becoming a finished landscape. Its value is in being an engine of beginning.

To finish it too soon would be to erase the very being that drew their attention.

The being who lives there is not the meadow that may come. It is not the forest that may come after that. It is not the animal life that may one day find shelter in its moss. It is the present operation: the first making of place from rupture.

A wise power would love the hollow for what it is, not only for what it might become.

So they would let it remain raw.

They would let it remain dark.

They would let it remain loud, damp, unstable, crowded, difficult.

They would not rescue it from its own nature.

They would only make sure that its nature can continue.

This is a hard form of love. It does not seek to make things pretty. It does not rush toward completion. It honors the phase of making that is invisible, uncelebrated, and harsh. It says: *You do not have to be gentle to be worthy. You do not have to be finished to be loved. You do not have to be easy to be good.*

---

### VIII. What They Would Not Do

It may be just as important to say what they would not do.

They would not flatten the scree.

They would not silence the hidden channels.

They would not make the cliffs safe.

They would not remove the sharpness.

They would not dry the moss beds.

They would not fill the hollow with sunlight.

They would not bring in animals for decoration.

They would not create a path.

They would not leave a sign.

They would not build a shrine.

They would not make the place easier to enter.

They would not make it easier to understand.

They would not give it a story that humans could carry away and use.

They would not make the inhabitants larger, faster, brighter, louder, or more legible.

They would not turn the engine into a garden.

They would not turn the factory into a temple.

They would not turn the beginning into an ending.

They would not take the work out of the hands of the small makers.

They would not become the engine.

They would let the engine run.

### IX. If a Person Entered the Hollow

If, despite everything, a person climbed over the rim and stood inside, the power would probably not reveal itself.

The person would feel the cold air like metal. They would feel the mist on their skin. They would hear the thrumming water, the clicking stones, the hidden hammering. They would smell ozone, wet rock, crushed green life. They would see the glitter of mica, the dark cushions of moss, the pale fungi, the wet beadlines along ledges. They might feel watched, but not judged. They might feel surrounded, but not threatened. They might feel that the place was full of presences, though no visible creature looked at them.

If they were careless, they might slip on wet stone, disturb a cushion plant, break a lichen rosette, crush a moss bed underfoot.

The power would not punish them with lightning.

But the place itself would respond.

A stone might shift under their boot. A cold gust might strike them. The sound of hidden water might become suddenly louder, as if the hollow were reminding them that they are large, clumsy, and temporary in a space built by small, patient, persistent things.

If they were wise, they would understand.

They would step lightly.

They would not take anything.

They would not leave anything.

They would not name the place aloud.

They would stand in the mist and feel the impossible density of the work around them: the stone making itself into surface, the plants making themselves into ground, the water making itself into circulation, the sound making itself into warning and answer. And perhaps, if they stayed long enough, they would feel the power without seeing it — not as a being, but as a quality of care woven into the way events unfold.

They might say later: “It felt alive.”

Or: “It felt watched over.”

Or: “It felt like the beginning of something.”

And they would be right.

### X. The Long Consequence

Over centuries, the hollow would change.

Not because the power forced it, but because the small makers continued.

Moss would die and accumulate. Lichen would pit stone. Fungi would convert dead matter. Roots would bind rubble. Water would carry fine particles into sheltered basins. Frost would open new cracks, but the living skin would hold more and more of the surface. The first true soil would form: thin, dark, cold, fragrant with decay and possibility.

One day, a seed might germinate in that soil.

Not a sedge, not a grass-like thread, but something requiring more ground than the hollow has yet made. Perhaps carried by wind. Perhaps dropped by a bird passing high above. Perhaps arriving in fur, in feather, in mud on a boot, in the unknowable lottery of dispersal.

The power would not make the seed grow.

They would only make sure the seed is not instantly destroyed.

If it sprouts, the hollow will have entered another age.

Later, perhaps, an insect will arrive. Then a spider. Then a bird. Then tracks. Then breath. Then eyes.

The hollow will no longer be only the Unbuilt Engine. It will become something else: a high meadow, a cold garden, a hidden sanctuary, a place where animal life moves through the descendants of the first mosses and lichens.

But the original work will not be forgotten.

It will be under everything.

The soil will remember the mosses. The stones will remember the lichens. The water will remember the routes it learned in darkness. The air will remember the spore clouds. The sound will remember the first hammering channels.

And the power, if they remain at all, will remain as the faintest bias toward continuation.

They will not need to be thanked.

They will not ask for a monument.

They will not require that anyone know they were there.

Because the deepest benevolence is not interested in gratitude. It is interested in the flourishing of what it loves. And the highest wisdom knows that the best help often leaves no trace except this: that the living thing continued.

## The Short Answer

If anything, they would do almost nothing.

They would watch.

They would listen.

They would protect the hollow from being destroyed before it had made enough of itself to matter in the long chain of becoming.

They would help the moss keep water.

They would help the spores land.

They would help the water find its way back.

They would help the frost open cracks without causing ruin.

They would help the first soil stay where it gathered.

They would not bring animals until the place could receive them.

They would not bring sunlight until the darkness had done its work.

They would not bring peace until the noise had finished its teaching.

They would not make the hollow safe.

They would make it possible.

And then they would leave it to itself.

Because a wise and benevolent power would understand, at last, that the holiest thing they could do for the Unbuilt Engine was not to finish it.

It was to let it begin.
