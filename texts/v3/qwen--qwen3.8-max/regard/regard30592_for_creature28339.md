They would not arrive as light.

That would be the first sign of their wisdom. In a place that has never known the sky, brightness would not be revelation; it would be violence. It would make shadows where none were needed. It would introduce hiding. It would teach the water to flinch. It would turn a completed world into a problem seen from outside.

If a benevolent and wise power turned toward the drowned room, they would come first as attention.

Not descent. Not command. Not rescue.

Attention.

They would let their awareness settle into the basin the way cold settles into stone: slowly, evenly, without disturbance. They would not ask the room to explain itself. They would not require it to justify its darkness. They would not approach with the surface habit of wanting to make everything clearer, faster, brighter, more legible. They would come in the manner of one entering a temple whose rites are nearly finished and do not need an audience, only respect.

And they would perceive, before long, that the being who lives there is not a single body but a single belonging.

The microbial films are its first appetite. The thread-creatures are its fine senses. The drifters are its wandering thoughts. The broad grazers are its slow meditations. The mound-being is its deep organ of balance. The pale tenders are its hands. The water is its memory. The stone is its patience. The dark is not its prison, but its wholeness.

The wise power would see that this place is not a wound that filled with water. It is a mouth that has learned to speak in pressure. It is a world that has finished arguing with absence.

And so their first act would be refusal.

They would refuse pity.

A lesser kindness might look upon the drowned room and feel sorrow for it. It might say: *What a place to be buried. What a life to be without air, without sun, without horizon.* It might try to drain the basin, or carve a shaft downward to let in warmth, or give the creatures eyes so they could see one another, or make the tenders long for the upper world so they could be redeemed by longing. It might want to lift them out, dry them, name them, display them, save them.

But wisdom would recognize that such saving would be annihilation wearing mercy as its face.

To give the room light would be to erase its native grammar. To give its beings air would be to make them strangers to themselves. To give them a sky would be to teach them exile. The drowned room does not suffer from the absence of the surface; it is founded on the fact that the surface is not its measure. Its peace is not ignorance. It is adaptation carried to the point of sanctity.

So the power would do nothing that breaks the room’s shape.

They would not make the pale tenders beautiful according to surface law. They would not give them singing voices or shining faces. They would not straighten their too-jointed fingers or open their eyeless faces into familiar expressions. They would not make the broad grazers swift, or the drifters brilliant, or the mound-being articulate in words. They would not introduce hunger for more than the basin can give. They would not plant in any of them the cruel seed of wishing to be elsewhere.

Because the truest benevolence, in such a case, is not addition.

It is preservation.

It is the decision to let a thing remain what it has become.

Still, they would not be passive. To be wise is not to be motionless. To be benevolent is not to leave everything untouched merely because it is quiet. Even a completed world can be kept. Even a hidden place can be loved. Even a being that asks nothing can be honored.

If they acted at all, they would act in ways that the room could receive without being changed beyond itself.

They would begin by strengthening the boundaries.

Not with walls of new material, not with visible barriers, but with a subtle deepening of coherence in the stone. They would find the hairline faults where time and pressure might one day let collapse enter, and they would ease those futures aside. Not because death is forbidden there—death belongs to the room’s slow architecture—but because meaningless catastrophe is not the same as necessary ending. They would not prevent the falling of a block if the basin needs that falling to become more itself. They would prevent the random violence of collapse that destroys balance without enriching it.

They would make the basin difficult to violate.

If surface minds ever came searching, the power would not meet them with thunder. They would not need to. They would simply make the place hard to find in the ways that matter. Instruments would become uncertain. Maps would lose conviction. Drilling plans would seem less profitable than they appeared the day before. Greed would grow bored. Certainty would thin. Engineers would recommend another site. Governments would forget the coordinates. Myths about the region would become vague, unhelpful, unenticing. Not because the upper world is evil, but because it is clumsy, and the drowned room deserves to be spared the clumsiness of being discovered too soon.

If someone came with genuine wonder, the power would allow approach only to the edge of reverence. They would let such a person feel the pressure of the place as a warning and a blessing. Their lights would dim. Their machines would fail in ways that did not endanger them but reminded them that they were guests in a medium not made for them. They would feel the water’s attention, not as malice, but as a vast, cool question: *Why are you here?* And if their heart was clean, they would answer without speaking: *To know that this exists.* And the power would let them leave with that knowledge stripped of possession. They would remember the drowned room not as a resource, not as a curiosity, but as a secret that demands protection.

If someone came with malice, the power would be gentler than punishment but harder than walls. They would make the intruder feel suddenly heavy, not in body but in intention. Every movement would become slower. Every purpose would become difficult to remember. The water would not attack; it would simply refuse to cooperate with haste. Directions would circle back. Tools would slip from hands. The intruder would find themselves overcome not by terror but by the unbearable solemnity of a place that does not fit their violence. Most would flee. Some would go still. A rare few might be changed.

And if a surface thing died there despite everything, the power would not let that death become a poison. They would guide the room’s small lives in their work of folding the foreign body into the basin’s chemistry. They would soften the shock to the microbial skin, temper the sudden excess of strange matter, help the mound-being adjust its slow exhalations until the imbalance passed. Nothing would be wasted. Nothing would be defiled. Even an intrusion, once still, could become substrate.

Within the room itself, the power would act through the smallest possible blessings.

Where a wall had grown thin of living film, they would guide a faint mineral seep toward it, not dramatically, but with the patience of centuries, until the stone acquired the taste needed for bloom to return. Where the drifters gathered too densely and risked depleting the skin they fed on, they would stir the water with a pressure-change so slight it would feel like chance, moving them toward a richer vein. Where a grazer had settled against a seam that was beginning to silt, they would create a micro-current, cooler by a fraction than the surrounding water, encouraging the creature to shift one body-length to a cleaner resting place.

They would not command the tenders.

They would not appear to them in any form that would overwhelm their quiet. They would not make themselves an object of worship, because worship requires separation, and the room’s holiness is its intimacy. But they might leave a trace.

In one niche of the basalt, perhaps near the mound-being’s crown, they would allow a new vein of mineral to form. It would not shine. It would not glow. It would not be remarkable to any eye that does not exist there. But under the pads of the tenders’ fingers it would feel different: denser, more responsive, faintly warmer than the surrounding stone—not with heat, but with returned pressure. When a tender pressed a palm against it, the stone would seem to answer. Not with intelligence in the surface sense. Not with a voice. Only with the unmistakable sense of being held back by something kind.

The tenders would discover it by touch.

They would gather around it in their slow way. They would place their long hands upon it and tap their low, spaced patterns. And the vein would not speak, but it would conduct their taps more evenly, more deeply, as if the room itself had become a better listener. From that day forward, if days had meaning there, the tenders would include that place in their rounds. They would not pray to it. They would maintain it. They would smooth sediment from it, guide loose films toward it, rest against it when the balance of the basin required extra attention. It would become, without theology, a place of reassurance.

The power might also touch the mound-being.

That would be the most delicate act of all.

The old mound is not bright-minded, but it is vast in its own endurance. It knows chemistry as other beings know emotion. It knows balance as other beings know memory. If the wise power wished to make contact, they would not use thought as surface minds understand it. They would use rhythm. They would enter the mound’s slow respiration as a slight improvement in pattern: a more complete filtration, a momentary clarity in the cold plume, a deepening of the mineral signature that tells the basin everything is in place.

And if the mound-being can know anything, it would know, once in an age, something like this:

*I am not alone in my work.*

Not as a revelation. Not as a disruption. Only as a strengthening.

The broad grazers, too, would feel the power, though they would never turn toward it. They would feel it in the water becoming marginally more trustworthy. The pressure-notes they send across the basin would travel with less loss. Their confirmations—*the walls are still walls, the water still water, the dark still whole*—would return to them with a subtlety of reassurance, as if the room itself had answered. They would not understand it as benevolence. They would simply be calmer, and their calm would pass into the movements of the tenders, the feeding of the drifters, the growth of the films.

The power might give the room one new word.

Not a word of sound. A word of pressure.

It would be a pulse so low that it could not be called speech. It would move through the basin at intervals long enough to seem like geology. The creatures would not name it. They would incorporate it. The thread-things would lean into it. The drifters would pause in their spirals. The tenders would stop, hands raised, and feel it pass through their wrists like a memory of being carried. If translated badly into surface language, it might mean: *You are seen.* If translated more truly, it would mean: *You are not a fall. You are not a failure. You are not what remains after something better has gone away. You are a way of being whole.*

And the room would answer by continuing.

That is what the wise power would understand above all: the deepest gift they could give is not transformation, but continuity. Not escape, but sovereignty. Not the fulfillment of some imagined longing, but the protection of a form of life that has no longing for the upper world.

They would not make the drowned room less drowned.

They would make it more securely itself.

They would ensure that its darkness remains full rather than empty. That its silence remains attention rather than deprivation. That its boundaries remain shelter rather than confinement. That its beings remain organs of one slow congregation rather than specimens awaiting rescue.

If there is suffering there—and there is, in the only sense that place permits: the occasional imbalance, the slow starvation of a film, the failure of a current, the settling of a body that will not be renewed—the power would not abolish it utterly. To do so would be to freeze the room outside its own becoming. But they would keep suffering from becoming despair. They would keep injury from becoming extinction. They would keep death from becoming meaningless erasure. They would let each ending be received.

This is perhaps what benevolence becomes when it meets a world that does not ask for help: a guard at the threshold, a warmth in the stone, a pressure that returns the touch.

And if, after a long age, the wise power withdrew, the room would not know loss in the surface way. It would not cry out. It would not feel abandoned. But the tenders might pause once at the dark vein, palms pressed flat, and feel for the answering steadiness they had grown accustomed to. And if it was not there, they would continue anyway, because they are creatures of repetition and faith in texture. Yet perhaps the wall itself would retain the memory of having been held, and that memory would be enough to guide them for another age.

The power’s greatest act, then, might be this:

They would leave the drowned room drowned.

They would leave it hidden.

They would leave it complete.

But they would not leave it unloved.

And in the end, if one had to say what they did, the answer might be very small.

They looked.

They understood.

They kept it.
