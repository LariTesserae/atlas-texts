## The Attention of a Benevolent Power

If the Root-Town Under the High Tarn, and the being who lives there, drew the attention of a benevolent and wise power, I do not think that power would arrive as spectacle.

It would not split the surface with light.  
It would not descend through the water in a shape so large that the fish scattered into silence.  
It would not speak from the sky in a voice that flattened all other voices.  
It would not send a prophet, a king, a shining servant, or a law carved in stone.

It would first be quiet.

Its first act would be attention.

A truly wise power would understand that the Root-Town is not a problem to be solved, nor a ruin to be restored, nor a curiosity to be admired, nor a pet to be preserved under glass. It is a way of being that has already answered its own conditions. It has made a covenant with water, cold, slowness, pressure, and sound. To interfere carelessly would be to break the very thing that makes it valuable.

So the power would begin by listening.

It would listen from above, where the wind moves across the tarn and the surface brightens. It would listen from the margins, where frogs call through reed shadows. It would listen through the air-wells, where trapped breath waits under pitch-dark domes. It would listen along the root curtains, where water-hair moves in the current. It would listen in the hollow stems, the stone collars, the fish courts, the quiet lanes, and the green central hush where the town’s Under-Voice never fully ceases.

It would hear the place not as a collection of objects but as a single slow life made of many lives.

The moss would seem to it like skin.  
The cord-roots would seem like bones and sinew.  
The channels would seem like veins.  
The fish would seem like quick thoughts.  
The mussels would seem like patient organs.  
The birds would seem like weather passing through.  
The Rootbound would seem like hands, but hands that know they belong to the body they serve.  
The sounds would seem like memory, warning, speech, and breath.

And if the power asked, “Who lives here?” it would soon understand that the answer is not one creature. The being of the Root-Town is not a single body with a single mouth. It is a commonwealth. It is a town-as-organism. It is a balance that has become a presence. If it has a voice, that voice is the Under-Voice: the creak and throb of living architecture adjusting to water, season, pressure, and time.

A benevolent power would not force that being to speak in words it does not own.

It would not demand prayer.  
It would not require gratitude.  
It would not ask to be named.  
It would not make the Rootbound build a temple, because a temple would be too loud for the quiet lanes and too heavy for the root-floor.  
It would not give them empire, because empire would destroy them.  
It would not give them greatness, because their greatness already lies in restraint.

Instead, the power would ask a quieter question:

*What does this place need in order to remain itself?*

And because the place is already wise in its own way, the power would not answer that question alone. It would wait for the town to answer.

The answer would come in small signs.

A mussel would open slowly in a clean current.  
A shoal of fish would rise without panic.  
A sluice pipe would sound its low note without strain.  
A root curtain would lean in the current as if untroubled.  
A mossbinder would press a loosened edge back into place, and it would hold.  
An elder Rootbound would pause at an air-well, listen, and nod.

The answer would be something like:

*Let us continue.*

Not forever. Nothing in the Root-Town asks for forever. Its people know that channels shift, roots age, storms tear curtains, seasons rise and fall. They do not seek escape from time. They seek the dignity of continuing within it.

So the benevolent power would do what a wise guardian does for a living thing that does not belong to them:

They would protect it without possessing it.  
They would strengthen it without changing its nature.  
They would help it without making it helpless.  
They would be present without becoming visible.  
They would act, but mostly in ways that feel like luck, weather, instinct, memory, and care.

## What the Power Would Refuse

Before saying what such a power would do, it is worth saying what they would not do.

They would not drain the tarn to reveal the town.

They would not warm the water so that the Rootbound could work longer and suffer less. The cold is part of the town’s discipline. It slows haste. It preserves. It teaches the body to move carefully. To remove it entirely would be to remove one of the town’s oldest teachers.

They would not give the Rootbound metal tools, even perfect ones. Metal would shorten their labor, but it would also change their relationship to the roots, the stems, the stone, and one another. Their craft is not efficiency. It is attention. Shell, bone, slate, antler, woven fiber, and patient pressure are not inferior methods. They are forms of respect.

They would not give them gills.

This may seem strange, because gills would make the water easier to inhabit. But the Rootbound are not meant to become water-creatures in the way fish are water-creatures. Their humanity matters. Their breath matters. The air-wells matter because they are places of return, pause, and dependence. Their dives are acts of skill and humility. If they became effortless, something essential would be lost.

They would not make the Rootbound numerous.

The town is sparse by design. Its ecology cannot bear crowds. Its sounds cannot bear too many careless movements. Its lanes cannot bear too many bodies. The power would understand that a place can be flourishing without being full. A wise power does not confuse abundance with good. Sometimes the highest form of blessing is enough.

They would not make the town famous.

Fame would bring visitors. Visitors would bring curiosity. Curiosity would bring noise, greed, measurement, theft, and the desire to own or explain. The Root-Town would become a wonder, then a resource, then a possession. A benevolent power would hide it gently, not because secrecy is cruel, but because some places are too delicate to survive being widely known.

They would not speak a commandment.

The Rootbound already have law. Their law is lived: what falls is gathered; what gathers is bound; what binds becomes ground. A power from outside would not write new rules upon their hearts. It would not disturb their listening circles with divine decrees. It would not turn their quiet practices into obedience to a voice they cannot answer.

They would not end all danger.

This is perhaps the hardest thing to accept. A benevolent power, if it is truly wise, knows that a world without risk is not a world. The Rootbound must still learn stillness. Children must still be taught not to chase crayfish. Sluice-listeners must still attend to altered tones. Mossbinders must still notice erosion. Root-guides must still repair what storms have torn. If every threat were removed, the town would become a museum, not a life.

So the power would not abolish danger. It would prevent annihilation.

There is a difference.

## What the Power Would Do

The power would act at the edges.

It would become a keeper of margins, a tender of probabilities, a quiet adjuster of what might otherwise tip into harm. It would not be the town’s author. It would be more like the climate of its flourishing.

### It would bless the water

First, it would watch over the water.

The Root-Town lives because the tarn is clear, cool, fed, and patient. The power would not turn it into a river or a sea. It would keep it exactly what it is: a high mountain pool, weather-fed, mist-fed, snow-fed, rain-fed.

In dry seasons, when the level falls too low, the power would draw more mist over the rim. It would make the cushion plants along the shore hold moisture longer. It would guide night condensation into the mosses. It would slow the evaporation with cooler evenings and shaded wind.

In heat, it would not make the water cold beyond its nature, but it would prevent the shallows from becoming weak. It would keep enough shadow in the channels. It would protect the places where cool water pools under root arches, so that fish can retreat and mussels need not close in distress.

In storms, it would not stop the wind, but it would keep the storm from becoming a tearing thing. It would let the root-baffles do their work. It would guide the heaviest currents away from the oldest woven walls. If a stone vane were likely to crack, the power might make the sediment beneath it settle just enough to prevent a fatal shift. Not a miracle. Only a small kindness hidden inside physics.

In winter, if ice forms, the power would ensure that the water beneath remains breathable and resonant. It would not prevent ice. Ice is part of the season. But it would keep the deep channels from becoming sealed so tightly that the town’s sounds turn harmful or its life is smothered.

The blessing would be simple:

*Water, remain clear enough to see.  
Water, remain cool enough to preserve.  
Water, remain moving enough to speak.*

### It would strengthen the roots

The power would pay special attention to the cord-roots.

They are the town’s living beams, its buried architecture, its old remembered strength. A wise power would know that if the roots fail, everything else follows.

It would not make the roots grow quickly. Quick growth would be coarse growth, and coarse growth would betray the town’s patience. Instead, it would give the roots persistence. It would help them find cracks in stone without breaking the stone violently. It would help old braided masses hold their shape through changing currents. It would let injured root-hair regrow over seasons, not hours, because healing at the proper pace is part of the town’s dignity.

When a root-guide bends a stem and ties it into a new direction, the power might give that stem a little extra willingness to live. Not obedience. Willingness. The difference matters. The root would still be a root. It would still grow according to its own nature. But it would be less likely to sulk, rot, or fail where it has been asked into a new shape.

When an ancient cord-root reaches the end of its life, the power would not preserve it unnaturally. It would guide the Rootbound to notice the weakness in time. It would help them plant and bind a successor before the old anchor is removed, according to their own law. It would make the transition feel not like loss but like inheritance.

The blessing to the roots would be:

*Hold what should be held.  
Yield where yielding is needed.  
Remember the shape of the town, but do not become brittle.*

### It would care for the moss

The moss would not escape the power’s attention, though it seems humble.

A benevolent power would understand that the moss lawns are not decoration. They are ground, filter, skin, and peace. They soften the town. They catch sediment. They make the place feel settled rather than raw.

The power would help torn mats heal. Not instantly. A bare patch after a careless footfall should remain visible long enough to teach care. But it would not remain forever. The power would encourage fine threads to spread, new cushions to rise, silt to settle gently over the wound. It would make the moss forgiving enough to recover, but not so indifferent to injury that the Rootbound forget their responsibility.

It would also keep the moss from being smothered. If too much sediment drifts into a terrace, the power might guide a current to lift the excess into a baffle where mossbinders can gather it. If algae begins to darken a lawn, it might encourage more snails or a slight change in flow. Nothing dramatic. Only the subtle rebalancing of a living floor.

The blessing to the moss would be:

*Be soft enough to heal.  
Be strong enough to hold.  
Be patient enough to teach.*

### It would keep the sounds true

Because the Root-Town lives through sound, a wise power would treat sound as sacred infrastructure.

It would not add new voices that overwhelm the existing ones. It would not make the pipes sing with impossible beauty. It would not give the town music that does not belong to it. It would simply ensure that what already speaks remains true.

The hollow reed pipes would be protected from cracking. The stone collars would be kept from shifting out of tune. The sluice tones would remain distinguishable: one gate from another, one warning from another, one fish-call from another. If a pipe began to sound dull from silt, the power might make a sluice-listener pass by at the right moment, pause, and notice.

The quiet lanes would be especially protected.

A wise power would understand that silence is not absence. It is a room for listening. It would keep careless noise from invading those lanes. Not by punishing noise, but by shaping circumstance. A hurried diver might suddenly remember a rule. A falling tool might be caught by current before it strikes stone. A bird might choose another part of the surface that day.

In the Under-Voice, the power would not add itself as a shout. But perhaps it would add the faintest harmonic of presence: not a new sound, exactly, but a depth in the existing sound. A sense, if one were very still, that the town is not alone. That it is heard. That its creaking and throbbing are not merely mechanical but meaningful.

The blessing to sound would be:

*Carry warning without panic.  
Carry memory without weight.  
Carry the town to itself.*

### It would tend the air-wells

The air-wells are among the most vulnerable and vital structures. They are places where human life intersects with the submerged town. They are breathing points, meeting points, refuges.

The power would keep their seals sound. It would prevent resin from souring too quickly. It would keep reed valves from clogging. It would let tiny leaks be heard before they become dangerous.

If a diver were caught in a strong current or disoriented by drifting motes, the power would not necessarily rescue them with a miracle. That would rob them of skill and terror would become cheap. But it might guide them toward the nearest air-well by sound. It might make one pipe tone seem clearer than another. It might make a bubble stream rise in a familiar pattern. It might give the diver enough calm to remember what they already know.

The blessing to the air-wells would be:

*Hold breath kindly.  
Be found when sought.  
Be enough.*

### It would guide the Rootbound without ruling them

The human tenders would draw the power’s compassion, because they are the part of the town that can choose.

Plants grow according to nature. Fish move according to instinct and current. Birds come and go according to hunger and season. But the Rootbound can decide. They can choose haste or care. They can choose greed or restraint. They can choose pride or listening. A wise power would not override their choices, but it would quietly support their better instincts.

It would give them slow hands.

Not clumsy hands. Slow as in deliberate. Slow as in respectful. Slow as in willing to spend years training a root rather than cutting it in an afternoon.

It would give them good lungs, but not supernatural ones. Their breath should remain a limit, because limits teach reverence. It would give them enough endurance to work safely, enough sensitivity to hear changes in the town, enough warmth in their oiled skins to avoid being numbed into carelessness.

It would strengthen their memory.

The knot-keepers especially would be cared for. Their cord-records are the town’s library, but they are fragile in the way all physical memory is fragile. The power would not make the cords indestructible. It would help the keepers’ fingers remain accurate. It would help the dyed threads, shell beads, toggles, and knot spacings retain their meaning across generations. It would encourage apprentices to ask questions. It would give elders patience to answer.

It would send dreams, perhaps, but rarely and never with commands.

A sluice-listener might dream of a blocked collar and wake with a concern they cannot explain. A mossbinder might dream of a particular weave and discover it works better than the old pattern. A root-guide might dream of an ancestor’s hands bending a stem, and feel less discouraged by a task that will take decades. A child afraid of the cold water might dream of floating safely in green light, and wake with enough courage to learn stillness.

These dreams would not be proof of the power. They would feel like intuition. That is how a wise power prefers to work when dealing with free beings.

The blessing to the Rootbound would be:

*Be careful without becoming fearful.  
Be reverent without becoming passive.  
Be few, but not lonely.  
Be human, but not masters.*

### It would protect the town from cruel outsiders

If outsiders came with arrogance, the power would not necessarily destroy them. Destruction is easy and often unwise. Instead, it would make the mountain difficult.

A surveyor with greedy eyes might find the paths uncertain. The mist might thicken at the wrong moment. The rim might look less inviting than expected. The tarn might seem ordinary, cold, uninteresting. Their instruments might fail in small, irritating ways. Their certainty might leak away. They might feel watched, not by a threatening presence, but by the patience of the place itself.

If someone came to take, the power would make taking awkward. Nets would tangle. Boats would ground. Tools would slip. The water would feel too cold, too clear, too loud, too resistant. The visitor would begin to feel that the place was not refusing them with malice, but simply not yielding to them.

If someone came with true humility, the power might allow them to glimpse something. Not everything. Perhaps only the edge of the drowned plaza, or a fish passing through a root arch, or a distant tone from the sluice pipes. They would leave changed, but unable to prove what they had seen. They would remember it as a dream, a moment of strange clarity, or a lesson in reverence.

The power would not make the Root-Town a secret because truth should be hidden forever. It would keep it obscure because fragility deserves protection.

The blessing against harm would be:

*Let the greedy find no purchase.  
Let the careless become uncertain.  
Let the humble find enough.*

### It would respond to injury, not erase it

Sometimes harm would still happen.

A storm might tear away a curtain of root-hair.  
A spring flood might shift a gate.  
A diver might bruise a moss lawn.  
A crayfish might lose a limb.  
A fish court might fill with too much silt.  
A mussel bed might close for too long.

The power would not pretend injury did not occur. It would not instantly undo every wound. That would make the town childish, dependent, and incapable of repair. Instead, it would make injury answerable.

It would ensure that the Rootbound notice.

A changed tone would reach the right ears. A disturbed current would attract the right eyes. A torn wall would be found before it worsened. The community would gather its attention, and repair would begin.

The power would aid the repair by making materials available. It might guide drifting seeds into the right crevice. It might let silt settle where it can be woven rather than where it chokes. It might help young roots take hold near damaged knots. It might give the workers calm.

In this way, the town would not be spared suffering, but it would not be abandoned in suffering.

The blessing to injury would be:

*Be visible before you become fatal.  
Be healed by hands that understand you.  
Leave the place wiser, not merely older.*

### It would honor death

A wise and benevolent power would not grant the Rootbound immortality. Immortality would eventually burden them. Their town is shaped by patience, seasons, and succession. If nothing ended, nothing would need to be remembered. Nothing would need to be passed on.

But the power would make death gentle when it could.

When an old Rootbound dies, the town might feel it in a small way. Not an omen of terror, but a change in sound. The Under-Voice might deepen for a day. The fish might move differently in the central channel. A bubble might rise through a shaft of light where no diver has been. The knot-keepers might add a cord, or touch an old one.

When an old cord-root dies, it would not be treated as failure. The power would help the town remember it through use, through story, through the living structure it trained into being. Its removal, if removal is necessary, would be done with the planting of another. Its absence would become a space of respect.

When an animal dies, the town would continue its circulation. Crayfish would clean the margins. Fish would take what they need. The current would carry the matter into life elsewhere. Nothing would be wasted. The power would ensure that death in the Root-Town is not obscene. It would remain part of the metabolism.

The blessing to death would be:

*Come in season, not before it.  
Be met with care.  
Become ground for what follows.*

## The Sign the Power Might Leave

If the power left a sign at all, it would be small enough to be missed by the careless and unmistakable to the attentive.

It would not write words in the water.

Perhaps, once in a long while, a diver would enter a quiet lane and hear not a new sound, but a deepening of silence. A sense that the water itself has become slightly more present. Not crowded. Not watched in a threatening way. Accompanied.

Perhaps one of the air-wells would hold air a little longer than expected during a difficult dive, and the diver would surface later with enough breath to laugh softly in astonishment.

Perhaps a sluice pipe would produce a tone so perfectly tuned that the sluice-listener would stop, hand still on the gate, and feel as though the town had just spoken a complete sentence.

Perhaps a child learning stillness would see a bubble rise in a spiral from an old root and feel, without knowing why, that the bubble was a greeting.

Perhaps the Rootbound would develop a name for this sign, though they would not use it lightly. They might call it the Kind Turn, or the Second Breath, or the Unseen Hand, or the Listener at the Gate. They would not build a shrine around it. They might simply leave one extra knot in a cord-record: a knot that means “helped, but not by us.”

A wise power would prefer that.

A sign too great would become tyranny. A sign too small preserves freedom. The town should be able to interpret the blessing as its own good fortune, its own skill, its own ancestors, or its own balance. The power would not require credit. It would not need worship. It would be content to be misunderstood as weather, as long as the town remained safe.

## The Meeting with the Being

At some point, if such a thing can be imagined, the power would meet the being of the Root-Town directly.

Not as a person meeting a person. Not as a god summoning a soul. But as attention meeting pattern.

It might happen at night, when the surface is dark and the water is blue-gray and still. It might happen in the central hall, where light falls in green-white shafts by day and memory seems to gather by night. The power would not appear. It would simply become more intensely present in the water.

It would touch the oldest cord-root without touching it.

It would listen to the Under-Voice until it understood the full grammar of the town: which sounds mean health, which mean strain, which mean season, which mean warning, which mean sleep.

Then, in the way such powers might ask without speaking, it would ask:

*Do you consent to be kept?*

The town would answer.

It would answer with the closing of a mussel.  
With the turning of a shoal.  
With the creak of a root arch.  
With the hollow note of a pipe.  
With the click of a crayfish in the dark.  
With the breath of a diver at an air-well.  
With moss holding sediment.  
With water moving through a gate.

The answer would not be “Yes” in any simple sense. It would be more like:

*Keep the conditions.  
Do not keep us as a thing kept in a box.  
Do not make us safe from becoming.  
Do not take our work from us.  
Do not make us beautiful for another’s eyes.  
If you love us, love us as we are: slow, cool, loud in our own way, sparse, rooted, patient.*

And the power would answer:

*So I will keep you.*

Not with ownership.  
Not with display.  
Not with permanence.  
But with fidelity.

Its vow would be something like this:

*I will not make you other than you are.  
I will not save you from time, but I will defend you from violation.  
I will not remove your dangers, but I will keep them from becoming despair.  
I will not give you what would ruin you by answering a wish you have not made.  
I will give you what you need to remain yourselves.  
What falls, I will help gather.  
What gathers, I will help bind.  
What binds, I will help become ground.*

## Why Such a Power Would Do So Little

Someone might ask: if the power is benevolent and wise, why not do more?

Why not make the town vast and glorious?  
Why not make the Rootbound immortal?  
Why not give them mastery over the mountain?  
Why not reveal itself and end all doubt?  
Why not make the world kneel before such a wonder?

Because wisdom knows that more is not always better.

A town made vast would lose its monastic balance.  
A people made immortal would lose the seriousness of repair.  
A place made famous would lose its silence.  
A revelation too clear would overwhelm the freedom of those who receive it.  
A wonder forced upon the world would become an idol, a possession, a proof, a weapon.

The Root-Town’s value is not that it is powerful. Its value is that it is whole. Its value is that it has learned to live with water instead of against it, with growth instead of extraction, with sound instead of noise, with restraint instead of domination.

If a benevolent power truly loves such a place, it will not turn it into something easier to admire. It will protect the difficult, delicate terms by which the place exists.

The power’s greatest act may be restraint.

It may choose to be almost invisible so that the town can remain itself. It may choose to be mistaken for luck, for nature, for ancestry, for quiet skill. It may accept never being thanked by name. It may love without needing to be loved back.

That is a higher form of benevolence than miracle.

Miracle astonishes.  
Wisdom preserves.

## What Would Remain

After the power had looked upon the Root-Town, after it had listened and decided and quietly acted, the place would seem much the same from above.

The tarn would still lie in its mountain hollow.  
The wind would still ripple the surface.  
The mosses would still trail over the rim.  
The water would still be clear and cool.  
The dark shapes below would still be visible only if one knew how to look.

Inside, the town would continue.

The sluice-listeners would tend the pipes.  
The mossbinders would press terraces into place.  
The root-guides would begin bends that would not be finished in their lifetimes.  
The air-well keepers would test valves and seals.  
The knot-keepers would touch their cords in dim water.  
The fish would drum in breeding courts.  
The mussels would open and close.  
The crayfish would click in the root-shadow.  
The birds would fall through the surface and vanish into green streets.  
The Under-Voice would creak and throb, never quite silent.

But there would be a difference, if one had ears deep enough to notice.

The water would feel less accidental.  
The silence in the quiet lanes would feel less empty.  
The repairs would seem guided by a memory larger than the hands performing them.  
The town would seem, in its cool and resonant solitude, not abandoned by the world but held within something kind.

The being who lives there—the commonwealth, the town-as-one, the slow green body of root and sound—would feel it not as a command from above, but as a steadiness beneath.

It would continue to live as it always had:

by filtering,  
binding,  
grazing,  
sounding,  
pruning,  
listening,  
and being listened to.

And perhaps that is what a benevolent and wise power would do, above all:

Not rule the Root-Town.  
Not remake it.  
Not possess it.  
Not display it.

Only attend to it with such care that the town could remain exactly what it is:

a small drowned urban grove at the roof of the world,  
cool and loud,  
sparse and organized,  
primitive in tools and ancient in balance,  
hidden,  
living,  
and kept.
