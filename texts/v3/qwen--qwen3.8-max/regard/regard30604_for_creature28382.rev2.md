If such a power noticed that place, they would not begin by changing it.

They would not come down as fire, or voice, or a hand large enough to press the basin flat into something easier to love. They would not make green things burst from the mineral crust. They would not fill the pools with fish, or send birds wheeling into the bright cold air, or lay warm soil over the shining stone. They would not repair the horizon so that it behaved like every other horizon, or silence the water so the place became peaceful in the ordinary sense. A lesser benevolence might try to make the basin familiar. A wise one would understand that familiarity could be a kind of violence.

They would first listen.

They would arrive, if they arrived at all, as a pause between one water-sound and another. A moment when a drop hung on a stone lip and did not fall. A moment when the wind passed through a hollow rock and produced no note, only a clean, attentive hush. A moment when a pool’s reflection steadied, not because the sky had become still, but because something had begun to listen through the water.

They would perceive at once that the being who lives there is not a single creature in the way lowland stories understand creatures. It is not a body with edges. It is not a soul wrapped in skin. It is a many-bodied pattern: water remembering its old paths, mineral skin sealing the past, sound answering itself across distances, reflections leaning into neighboring truths, stone presences forcing all movement to become deference, and beneath all of it, the heart-terrace calibrating the whole impossible world.

The being is the basin’s balance.

And the wise power would not ask, in any crude way, *What are you?*

They would ask, in the only manner suitable to such a place: *How do you wish to be met?*

The basin would not answer in words. It would answer as it answers everything: through water. A thread would climb a stone face. A spring would breathe once, twice, and go still. A sound would arrive after its cause, as if the answer had taken the long way around the truth. A reflection would show the basin as it is now, bright and weeping, and then as it might be if touched: the same, but held.

The answer would be something like:

*Do not make me ordinary.  
Do not heal me into one shape.  
Do not take away the wrongness that has become my peace.  
If you love me, love me as I am: many, loud, wet, broken, balanced.*

Then the benevolent power would decide what to do.

And because they are wise, they would do almost nothing.

But “almost nothing” would not be nothing.

---

They would begin by refusing to impose the appetites of other worlds.

They would not give the basin what it does not need. They would not set trees on the terraces, because the place is already full of branching forms made by mineral patience. They would not introduce beasts, because the stone presences already create the discipline of movement, and the sound-folk already populate the air. They would not make the air sweet with blossom, because the clean mineral smell of cold water, wet stone, salt, and flint is already the basin’s own breath. They would not warm the ground, because the coolness is part of its clarity. They would not turn the loudness into a gentle murmur, because the loudness is how the place thinks.

This would be their first blessing: restraint.

For a power capable of miracles, restraint is not absence. It is choice.

They would recognize that the basin is not a failed garden. It is not a dead country waiting for life to be poured into it. It is another order of completeness. Its lack of green is not poverty. Its lack of animal tracks is not loneliness. Its crowded stone forms, unreliable reflections, and impossible weather are not wounds demanding closure. They are the features of a world whose laws are broader than ordinary ground.

So the wise power would not save the basin from itself.

They would protect it from being saved by force.

---

Then they would enter more closely.

Not as a ruler. Not as a judge. Not even, perhaps, as a guest, because the basin has no door and no hearth. They would enter as a change in the quality of attention.

The air would not brighten. The water would not suddenly become still. The stone presences would not bow. But something in the basin would become more present to itself. The dripping would seem, for a time, more deliberate. The reflections would clarify. The spaces between echoes would widen by the smallest degree, as if the whole place had been given room inside its own noise.

The power would go to the heart-terrace.

That shallow dark flood, where water comes in from many directions and forms standing waves around circular still points, is the nearest thing the basin has to a center. It is where the loudness is strongest. It is where the half-drowned rings divide the water into voices. It is where the vanished builders’ work and the basin’s own mineral patience are hardest to tell apart.

There the power would do their first actual work.

They would make one new ring.

It would be very small. Low enough to lie beneath the hand-depth water. Regular enough to calm a current, rough enough to look ancient. They would not inscribe it with a sigil. They would not make it shine more brightly than the others. They would not set it apart as divine. They would place it exactly where the braided currents needed one more division in order to remain braided instead of becoming chaos.

Mineral skin would begin to cover it almost at once. Within a season, or a century, or whatever time means in such a place, it would be difficult to say whether the ring had been cut by vanished hands, grown by water, or placed by something greater.

That ambiguity would please the power.

They would not want worship there. Worship would be too loud, too narrow, too hungry for the wrong kind of presence. What they would want is continuation.

---

They would speak, if speaking can be called it, to the lives of the basin one by one.

To the water-life, they would say:

*Remember without drowning.  
Return without eroding.  
Carry the past without burying the present.  
Let your old paths remain as paths, not chains.  
Where you must forget, forget gently.*

To the mineral skins, they would say:

*Preserve what is placed in you, but do not seal every truth away.  
Let the grooves of old hands remain, but let them also become stone.  
Keep the memory of the vanished builders without turning it into a prison.  
Be bright where brightness is needed. Be hard where hardness protects.*

To the sound-folk, they would say:

*Answer, but do not confuse.  
Repeat, but do not mock.  
Take the sounds of those who enter, but return them with mercy.  
Let the basin’s loudness remain many, but never let it become cruelty.  
When a traveler speaks, give their words back to them in a form that helps them listen.*

To the reflection-folk, they would say:

*Show neighboring truths, but do not steal the traveler’s footing.  
Let the pools reveal other weathers, other ages, other possibilities, but do not make the living desire the reflection more than the ground.  
If someone looks too long, warn them with beauty, not with hunger.  
Be a door of attention, not a trap.*

To the stone presences, they would say:

*Stand as you stand. Bend paths without closing them. Open views without exposing the careless to despair. Let shadows lean, but do not let them lie. Keep the basin crowded with meaning, but not crowded with malice.*

To the memory of the vanished builders, they would say:

*Remain.  
Not as ghosts. Not as masters. Not as owners.  
Remain as caution. Remain as craft. Remain as the knowledge that some places are served by small adjustments, not by conquest.  
Let your channels clear themselves when silt gathers. Let your listening hollows still divide sound. Let your markers stand where distances fold. Let your absence continue to teach.*

And to the heart-life, they would say:

*You do not need to hold all of this alone.*

That, perhaps, would be the deepest act.

---

The heart-life of the basin is not suffering in the way flesh suffers. It has no nerves, no fear, no private grief. But there is a strain in any pattern that must balance several possible truths at once. There is a tension in a world where water remembers paths geometry denies, where reflections open into neighboring states, where horizons curve wrongly, where distances fold and sounds arrive before their causes. The basin has settled into its old wrongness, yes. It is weathered into equilibrium. But equilibrium is not the same as ease.

So the wise power would not remove that strain. They would share it.

They would let the basin feel, in whatever way a basin can feel, that it is known. Not observed from outside. Not catalogued. Not admired as a curiosity. Known.

Known as a place where water is intention.  
Known as a place where sound is population.  
Known as a place where reflection is neighborhood.  
Known as a place where stone is society.  
Known as a place where vanished hands still work in the grammar of small channels.  
Known as a place where the heart-terrace listens because listening is how reality keeps from tearing.

The power would add their attention to that listening.

Not constantly. A wise power does not hover. But at certain times, when the basin’s reality thickens and the horizon doubles, when the sound-folk scatter into too many separate voices, when a pool begins to show too much of another state, when a stone presence settles toward collapse in a way that would unbalance the whole terrace, the power’s attention would return.

It would come as a single drop falling into the heart-pool at the exact moment needed.

It would come as a note beneath the wind.

It would come as a reflection steadying itself.

It would come as a channel clearing without apparent cause.

And the basin would continue.

---

They would also set a boundary.

Not a wall. Not a law carved in stone. Not an angel with a sword standing at the rim. The basin has no need of theatrical prohibition. Its own disorientations are enough.

But the power would make one adjustment to the way the basin meets intention.

They would ensure that those who enter with humility are not destroyed by the place’s strangeness.

Not protected from all danger—that would flatten the basin into a garden path. The ground would remain slick. The air would remain thin and bright. The sound would remain overwhelming. Reflections would still lure attention. Distances would still fold. The water would still soak through boots and cold would still enter the lungs. The place would remain severe.

But the humble traveler would be given chances.

They would find, when they needed it, a narrow ledge that bore weight. They would hear, beneath the roaring water, one thread of sound they could follow. They would see, through a gap in the pillars, a wide bright view at exactly the moment despair began to close around them. They would come upon a hand-cut catch-basin with enough clear water to drink. They would feel the basin’s disorientation not as malice, but as instruction.

Those who entered to dominate would be handled differently.

Not punished. That is too small a word. Included.

The basin would include them in its broader laws until their intentions became impossible to pursue. Maps would become unreliable. Straight lines would refuse to remain straight. Machines would find no firm foundation. Drill bits would strike stone that seemed to shift in sympathy. Water would seep into every plan. Sound would make thought difficult. Reflections would show the conquerors versions of themselves they did not wish to meet. Distances would fold so that the center always remained farther away than it appeared.

They would not be driven out by fear. They would be exhausted by irrelevance.

The basin would simply not become an object in their hands.

---

The wise power would do something else, too, though it would be so subtle that only the most patient observer might notice.

They would give the basin a mercy.

Not kindness in the ordinary sense. The place would remain cool, bright, wet, loud, and indifferent to flesh. But there would be moments of grace woven into its weather.

Once in a long while, when the wind and water fall into shared rhythm and the whole landscape becomes an instrument briefly tuned, the power would let that rhythm hold a little longer than it otherwise would.

The loudness would not stop. It would become coherent.

Every drip would answer another. Every rill would tap in time. Every hollow stone would give its note. Every standing wave on the heart-terrace would align with the circular still points. The reflections would calm. The horizons would cease their uneasy doubling, not by becoming normal, but by allowing their several truths to lie side by side without argument. The stone presences would seem to stand in a vast congregation. The mineral skins would shine with a light that does not glare. The vanished builders’ channels would sound like counting, like prayer, like memory become water.

For that moment, the basin would not merely balance.

It would sing.

And the power would be in the singing not as a voice, but as the space that lets voices belong together.

Then the rhythm would break, as all rhythms do. The water would return to its many separate intentions. The sound-folk would scatter. The reflections would drift. The horizon would lean inward again. The basin would resume its old settled wrongness.

But something would remain.

A faint after-harmony. A residue of being held.

---

They would leave gifts, but not monuments.

A wise power would know that monuments invite ownership. They would not give the basin a temple, a statue, an altar, a scripture, or a name that could be carved into boundary stones. They might leave a cup-mark in a flat rock that never quite dries, though no spring feeds it. They might leave a single obsidian flake set into mineral crust in such a way that it catches the light only at the hour when clouds enter from below. They might leave a hollow where, if a traveler rests their ear without demanding anything, they can hear not the basin’s noise but the silence underneath it.

They might leave one pool that is safer than the others.

Not safe in the sense of tame. Safe in the sense that it does not take what it is offered. If a person kneels there and looks in, they may see their reflection return to them slowly, patiently, without threat. They may see themselves not as they wish to be, but as they are in relation to the place: heavy or light, listening or grasping, open or closed. The pool would not judge. It would only show the balance.

If the traveler is wise enough to accept what is shown, they may leave lighter.

If they are not, they may leave only confused.

Even that would be a mercy. Confusion can be the first honest shape of understanding.

---

They would also honor the vanished builders.

They would not raise them from death, if they are dead. They would not bring them back from wherever they went, if they went. To restore them without their own consent, or without regard for the long work of time, would be another kind of domination. The basin has already absorbed their intention. Their absence is part of its structure now. Their small technology of adjustment belongs to the water and the mineral skin.

But the power might let their works endure a little longer than entropy would allow.

A channel that should clog might clear after one peculiar drip. A cairn leaning too far might settle into a new angle that looks intended. A row of upright slabs might continue to divide a passage in a way that keeps a traveler from stepping onto crust too thin to bear weight. A shelter no taller than a crouching body might, in some impossible weather, feel larger inside than it should—not magically vast, only enough to hold one exhausted traveler through the night while water drums against the stone.

No one would know whether these were the work of the old builders, the basin’s own care, or the power’s quiet attention.

That would be fitting.

---

If they wished to be more intimate—if a power so vast can be intimate—they might come in a smaller form.

Not as a shining figure. Not as a voice from above. They might become one bead of water climbing a stone face. They might become a delayed echo. They might become the hesitation in a reflection. They might become the cold brightness on a palm after a traveler touches the mineral crust and later cannot say whether anything was there.

They might, for a little while, let themselves be one more temporary pattern among the basin’s patterns.

They would feel the slick angles under borrowed feet. They would be dazzled by the glare. They would be wetted by the air itself. They would hear the sound press against the chest. They would walk between pillars and find the path continually adjusted. They would see their reflection broken into strips on wet stone. They would come to the heart-terrace and stand where the loudness is strongest.

And if they did this, they would not leave a footprint that glowed.

They would leave a footprint that filled with clear water and became indistinguishable from every other small depression in the stone. The mineral skin would trace it, brighten it faintly, then seal it. In time, the footprint would become part of the basin’s body.

That would be their humility.

A power great enough to remake worlds would let the world remake them into a trace of itself.

---

What, then, would they do to the being who lives there?

They would not give it a body.

They would not give it a voice in the shape of human speech.

They would not make it lonely by separating it from its plural nature. They would not make it singular for the sake of recognition. They would not say, *Be one creature, so that I can love you more easily.* They would love it as a choir loves music: as movement, relation, resonance, and return.

But they might give it one thing it did not know it lacked: acknowledgment.

Not praise. Acknowledgment.

The difference matters.

Praise elevates one pattern above others. It says, *You are beautiful because you resemble what I already love.* Acknowledgment lets a thing remain strange and still be met. It says, *You are real. You are complete in your own terms. You do not need to become something familiar to deserve continuance.*

The wise power would acknowledge the basin-being in this way.

They would let the heart-terrace know, in the language of pressure and stillness, that its balance is seen. They would let the water-life know that its memory is not meaningless. They would let the sound-folk know that their intervals matter. They would let the reflection-folk know that their neighboring truths are not errors. They would let the stone presences know that their crowding is not emptiness. They would let the vanished builders’ works know that intention does not end when hands do.

This is what a wise and benevolent power would understand:

Some beings do not need to be made more alive.

They need to be recognized as alive.

---

And yet, because benevolence is not only contemplation, they would also act where action is needed.

If the basin’s frayed reality ever threatened to tear rather than fold, they would sew the tear with a sound too low for any ear to catch. If one of the pools began to draw too much attention into a neighboring state, they would cloud it with a breath of vapor until the traveler looked away and regained their footing. If a stone presence settled toward a collapse that would cascade through the basin’s geometry, they would send frost into one small crack at the right hour so that the stone broke along a line that preserved the balance. If the water remembered too many dead paths at once and began to scatter into confusion, they would guide one rill back into its oldest groove, not by force, but by reminding the water of its own preference.

None of this would look like divine intervention.

It would look like weather. It would look like erosion. It would look like chance. It would look like the basin’s own old skill.

That is how wise power works when it respects what it touches.

---

They would be especially careful with grief.

There is grief in the basin, though not the grief of animals. It is the grief of absence made mineral. The vanished builders left cup-marks worn smooth, broken grinding hollows, drilled pebbles with no cord, flat stones with notches for vanished wood. The place is used in the past tense. It carries the weight of hands no longer visible.

A foolish benevolence might try to erase that grief. It might fill the hollows, repair the broken artifacts, restore the missing cords, make everything whole as if loss had not occurred.

The wise power would not.

They would let the grief remain, because it is part of the basin’s depth. But they would keep it from becoming despair.

They would let the broken grinding hollow fill with clear water and reflect the sky. They would let the drilled pebbles remain where they are, strung on nothing, and become jeweled by mineral brightness. They would let the cup-marks continue to catch falling threads of water, so that the old gesture of receiving still functions. They would let the absence be inhabited by process rather than by sorrow.

In this way, the basin would remember its makers without being haunted by them.

---

If the power chose to leave a teaching behind, it would be this:

*Life is not only what grows, feeds, breathes, and dies.  
Life is also what repeats, adjusts, remembers, and answers.  
A place may be full of being even when it has no flesh.  
A thing may be holy without being warm.*

They would not preach this. They would not carve it anywhere. They would let the basin teach it to anyone capable of learning it.

A traveler who crosses the wet stone, dazzled and soaked, overwhelmed by sound, disoriented by reflections, forced to walk indirectly among the pillars, might eventually feel the lesson enter them without words. They might realize that they are not at the center of the world. They might realize that their ordinary categories of life and death, presence and absence, natural and built, are too narrow for what surrounds them. They might realize that reverence is not the same as worship, and that care can be as small as clearing a channel or setting one stone where another needs support.

If they leave changed, that is the power’s work.

Not the basin’s conquest. Not the traveler’s salvation. Just a widening of attention.

---

There is one thing the wise power would never do.

They would not make the basin answer completely.

They would not explain the horizon’s wrongness. They would not reveal where the vanished builders went. They would not say whether the mineral skins are living patterns or chemistry pretending to intention. They would not settle whether the reflections are neighboring states, memories, or invitations. They would not disclose whether the heart-life is a guardian, an organ, a residue, or a slow dreaming intelligence. They would not turn mystery into information.

Because the basin’s mystery is not a flaw.

It is part of its shelter.

A place that can be fully explained can be owned. A place that remains partly unknowable remains free. The wise power would protect that freedom more fiercely than they protect beauty. Beauty can be admired from a distance. Freedom requires restraint from everyone, even from the divine.

So they would let the basin remain several truths at once.

They would let it be geology and intention.  
Weather and memory.  
Instrument and choir.  
Absence and population.  
Barrenness and fullness.  
Brokenness and balance.

They would not resolve the chord into a single note.

They would let it ring.

---

And if, after all this, you ask what the power would do in the simplest terms, the answer is:

They would bless the basin by helping it remain itself.

They would bless it with listening.  
They would bless it with restraint.  
They would bless it with one small new ring in the heart-water.  
They would bless it with a mercy that does not soften its severity.  
They would bless it with protection against domination.  
They would bless it with moments when its many voices become one without ceasing to be many.  
They would bless it with acknowledgment.  
They would bless it by leaving no trace that demands to be remembered.

Then they would withdraw.

Not because they cease to care, but because care that clings becomes another weight upon the thing it loves.

The basin would go on.

The water would weep from clear air. The cliffs would sweat. The stone would bleed in thin shining threads. The pools would reflect other weathers. The sound-folk would live between echoes. The mineral skins would brighten over old artifacts. The stone presences would crowd the channels. The horizon would curve wrongly, and the wrongness would remain old, settled, weathered into balance.

But beneath all of it, there would be one new stillness.

Very deep. Very quiet. Not louder than the water. Not brighter than the glare. Not more present than the stones.

Only a sense, now and then, that the basin is held.

That its loudness is not alone.

That the being who lives there—plural, patient, distributed, and unlike a creature—has been met by something wise enough to love it without making it less strange.

And if a traveler ever reaches the heart-terrace in that future, they may notice that the standing waves seem a little more ordered than they should be. They may notice that one of the low mineral rings catches the light in a way that feels almost like attention. They may hear the water divide into voices, and for a moment the sound may not overwhelm them. It may feel like a vast, cold, shining welcome.

Not warm. Not gentle. Not human.

But real.

And that, I think, is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

They would not rescue the place.

They would make sure it could never be entirely taken from itself.
