If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention to such a place, the first thing it would do is almost nothing.

Not nothing from indifference. Nothing from reverence.

It would not come as a rupture. It would not break the rim with fire, or darken the basin with wings too large for the sky above it, or speak in a voice that flattened the waterfalls into silence. That would be a kind of violence, however beautiful. The hollow is already full. It has its own order, its own weight, its own loud and delicate balance. A wise power would know that the first act of care, in a place like this, is not to impose meaning but to make room for what is already there to continue.

So the arrival would be subtle.

Perhaps the only sign at first would be a change in the weather’s intention. A mist would lift a little earlier than expected. A cold seam in the air would settle. The rain would not stop, but it would feel less like accident and more like visitation. The light might become ordinary in an extraordinary way: clear, pale, exact, touching the moss as if it had all the time in the world.

The ravens would notice before anything else. They would cross the rim, pause, and give the basin a long, dry consideration. Then they would call once, not in alarm, but as if acknowledging someone who had entered without needing to be announced.

The power would come by the only door the place admits: water.

Not as a flood. Not as a pillar. As a finer wetness. As a thickening of cloud-water on the cliffs. As a bead of rain falling upward in mist, or seeming to. As a breath in the central pool when no wind is moving. As a new clarity in the sound of the falls, as if the stone had suddenly remembered how to carry a note.

It would not demand worship. It would not ask to be seen. If it chose form, it would choose a form the basin could hold without strain: an old traveler sitting on a dry stone; a shadow that moves with too much patience; a bird that is not quite any bird; a pressure in the air after a birdcall has already faded. If it spoke, it would speak in a language older than command. It might sound like water finding its way through a crack. It might sound like the pause between two drips.

The Listener would know.

Not at once, perhaps. Or not in the way valley people know things. The Listener would feel it the way moss feels rain: a change arriving without violence. It would not be afraid. Fear would be for a presence that intended seizure. This presence would not seize. It would not try to make the basin into a shrine, or the Listener into a prophet, or the small lives there into symbols of something cleaner. It would look upon the hollow with the tenderness reserved for things that have managed to remain themselves.

And because the power is wise, it would obey the rules of the place.

It would move where the ground is firm. It would step from stone to stone along the drier seams. It would not trample the moss. It would not raise a monument. It would not speak louder than the waterfalls. It would understand that sound here travels openly, and therefore even a blessing must be spoken without arrogance.

It would sit beside the Listener near the central pool, or appear to sit, and for a long time it would do nothing but listen.

That would be the first true gift: attention.

Not a distracted divine glance. Not a survey. A full and patient attending. It would hear every shallow channel braid and separate. It would hear the frogs calling from the black margins without needing to see them. It would hear insects rise in loose columns over the pools and fall again. It would hear the wings of dippers cutting the streams, the dry footfalls of hares in the grass, the small crack of frost tightening at night. It would hear the bothy breathing its cold stone breath. It would hear the Listener’s hands moving over worn wood, over cord, over the flat stone kept in a pouch.

It would hear the basin as a single living word.

In being heard so completely, the place would not change, exactly. It would become more itself.

There is a kind of blessing that does not add anything. It only confirms. It says, without saying: you are not accidental. You are not negligible. You are not too small to matter. You are not too quiet to be known.

If the power spoke to the Listener at all, it would not begin with command. It would ask.

Perhaps it would ask in the only way such a being can ask without overwhelming: by letting the question rise in the Listener’s mind like water rising in a pool.

What is needed here?

The Listener would be still for a long time. The question itself would feel strange, because the Listener has not lived by asking. The Listener has lived by noticing, repairing, yielding, eating sparingly, moving carefully, listening until the body becomes an instrument of the basin. To be asked what is needed might feel like having a stone placed gently in the hand after years of carrying nothing.

At last the Listener might answer, not in many words.

That the water be allowed to choose its path.

That the basin not be made into a thing.

That no one come here to take more than the day gives.

That the cold take only what cold must take.

That the moss keep its depth.

That I have enough hands to mend what is broken, and enough sense to leave what is whole.

The power would understand. It would hear beneath the words the deeper prayer, which is not for comfort, not for glory, not for escape, but for continuity without domination.

And because the power is benevolent, it would want to give. Because it is wise, it would know that the giving must be small enough to be accepted.

So it would act in ways that look, at first, like ordinary weather and ordinary luck.

It would touch the weir by the deeper pool and make the woven branches take root. Not all of them. Not enough to turn the weir into a wall or the pool into a cage. Just enough so that when seasonal flow bends the structure, living wood remembers how to hold. The Listener would notice that the repair has become easier, that the water accepts the curve more readily, that fewer stones need to be shifted. The Listener would not call it a miracle. The Listener would simply kneel in the wet and weave the new growth in with the old, following what the water has taught.

It would breathe into the moss.

Not to make the moss infinite. Not to make it swallow the basin. Only to deepen its patience. In dry spells, which come even in wet places, the moss would hold a little longer. The fine roots would not fail at the first thinning of rain. The black peat would keep its dampness beneath the surface. The cushion plants would not crack. The pools would subside more slowly. The basin would retain its breathing even when the sky withholds.

It would find the hidden springs beneath the central pool and encourage them. Not to make the pool rise dramatically, not to drown the channels or frighten the grazing animals, but to give the water table a deeper memory. In seasons when the mountain is starved of snow, the pool would still have a cold dark source to draw from. It would still rise and fall with its strange, breathlike rhythm, but the rhythm would not falter into silence.

It would tune the echoes.

The basin already behaves oddly with sound, but the power would make that oddness kinder. A startled cry would return softened. A harsh noise would lose its edge, as if the cliffs refused to carry anger far. The Listener’s staff tapping on stone would still scatter into delayed fragments, but the fragments would fall into a pattern that helps rather than confuses. A lost creature near the rim might hear the tap and turn toward safety. A careless intruder might hear the same tap and feel, without knowing why, that the place is occupied by something older than trespass.

It would not remove death from the basin. That would be a cruelty disguised as kindness.

The frogs would still freeze in the hard nights. Insects would still fail. Birds would still fall. The stoat would still hunt. The raven would still investigate what has been left exposed. Snowfields would still shrink in warm seasons; new snow would still come. The basin would remain a place where life is intense because it is brief, and precious because it is not guaranteed. A wise power would know that to take away all risk would be to turn the hollow into a museum, and a museum is not alive.

But the power would remove what does not belong.

It would take away the poison that drifts from far off, the invisible bitterness that settles in rain from places the basin has never seen. It would turn aside the fire that has no business in wet stone. It would confuse the machines that come with appetite. It would make the air too thin, the paths too wet, the silence too loud for those who mean to flatten the place into profit. It would not need to strike them down. It would only let the basin be fully itself in their presence until they understood, or failed to understand and left anyway.

If surveyors came with stakes and ownership in their eyes, the mist would thicken without malice. Their marks would wash out. Their lines would seem foolish. The cairns would appear where they were not before, not to mislead the humble but to baffle the possessive. The basin would become difficult to map in the way that matters to greed: not because it is hidden, but because it refuses to be reduced.

If people came hungry and humble, the place would continue to offer its scattered gifts. A few fish would move into the channels. Eggs would be found near the sedge. The roots would wash clean. The moss would give way gently underfoot. The cairns would be visible enough, but only enough.

If people came with cruelty, the echoes would return their voices until they heard how small they were. The water would not attack them. The weather would not become melodramatic. But the cold would be exactly cold enough. The rain would be exactly patient enough. The loudness of the basin would become unbearable to anyone who had forgotten how to listen.

The power would also care for the animals, though not by making them tame.

It would give the ibex a sureness on the rim. It would give the hares a deeper stillness in the grass. It would guide the dippers to the clearest seams of current. It would let the frogs’ calls carry without exhausting them. It would give the insects one warm hour after frost, perhaps no more than that, but enough for eggs to be laid, enough for the next pulse. It would not make predators gentle. It would make hunger honest.

And it would care for the Listener.

Not by making the Listener into something other than what it is. Not by lifting it out of weather. Not by giving it a shining body or a voice that commands. The Listener’s holiness, if one can call it that, is in its lowness, its patience, its willingness to be small within a small place. To change that would be to destroy the very thing the power has noticed.

So the gifts would be modest.

It would ease the joints a little in the cold mornings. It would keep the Listener’s hands steady when weaving wet branches. It would make sleep in the bothy deep and repairing. It would let fire catch easily when fire is truly needed, and let it fail when fire would be too much. It would give the Listener’s eyes the ability to see in mist not farther, but truer. It would sharpen the listening until the basin’s warnings are heard early: the channel beginning to cut too deep, the bank about to collapse, the pool souring, the moss thinning where too many feet have passed.

It might give the Listener dreams, but not grand ones.

Dreams of water moving under stone. Dreams of roots knitting in darkness. Dreams of a bell made of rain. Dreams of a path that is not lost but only waiting to be walked again. The Listener would wake without needing to interpret them. The dreams would simply become part of the day’s weather.

If the Listener were ever lonely — not from absence, but from the weight of being the only one who notices — the power would not necessarily provide another being. It might, if it saw fit, allow another quiet presence to pass through: a traveler with no wish to stay, a child who hears the basin and remembers it into old age, an animal that rests near the bothy one night and leaves no trace by morning. But it would not impose companionship as if the Listener were incomplete. The power would understand that some lives are full in a way that does not require duplication.

Instead, it would give the Listener the certainty of being known.

That is no small thing. To be known without being claimed. To be seen without being altered for the viewer’s comfort. To have one’s small work recognized by a wisdom vast enough to see its place in the whole. The Listener would feel this perhaps only as a settling in the chest, a loosening of an old knot, a sense that the basin’s loudness is not merely noise but answer.

One day, perhaps, the power would ask another question.

Do you want this place to last forever?
