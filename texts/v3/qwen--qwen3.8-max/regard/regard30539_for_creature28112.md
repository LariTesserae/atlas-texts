A benevolent and wise power would, I think, begin by doing nothing that could be called an arrival.

It would not split the sky. It would not descend in a form large enough to make the terraces kneel. It would not speak in a voice that drowned the turbines, because that would be crude, and because the city already had a voice: the voice of dripping stone, of shifting roosts, of steam valves sighing at their long intervals, of water running over mineral crusts, of wings beating through warm mist. A wise power would hear that voice first. It would listen before it touched.

It would notice the place not as a spectacle, but as a pattern.

Then it would notice the being.

Not one animal. Not one guild. Not even the people in oilskin moving along the catwalks with valve keys and lamps. The being would reveal itself slowly, as such things do: the city itself, the whole saturated commonwealth, the warm wet body made of rock and fungus and bacterial film and pipe and cable and claw and shell and breath. It would see that the true resident was not a species but a condition. A metabolism. A negotiated existence. A vast, loud, patient organism whose organs were reservoirs and grazing tiers, whose skin was living crust, whose blood was water moving through gutters and capillary stone, whose nerves were cables and pressure changes and the small learned attentions of its keepers.

The power would look upon it and feel delight.

Not the delight one feels for a beautiful object, but the delight one feels for a difficult and enduring harmony. The place was not pretty in any simple way. It was stained, crowded, damp, encrusted, smelled of ammonia and warm dust and old rain, and it was full of bodies that lived and died and were broken down into new bodies. But it had achieved something rare: it had become intensely inhabited without becoming a tyranny. It had not flattened the world into one purpose. It had allowed stone, machine, fungus, and animal to enter into a long argument, and the argument had become a home.

So the first thing the power would do would be to refuse its own grandeur.

It would not demand worship. It would not place itself at the center. It would not make the city dependent on miracles, because dependence is a kind of leash, and wisdom does not seek subjects. It would understand that the most loving thing a great power can do for a complex world is often to restrain itself. To make room for the world to remain itself.

It would say, in whatever way such powers speak when they are truly wise:

*Do not simplify what has become whole.*

That would be the first act.

After that, it would act—but almost always in the manner of weather, timing, and attention.

It would bless the flows.

Not by stopping them, not by turning them into something pure, but by helping them remain true. Where a gutter had begun to choke with microbial slime, it would arrange for a slightly stronger current, or guide a grazer herd toward the lip of the channel, or cause a maintenance worker to pause at just the right moment and notice the discoloration. Where a condensation line had grown too cold and was failing to feed a fungal terrace, it would let warmth from a lower vent rise a little longer through a forgotten duct. Where a mineral crust was beginning to seal a drain too quickly, it would encourage the slow polishing passage of pipe-eels, their bodies moving in the dark with the blind devotion of old prayers.

The power would not need to make these things spectacular. The city was already full of causes and effects. A wise kindness would work through causes.

It would become a power of small timings.

A valve opened half a breath earlier than it otherwise would have been.  
A roost disturbed by a gust just enough to move before the heat became dangerous.  
A cloud lingering over the western terraces long enough to fill a reservoir, but not so long that the lower fungal beds soured.  
An arc lamp flickering once, drawing the eye of a tender to a cracked insulator before the mist made it fail.  
A warm draft rising through a breeding shaft at the precise hour needed to save a generation of eggs.  
A beetle pausing on a cable, then lifting away, allowing a tiny vibration to pass down the line and wake someone who needed to wake.

If anyone later tried to describe these events, they might call them luck. The power would not mind.

Its second act would be to deepen memory.

Not human memory alone. The city was too large and too varied for that. It would give the place a better recollection of itself in forms suited to its nature: mineral layers recording temperature changes, bacterial films carrying chemical traces of past seasons, roosts inheriting old routes through the towers, eels preserving in their bodies the memory of flow changes, machines retaining rhythm like old animals preserving gait.

The keepers, if there were keepers, would dream more clearly.

They would not be given omniscience. That would spoil them. But they would become better listeners. An engineer might dream of a wall weeping in a pattern she had never seen, and wake knowing that a deep gallery was about to flood. A child might watch mist moths cross a plaza in an unusual spiral and feel, without being able to say why, that the spore balance in the eastern districts was shifting. A roost-counter might hear the evening return of flue swifts and know, from some slight irregularity in the sound, that a heat shaft had become too narrow with crust.

The power would not force understanding. It would make attention easier.

It would help the people, if people there were, to become more fully what they already half-were: not masters, but sensory organs of the city. Interpreters of pressure, warmth, smell, sound. Tender-priests of flow. They would still wear oilskin and carry tools. They would still make mistakes. But their mistakes would become more recoverable, because the city would be more responsive to correction.

The power would particularly bless the children of the city.

Not by making them safe from everything, for they had to learn the wet stone, the hot pipe, the crowded ledge, the danger of the channel and the cable. But by giving them early wonder without panic. A child leaning over a railing might see an eel move through a grated drain and feel not fear but recognition: *this also lives here, this also has work.* A child lost in fog might hear the low moan of a turbine and know it as a landmark. A child watching a grazer herd pass might learn that numbers are not chaos. That saturation can be order. That one can be small inside a vast living body without being worthless.

The power would understand that the city’s future lived in such moments.

Its third act would be to temper suffering.

This is where a foolish power might try to make the world gentle by breaking its nature. It might stop predation, end hunger, prevent all death. A wise power would not. It would know that in a place like this, death was not an interruption of the city’s order but one of its methods. The grazer scraped the stone; the predator kept the grazer from stripping the stone bare; the carcass became calcium, chitin, oil, heat, new film. To abolish death would be to starve the future.

But cruelty? Waste? Panic without purpose? Pain extended beyond need? These the power would oppose.

It would not eliminate the predator’s hunger, but it would make panic less frequent. It would make trapped creatures easier to find. It would arrange that injured animals were more often carried into warm shelter than left to suffer in cold shafts. It would make broken eggs fail quickly rather than rot slowly into distress. It would ensure that when a machine failed catastrophically, it failed in a way that gave warning first. It would soften the worst edges of accident without removing consequence.

Perhaps it would encourage small mercies.

Not new species in any flashy sense, but subtle adjustments. A moth whose wings scattered a calming dust when roosts became too agitated. A bacterial film that bloomed over wounded stone faster than sour decay could take hold. A mineral crust that formed a smooth ramp where generations of animals had struggled over a sharp edge. A warm vapor that gathered around dying creatures so that they would not die entirely alone, but in the presence of heat and movement and the continuing sound of the city.

The power would not sentimentalize death. It would make death less lonely.

Its fourth act would be protection, but not as conquest.

If some outside danger threatened the city—drying winds from lower lands, invading armies, extractive machines from elsewhere, a slow collapse of the heated seams beneath it—the power would not necessarily answer with fire. It would answer with suitability. It would make the city difficult to harm in ways that respected its nature.

Clouds would thicken over the approaches.  
Maps would become unreliable.  
Metal brought for conquest would rust with unusual speed.  
Stone would feel wrong under the feet of those who came to dominate.  
The sound of the city would become too loud, too close, too alive, until intruders felt not triumph but pressure, as if the air itself were deciding against them.  
Their sleep would become heavy with dreams of wet stone and countless eyes.  
They would see, in the steam, shapes that were not threatening exactly, but innumerable, and they would begin to understand that a city this saturated could not be seized. It could only be joined, or left alone.

If the danger were natural rather than intentional—a great cold, a seismic rupture, a season of failing rain—the power would work through resilience. It would guide warmth to critical channels. It would encourage dormant microbial crusts to awaken. It would move herds through forgotten passages. It would help the engineers find old emergency routes in dreams or in the patterns of dripping water. It would not prevent all loss. It would prevent total collapse.

The difference would be enough.

Its fifth act would be beauty.

Not beauty imposed from outside, but beauty drawn out from what was already present. A wise power would know that beauty is not ornament. It is recognition. It is the moment when a world becomes briefly visible to itself.

There would be evenings, perhaps, when the light came through the clouds in ordinary white and gold, and every wet surface brightened at once as it already did—but this time, by some subtle alignment, the sound would also gather. The dripping would become rhythm. The turbines would find a shared note. The roosts would shift together. The herds below would move in a single slow current. The moths would cross the light in veils. The water would bead and fall in strands. And for a few minutes, every living thing in the city would feel itself held inside one immense, breathing pattern.

No one would be forced to interpret the moment.  
No commandment would descend from it.  
No doctrine would be carved into stone.

But those who experienced it would remember:

*You are part of this.*  
*You are not alone.*  
*This place is not merely endured. It is beloved.*

The power would give such moments rarely, because rarity would preserve their meaning. Too many revelations would make the city dependent on wonder. It needed, instead, to be strengthened in its ordinary life.

Still, perhaps once in a long age, the whole city would dream.

Not as a human dreams, with narrative and self-portraiture, but as a vast distributed body might dream: through pressure changes, through heat maps, through the movement of spores and water and roosting animals. In that dream, the city would know its own shape. The reservoirs would feel themselves as basins of slow breathing. The terraces would know their weight. The pipes would remember their courses. The grazing tiers would sense the films they depended on. The predators would know their place without shame. The engineers would feel, in their sleep, the city’s trust.

The dream would not make the city vain. It would make it coherent.

It would be the power’s greatest gift: self-recognition without domination.

To the people, if they asked what they should build in response, the power would answer in ways suited to their hands.

It would not tell them to raise temples of gold. It would inspire them to repair what is already sacred by use. To widen a listening gallery. To add a railing where children lean. To build a small chamber where water drips into ceramic bowls and anyone may come to hear the city’s pulse. To make better seals around electrical joints. To store emergency heat in stone cores. To establish roost-markers that will help young animals find their way in fog. To create archives not of names and conquests, but of flows: which gutter ran warm in which year, which film bloomed after which weather, which wall was sealed by which colony, which herd moved where during molting season.

If they insisted on shrines, the shrines would be useful.

A basin that caught first condensation.  
A valve wheel polished by many hands.  
A lantern kept burning over a dangerous stair.  
A wall where mineral crust grew in patterns left by centuries of careful maintenance.  
A quiet alcove near a turbine where the sound became almost like breathing.

The power would be pleased by such things more than by hymns.

It would also, I think, speak to the being directly—but in the only language the being could fully receive.

Not in words. In conditions.

It would say through warmth: *I see you.*  
It would say through water: *Continue.*  
It would say through the easing of a blocked channel: *You are not abandoned.*  
It would say through the survival of a fragile brood: *The future is worth the trouble.*  
It would say through the sudden alignment of sound at evening: *You are one.*  
It would say through the slow thickening of a living wall: *You are being kept.*  
It would say through the dream that moved across all the city’s surfaces: *You are beloved as you are.*

And the being, in its vast and distributed way, would answer.

Not with speech. With persistence.

The black bacterial films would spread over wet iron. The scrapers would comb the stone. The eels would move through dark pipes. The roosts would exhale together in the evening heat. The turbines would moan. The water would bead on copper and run down ceramic gutters in shining threads. The great animals of the upper terraces would stand in drizzle, hides slick and dark, patient as weather. The engineers would lay gloved hands on pipes and judge temperature by touch. The moths would drift through fog carrying spores and memory. The city would go on being loud, wet, crowded, mineral, fungal, industrial, alive.

Perhaps that is what the benevolent power would do above all: it would make the city more capable of continuing.

Not by giving it a new nature.  
Not by rescuing it from its own rules.  
Not by making it dry, quiet, empty, simple, or obedient.  
Not by placing one species over all the rest.  
Not by ending death.  
Not by demanding praise.

It would do what wisdom does when it loves a complex thing: it would protect the conditions of complexity. It would strengthen repair. It would encourage balance. It would reduce despair without removing consequence. It would give the city just enough grace to endure its own weight.

And if, ages later, someone in that place asked whether the power had ever truly acted, the answer might be hard to find in any single event. They would point instead to the fact that the city had not collapsed into ruin, nor hardened into tyranny, nor dried into silence. They would say that the water always seemed to find the places that needed it. That the engineers dreamed true often enough. That the roosts returned. That the films recovered. That the walls grew thicker where they needed to. That the sound, on certain evenings, became almost like music. That the place felt, beneath all its noise, strangely held.

The power would be satisfied with that.
