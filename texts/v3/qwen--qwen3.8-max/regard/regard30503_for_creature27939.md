The power did not arrive as a storm.

It did not come down in fire, or voice, or a sudden brightening of the ordinary air. It did not split the young ridges, still the water, or make the stone bow. A wise power, seeing such a place, would know better than to announce itself by undoing it.

It came first as attention.

Not as presence, perhaps, but as a leaning-near. A noticing so complete that the basin itself seemed to become slightly more itself under the regard. The wet stone grew a little clearer in its own wetness. The silence became more deliberate. The small sounds of water — bead, drip, whisper, release — stood out not because they had grown louder, but because something was listening to them fully.

And what it saw was this:

A high bowl of new land, still raw from its making. Warm water held in stone and soil. A city built not against the wet but through it. Emptiness left intentionally. People few, patient, and distributed like stones in a streambed. No animal life, no hunted or hunting things, no wings or tracks or bones. Only plants, water, mineral ground, and hands that had learned to guide without forcing.

The power saw that the place was not suffering in any simple way. It was not a wound crying out to be healed. It was not a prison. It was not a failure waiting for correction.

It was a balance.

A fragile balance, perhaps. All living balances are fragile. But it was a balance chosen and maintained, not one imposed by helplessness. The people did not lack because they had been denied; they had shaped their world around restraint. Their city breathed because it was not full. Their silence endured because they did not fill every pause with noise. Their tools remained simple because they had not tried to overpower the basin with engines. Their survival depended on attention: on reading seepage, listening to springs, clearing runnels, repairing sluices, knowing where water wanted to go and giving it a path.

A benevolent power, if it is also wise, must ask before acting:

What kind of help would not become violence here?

What gift would not turn into dependence?

What protection would not steal dignity?

What miracle would not flatten the very qualities that make this place precious?

And perhaps the first thing such a power would do is refuse the easiest answers.

It would refuse to dry the basin.

That would be the obvious miracle for an outsider. To see so much water, so much damp, so much saturation, and to think: let there be dry ground. Let the marsh become field. Let the walls cease weeping. Let the air clear. Let the city stand firm and convenient.

But the wise power would understand that dryness would not save this place. It would erase it.

The city exists because water has right of way. Its whole meaning is in channels, weirs, causeways, cisterns, overflow beds, and the patient distribution of wetness. To dry it would be to make its architecture meaningless, its knowledge obsolete, its people strangers in their own home. The power would not commit that kindness.

It would refuse to fill the empty spaces.

The emptiness is not neglect. It is capacity. The broad sheets of standing water, the unused stairways, the terraces with no houses, the plazas of wet gravel opening into silence — these are not signs that the city has failed to become crowded. They are signs that it has chosen a different measure of fullness. The basin breathes through those spaces. The people do not need to stand shoulder to shoulder in order to be real.

A wise power would not reward restraint by making them numerous. It would not bless them by pressing them into denser lives. It would know that some places are holy because they are sparse, and that a blessing which removes quiet is often a conquest wearing a gentle face.

It would refuse to give them dominion.

No great machines. No conquering tools. No engines that lift mountains or drain marshes in a season. No sudden mastery over stone, heat, or water. Not because the people are unworthy, but because mastery of that kind would change the shape of their attention. Instead of listening to the basin, they would begin to command it. Instead of moving slowly, they would move efficiently. Instead of asking where water wants to go, they would decide where it must go.

That might look like progress. But the power, being wise, would see the future hidden inside such gifts: noise, crowding, exhaustion, the loss of the small clean sounds, the disappearance of the long patient hands.

So the power would bind itself.

It would make a law for its own kindness:

*I will not do what cannot drain away.*

*I will not speak so loudly that my voice replaces their listening.*

*I will not make them need me in order to remain themselves.*

*I will not enter this basin as a master, but as water enters: by the lowest path, with consent, and only as far as it can pass without breaking what has been built.*

Then, and only then, would it act.

And its actions would be almost invisible.

First, it would steady the hidden dangers.

The basin is young. Its stone is newly exposed. Beneath it there may be heat, pressure, fractured rock, unsettled ground. The same warmth that makes the place livable could, if unchecked, turn cruel: a spring growing scalding, a hidden vent opening, a cliff face loosened by steam, a sudden flood from a burst cistern of earth. The people can watch the surface, but they cannot see everything below.

So the power would attend to the deep places.

Not with drama. Not with great walls of divine stone raised in a night. Instead, it would ease the hidden strain. It would cool a too-hot vein before it cracked. It would guide a thread of steam into a harmless seep. It would encourage mineral water to deposit silica in a fracture, sealing it slowly, naturally, in a way that looked like ordinary geology. It would make certain slopes settle a little more firmly. It would keep one or two springs from turning sour. It would make the warmth remain mild, breath-like, steady, never feverish.

The people would notice only that the basin continued kind.

They might say to one another, in their soft voices, “The water is good this year,” or “That cliff has stopped sweating so much,” or “The old spring is still sweet.” They would not know exactly why. They would not need to.

The power would be content to be unnamed.

Second, it would sharpen their attention without replacing it.

It would not send prophecies. It would not write warnings in the air. It would not make voices rise from pools saying, *Do not open the eastern sluice.* That would destroy the texture of their lives. Instead, it would work through their existing skill.

A person’s hand would pause on a rope at the exact moment a hidden knot was about to slip.

A child would notice a hairline crack in a terrace wall and point to it, not knowing why it mattered.

A sluice-tender would dream of water running backward through a narrow channel and wake with the sense that something had been blocked upstream.

A builder pressing clay into a repair would suddenly remember, as if from old habit, that a certain mixture with crushed tuff would breathe better in damp weather.

None of these would feel like command. They would feel like memory. Like recognition. Like the natural deepening of care.

This, too, is wisdom: not to give people knowledge that alienates them from their own lives, but to help them see more truly what they already half-see.

Third, it would make one place of communication — but only a small one, and only with consent.

Somewhere in the low central part of the basin, where pale stone borders dark water, there would be a shallow pool set apart from the ordinary channels. Not grand. Not temple-like. A square or rounded basin, perhaps, carved from tuff, no larger than a household cistern. It would have one inlet and one outlet. It would be open to the sky. No roof would cover it. Water would be allowed to enter and leave freely, because even a sacred place in this city must obey the rule that water has right of way.

The power would not speak there in words.

Instead, it would allow the water to answer in water’s own manner.

If a person came with a practical question — *Is this channel safe to open? Will this wall hold if the spring rises? Is there danger beneath this terrace?* — and if they asked calmly, without greed, without panic, without the desire to control others, then the water might respond.

A bead would gather on the rim.

It would fall.

One drop might mean: yes.

Two might mean: wait.

A still surface might mean: the question is wrongly made.

A slight overflow might mean: the danger is greater than you know; call others.

But the pool would not answer every question.

It would not answer questions about love. It would not answer questions about fate. It would not tell one person what another person desired. It would not reveal enemies. It would not promise victory. It would not grant permission for conquest. It would not become an oracle for ambition.

If someone tried to use it that way, the water would simply remain water: glossy, ordinary, quiet, reflecting the low sky.
