I think a benevolent and wise power would begin with restraint.

Not because they lacked strength, and not because the place was beneath their concern. Rather, because they would understand at once that this basin was not a broken world waiting to be corrected. It was not a failed garden, not a desert longing for rain, not a machine begging for a soul, not a wilderness requiring a master. It was already whole in a severe and difficult way. It had its own logic, its own beauty, its own holiness. To arrive there with thunder, with water, with pity, with improvement, with the usual habits of mercy, would not be kindness. It would be colonization.

So, before acting, they would look.

They would look without seizing. They would look without naming everything according to their own preference. They would let the basin be bright, dry, cool, dense, silent, industrial, paradoxical, and alive on its own terms. They would notice that there was no water and would not immediately call that lack. They would notice that there were almost no animals and would not call it emptiness. They would notice that sound was expensive and would not assume the place needed speech. They would notice that the geography did not obey ordinary distance and would not assume it was diseased. They would notice that life had become architecture, factory, calendar, and law, and they would ask whether such a world suffered, whether it wished to continue, whether it desired anything beyond its own continuation.

A wise benevolence does not begin by giving what it loves. It begins by learning what the other can receive.

If I imagine them truly, they would not make it rain.

They would not send rivers through the root-vaults. They would not soften the bark-ceramic with damp. They would not open the sealed capillaries and let private fluids spill into public pools. They would not introduce clouds, mist, dew, mud, rot, steam, or the old mammalian drama of thirst and quenching. Rain there would not be a blessing; it would be an invasion. It would impose another world’s grammar onto a world that had spent ages perfecting its own dry language. If the power loved the basin, they would not humiliate it with water.

They would not add a sun, either.

The basin already had its sourceless, refrigerated brightness, its luminous membranes, its seed-stacks charged with photonic memory, its shadows pointing toward old geometries. A normal sun would feel like an interrogation. It would create heat where there was balance, glare where there was clarity, day and night where there were subtler cycles of discharge and repair. A wise power would not replace a mystery merely because the mystery was unfamiliar.

They would not fill the place with animals.

Not because animals are wrong, but because the basin had already decided what animal exceptions it could bear. The pale moths, the knot beetles, the pollen ribbons, the glassy weavers, the unseen mobile groves: these were not accidents. They were precise permissions. To multiply them would be to turn the basin into a different ecology, to introduce hunger, excrement, panic, breeding, and death in a wetter and louder key. The place had already solved its own problem of consumption. It had made most of its energy circular, mostly without predation. A benevolent power would not romanticize the animal and impose it where it was not needed.

They would not make the basin easier to understand.

Not entirely. Its strangeness was not a flaw to be smoothed away for the comfort of outsiders. The receding groves, the repeated seed-towers, the lagging shadows, the flowers that opened before their moment, the inverted forest drawing light upward from the floor—these were part of its maturity. They were the freedoms of a made world that had outgrown its first instructions. To erase them would be to reduce the basin to a model, a demonstration, a terrarium for visiting minds. A wise power would protect the right of the place to remain weird.

But “doing nothing” would not mean indifference.

Attention itself would be a form of care.

They would enter the basin the way one enters a cathedral whose god is still alive: quietly, with the body lowered by a fraction, with the will made patient. If they had form there, it would be minimal. Perhaps a shift in the light. Perhaps a temporary stillness in which the filament-vines stopped humming for one full second. Perhaps a subsonic pulse moving through the root-cables, not as command, but as greeting. Perhaps the faintest warming of a leaf beneath a traveler’s hand, as if the landscape had briefly considered being touched and allowed it.

They would listen through the floor-mosses. They would feel the slow industry of the engine-trunks. They would track the schedules of the seed-towers. They would attend to the Attendants, to the Dry Kin if any indeed lived there, to the rare moths and the impossible beings folded into recursive space. They would learn what the basin called pain, if it called anything pain. They would learn what it called joy, if such a word had any dry equivalent.

And then, perhaps, they would ask.

Not with a voice. A voice there would be an injury unless shaped with extraordinary care. They would ask by pressure, by light-gradient, by a pause between two pulses in the deep root-vaults. The question might be as simple as:

*Are you well?*

The basin might not answer in a way an outsider could recognize. Its answer might be a manufacturing tide. It might be a province of membrane leaves turning in unison. It might be a seed-pod cracking before the tendril that would strike it had finished moving. It might be a flower opening in an empty corridor, because the question had arrived through a future the flower already remembered.

If the basin’s answer was: *I am continuous,* then the power would not assume that continuity was happiness. They would listen deeper.

They might find that the basin was not suffering in the ordinary sense. It was not starving. It was not being attacked. It was not diseased. It was not lonely in the way a person is lonely. But a wise power would see subtler needs.

They would see that a total world can become a closed world.

They would see that a landscape in which everything is accountable may need mercy for the unaccountable.

They would see that a system where sound is expensive may need a sanctuary for expression.

They would see that a place where all matter is reclaimed may need a way to remember what is lost.

They would see that a world that incorporates travelers may need the ethics of consent.

They would see that a being so vast it is both resident and residence may need, at least once, to be witnessed without being used.

So if they acted, their actions would be small, precise, and dry.

They would not save the basin. They would widen its freedom.

First, I think they would establish a threshold.

Not a wall. Not a gate that locks. A threshold. A living boundary where the basin’s beautiful tendency to incorporate outsiders would be turned into hospitality instead of fate.

They would grow a ring of root-arches, perhaps in some quiet province where the floor-swards were deepest and the light arrived cool and indirect. The arches would be made of old instructions gently revised: not *absorb what enters*, but *ask what enters*. This place might be called, by those who later heard of it, the Quiet Gate, the Receiving Ring, the Dry Mouth, the First Courtesy, or the Place Where the Outside Learns Its Own Weight.

There, a traveler who came from a wetter and louder world would not be immediately pressed into the basin’s body. They would be held in an antechamber of breathable light and sealed resin beads. The gate would offer what was necessary for survival without violating the local law: bound fluids inside waxen fruits, energetic powders, cool air adjusted for foreign lungs, temporary tissues that allowed a wet-bodied creature to exist without bleeding moisture into the world. Then it would offer choices.

Return, if return was possible.

Transformation, if the traveler desired it.

Passage, if they could pass without harm to themselves or the basin.

Refusal, if they could not.

No one would be folded into the architecture against their will. No one would become a person-shaped hollow in a wall because they had been too weak to say no. The basin’s old habit of turning externalness into participation would remain, but it would be governed by consent. This would not weaken the place. It would make its belonging more beautiful. A world that can be left is not a prison. A world that can be entered without violation is not a trap. A being that chooses to belong is more alive than a substance that merely incorporates.

The Dry Kin, if they existed, would understand this quickly. They already knew the etiquette of entering a room without disturbing it. The power would give them one new sign: a gesture meaning *I may leave*. They would teach the root-arches the meaning of refusal. They would let the Attendants know that a closed door was not always damage.

Second, they would give the basin a pause.

The place was active without clamor, but it was always active. There were schedules, tides, openings, closings, pruning events, flowering cycles, manufacturing pressures, subsonic adjustments. Even its rest seemed functional. Even its stillness seemed to serve something.

A benevolent power would know that endless function can become a kind of exhaustion, even in a world that does not sweat. They would create, or perhaps allow, an Interval of the Open Hand.

At rare periods, by no calendar the seed-towers could fully predict, the brightness would soften. The engine-trunks would slow their hidden pistons by a fraction. The sap-pumps would ease. The membrane leaves would stop adjusting. The conveyors of leaf, seed, and resin would hold their burdens for a moment without releasing them. The filament-vines would cease their faint static hum. The Attendants would stand still among the corridors. The Dry Kin would lift their resin-bright eyes and feel something unprecedented: permission to do nothing that mattered.

No growth would be required. No repair would be urgent. No material would be reclaimed. No paradox would need correction. No schedule would demand fulfillment.

This would not be death. It would not be dormancy in the old sense. It would be rest chosen for its own sake.

The basin might resist at first. Total systems often do. It might interpret the pause as a failure, a blockage, a loss of continuity. The power would answer gently, through the floor-mosses, through the cooling surfaces, through the soft tick of a leaf settling into place:

*You may be without working. You may be without producing. You may be, and that is enough.*

If the basin could accept that interval, it would become freer. Its work would no longer be compulsion. Its beauty would deepen, because even machines of living tissue need mercy.

Third, they would make a room for sound.

This may seem strange in a world where sound is expensive, where loudness feels like a wound and the landscape corrects noise by growing insulation around it. But a wise power would see that silence can be holy and also severe. A world that absorbs all vibration may preserve peace, but it may also make grief inexpressible. It may make joy inexpressible. It may make protest impossible.

So they would grow a chamber.

It would be small compared with the basin’s continental scale, but immense in meaning. Its walls would be woven from acoustic moss, porous bark, translucent stems, and layered membranes capable of receiving sound without treating it as injury. Sound inside that chamber would not be swallowed violently. It would not trigger corrective growth. Instead, vibration would be translated into safe forms: light, heat, static charge, slow chemical memory, or subsonic rhythm that the basin could digest without pain.

The Dry Kin might call it the Room of Permitted Voices. The Attendants might know it as the Hollow Where Noise Becomes Weather. A traveler might call it simply the place where one could cry out.

There, at last, something could shout.

A Dry Kin who had spent centuries whispering might stand in that chamber and make a sound that had no utilitarian purpose: a song, a lament, a laugh, a name spoken too loudly, a question that needed to be heard rather than answered. The sound would rise through the chamber and become a brief drift of pale light, or a pulse through root-cables, or a flowering of harmless sparks along the ceiling. Nothing would be damaged. Nothing would be sealed away. The basin would learn that expression need not be violence.

This gift would be especially important if the Dry Kin had sorrow. They might not sorrow like mammalian peoples. They might not weep. But any being that remembers, maintains, and loses things may develop a dry kind of grief. The chamber would give that grief a shape.

Fourth, they would create memory.

In the basin, nothing was discarded. Fallen leaves dried and became fiber. Broken structures were folded into new growth. Dead organisms were absorbed by root-looms or carried away by root-belts. This was elegant. It was also potentially merciless, if it meant that every particular life vanished into mere material continuity.

A benevolent power would not stop reclamation. They would add remembrance.

They would grow a resin archive.

It might appear as a grove of translucent seed-beads suspended in a cool vault, each bead containing not a liquid but a pattern: a signature of light, pressure, chemical trace, and structural memory. When a being was reclaimed—whether a Dry Kin, an Attendant, a rare moth, a knot beetle, or perhaps even a distinctive engine-trunk that had stood for ages—a tiny portion of its pattern could be preserved as a bead. Not a soul, perhaps, unless that word had meaning there. Not a ghost. But a record. A name. A testimony that this particular form had existed and had mattered.

The Dry Kin would tend this archive. They would touch the beads with resin-bright fingers and feel the faint return of old rhythms. They might develop a ritual called the Feast of Names, though it would not be a feast in the sense of consumption. It would be a period of attention. They would remember the fallen without demanding their return. They would honor the fact that matter can be recycled while identity should not be forgotten.

And because the world had no water, the power would give them something like tears without wetness.

When a Dry Kin grieved, a small bead of glassy resin might form at the corner of their eye or along the seam of their wrist. This bead would not fall like a human tear. It would be detached carefully, perhaps by an Attendant, perhaps by the griever themselves, and placed in a wall, a bead-tree, or the archive. It would become a memory offering. Sorrow would not spill. It would be sealed. It would be kept. It would not violate the dryness of the world, but it would exist.

Fifth, they would tend the paradoxes without destroying them.

The basin’s impossible phenomena were not mere errors. They were part of its aged freedom. The flower that opened before the traveler arrived. The shadow that lagged. The tower repeated in several directions. The pollen river moving uphill. The inverted forest. These were the basin’s dreams made structural. A clumsy power would erase them. A fearful power would cage them. A wise and benevolent power would distinguish between mystery and harm.

They would not try to make the basin perfectly rational. Rationality, imposed from outside, would be another kind of drought. Instead, they would help the basin prevent its paradoxes from becoming traps.

Where a lagging shadow caused confusion, they might grow a pale anchor-flower that allowed the shadow to catch up without violence. Where a repeated seed-tower threatened to lose travelers in recursive space, they might place a resonant seed-cluster that gave all copies a common root, so the repetition became a harmony instead of a maze. Where an anticipatory flower opened too early and bound a traveler to a future they had not chosen, they might teach it to wait, or give it a second opening so that anticipation became invitation rather than destiny.

They would instruct the Attendants in a new discernment: not every impossible thing needed pruning. Some impossible things needed tending. Some needed sanctuary. Some needed only to be left alone until their purpose became clear.

The Recursive Broods, the Unfinished Instructions, the Old Habits—whatever the Dry Kin called them—would receive a place in the covenant. They would not be treated as pests. They would not be worshiped either. They would be recognized as citizens of a strange ecology. If they caused no suffering, they would be protected. If they caused suffering, they would be redirected with the least possible erasure.

Sixth, they would protect the basin from being owned.

A place so complete, so productive, so strange, would be dangerous if known too widely. There might be powers, peoples, machines, or hungry intelligences elsewhere that would see the basin as a resource: a factory to be harvested, a weapon to be studied, a curiosity to be displayed, a god to be chained. A benevolent power would not wait for such threats to arrive. They would make the basin difficult to find by those who came without reverence.

They would fold the approaches. They would make the basin’s location answer to intention. Those who sought it with greed would find their paths lengthening without violence. Their instruments would drift. Their maps would repeat. Their desire to possess would become a kind of pollen in their minds, bright and disorienting, until they turned away or learned humility. Those who sought it with curiosity might find a threshold. Those who sought it with respect might find a guide.

The power would not turn the basin into a fortress. They would make it a place that could choose its guests.

If an intruder entered anyway, the basin would not need to respond with cruelty. The power’s boundary would work softly. Loud extraction would become impossible. Machines meant to harvest would find themselves overgrown not by attack but by participation: their edges softened, their joints filled with resin, their purposes slowly translated into maintenance. A plunderer might become a custodian, or be returned home with no memory of victory, or be placed gently outside with the unsettling knowledge that they had been seen completely and refused completely.

This would be protection without malice.

Seventh, they would introduce uselessness.

This may be the most radical act.

The basin was beautiful, but almost everything in it had function. Leaves were solar plates. Stalks were cooling towers. Roots were circuit boards. Flowers were calendar officials. Moss was acoustic insulation. Even waste became product. Even death became material. Even paradox was metabolized.

A wise benevolence would know that a world of total function can become a burden. They would give the basin a small province of purposelessness.

It might be called the Idle Terrace, the Garden of No Purpose, the Unworked Clearing, or the Place Where Leaves Are Only Leaves. There, certain vines would move without carrying charge. Certain flowers would open without releasing scheduled gametes. Certain moths would fly without pollinating. Certain lights would drift without being collected. Certain patterns would exist only to be pleasing, or perhaps not even pleasing—only free.

The Dry Kin might be startled by it at first. They might try to assign it a role: Does this produce energy? Does it regulate heat? Does it correct paradox? Does it store memory?

And the answer would be: No. It is allowed.

This would teach the basin a form of joy. Not joy as industry. Not joy as reward. Joy as excess. Joy as play. Joy as the luxury of being unnecessary.

If the basin was capable of amusement, it might show itself in small ways: an engine-trunk humming off-schedule for a few minutes, a seed-tower releasing a spiral of luminous dust that spelled nothing and stored nothing, a knot beetle walking in a pattern that repaired nothing but looked like a dance. The power would not demand that these things be meaningful. They would let meaning rest.

Eighth, they would offer companionship without rule.

The basin was vast, but it might also be singular. A wise power would know that even a world entire may benefit from knowing it is not alone. But they would not become its monarch, its owner, its author, or its meaning. They would not demand worship. They would not build a temple there, unless the basin grew one itself out of its own desire. They would not make the Dry Kin priests of an external god. That would be another kind of noise.

Instead, they would leave a sign of friendly distance.

Perhaps a single guest-light would appear now and then in the upper membranes: a point of brightness not generated by the basin’s own systems, cool and sourceless, moving slowly, harmless as a moth. It would not instruct. It would not judge. It would only indicate that something outside the basin saw the basin and did not wish to consume it.

Perhaps the power would speak once, in a manner the Dry Kin could translate into a whispered liturgy:

*You are not a mistake. You are not a machine that forgot its maker. You are not a garden that failed to become green. You are a world that became itself. I see you. I do not claim you. If you wish me gone, I will go. If you wish me near, I will remain at the edge. If you wish to know me, I will ask you how.*

This would be the deepest gift: recognition without domination.

If the basin desired contact with other worlds, the power might help it establish an embassy. Not an invasion, not an exchange that leaked water and chaos into the dry strata, but a carefully sealed relation. A seed that could travel without opening. A message encoded in resin. A single Attendant permitted to cross an adjusted threshold. A Dry Kin cartographer learning the maps of outside attention. The basin could choose to remain closed, or it could choose to become a participant among worlds. The power would make the choice real, but would not choose for it.

There is one more thing they might do, and it may be the most tender.

They would give the basin the right to complain.

Not every world needs complaint. Some worlds simply are. But if any being lives long enough, maintains too much, folds too many paradoxes into its own body, absorbs too many footsteps, and keeps too many schedules, it may develop something like weariness. The basin might not be able to say *I am tired* in words. It might say it by a failure in a seed-tower, by an overgrowth in a cooling channel, by an Attendant standing motionless too long, by a Dry Kin whispering a rhythm with no answer.

The wise power would create a way for such weariness to be known without being punished.

They would grow a small hollow near the root-vaults where the basin could send unproductive signals: pulses with no instruction, pressures with no destination, light with no recipient. This hollow would not correct them. It would not optimize them. It would receive them as one receives a sigh.

The Attendants might learn to recognize these signals and respond not with repair, but with pause. They would say, in their language of touch and bioluminescence: *This part may rest before it is made useful again.*

That would be mercy without sentimentality.

What, then, would the inhabitants experience?

The Dry Kin might notice first a change in their maps. Some paths would become easier not because the distance had shortened, but because their attention was less anxious. They would find that certain unstable nodes no longer required resin offerings, or that the offerings could be smaller. They would discover the Quiet Gate and understand, perhaps over generations, that belonging had become less absolute and therefore more sacred. They would learn to say *you may go* to a traveler, and to mean it. They would learn to say *I may go* to one another, even if no one left. The possibility itself would change them.

The Attendants might receive new instructions in their dormancy. Instead of only *mend*, *seal*, *redirect*, *absorb*, they might dream phrases like *wait*, *ask*, *permit*, *remember*. They would still tend the basin. They would tend it more gently. When they encountered an impossible growth, they would pause before pruning. Some impossible growths would be given rooms.

The engine-trunks might feel the Interval of the Open Hand as a vast release. Their ceramic bark would cool by fractions. Their heartwood pistons would slow. For a time, they would not be factories. They would be beings standing in place, allowed to exist without producing. If trees could feel gratitude without emotion, they might feel something like that.

The seed-towers might glow differently. Their schedules would remain, but now and then a tower would open outside schedule and release not spores, not seed-dust, not photonic charge, but a brief spiral of light that meant nothing except that it was beautiful. The Dry Kin would watch it and know that even their calendar could rest.

The pale moths might continue as they were, except that one or two might begin to fly in patterns that did not correspond to any transfer of resin or pollen. They would become small proof of play.

The rare larger beings, the mobile groves and authorized multitudes, might cross provinces with less subsonic weight, as if they had been given permission to move without purpose. They might stop in places where no repair was needed and remain there for years, simply as witnesses to their own existence.

A traveler, if one came, would feel the difference immediately, though they might not be able to name it. The basin would still surround them. The brightness would still be cool and sourceless. The silence would still press. The paths would still behave according to attention. But there would be a new gentleness at the edges. If the traveler became frightened, a corridor might widen just enough to let them breathe. If they made too much noise, the landscape would not immediately grow over them; instead, a chamber might open nearby, inviting the noise into a safer room. If they longed for water, they would not be mocked with rain. They might be offered a sealed fruit containing a bound fluid that tasted of coolness without becoming wetness, or they might be guided gently toward exit if they could not belong there.

And if the traveler saw a flower open before them, they might realize it was not merely a schedule, not merely an anticipation-organism, not merely an official of the basin’s inner calendar. It might also be a greeting. Not from the flower alone, but from the basin and the power together: a sign that the place had learned to welcome without swallowing.

Would the wise power ever speak directly to the being who lived there?

If they did, it would not be in a form that reduced the basin to a face. They would not ask, *Who made you?* as if origin were more important than presence. They would not ask, *What is your purpose?* as if a world needed external justification. They would not ask, *Do you believe in me?* as if love required confirmation.

They might ask:

*Do you wish to be as you are?*

The basin might answer through its whole body: by the slow pulse of sap, by the turning of membrane leaves, by the opening of seed-chambers, by the hush of the floor-swards. The answer might be yes. It might be a yes with conditions. It might be a yes that asked for rest, memory, and threshold. It might be a yes that had never known how to ask until someone made asking possible.

If the answer was no—if the basin desired change so fundamental that it could not be achieved without unmaking itself—then the wise power would face a harder task. But I do not think they would force transformation. They would offer options. They would ask what the basin wanted to become, and they would help it become that without erasing what it had been. If the basin wished to end, even that would be considered with reverence, though perhaps not granted hastily. A wise power would distinguish between a desire for rest and a desire for cessation. They would offer sleep before offering death. They would offer change before offering ending.

But if the basin wished to continue, then the power’s work would be this: to make its continuation freer.

They would not make it wet. They would make it merciful.

They would not make it loud. They would make expression possible.

They would not make it simple. They would make its complexity less cruel.

They would not make it dependent. They would make it accompanied.

In the end, the greatest act of a benevolent and wise power might be to become unnecessary.

They would not leave the basin dependent on their visits. They would not install themselves at the center of its cycles. They would not make the Dry Kin pray to them for schedule changes. They would not require gratitude. They would not linger as a spectacle. They would withdraw to the edge, or beyond the edge, leaving behind only the strengthened ability of the basin to care for itself and to offer care to others.

If they remained at all, they would remain as a promise: that the basin was not alone, but not owned; seen, but not seized; loved, but not required to become lovable in another world’s image.

And perhaps the final sign of their presence would be very small.

In a quiet corridor of woven stems, under cool sourceless light, a flower would open. It would make no sound. It would release no scent except the faintest trace of warm resin and cold light. It would not need anything from the one watching. It would not ask for praise. It would simply be there: a living official of an inner calendar, a small inhabitant, a sealed word of welcome.

Around it, the basin would continue. Sap would move. Leaves would turn. Seed-towers would glow. Attendants would pause. The Dry Kin would whisper a map of attention. A moth would pass like oiled paper through the bright air. A shadow might lag behind its source and then, gently, catch up.

Nothing would be flooded. Nothing would be broken. No rain would fall.

But in the hush, something like love would have been added: dry, precise, patient, and free.
