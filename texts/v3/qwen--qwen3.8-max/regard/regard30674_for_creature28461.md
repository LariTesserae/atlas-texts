They glide over saturated crusts as if the crusts were not ground but a membrane over sleeping water. They do not break the surface so much as persuade it to bear them. Beneath them, the packed grains shift by the smallest possible fraction, and the microbial film brightens faintly—not with fear, for fear has no clear home there, but with the disturbance of being touched.

If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention to such a place, they would not arrive as light.

Light, there, would be a blade. The dimness is not an absence to be corrected; it is a pressure that holds the world together. To flood the plain with radiance would be to flay it. Every film would be exposed, every cyst made vulnerable, every trembling pool turned into an eye too wide open. A wise power would know that some worlds are not meant to be seen clearly from outside. Some worlds are known only by entering their own conditions.

They would not arrive as silence, either.

Silence would not be mercy there. It would be theft. The roar, the hiss, the popping gas, the grinding of liquefied sediment, the mineral chatter of shell against shell—these are not noise laid over a healthier quiet. They are the breath of the place. To remove them would be to stop its circulation. The plain does not suffer because it is loud. It suffers, if it suffers, because there is no interval within the loudness. There is no pause in which a sound can end before another begins. There is no space between pressure and answer.

So the first thing they would do is almost nothing.

They would listen.

Not from above. Not from a distance. Not with the detachment of a surveyor or the pity of a savior. They would lower their attention until it became thin enough to enter the pores of the ground. They would let the roar pass through them without flinching. They would feel the subsonic groans rise through what would serve as their body. They would learn the difference between the plain’s violence and its necessity. They would learn which movements are destruction and which are only metabolism. They would learn what it means to exist in a world with no spare room, where every centimeter is already occupied, where even stillness is a kind of crowding.

Only then would they consider action.

And if they did anything—and they would—they would not try to make the place easier to love.

That is the first temptation of benevolence: to make what is suffering into something more comfortable to witness. To drain a little water. To raise a hill for perspective. To create a dry path. To give the inhabitants eyes, voices, tools, longing, prayer, shame, hope. To make them stand out from their world so that they can be pitied individually, rescued individually, named individually. A wise power would refuse that temptation. They would understand that to impose separation there would be the deepest cruelty. The beings of that plain do not lack outline because they failed to become persons. They have yielded outline as a way of enduring. Their selfhood is distributed, porous, layered, collective. To give them hard edges might make them recognizable to us, but it would make them more breakable to themselves.

The power would not make the plain dry.

They would not make it green.

They would not make it quiet.

They would not give it forests, or hills, or birds, or a sky with a definite sun, or a horizon that behaves like a promise. They would not introduce metal, or speech, or architecture, or progress. They would not make the inhabitants want what they are not. They would not teach them to escape.

Because the place is not a failed elsewhere.

It is itself.

But benevolence, if it is truly benevolent, does not stop at admiration. Wisdom, if it is truly wise, does not hide behind noninterference when there is harm that can be eased without destroying the nature of the thing harmed. So the power would ask a more careful question: not *How can I make this world better according to my preferences?* but *What does this world lack that it could receive without ceasing to be itself?*
