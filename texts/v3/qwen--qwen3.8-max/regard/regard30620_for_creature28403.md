If such a power looked upon that high, hot, plant-made tableland, they would not begin by changing it.

They would listen first.

Not from above, not from outside, not as a distant judge weighing the value of one life against another, but from within the pattern itself. They would enter the world as attention: a subtle alteration in the heat, a change in the quality of the air, a hush so brief and careful that it would not be mistaken for danger. In a place where silence can mean predator, storm, disease, or death, a benevolent wisdom would not announce itself with silence. It would announce itself as a pause inside the noise — a rest in the music, not the absence of it.

They would hear the layers.

First, the small lives: the rasp and tick and percussion of insects, the chewing inside old heartwood, the wing-beat of swarms moving like weather between the spaced giants. They would hear the hollow stems being used as drums, the seed-cases ringing under impact, the bark membranes vibrating with territorial calls, the low grinding of larvae working in unison beneath the surface of the world. They would hear the place as an instrument, already tuned by long ages of use.

Then the larger movements: the slow procession of grazers crossing a bark ridge, the cautious tapping of climbers testing hollow ground, the sudden eruption of bat-like creatures from an internode at dusk, the predators listening not for sound alone but for broken rhythm. They would hear hunger, fear, exhaustion, mating urgency, nest-defense, the blind pressure of too many lives in one place, the relief of shade, the need for water.

And then the people.

They would hear them before they saw them: the soft scuff of broad feet on polished root-slope, the creak of a lashing tested for strength, the tap-tap-tap of a knuckle against a hollow wall, the low chest-hum passed from one person to another when speech is swallowed by distance. They would hear the cordage being tied, the resin being warmed, the careful breath of a firekeeper bending over a contained ember. They would hear the restraint in them. The lightness of their marks. The way they moved through that immense living body as guests, not owners.

Only after listening would they consider what, if anything, to do.

And because they are wise, they would understand at once that the world is not empty, not ruined, not waiting to be corrected. It is already established. It has its own equilibrium, its own severities, its own beauty. It does not need to be made into a gentler world at the cost of being less itself. A tableland without predators would become unbalanced. A tableland without hunger would become stagnant. A tableland without the pressure of sound would lose one of the primary ways its creatures recognize one another and survive. Even the violence of the place is part of its coherence.

So the first thing such a power would do is refrain.

They would not abolish heat, because the heat drives the circulation of air, opens certain flowers, releases scent, warms the high surfaces into slow radiance at dusk. They would not flood the tableland with rivers, because too much water would rot the fibrous plains, collapse the hollows, breed disease, dissolve the careful architecture of hidden reservoirs. They would not silence the fauna, because the noise is information, territory, courtship, warning, life asserting itself. They would not multiply the people, because the landscape cannot bear density without being scarred. They would not make the plant substrate inert, because its responsiveness is one of the deep facts of the world.

They would not impose paradise.

Instead, they would create margins.

That would be their first active kindness: not the removal of hardship, but the widening of survival.

In the hottest stretches of the tableland, where sun-baked leaf plains shimmer and the air bends with heat, they would encourage hidden refuges. A root swelling might grow just a little thicker, storing more water through the dry pulse than it had before. A knot-bowl might deepen by the smallest degree, enough to hold condensation longer into the morning. A hollow internode might become slightly more resistant to collapse, its walls strengthened by fungal threads and slow deposition of resin, so that creatures could shelter there during a storm or a swarm. A broad crown might spread one degree wider, casting a cooler shadow over a drinking place at the hour when the light is harshest.

They would not make water abundant. That would change everything too much. But they would make hidden water more reliable.

They would teach the landscape to remember thirst without being destroyed by it.

Where mist is drawn up from lower cloud layers, they might incline certain crown-spines to catch it more efficiently, letting the droplets gather and drip down into lower root terraces. Where dew forms on leaf hairs at dawn, they might encourage those hairs to hold the beads a moment longer before evaporation claims them, giving small creatures time to drink. Where pitcher-like growths hold moisture, they might make the lips less treacherous for the very young, the very old, the clumsy, the desperate. Not safe for everyone. Not harmless. Just less arbitrarily fatal.

A wise power would know that mercy often lives in small alterations.

They would also tend the matter of fire.

Fire in such a world is both tool and terror. Nearly everything is plant matter, even when much of it is damp, massive, or slow to ignite. The people know this. They carry embers in closed seed-shells, build hearths inside dense bark rings, choose fuel carefully, and treat flame as something sacred and dangerous. A benevolent power would honor that reverence rather than remove the danger entirely. Fire is useful. Fire is knowledge. Fire is also a guest that must never be allowed to become the host.

So they would encourage green seams and moist barriers in the living substrate: belts of sappy tissue that resist ignition, fungal mats that retain dampness, root platforms that sweat faintly when heat becomes excessive. They would make certain seed-cases crack open less violently in flame, reducing the scatter of burning fragments. They would guide resin flows away from dry chaff where possible. They would not make the world fireproof, because that would alter its character and perhaps its ecology, but they would make catastrophic fire less likely.

And if a careless ember escaped, if a hearth-ring failed, if a dry wind rose at the wrong moment, then perhaps they would act more directly: a sudden shift in air current, a localized rain cell forming over a threatened crown, a swelling root rupturing with moisture just enough to blacken a creeping line of flame. Not with spectacle. Not with punishment. With correction. The way a gardener catches a falling branch before it strikes the ground.

They would also make quiet places.

This would be one of their strangest gifts.

In a world so loud that sound becomes pressure, the most merciful thing may not be more safety, more food, or more water, but brief relief from noise. Not silence as danger, but silence as shelter. They would create acoustic hollows where the roar softens: chambers lined with fungal felt, dense root curtains that absorb vibration, seed-shell barriers arranged by wind and growth into baffles. These places would not be common. If they were everywhere, they would alter communication, hunting, warning, and the whole acoustic ecology of the tableland. But here and there, hidden and discoverable, they would exist.

A mother could rest there with an infant whose crying has become ragged.

An old hunter could lie in one and feel the endless chorus withdraw to a distant murmur.

A predator could pause in one without triggering panic.

A person could enter one and hear, for the first time in days, the small sounds of their own breathing.

Such places would become sacred without being named. The people would find them by accident, then remember them by knot and gesture. They would teach one another not to speak of them too openly, because a refuge too well known becomes contested. A wise power would make them just rare enough that they remain gifts rather than possessions.

They would also work through the plant body itself.

If the tableland is a being — and in a sense it is, not a single mind but a vast responsive organism, a slow body made of root-felted plains, colossal stalks, buttress cliffs, hollow internodes, and hidden reservoirs — then a benevolent power would tend it as one tends a living body. They would not command it. They would support its own intelligence.

Where a wound has opened in an ancient stem, they would encourage resin to flow and tissue to seal. Where a root bridge has begun to weaken under generations of passage, they would stimulate supporting growth below, not to forbid crossing, but to prevent sudden collapse. Where a hollow has become foul with stagnant water and parasites, they might guide a scavenger beetle there, or encourage a rootlet to pierce the wall and drink the corruption dry. Where the wind corridors have become blocked by fallen material, they might send a few more borers to work the deadwood, opening passages again over time.

They would think in circulation, blockage, fever, healing.

A hot dry interval would be treated not as an enemy to be destroyed but as a stress to be endured. They would not end it immediately, because drought also selects, concentrates, reveals. But they would remember the vulnerable places: the shallow cups, the young root platforms, the hollows where small creatures are trapped without escape. And they would let those places be found by those with the knowledge to use them. They would not save every life. That is not wisdom. But they would preserve the future.

For the fauna, they would offer pattern rather than dominion.

They would not stop predation. Predation is part of the great pressure of the place. But they might make it less capricious. They would strengthen warning signals: a particular vibration in hollow stems before a storm, a scent released by certain leaves when a predator swarm is near, a change in insect chorus that can be recognized by many kinds of prey. They would encourage refuges that are not too easy: crevices that close slightly against intrusion, bark seams that admit small bodies but not larger ones, seed-shell shelters that can be entered but not easily broken.

They would make the world more legible to its inhabitants.

Not safe. Legible.

That is a different thing. A safe world can become a dead one. A legible world can be survived, understood, respected.

For the people, their intervention would be even more careful.

They would not appear as a ruler. They would not descend in visible form and demand worship. Such a manifestation would distort the culture of the place immediately. The people are already shaped by restraint; they make light marks, temporary shelters, portable tools. A god who demanded monuments would turn them into something they are not. A wise power would know that the surest way to ruin a people is to make them dependent on the divine.

So if they spoke at all, they would speak in the language already native to the people: knot, rhythm, resonance, scent, dream.

A dream might come to a water-finder: a pattern of dark veins in bark, leading to a swollen root that holds clean liquid. Not a revelation of infinite abundance, but a hint. A memory placed gently in the mind. A hunch that proves true.

A knot-maker might discover, while working fiber in the heat, that a certain loop resists tightening when the living surface grows. From that accidental wisdom, a new lashing could emerge: one that holds firm for a season and then loosens rather than strangling the plant beneath it. The power would not hand them the finished technique. They would plant the first recognition, then let human hands refine it.

A firekeeper might hear, in the pause between gusts, a rhythm that suggests a better way to pack embers in moist fiber. A hunter might notice that a particular distress call, if struck against a hollow at a certain angle, becomes confused and less effective at drawing prey into danger. Not because the power has forbidden hunting, but because they have introduced nuance. The hunter must choose more carefully. Choice is part of wisdom.

The people would receive laws not as commandments but as discovered truths.

They would say to one another, in their own way:

Listen before crossing.

Take only what can be carried.

Leave one cup untouched.

Fire must have a ring, a watcher, and a way to die.

No mark should be made that cannot heal.

When the sound changes, stop.

If you find shelter, do not break it.

If you find water, do not own it.

If you kill, do not waste.

If you hear silence, ask what it means before you move.

These would not be arbitrary rules. They would be the shape of survival in that world, made clearer by benevolent attention. The power would help the people see what they already half-knew. That is often what wisdom does: not introduce new truth, but strengthen recognition of truth already present.

They might also teach the people to become the tableland’s nervous system.

Because the inhabitants are few, mobile, and acutely aware, they are uniquely able to notice certain things the larger slow body cannot notice quickly. A hollow beginning to rot from within. A knot-bowl choked with debris. A fire risk building in a dry wind corridor. A nest of dangerous parasites spreading near a water source. A root bridge weakening at a hidden joint. The people cannot control these things, but they can observe, maintain, warn, and lightly repair.

A wise power would bless that role.

Not as masters. Not as owners. As listeners. As tenders. As those who carry knowledge between places.

They would encourage the cord-maps. They would make memory mobile and shared. Perhaps they would inspire a particular knot language used only for danger: three close loops meaning fire-risk, a long braid crossed with a thorn meaning poisoned water, a soft knot inside a hard one meaning refuge nearby. Perhaps they would guide the people to leave such cords not as permanent claims but as temporary messages, biodegradable and humble, eventually folded back into the living mass.

They would want the people to remain few, but not lonely.

That is a delicate balance. The tableland does not support dense settlement. Crowding would exhaust water, invite conflict, amplify disease, and draw too much insect or predator attention. Yet total isolation would be cruel and fragile. Knowledge would die with individuals. Children would have no one to learn from except a few nearby adults. Injury could become fatal simply because no one was near enough to help.

So the power would encourage loose connection: paths that are not roads, signals that are not shouts, gatherings that are temporary and seasonal. During flowering pulses or insect emergences, groups might meet, trade, exchange knowledge of water, share songs or rhythms, arrange marriages, tell stories, and then disperse again before the pressure of numbers becomes harmful. The power would favor these gatherings, not by making them grand, but by making them slightly safer. A favorable wind. A dry interval. A hidden reservoir found in time. A shared hearth that does not spread.

They would bless exchange without encouraging accumulation.

In such a world, wealth cannot be heavy. Metal is rare. Stone is absent in the usual sense. Large stores would attract pests, decay, theft, or the attention of creatures that chew through fiber and nest in accumulated matter. A wise power would help the people understand that their true wealth is relational: who knows which water, who remembers which route, who can be trusted in a storm, who carries the knot-patterns of old places. Their riches would be carried in the body, in memory, in obligation, in skill.

They would also care for death.

This would be one of the quietest and most important acts of their benevolence.

In a world where death is entered rather than buried, where a fallen body becomes a site of habitation almost immediately, there is room for horror but also for holiness. A wise power would not prevent death. They would make it less abandoned. They would encourage the decomposers to come swiftly but gently. They would let the dead be received by the world rather than rejected by it.

For the people, they might inspire small rites. Not grand tombs, because tombs are impossible and inappropriate. Not permanent graves, because the ground is alive and will move. But perhaps a knot placed near the remains. Perhaps a rhythm tapped on a hollow stem so that the sound carries once, then fades. Perhaps a seed placed in the mouth of the dead, not to grow as a monument, but to be carried away by wind or animal. Perhaps a brief covering of bracts, enough to protect the body from scavenging frenzy for the first hours, then removed or allowed to decay.

They would teach that the dead are not discarded.

They become texture. They become swelling in leaf-felt, dark patches on bark, hollows where something nests. This could be frightening, but it could also be tender. A wise power would make tenderness possible without lying. The dead do not vanish. They are folded in. The world keeps them, changed.

If the being who lives in the place is not the tableland itself but a single person — one among the scattered, light-footed inhabitants — then the power’s attention would be even more intimate.

They would not lift that person out of their world.

To remove them would be to deny them.

Instead, they would deepen their belonging. They might grant that person a more acute ear for resonance, so they can hear the difference between a hollow that is safe and one that is about to split. They might give them a dream of water in a dry season. They might let them be found by another when loneliness has become dangerous. They might place in their hands the knack of a knot, the patience of a firekeeper, the stillness of a hunter, the courage to stop when everyone else wants to run.

They would not make the person invulnerable. That would isolate them. They would make them more capable, more connected, more alert.

If that person suffered, the power would not necessarily remove the suffering. Sometimes suffering is the price of being alive in a world that matters. But they would make sure it was not meaningless. They would let it become knowledge, compassion, warning, song. They would let the person survive it if survival was possible. If it was not, they would let the person be received.

A benevolent and wise power would know the difference between saving and preserving.

Saving every individual life would unravel the web. Preserving the possibility of life, meaning, and continuation is the deeper kindness.

Still, there would be times when they acted more openly.

Not often.

If a fire threatened one of the great ancient trunk-forms whose loss would alter weather patterns across a vast portion of the tableland, they might intervene with sudden rain. If a swarm pulse became so overwhelming that it threatened to strip entire flowering platforms bare and collapse future food sources, they might encourage a counter-signal: a vibration that confuses the swarm briefly, or a scent that draws it away toward less vulnerable growth. If a band of people were caught in a thermal storm with nowhere to shelter, they might guide them toward a hollow they had not known before, not by miracle, but by a glint of light on resin, a remembered shape, a familiar tap of wind against bark.

If cruelty arose among the people — the hoarding of water, the deliberate burning of living surfaces, the destruction of shelters out of spite, the abandonment of children or elders in a way that violated the basic covenant of survival — then the power would not strike them down with lightning. That would create terror, not wisdom. But they would let consequences find shape. The hoarder’s water cup might dry. The fire-starter’s path might become difficult. The cruel person might find their knots refusing to hold, their signals unanswered, their presence unwelcome in the acoustic life of the place. Not as supernatural punishment, but as the world’s rejection of imbalance.

In a responsive landscape, disapproval can be felt.

A hollow could sound wrong when they approached.

Insects could fall silent.

Root surfaces could become slippery under their feet.

Not because the world is vengeful, but because it is alive and has learned, through long ages, what patterns sustain it.

The wise power would strengthen that learning.

They would also, perhaps, give one great gift that was almost invisible: a sense of being known.

Not watched in a cold sense. Known.

The people would not necessarily call it a god. They might call it the Listening, the Warm Attention, the Knot-Binder, the Quiet Within Sound. They might not name it at all. They would simply feel, at rare moments, that the world was not indifferent. That their lightness had value. That their restraint was seen. That when they moved carefully, when they left no scar, when they shared water, when they listened before crossing, they were not merely surviving. They were right.

That feeling would be more powerful than any miracle.

It would teach them to continue.

And what would the power receive in return?

Nothing that could be called payment.

Perhaps only this: the world continuing. The great stalks standing. The heat rising. The swarms moving like weather. The people tying knots, carrying embers, finding water, dispersing into distance. The tableland remaining itself, only a little less lonely, a little less cruel, a little more capable of enduring its own beauty.

If the power loved the place, they would not love it as something to be owned. They would love it as one loves a vast living being whose life is longer than one’s own: with patience, with grief, with delight, with restraint.

In the end, they might do what all truly wise powers do.

They would make themselves unnecessary.
