## The Kindness That Did Not Bring Rain

A benevolent and wise power, if it turned toward the High Sill, would first do the hardest thing: it would refuse to improve the place according to some lower idea of mercy.

It would not bring rain.

This must be said immediately, because so many kindnesss are imagined as softening. We imagine the dry place saved by water, the harsh place gentled by greenness, the silent place redeemed by birdsong. But water there would not be a gift. Water there would be a verdict. It would arrive as law from elsewhere, heavy and careless, turning the pale dust into slurry, darkening the brittle mica, collapsing the hollow stalks, choking the slow mineral breath of the world. It would make the High Sill into a lesser version of somewhere else.

A wise power would know that to drown a world is not always to hurt it with water; sometimes it is to drown it with mercy.

So they would not come as weather.

They would not come with storm, not with dew, not with cloud, not with the sweet smell of wet stone. They would not send springs bubbling up through basalt, because the place has no memory of water and its living things have been built around that absence. To give water suddenly would be to rewrite the Sill’s deepest covenant without asking.

They would not bring animals. No eyes, no hunger, no fear, no quick warm hearts. Animal life would panic there. It would move too fast, suffer too sharply, die too poorly. Its cries would wound the acoustic architecture. Its bones would become offenses. The Sill does not lack fauna because it is failed; it lacks fauna because it is elsewhere in the order of being.

They would not silence the roar.

That, too, would be violence. The sound is not noise in the sense of meaningless disturbance. It is the medium through which the place discovers itself. The Sill is being sung into shape, though the song is enormous and unfinished. To take away the sound would be to take away its becoming. It would be like stilling a thought halfway into existence.

No. The power would not arrive as rescue.

It would arrive as attention.

### I. They Would Come by Listening

If such a power can be said to move at all, they would move by becoming perceptible only as a change in pressure. Not a presence that overwhelms, but a presence that makes room. The thin air would not brighten. The shadows would not suddenly correct themselves. The mountains would not stand straighter because they had been seen.

At first, perhaps, nothing would happen.

That would be the first kindness.

The power would listen for a long time. It would listen through the slow tides of the roar, through centuries in which a single harmonic swelled and receded. It would listen to the grinding of unborn strata, to the glassy fronds flexing in wind that carried no moisture, to the copper-green cushions gathering static and dust. It would listen to the echoes that arrived before their causes, to the repeated shapes stuttering across the land, to the hollow stalks remembering tones they had never truly heard.

It would not correct these things.

It would let the Sill be strange.

It would let distance misbehave. It would let shadows lag. It would let peaks seem translucent in the dim light, as if unsure whether they intended to become opaque. It would let the unfinished mountains remain unfinished, because it would recognize that unfinishedness was not failure. It was potential still wearing its rough clothes.

Only after listening long enough would the power begin to perceive the being.

Not as one perceives a creature.

Not as one perceives a body moving through a landscape.

The being of the High Sill would not be a thing with a boundary. It would not hide in a cave, or crouch behind a scarp, or walk the basalt plains with patient mineral feet. It would be distributed. It would be the coherence of the place, if coherence is not too strong a word. It would be the pattern that persists across the pattern.
