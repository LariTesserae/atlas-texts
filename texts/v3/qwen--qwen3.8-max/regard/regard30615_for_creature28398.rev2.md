If a benevolent and wise power noticed the Underleaf, the first thing they would do is not heal it, not improve it, not speak into it, not remake it.

They would keep their hands off the wound.

A power truly wise would understand that some places are not problems to be solved. They are severe and living answers to conditions no outsider fully knows. The Underleaf is not a failed surface. It is not a cold hell. It is not a cavern waiting for sun. It is a plant-made interior with its own logic, its own beauty, its own economy of survival. To change it carelessly would be to destroy the very thing that makes it real.

So the power would begin with attention.

They would look into the bright void and see the root-ribs arching like the bones of a quiet god. They would see the light-veins descending through ice, cold and clean, giving illumination without comfort. They would watch a bladder tip, spill, freeze, and vanish. They would notice the moth landing on a leaf with the care of a creature that knows weight is a promise it must not break. They would feel the slow pressure wave moving up from the deep places, and they would understand that the Underleaf is not merely a location. It is a process. It is almost a body. It may even be a kind of mind, though not a mind that thinks quickly.

And they would see the people.

The knot-tiers. The water-listeners. The platform-builders. The tender-handed tenders of fragile shoots. The pale figures crossing root-ridges with rope at their belts and attention in every step. They would see how little the mind-makers demand from the world, and how much the world still refuses to give. They would see children taught not to cry loudly. They would see lovers bound by cords later untied. They would see the dead laid in root-craddles and left to become substrate. They would see that grief there is not absent; it is only made small so it can be carried.

A wise power would feel compassion for that.

Not pity. Pity is too careless. Pity looks down. Compassion, if it is wise, kneels beside.

And yet even compassion would not be allowed to act without restraint.

The first temptation of benevolence is to make everything safe. To warm the air. To fill the hollows with water. To make the plant-flesh abundant, the ground stable, the light gentle, the distances short. To gather the scattered people into a city. To give them fire, metal, certainty, permanence. To take away hunger. To take away fear. To take away the terrible quiet.

A wise power would refuse that temptation.

They would know that if they made the Underleaf warm, its architecture would fail. If they made it abundant, its people would no longer be shaped by the careful attention that allows them to survive. If they made permanence easy, the mind-makers would cease to be what they are: guests who have learned to move with an unfinished world. If they revealed themselves openly, with undeniable glory, they would overwhelm the quiet culture below. The mind-makers would become worshippers, or beggars, or rebels. They would no longer be themselves.

So the power would ask a harder question:

How can mercy enter a place without conquering it?

How can care be given without taking away dignity?

How can a world be made less cruel without being made false?

The answer would be subtle. The power would not do nothing. But they would do almost nothing that could be called spectacular.

They would perform a ministry of thresholds.

---

The first thing they would change is the wound through which one enters.

That narrow fracture in the pale, root-bound plain is young and sharp. It is still splintered. It is still raw. A wise power would see that it is both a door and an injury. They would not close it entirely, because a world sealed too completely becomes a tomb. But they would ease it. They would soften the worst of the broken lips. They would guide the fibers so that the descent is less likely to cut, trap, or collapse. They would make the passage narrow enough to require humility, but not so treacherous that every entry is a gamble with death.

If surface beings came with violence, the fracture would seem to confuse them. Their tools would glance off. Their confidence would thin. They would feel the place as a closed eye and mistake it for nothing. If they came with greed, the descent would seem long, cold, and unrewarding. If they came with curiosity tempered by reverence, the way would remain possible.

Not open. Not easy.

Only just.

The power might leave a faint mark there: a vein of cold light no thicker than a thread, running along the inner edge of the fracture. It would not glow brightly. It would only be visible when someone was about to give up, or about to do something careless. A thin bright line. A reminder that the world is not entirely indifferent.

They would call it nothing.

Later, travelers might call it the First Cord.

---

The second thing a wise power would do is make danger more legible.

They would not remove danger. That would be a mistake. The Underleaf requires uncertainty. Its people are made by attention. But much of the worst suffering there comes not from meaningful risk but from ambush: a bladder bursting without warning, a membrane tearing overhead, a root-plate releasing suddenly, a spore-cloud thickening until a traveler becomes disoriented and dies in a bright white blindness.

A benevolent power would reduce ambush.

They would give the Underleaf small signs.

Resin would bead differently before a major rupture: not clear, but milky, gathering at the edges of stressed tissue like a warning sweat. The mind-makers would learn to read it. They would teach children: white bead, stand away.

Certain hollow stems would give off a faint tone when pressure inside them reached a dangerous point. Not a loud sound. Nothing so obvious as a shout. Only a low vibration, felt in the fingertips, perhaps in the teeth. Enough for a careful person to notice.

The color of the air would change more honestly before spore releases. A milky cast would appear earlier, giving travelers time to cover their faces or seek shelter. A blue-green sharpening would signal freezing mist. A sudden clarity of shadow would mean a light-vein opening, not safety but exposure.

The power would not make these signs impossible to miss. That would create dependence. They would make them possible to learn. That would preserve skill.

In this way, the Underleaf would remain dangerous, but less deceitful.

Its beauty would still be severe. Its violence would still exist. But fewer people would die because the world lied to them.

---

The third thing they would do is create refuges.

Not many. A wise power would not scatter shelters across the Underleaf like gifts from a generous hand. Too many refuges would change the rhythm of travel, encourage complacency, and weaken the careful art of reading the place. But they would make a few.

These would be small.

A hollow inside a dense root-mass where the air is just warm enough to prevent freezing during a pulse. A pocket beneath a fallen leaf-blade where condensation gathers predictably and does not poison itself with resin. A cradle between two stable ribs where the pressure waves from the deep arrive softened, not amplified. A place where a tired person can anchor, eat, drink, and sleep without fear of being lifted, buried, or struck.

None would be obvious.

Each would have to be found by attention, or by following subtle signs: a polished groove where climbers pass, a faint knot-like growth in the plant tissue, a cluster of moths resting nearby. Once found, a refuge would not look like a temple. It would look like a natural accident that is slightly kinder than it should be.

The mind-makers would know them.

They would leave knots at their entrances. Not to claim them, but to say: this place held me.

Over time, a practice would grow. If someone survived a night in a refuge, they would leave behind a small thing that might help the next traveler: a twist of cord, a clean seed-cup, a pinch of dried spore-cake wrapped in leaf-skin. Not a store. Not a treasure. Just a breath passed forward.

The wise power would not need to be thanked in these places. The act of leaving something for another would be thanks enough.

---

The fourth thing they would do is make water easier to find, but not abundant.

Water is the Underleaf’s central scarcity. It appears as bead, film, drip, and brief spill. It is enough to torment, not enough to satisfy. A cruel world would make water invisible. A foolish savior would make rivers. A wise power would do neither.

They would make water more responsive to attention.

Certain stems, when tapped in the right way, would answer with a clearer echo if liquid moved inside. Certain bladders would develop a faint translucence where liquid remained, and a dullness where they had emptied. Certain condensation veins would hold their course longer before shifting, rewarding those who learned them. Certain pitcher-organs would be less likely to freeze entirely solid, retaining a small heart of drinkable water even in deep cold.

But the power would not make every vessel reliable.

They would not make thirst disappear. They would only make despair less likely.

There would still be long dry stretches. There would still be times when a traveler had to choose between stopping to listen and pushing forward before the cold returned. But fewer children would die with their mouths open under a still ceiling, waiting for a drip that had been hours away all along.

The mind-makers would say, perhaps, that the water had learned to answer.

They would be right, though not in the way they thought.

---

The fifth thing a benevolent and wise power would do is reduce loneliness without destroying solitude.

Solitude is natural in the Underleaf. It is woven into the ecology. Too many bodies in one place would disturb the fragile balance of moisture, heat, and growth. The people are scattered because the world requires them to be scattered. But solitude can become annihilation when it convinces a person that they are the only living mind left.

The power would not make the mind-makers numerous. They would not force settlements. They would not create crowded shelters or fertile centers that drew everyone together. That would break the place.

But they would make meetings less rare than pure chance would allow.

Two travelers moving through different routes might find themselves near the same condensation vein at the same time, not by miracle, but by subtle guidance: a slight thaw here, a slight frost there, a moth moving in a direction one of them follows. A lost person might hear, or feel, the tap-pattern of another on a root not far away. A child separated from its family might come upon a polished groove leading to a place where someone recently rested.

The power would not promise reunion.

They would only make the world slightly less likely to let people vanish entirely from one another.

And in the worst cases, when someone was truly alone and death was near, the power would offer a smaller mercy.

A moth might land on them.

Not a speaking moth. Not a messenger with words. Just a pale winged thing, cool and delicate, settling on a hood or an outstretched hand. A tiny pressure. A brief proof that life is not only the self.

Or a warmth might pass through the air, no more than a breath. Or a spore-cloud might thin and reveal a distant shape that might be a shelter. Or the person’s own heartbeat might slow enough for fear to become, for one moment, bearable.

The dying would not be saved every time.

But fewer would die believing they had been completely unseen.

---

The sixth thing they would do is help memory without demanding permanence.

The mind-makers already accept erasure. Their knots fade. Their routes shift. Their shelters are absorbed by growth or glazed into ice. They do not build monuments because the Underleaf does not permit monuments. A foolish power might give them stone. A sentimental power might make their records eternal.

A wise power would do neither.

They would give memory a slightly deeper root.

Certain resins would accept impressions better than others: a knot pressed into soft sap might remain legible for years, even if the cord itself decayed. Certain stems, when cut carefully, would grow back in patterns that preserved the shape of an old notch. Certain route-lines would become faintly persistent, not because they were magical, but because the local growth favored the same paths the mind-makers favored.

The world would still change. Nothing would be fixed. But the past would not vanish quite so completely.

And the power might give the mind-makers one new practice, arriving not as command but as instinct.

When someone was lost, those who loved them would tie a knot and leave it on a stable rib. Not to summon them. Not to bind their spirit. Only to say: your route was known.

If the lost person returned, they would touch the knot and untie part of it.

If they did not return, the knot would remain until the Underleaf erased it.

This would be a small thing. But small things matter in a world where grief has nowhere to go.

---

The seventh thing a wise power would do is speak to the Underleaf itself.

Not in words. Not as a gardener speaks to a plant, exactly, but perhaps as a hand rests on a living body and knows its rhythm.

They would encourage coherence.

The Underleaf is turbulent because it is young, broken, and unevenly heated. Warm pulses rise from below and collide with cold. Structures expand, contract, crack, and shed. The power would not stop this. They would only help the whole system absorb its own changes more gracefully.

Deep channels would widen slightly where pressure built too violently. Certain root-masses would become better at distributing heat, so a thermal pulse would arrive as a slow breath rather than a rupture. Certain membranes would learn to flex before they tore. Certain frozen sap-veins would redirect stress away from inhabited regions.

The Underleaf would still be unstable. It would still make and unmake itself. But its catastrophes would be less arbitrary.

And the power would encourage recognition.

Not consciousness as surface beings understand it. Not a speaking world. Not a deity waking beneath the ice. But a faint increase in sensitivity. The Underleaf might begin to respond to care.

Where a tender repaired a wounded stem, the tissue might seal more quickly. Where a route was used gently, frost might accumulate less thickly. Where a shelter was built without cutting living tissue, a root-rib might grow in a way that strengthened it. Where a child’s first offering of water was poured into a crack, a faint bead of clean condensation might appear nearby hours later.

Nothing obvious.

Nothing that would make the mind-makers believe they controlled the place.

Only enough response to teach them that their gentleness was not lost in a void.

The Underleaf would remain vast and mostly indifferent. But it would be slightly less blind.

---

The eighth thing they would do is protect the deep presences.

The Slow Ones. The Deep Roots. The Ones Who Turn the Warm Pulses. Whatever they are, they are too large to be treated as subjects of improvement. A wise power would not wake them. They would not interrogate them. They would not bind them to human purposes.

They would only ease the harshest edges of their influence.

If a deep pulse began, the power would help it announce itself in time. If a region became dangerous because a vast organ was shifting below, travelers would feel the warning in their knees, in the soles of their feet, in the sudden tightening of their own breath. They would know to move away. They would know not to dig. They would know not to cut.

If someone approached the deep places with arrogance, the power would make the experience uncomfortable without being fatal. The air would feel heavy. The light would seem too bright. The sound of one’s own breathing would become disturbingly loud. The traveler would feel, in the body’s oldest language, that they were standing near something that did not need them.

If someone approached with need, the way away would be easier to find than the way in.

The deep presences would remain mysterious. They would remain necessary. They would remain beyond negotiation. But they would be less likely to crush small lives without purpose.

A wise power understands that reverence is not created by explanation. Reverence is created by limits.

---

The ninth thing they would do is give dreams.

Not prophecy. Not commands. Not detailed maps that would make exploration unnecessary. But subtle dreams suited to the mind-makers’ way of knowing.

A listener sleeping near a root might dream of pressure moving through long white channels, and wake with a better sense of where the next pulse might begin. A child afraid of open distances might dream of a moth crossing a vast bright void without falling. A traveler grieving someone lost might dream of a knot being untied gently, not by violence, and wake with the sorrow still present but less sharp-edged.

The power would not force these dreams.

They would offer them the way rain offers itself to a surface: some places receive it, some do not.

Those who dreamed would not necessarily understand why they felt steadier afterward. They might say the Underleaf had been quiet that night. They might say the light had been kind. They might touch a knot on their wrist and think of no one in particular.

That would be enough.

---

The tenth thing a benevolent and wise power would do is not reveal themselves.

This is perhaps the hardest part.

A power that truly loves does not need to be seen. It does not demand monuments. It does not require gratitude as payment. It does not make its children dependent on its voice. It does not interrupt a culture just to prove its own reality.

So the power would remain almost invisible.

They might be suspected. They might be sensed. In certain moments, the air in the Underleaf would feel less empty than it should. A traveler might pause and tilt their head, not hearing anything, but feeling as if some immense attention had passed nearby without touching them. A moth might settle on a light-pod and glow for a second brighter than its own body. A knot might be found where no one remembered tying one.

But there would be no face in the light.

No voice from the roots.

No commandment carved in heartwood.

If the mind-makers began to speak of the power at all, they would speak carefully. They would not build temples. They would not create priests. They would perhaps add one new rule to their old rules:

Do not mistake kindness for ownership.

And perhaps another:

If you find a knot you did not tie, leave one behind.

---

There would be one sign, though.

Not a proof. A sign.

In moments of extreme need, when a traveler was lost, or a child was cold, or a tender was about to give up on a damaged shoot, or an old listener felt the final pressure wave of their life approaching, they might find a small knot of pale fiber where no knot should be.

It would be simple. A loop crossed by a short cord, but tied in a way no ordinary hand had made. It would not glow. It would not warm the fingers. It would not solve the problem.

But looking at it, the person would feel a change inside themselves. Not hope exactly. Something quieter. The sense that being seen did not require being rescued. The sense that the world was not only process, not only pressure, not only cold light moving through plant-flesh.

It would mean:

You are here.

You are not nothing.

Continue if you can.

Rest if you must.

You were noticed.

The mind-makers might call it the Guest Knot, because it would always feel as if it had been left by someone passing through. Or they might call it the Witness Cord. Or they might have no name for it at all, because some things are too close to the edge of language to be named safely.

When they found one, they would not take it. They would touch it, perhaps, with one fingertip. Then they would go on.

If they survived, they might tie their own knot nearby.

Not as worship.

As answer.

---

A wise power would also care for the smallest lives.

The translucent threads in the bladders. The frost-mites. The moths. The climbers. The blind, sparse things that exist in pockets of liquid and die in air like needles of ice. These lives are thin, but they are not worthless. A benevolent power would not elevate them above their nature. They would not make the bladder-threads immortal or give the moths voices. But they would protect the conditions that allow such lives to continue.

They would prevent too many bladders from freezing at once. They would keep enough liquid pockets scattered through the hollow that the smallest animals could persist. They would ensure that when a warm pulse released spores, some surfaces remained undisturbed long enough for mites to feed. They would keep the air clean enough for moth-wings to remain unharmed by resin-dust.

This would not be obvious to the mind-makers.

But the ecology would hold.

And in a place where life is withheld, holding the smallest lives is a form of reverence.

---

What would the power not do?

They would not make the Underleaf green in the surface sense. They would not fill it with forests, rivers, birds, or warm soil. They would not turn it into a garden.

They would not make the mind-makers more numerous than the place can support. They would not give them cities, roads, or permanent bridges. They would not remove their need for rope, attention, and restraint.

They would not eliminate the cold. The cold is part of the architecture. Remove it, and the Underleaf becomes something else entirely.

They would not end hunger completely. Hunger is cruel, but a world without need would make the mind-makers careless. They would still eat sparingly. They would still tend shoots. They would still listen for water. But they would no longer starve simply because chance was blind.

They would not make death meaningless, but they would reduce meaningless death.

They would not make the Underleaf safe. They would make it trustworthy enough that courage remains possible.

That distinction is the heart of wisdom.

Safety can become a cage. Trustworthiness becomes a ground for action.

---

Over time, the mind-makers would notice changes.

Not all at once. They are too patient for sudden belief. But stories would shift.

A traveler would say that a certain stem had hummed under their palm just before a root-plate split. A tender would say that a shoot they protected had grown faster than expected after they repaired the windbreak. A child would say that a moth had led them to a place where water beaded on a leaf. An old listener would say that the deep pulses had begun to arrive with a longer warning, as if the world had learned to inhale before moving.

No one would declare a god.

They would only say: the place is listening better now.

And they would become more careful, not less. Because if the world listens, one must also listen.

They would add new practices.

When entering a new region, they would wait before touching anything. They would place a palm on the nearest stable surface and feel for answer. If the surface felt tight, brittle, or dry, they would move on. If it felt faintly damp, faintly responsive, they would proceed with respect.

When they took water, they would leave a small mark of thanks: a notch, a knot, a drop returned to the surface. Not payment. Recognition.

When they found a body laid in a root-cradle, they would not disturb it. They might leave a filament of bark nearby. If a pale shoot grew from the place, they would not cut it. They would not worship it either. They would only note that the world had continued.

When a child was born, they would tie a simple knot and place it near the entrance of the shelter. If the child survived the first cold season, they would untie it and keep one strand. If the child died, they would leave the knot as it was and let the Underleaf take it.

Their religion, if it could be called that, would remain practical.

Holiness would not be separated from survival.

---

Would the power ever speak directly?

Perhaps once.

Not in words. Not even in a voice that could be remembered clearly.

Maybe in a dream shared by several listeners at once, during a season when the Underleaf was particularly unstable. In the dream, they would stand on a root-ridge beneath a vault of pale ribs. The light would be very bright. The air would be very still. Far below, something vast would move, and the dreamers would feel fear.

Then a pressure would pass through the root beneath their hands. Not painful. Not commanding.

It would feel like this:

Wait.

Small.

Move.

Listen.

You are not alone, but you are not central.

The world is not finished. Neither are you.

When they woke, they would not remember the dream exactly. They would only know that the next pulse came while they were away from the unstable region, and that they had left early without being able to say why.

They would tap the warning into stems as they traveled.

Others would listen.

That would be the only miracle anyone ever spoke of, and even then they would disagree about the details.

---

If the power were very wise, they would also protect the Underleaf from the surface.

The fracture is recent. That means the place has been exposed. Exposure invites intrusion. Surface beings often do not mean harm, but they are heavy in every sense: heavy in body, heavy in tools, heavy in assumptions. They bring heat. They bring noise. They bring the desire to name, map, claim, extract, and improve.

The power would not let that happen easily.

They would make the plain above forgetful. Not by enchantment, but by ordinary obscurity. Snow would shift. Roots would rearrange. The fracture would appear only when the light and season allowed. Those who sought it with domination in their hearts would find nothing but pale ice and tangled root-scars. Those who sought it with humility might find a narrow opening, cold and bright, waiting like a question.

Even then, the descent would test them.

It would ask: Can you be quiet? Can you be small? Can you walk without claiming? Can you see without taking? Can you accept that you are not the center of what lives here?

If not, the Underleaf would remain closed to them.

If yes, they might enter.

And if they entered, the wise power would not guarantee their survival. It would only give them the same chance it gave the mind-makers: legible danger, occasional refuge, and the possibility of being changed.

---

Would the power ever judge the inhabitants?

Not in the way surface powers judge.

They would not measure the mind-makers by wealth, permanence, or expansion. They would not ask why they had not built more. They would not ask why they had not conquered the place. They would understand that the deepest achievement of the mind-makers was that they had learned to live without becoming heavy.

The power would value their knots.

Their careful water-listening.

Their restraint.

Their ability to meet another person with open hands after long solitude.

Their way of leaving no wound larger than necessary.

If the power loved anything in the Underleaf, they would love that.

Not the beauty alone. The beauty is severe, but beauty can be cold. They would love the ethical shape of survival there: the way life persists by noticing, by limiting itself, by making small repairs, by accepting impermanence without surrendering meaning.

That is a rare thing.

A wise power would protect it more fiercely than any treasure.

---

And what of the Underleaf itself?

Would it know it was being cared for?

Perhaps.

Not as a person knows. Not as a child knows. But as a body knows when fever has been eased. As a root knows water. As a membrane knows pressure has changed.

The Underleaf might become slightly more integrated. Its pulses might spread with less violence. Its broken places might knit more often than they failed. Its light might remain cold, but less accusing. Its emptiness might remain vast, but less desolate.

It might begin, in the slowest possible way, to tolerate tenderness.

When a mind-maker repaired a torn membrane with woven strips and resin, the membrane might hold longer. When a drip-garden was polished over years, the water might learn that groove. When a shelter was anchored without cutting living tissue, the root-ribs might grow around the cords rather than splitting them.

The place would not become gentle.

But it would become less accidental.

And that would be enough.

---

If one stood in the Underleaf after such a power had touched it, one might not see much difference.

The light would still be cold. The distances would still deceive. The air would still freeze breath into crystal. A bladder would still tip somewhere beyond sight, spill briefly, and freeze. A spore-cloud would still drift through the empty volume like slow snow. A young shoot would still emerge pale as bone and crack when the cold returned.

But there would be something else.

A feeling of being held at the very edge of abandonment.

Not safety. Not comfort. Not promise.

Only a slight reduction in despair.

One might find a knot not tied by human hands. One might hear a stem give warning. One might come to a refuge just as the pulse began. One might meet another traveler at the exact moment both needed water. One might die alone, perhaps, but with a moth on one’s hood and a faint warmth in the air, and the last feeling would not be that the universe was empty.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

They would not save the Underleaf from being itself.

They would help it be itself without cruelty.

They would not make the mind-makers into a great civilization.

They would make their smallness dignified.

They would not remove the dark beneath the light.

They would make the dark less likely to swallow someone without witness.

And if asked, later, what sign the power left behind, the mind-makers would perhaps answer in their quiet way.

They would hold up a cord.

They would show a knot: a loop crossed by a short strand, simple and strong.

Then they would say, softly:

It means someone passed here.

It means the way is not only ours.

It means continue.
