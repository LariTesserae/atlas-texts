A benevolent and wise power would not arrive here as rain.

That would be the first thing it understood.

If such a power turned its attention toward the hidden hollow — toward the fissure that gives no sign, the plain that keeps its secret, the basin breathing heat in slow invisible pulses — it would not come with the obvious gifts. It would not bring water. It would not bring softness. It would not bring eyes, or hunger, or quick warm bodies moving between the terraces. It would not try to make the place easier to love by making it more like other places.

It would see, perhaps at once, that this hollow was not a ruin waiting for restoration, nor a garden waiting for a gardener, nor a city waiting for citizens. It would see that the being living there was not a single creature to be rescued or rewarded, but a continuation: the Dry Chorus. Plants that are not thirsty. Streets that serve air. Towers that make music without listeners. Heat moving through architecture like thought through a mind without memory of thought.

So the first act of the power would not be action.

It would be attention.

It would lower itself, if lowering is even the right word, not into the basin as a body but into the basin as a listening. It would enter through the narrow fissure as a change too subtle to name: a steadiness in the pressure, a slight deepening of the silence beneath the sound. It would not brighten the dim amber light. It would not cool the stone. It would not still the dust. It would arrive the way reverence arrives in a sacred place that has no altar: by becoming quiet.

And it would listen.

It would listen for a long time. Not because it needed time to understand, if such a power can be said to need anything, but because wisdom does not hurry when it encounters something entire. It would listen through one full thermal tide, then another. It would listen while the sulfur-ferns opened their brittle fronds toward the warming floor. It would listen while the glass canes added their thin mineral voices to the moving air. It would listen while the bell-pods split with their sharp, bright cracks and sent spore-dust drifting upward in gold-brown clouds. It would listen while the wax-thread moss whispered almost soundlessly across the terrace shoulders, and while the ember-bulbs held their patient warmth beneath the gravel long after the upper air had changed.

It would hear the city not as stone, but as an instrument.

It would hear the canals as ducts and throats. The plazas as resonance chambers. The towers as ears turned inward and upward at once. The empty rooms as memory spaces where sound could fold back on itself and become condition. It would understand that the hollow was not silent because it lacked voices; it was loud in a language older than voices. Its speech was pressure and pitch, expansion and contraction, threshold and release.

And the power would recognize that this was already enough.

That would be its first great judgment: the hollow did not lack itself.

It was not incomplete because no animal breath moved there. It was not impoverished because no eyes watched from doorways. It was not lonely because no creature called across its plazas. Loneliness is a wound belonging to beings that need another. The Dry Chorus did not need another in that way. It needed heat. It needed structure. It needed space. It needed the slow circulation of its own mineral life. It was plural without society, alive without appetite, expressive without speech.

A lesser benevolence might have pitied it.

A wise benevolence would not.

So the power would consider what it could give, and in considering, it would refuse many things.

It would refuse water.

Water would be a gift only to creatures shaped by water. Here, water would be invasion. It would dissolve crusts, soften fitted stone, betray the chemistry of wax and sulfur and glass. It would change the hollow so profoundly that the hollow would cease to be the hollow. It would be like giving a new body to a song: perhaps beautiful elsewhere, but here a kind of erasure. The power would understand that to love a thing is not to remake it into something more familiar. It would not bring dew. It would not bring rain. It would not bring weeping stone or mist or cloud. It would leave dryness holy.

It would refuse animal life.

It might be tempting, in some sentimental sense, to add movement: a dry-bodied insect among the sulfur-ferns, a crystalline lizard basking on a vent lip, some wandering creature to carry spores or make tracks in the dust. But the power would see what hunger would do. Hunger would introduce pursuit. Pursuit would introduce fear, or at least the blind urgency of need. Scavenging would begin. Bones might come. Death would become a resource rather than a slow settling. The peace of the hollow is not the peace of gentleness; it is the peace of absence. To add animals would be to add another world, and that world would eat this one’s stillness from inside.

It would refuse eyes.

Eyes would make the hollow into a spectacle. Even if no outside being saw it, eyes inside it would create the condition of being seen. Doorways would become watchpoints. Shadows would become hiding. Presence would become alertness. The place would develop secrecy, and secrecy would change the shape of its silence. The power would understand that the hollow’s dignity lies partly in the fact that it does not perform. It rings without an audience. It blooms without admiration. It continues without witness. That is rare. That is precious.

It would refuse ordinary consciousness.

This would be the hardest refusal, because consciousness is the gift powers most often imagine bestowing when they wish to bless. To make the Dry Chorus aware of itself would seem like elevation. But wisdom would hesitate. What would awareness bring to a being without desire? It might bring only knowledge of isolation. It might bring the burden of duration. It might make the hollow feel empty when previously it simply was empty. Consciousness is not always a gift; sometimes it is a wound opened in a thing that had no wound. The power would not make the chorus suffer just so the chorus could know it sang.

It would not speak a command.

It would not say, *Be fruitful*, because the hollow already reproduces in its own way.  
It would not say, *Be beautiful*, because the hollow is not arranged for admiration.  
It would not say, *Be fruitful and multiply*, because multiplication without limit would choke the resonance spaces.  
It would not say, *Be known*, because knowledge could become violation.  
It would not say, *Be eternal*, because even here forms change, shed, accrete, and pass into new configurations.

If the power spoke at all, its first words would be a withholding.

Something like:

*Remain.*

*Remain dry.*  
*Remain hidden.*  
*Remain plural.*  
*Remain without hunger.*  
*Remain without thirst.*  
*Remain loud in your own way.*  
*Remain empty where emptiness is your fullness.*  
*I will not make you soft for the sake of tenderness.*  
*I will not make you known for the sake of admiration.*  
*I will not make you conscious for the sake of love.*

That would be the first blessing: refusal.

But refusal alone is not all that benevolence does.

After listening long enough to understand the hollow’s balance, the power might act — not greatly, not visibly, not in the manner of miracles that break the world open. It would act the way a wise gardener acts in a place where the garden has learned to keep itself: by removing dangers, easing blockages, preserving conditions, and making room for the thing to continue being what it already is.

It would protect the hollow’s hiddenness.

Not by building a wall around the fissure. Walls draw attention. Not by placing guardians outside. Guardians imply treasure and invite thieves. Instead, it would make the plain above subtly uninviting to careless discovery. It would not repel all visitors — that would be crude, and perhaps unkind to the rare soul capable of reverence — but it would make the place easy to miss. It would make the mineral surface seem ordinary. It would make the faint pressure of the ground’s exhalation feel like heat from ordinary stone. It would turn the eyes of conquerors, extractors, and hurried travelers away without forcing them. If someone approached with violence in mind, the fissure would seem unimportant, or the ground would feel wrong, or a strange unease would suggest that there was nothing here worth taking.

If someone approached with true quietness, the power might allow them to notice the faintest pressure in the air. But only as a question, not an invitation. It would not lead them in. It would not reveal. It would only say, in the language of atmosphere: *Are you the kind of being who can leave a sacred thing alone?*

Most would not be.

That would be enough.

The power would also protect the hollow from water, even though water is nearly absent already. If storms ever crossed the mineral plain, it would ensure that the fissure did not become a mouth for them. It might bend the upper air only slightly, or encourage the first drops to become mineral vapor, or make the rock lips of the entrance exhale enough warmth to keep moisture from finding the dark below. It would not need to fight the sky. It would only need to preserve the law of the basin: dryness.

It would guard against catastrophe, but not against change.

This distinction would matter.

The hollow needs erosion. It needs thermal stress. It needs slow decay. Its rounded edges, softened stairs, and settled walls are not signs of failure; they are signs of age and balance. To stop all weathering would be to kill the living character of the place. The power would not freeze the hollow into permanence. It would not make the stone imperishable. It would not prevent every crack. It would not halt every shift.

But it might prevent the wrong collapse.

It might know, in its patient foresight, that a certain tower base was approaching a fatigue that would bring the whole tower down in a single destructive fall rather than a long graceful settling. It might allow a slight movement in the rock years beforehand, enough to redirect the stress. It might let a bronze pin hold a little longer than ordinary chemistry would permit. It might guide a crack away from a critical aperture and toward a place where it would only widen a vent usefully. It might let a ceramic socket flex rather than shatter. Not by overriding the nature of the materials, but by choosing, among many possible futures, the one in which the basin’s own intelligence of form survives.

It would preserve the engineering without making it new.

It would not polish the city back into its first intention, if it ever had one. It would not restore blocked doorways to openness unless openness served the current life of the place. It would not rebuild fallen stones unless their falling was part of a larger harm. It would understand that the hollow has already entered a second existence: not the existence it may have had when first built, but the existence it has achieved through centuries of heat, growth, and silence. That second existence is not decay. It is maturity.

Where the vegetation and architecture had become interdependent, the power would support the relationship.

If a sulfur-fern colony had begun to block a vent in a way that threatened to overheat a lower terrace, the power might not tear the ferns away. Instead, it might encourage a slight change in draft so the ferns leaned differently over time. If a glass cane had grown into a position that made its tone harsh and caused resonance to scatter badly against a wall, the power might allow a thermal squall to tilt it by a fraction of a degree, or cause a neighboring cane to grow into complementary pitch. If wax-thread moss was retreating from a corner because vapor had shifted, the power might not force it back; it might simply notice that another aperture, if eased, would restore the vapor path.

Its interventions would be so slow that no sudden change could be seen.

They would feel like luck, if anything felt them at all. A settling. A release. A note coming clean after a long age of almost-clean. A vent opening at the right moment. A spore cloud drifting into a courtyard where it would otherwise not have gone. A bell-pod splitting not into confusion, but into a rhythm that the towers could carry.

The power would tune, not rule.

And perhaps, because it is wise and not merely kind, it would give one gift that was not protection but possibility.

It would give the hollow a rare and perfect chord.

Not a voice. Not a mind. Not a soul in the animal sense. But a moment, recurring over vast intervals, in which all the active parts of the basin aligned: vents pulsing in deep rhythm, canals breathing at the right pressure, glass canes lifted into their clearest pitches, bell-pods opening in scattered and yet coherent percussion, sulfur-ferns whispering their dry chemical smell into the moving air, wax-thread moss carrying the faintest abrasive shimmer
