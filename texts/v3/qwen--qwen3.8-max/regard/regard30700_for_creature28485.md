One could spend a long time in that place and never know whether one had seen the dweller or only the hollow’s own patience briefly become a body.

If such a place and such a being drew the attention of a benevolent and wise power, the first thing they would do is refrain from arriving.

They would not come as a figure of light. They would not descend with sound, or with retinue, or with any sign that demanded the hollow become something other than itself. Fire would be too bright for the moss. Thunder would be too blunt for the water’s fine listening. A voice, even a kind one, would have edges there. In that high, warm, wet room, even blessing might feel like a tool if it were spoken too plainly.

So the power would stand, if standing is the right word, outside the hollow and yet fully aware of it. They would give it the oldest gift wisdom knows: attention without seizure. They would look without claiming. They would listen without interrupting. They would let the place be the size of itself.

And what would they hear?

Not merely water. Not merely wind. They would hear the hollow as a single instrument played by slow causes: the drip answering the drip, the sliding film of moisture hissing over mineral lips, the low divided breath of air passing among fern-fronds and hanging roots. They would hear how the stone shapes sound, how the curvature gives the drops their multiplied life, how the basalt center gathers the resonance and makes of it a pulse. They would hear the dweller’s membranes trembling with all of it, each fold opening and closing like a small dark ear made of water and patience.

They would see the creature not as an occupant of the hollow but as the hollow’s stillness become flesh. They would see how it chooses the central stone not from possession but from affinity. They would see how the moss on its shoulder is not ornament but covenant. They would see how the rare life of the place moves around it without fear, not because the dweller is gentle in the moral sense, but because it has become part of the weather of the hollow. It is not a danger. It is a condition.

A lesser kindness might try to improve such a place.

It might give the dweller speech, so that it could name what it is. It might give it hands, so that it could shape what it needs. It might give it companions, so that it would not be alone. It might roof the hollow against excess rain, or deepen the pools, or widen the entrance, or make the plants flower in conspicuous colors. It might carve a path to the rim so that pilgrims could come and admire it. It might leave a mark there, a sign, a blessing, a proof of divine favor.

A wise power would know better.

They would understand that the hollow is not incomplete. Its simplicity is not poverty. Its lack of built things is not absence. Its smallness is not limitation. Its strangeness is not a flaw waiting to be explained. It has arrived at a kind of completion that many larger and louder places never reach: it has become faithful to its own nature.

To give the dweller speech might make it ask for things. To give it tools might make it separate from the place. To make it visibly sacred might bring hands toward it. To make it known too widely might turn it into a destination, and destinations attract taking.

So the power would not give the dweller what it does not need.

They would not make it wise as humans are wise, because human wisdom often begins in distance and ends in dominion. They would not make it lonely by giving it a mind full of comparison. They would not make it immortal in a way that severed it from the slow changes it belongs to. They would not make it a symbol. They would let it remain what it is: a body fitted to wet stone, to vibration, to mist, to the long discipline of continuing.

But benevolence is not passive. Wisdom is not mere restraint.

If they did anything, it would be subtle, and most of it would happen at the margins.

They would protect the hollow without making a fortress of it. They would not surround it with walls or warnings, because that would introduce the very logic of possession the place lacks. Instead, they would make the world around it slightly less likely to break in.

If a road were planned along the mountain, the surveyor’s eye would slide elsewhere. Not because the power deceived, but because there are places that should remain unmeasured, and a wise kindness knows how to let such places be forgotten by ambition. If a fire came up from below, the mist would thicken at the rim without explanation. If a drought stretched the sky thin for longer than usual, some hidden seam of water in the rock would continue to weep—not enough to make the hollow lush beyond its character, but enough to keep the lowest pool from becoming dust. If frost came too harshly, the warm breath stored in the stone would linger a little longer than expected. If a collector sought the orchid-like plants, the slope would seem less promising, the light less certain, the desire would fade before it became a plan.

None of this would look like miracle. That would be part of the point.

The power would not abolish the hollow’s vulnerabilities. They would not remove all hardship, because the dweller’s life is made in part by its adjustments: the retreat into the cleft during dryness, the revival when mist returns, the long quiet that is not quite sleep. To take away every difficulty would be to take away the shape of the being. But the power would keep difficulty from becoming annihilation. They would preserve the possibility of return.

They would also allow the hollow to be witnessed, but rarely, and only by those capable of leaving it unharmed.

Once in a long while, they might let a person climb near the rim—not someone seeking conquest, not someone seeking proof, not someone with hands eager to name and carry away, but someone quieted by altitude, by weather, by some inward fatigue that had burned off the need to take. Such a person might stand at the edge in the thinning mist and see, for a few breaths, the whole arrangement: the black central stone, the pools, the spaced plants, the shining threads on the rock, the rounded shape that was not quite a boulder.

They might not understand what they saw. That would be good.

If the dweller became aware of them, it would not flee. The person, if they were the right kind, would feel a strange hesitation—not fear, but reverence arriving without permission. They would sense that to enter carelessly would be to spoil something. They would sense that even a footprint could be a violence there.
