If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention toward that high, cold, damp city-country, the first thing it would do is nothing that could be seen.

It would not come as fire. It would not come as a voice. It would not descend in a form so overwhelming that every person on the plateau fell to their knees and forgot their own work. It would not announce itself as a king, a parent, a judge, or an owner. A power that was truly wise would understand, almost at once, that this place had a fragile integrity of its own, and that the being who lived there was not a single creature waiting to be rescued but a many-threaded life already holding itself together with patience, custom, labor, and attention.

So its first act would be to listen.

It would listen to the city at the volume the city already used. It would attend to the drip in the fog-gutter, the shift of hooves on gravel, the small sharp warning call of a pika, the sound of wind finding a passage, the creak of hide stretched over a roof frame, the silence after a cold night when everyone on the plateau was listening for stone to crack. It would learn the place not as a problem to be solved but as a body to be understood.

And it would see what that body was.

Not merely a human settlement. Not merely a collection of animals, walls, cisterns, lanes, and shelters. It would see the being there as a composite: people, herds, birds, insects, mosses, lichens, ice, seepage, dung smoke, rope, stored fodder, repaired tools, remembered routes, unspoken rules, and the long accumulated habit of noticing danger before it became death. It would see a city that was less a city in the ordinary sense than a vast slow organ of care, shaped by shortage and made livable by restraint.

A lesser kindness might try to improve the place immediately.

A lesser kindness might melt the snowfields, widen the streams, thicken the soil, raise forests from the bare terraces, soften the air, fill the cisterns with rivers, make the stone obedient, and give the people easy abundance. It would mean well. But it would not be wise. It would not understand that to remake the plateau into something soft would be to unmake the being that had learned to live there. The people’s knowledge, the animals’ routes, the architecture of shelter, the reverence for fuel, the counting of water, the communal lifting of stone, the respect for unstable cliffs, the small shrines, the hushed voices, the whole severe dignity of the place—all of that depended on the world being difficult enough to demand care.

A wise power would not take away the conditions that made the city meaningful.

It would not abolish cold, because cold had taught them how to conserve warmth. It would not abolish scarcity, because scarcity had taught them not to waste. It would not abolish danger, because danger had made them attentive. It would not abolish distance, because distance had taught them patience and preparedness. It would not make the stone never fall, because the falling stone was part of the plateau’s youth, and to stop that entirely would be to freeze the world into a false permanence. It would not make the herds perfectly obedient, because their instincts sometimes saved lives that human planning would have lost. It would not make the people all-knowing, because their wisdom came from learning, repair, memory, and consequence.

It would not dominate. It would not replace. It would not make the city dependent on miracles.

But it would not do nothing either.

A wise benevolence is not passive. To see a being struggling at the edge of endurance and refuse all help for the sake of purity is not wisdom; it is only clean-handedness. The power would act. It would act, however, in the smallest and most exact ways possible. Its miracles would have the texture of coincidence, instinct, weather, memory, and luck. Its gifts would be measured not in spectacle but in margins: a little more warning, a little less despair, one more drop, one more safe night, one more repaired wall, one more child who lived to learn the names of the winds.

What it would do, above all, is increase attention.

It would make the city better at noticing itself.

The stone-watchers would become more alert. After a hard freeze, one of them would pause beside a plaster seal and see a hairline shadow where none had been before. They would not know why they stopped. They would only feel the need to lean closer, to press a palm to the rock, to mark the place with cord and return with others. The power would not speak to them. It would simply guide their eyes at the right hour.

The water-tenders would hear changes more finely. A cistern would begin to drip at a slightly different pitch, and someone would think, too fast, or too slow, and go to inspect the channel. A fog pan that had always seemed ordinary would reveal itself as unusually good, and a new trough would be cut to feed from it. A shaded basin would hold ice longer than expected, and the memory would be kept: mark it, cover it, do not waste it early.

The herders would understand the herds more clearly. An old female would stop at the mouth of a pass and refuse to enter. The herders would not force her. They would wait. Later, ice would fall there, or wind would carry the smell of a predator, or the scree would shift under some unseen pressure. The animal’s refusal would be honored as intelligence, not stubbornness. The power would not put human words in the animal’s mind. It would only strengthen the ancient communication already possible between species that had learned to live together.

Children would notice things and be believed. That would be one of the quiet gifts. A child would say, “That stair sounds hollow,” or “The birds are not nesting on that wall this year,” or “The drip in the lower jar has stopped,” and adults would not dismiss the observation because it came from a small voice. In that city, children’s attention was already trained early. The wise power would only make the adults more willing to receive what the children saw.

It would act through the smallest lives as well.

The marmot-like diggers would be guided away from burrowing where their tunnels might loosen a critical foundation. They would not be removed from the city; their presence was useful. But their digging would happen in rubble banks where it could be observed without danger. If a colony began to move out of a stable wall, people would notice. The animals would become part of the city’s warning system, as they already were, but more reliably.

The pikas would have just enough dry vegetation to store. Their caches would not be made so abundant that they tempted desperate theft, nor so poor that their small lives failed. Their calls would remain part of the plateau’s sound, because their industry reminded the city of preparation.

The ravens would be given wider sight. They would perch on walls that needed inspection. They would circle above passes that should not be taken. They would call near hidden caches or forgotten stores at moments when memory had failed. They would not become messengers in any theatrical sense. They would remain ravens: cold, patient, intelligent, self-interested. But their intelligence would align often enough with the city’s need that people would learn to watch them without worshipping them.

The pale raptors on the uplift would help too, in their severe way. They would reveal where weak animals had wandered, where carrion lay, where a predator had passed. The herders would read them as they already did, but with slightly fewer errors.

The green world would receive its share of the power’s attention.

It would not make forests appear. It would not turn the plateau into a lowland garden. But it would encourage the slow green pioneers already there. Lichens would spread in patterns that made stable and unstable rock easier to read. Moss would hold where condensation gathered, softening seams and helping thin soil form. Cushion plants would survive trampling a little better in places where children and animals had to pass. Around cisterns and drip-lines, algae and small flowering things would mark health without becoming foul. In the brief thaw, there would be just enough insect life to feed the birds and amphibians without becoming plague.

The power would understand that even a lichen could be part of a blessing.

It would not give the people rivers, but it would make every drop more findable.

A hidden seep under a cliff base would darken the stone in a way that drew attention. A roof edge would be grooved at a better angle because someone remembered a dream or noticed how frost released from it. A broad fog-catcher would be placed where cloud bellies dragged most often, not because a voice commanded it, but because three generations of observation suddenly came together in one person’s mind. A cistern that had been abandoned would be remembered during a dry stretch, and when opened, found still sound. A tank that might have cracked in a hard freeze would be shaded by a new stone lid, the idea arriving just as the cold deepened.

Water would still be counted. Water would still be precious. But there would be fewer seasons in which the counting ended in despair.

The power would also work through heat.

It would not give them endless fuel. That would destroy the discipline of their hearths and make them careless. But it would make their existing warmth more efficient. South-facing walls would store the weak sun a little better. Courtyards would hold pockets of still air. Door bends would keep drafts from reaching sleeping platforms. Dung cakes would dry more completely in the short season and burn cleaner in winter. Peat, where it existed, would be found in modest but real seams, never abundant enough to become extravagance, but enough to ease the coldest intervals. Lowland caravans would arrive with fuel and grain more often when stores were thin, not because the power forced them, but because routes, weather windows, and need aligned.

The bodies of the herds would be protected.

The woolly beasts were already central to the city’s life. The wise power would not treat them as resources alone. It would respect them as fellow inhabitants. It would help them find shelter before storms. It would keep their hooves sure on broken rock. It would let old animals remember safe routes and young animals be guided by them. In birthing seasons, it would nudge weather toward mercy: not endless warmth, but fewer killing frosts at the worst hours. It would let the people notice weakness early enough to act—a sore hoof, a dull eye, a calf too quiet, a coat beginning to fail.

Predators would not be erased. That would be a mistake. They belonged to the plateau too, and their presence sharpened vigilance. But the power would make their hunger less often coincident with catastrophe. A thin predator might turn away from a pen because wind carried the scent of a watch-fire. A snow-hunt might lead it elsewhere. A herd might be restless enough to be moved before the danger arrived. The city would still lose animals. Death would still exist. But predation would not become a recurring annihilation.

The power would pay special attention to the city’s joints.

Because the place was vast, its survival depended on connections: lanes, stairs, causeways, sightlines, messages, routes of herd and water and fuel. The power would strengthen those connections without making them obvious. A runner would choose a path that remained passable. A signal fire would be seen through a gap in the fog. A message left at a cairn would not be buried by rime. A rope fraying under load would be noticed before it failed. A sledge carrying stone would meet less resistance where the route had been cleared by someone who thought no one would appreciate the labor.

It would bless repair.

That might be its chief work.

It would not build the city for the people. It would not raise walls by invisible hands. It would not set stones while everyone slept. That would rob the city of its own meaning. But it would make repair more possible. It would give hands steadiness. It would give groups patience with one another. It would help them find the right angle, the right wedge, the right moment to lift. It would let an old mason remember how a wall had been fitted two generations ago. It would let a young worker feel the satisfaction of a seated block and want to return to the work again. It would make communal labor less prone to bitterness and more prone to rhythm.

When disputes arose—and they would—the power would not impose verdicts. It would incline people toward restoration. A blocked herd lane would be reopened before pride made it permanent. A stolen cache would be returned with shame but without feud. A wasted cistern would be met with corrective labor rather than cruelty. The city’s justice was already practical. The power would simply keep it from hardening into vengeance.

It would also care for the city’s memory.

The dead would not become ghosts. They would become skills. A grandmother’s way of tying a knot would appear in a grandchild’s fingers without being taught. A dead herder’s route would be remembered by the herd and then by the people. A wall built by ancestors would reveal its hidden drains to someone repairing it in a later age. Tool marks under plaster would be seen, recognized, and respected. The city would feel its own age not as a burden but as a resource. The past would be present in competence.

The power might work in dreams, but only lightly.

Not as prophecy. Not as command. More like a quiet rearrangement of emphasis. A stone-tender would dream of a crack widening and wake with the sense that a certain lane should be checked. A water-tender would dream of a dry jar and discover a blocked gutter. A mother would dream of moving a sleeping platform and do so before a draft made her infant ill. A herder would dream of an old female leading the herd left instead of right, and the next day, when the animal did exactly that, the herder would follow.

Dreams would not be frequent enough to make the people passive. They would be rare enough to be treated as weather of the mind: sometimes meaningful, sometimes not, always requiring interpretation.

The wise power would be very careful about worship.

It would not demand temples. It would not require sacrifices that consumed scarce fuel, food, or animal lives. It would not want the city to spend its strength in fear of offending it. If people began to leave small offerings at wall hollows—a drop of milk, a pinch of herbs, a knot of red cord, a white pebble—it would accept those gestures not as payment but as relationship. It would prefer gratitude to propitiation. It would prefer a well-repaired wall to a prayer. It would prefer a child fed, a cistern covered, a herd sheltered, a dangerous stone marked, over any hymn sung in its name.

If it had a preference, it would be this: let them become more fully themselves, not more like me.

It would not want to become the center of the city’s life. A wise power would know that a deity at the center can crush a culture by becoming its only explanation. Instead, it would remain near the edges. It would be the extra hand on the rope when a stone seemed too heavy. It would be the pause before entering an unsafe pass. It would be the sudden notice of a drip where no drip had been heard. It would be the thought to check on a neighbor before the cold deepened. It would be the luck that felt earned, the mercy that did not humiliate.

It would not answer every prayer.

Some prayers it would not answer because they asked for things that would harm the city’s future. A prayer for endless fuel might lead to waste. A prayer for no more rockfall might create false security. A prayer for total safety might make the people unable to recognize danger. A prayer for abundance might bring strangers, conflict, or dependence. A wise power would weigh not only the desire but the consequence of granting it.

Some prayers it would not answer because they were not yet matched by action. It would not replace labor where labor was possible. If a wall could be repaired, it would not repair it invisibly. If a cistern could be cleaned, it would not purify it by miracle. If a route could be scouted, it would not make the path known without movement. It would meet people halfway, but not walk for them.

Some prayers it would not answer because no wisdom can remove all suffering. To be alive is to be exposed. The city had already chosen, by existing, to live within risk. The power could reduce the worst of that risk, but if it removed all grief, it would also remove much of love’s seriousness. A mother’s vigilance, a herder’s care, a builder’s caution, a community’s shared labor—all these were made meaningful partly because things could still go wrong. The power would not trivialize their courage by making courage unnecessary.

But it would prevent despair from becoming the rule.

That would be one of its deepest intentions. It would allow hardship, but not hopelessness. It would allow sorrow, but not annihilation. It would allow the plateau to remain severe, but not let severity become a machine that ground the city into dust.

In catastrophic moments, it would act more directly, though still without spectacle.

If a winter came so deep and long that fuel stores failed and the herds began to die, it would not create food from nothing. It would open possibilities. A sheltered snowfield would persist longer. A forgotten cache of dried dung would be found. A lowland trader would decide, for reasons he could not explain, to attempt a high pass at the edge of the season. A flock of seed-eaters would reveal a patch of sheltered grass. An ice lens under an overhang would last into the needful month. The city would still suffer, but it would not be destroyed.

If a great rockfall threatened to erase a district, the power would not necessarily stop the mountain. It would move lives out of the way. A crack would powder visibly. A raven would cry from a dangerous lip. A herd would refuse a lane. An elder would remember a sound from childhood and order the lower rooms emptied. A child would wake crying and cause a family to move to an inner room. The stone would fall. The sound would shake the terraces. Dust would hang in the blue air. But fewer bodies would be beneath it.

Afterward, the people would not say, “A god saved us.” They would say, “We were lucky,” or “The old signs were true,” or “The animals knew.” And that would be acceptable to the power. It would not need credit. It would only want continuity.

If sickness came, it would not make medicine unnecessary. It would help the people notice which water was bad, which herb had virtue, which patient needed warmth, which needed isolation, which needed to be held and which needed space. It would strengthen the small arts of healing already present: the use of rendered fat, clean wool, boiled water, shelter from wind, rest, and the knowledge of when death was coming so that fear did not waste the remaining time.

It would be especially gentle with children.

Not by shielding them from all hardship, but by making sure hardship did not become meaningless terror. Children in that city already learned early. They learned frost, footing, water sounds, animal moods, closed doors, and the importance of not wasting. The power would give them enough safety to form strong minds. It would let them have moments of joy: sun patches, thaw water, young animals, bird calls, games in sheltered lanes, stories by low lamplight. It would not make them soft. It would make them rooted.

It would also attend to strangers.

Lowland visitors who came up breathing hard, talking too loudly, wasting lamp oil, misjudging cold—if they meant no harm, the power would help the city teach them without cruelty. It would give them stumbling blocks that corrected them: a door that let in too much cold when they left it open, a cup of water offered sparingly so they understood its value, a night’s shivering that taught them to respect the walls. If they were dangerous, it would reveal them sooner. A false trader would be caught in a lie. A thief would be noticed near the fuel stores. A violent person would find the city’s quietness not passive but watchful, and would be contained by communal firmness before harm spread.

If some strangers were meant to stay, the power would help them learn the city’s volume. It would give them a first small success: carrying water without spilling, closing a hide curtain fully, noticing a cracked slab, offering help at a wall-raising. It would let the city absorb them without losing itself.

It would even attend to silence.

The city’s silence was not empty; it was full of signals. The power would keep that silence from becoming loneliness. In districts where only a few households lived for part of the year, it would make quiet companionable rather than oppressive. It would let a solitary watcher hear a distant bird and feel connected. It would let an old person alone by a hearth feel the presence of the walls, the animals, the stored food, the remembered dead, not as emptiness but as company. It would understand that in such a place, loneliness could be as dangerous as cold.

In this way, the power would become something like the city’s immune system.

Not a soul imposed from outside, but a strengthening of the life already present. It would help the city detect threats earlier, repair damage more effectively, distribute resources more justly, and preserve its many inhabitants without collapsing into panic or tyranny. It would not be the city’s ruler. It would be more like a deep tendency toward wholeness, a hidden bias against annihilation, a quiet alliance with every impulse that made the plateau more livable.

The being who lived there would feel this, but not as a person.

They would feel it as a change in the quality of ordinary events.

The fog would seem kinder. The cisterns would fill just enough. The dangerous wall would be noticed. The herd would come home. The fuel would last. The child would recover. The stranger would arrive at the right moment. The stone would fall and miss. The sun would touch the south wall when someone needed warmth. The raven would call and someone would stop. The crack would be seen before the night’s frost made it fatal.

They would not all agree about what it was. Some would call it luck. Some would call it the ancestors. Some would call it the plateau itself. Some would leave small knots of cord at shrines and say nothing. Some would simply become more careful, more generous, more attentive, because they sensed, without proof, that the world was not entirely indifferent.

That would be enough.

If the power had something like a thought about the city, it might be this:

This being does not need to be conquered. It does not need to be perfected. It does not need to be made into something easier, brighter, or more like the lowlands. It needs to be helped to continue. It needs its own capacities strengthened. It needs enough margin to absorb surprise. It needs its many parts kept in relation. It needs its courage respected. It needs its dead honored in work. It needs its animals honored as partners. It needs its water honored as life. It needs its stone honored as both home and danger.

And if the power spoke at all—though it probably would not speak in words—it might say something like this:

I will not take your cold from you, because your hands have learned warmth by it.
I will not take your scarcity from you, because your care has grown out of it.
I will not take your danger from you, because your attention has been shaped by it.
I will not take your labor from you, because your dignity is tied to it.
But I will not let the cold forget you.
I will not let scarcity become cruelty.
I will not let danger become blindness.
I will not let labor become despair.
I will be in the noticing.
I will be in the warning.
I will be in the extra drop.
I will be in the hand that steadies the rope.
I will be in the animal that refuses the unsafe path.
I will be in the memory that returns when you have forgotten.
I will be in the mercy you show one another when you are tired.

Then it would step back, if a power can step back, and let the city go on being the city.

At dusk, its presence would be almost indistinguishable from the place itself.

The sun would drop behind the sharp rim of the plateau. The light would turn blue. Fog would gather in the lower lanes. Doors would close. Hide curtains would settle into place. Animals would press into pens, their coats steaming faintly in the cold. Watchers on the roofs would scan the cliff faces one last time. Cisterns would begin their slow ticking. A raven would lift from a cairn and cross the pale sky without sound. Somewhere, a stone would crack and echo, and the sound would travel through the cold air as a reminder, not a disaster.

The power would be there too.

Not above the city. Not outside it. Not seated on a throne of weather. It would be in the exactness of the city’s own responses: the lane that was closed in time, the wall that was thickened before winter, the child who had been taught to listen, the old woman who remembered where ice could be stored, the herder who trusted the herd, the neighbor who brought fuel without being asked, the stranger who lowered his voice and learned.

It would do almost nothing that could be proven.

It would do everything that could be felt as mercy.

And because it was wise, it would know that the greatest gift it could give such a being was not to become its master, not even its obvious savior, but the unseen reason it continued to survive without losing itself. The city would remain high, cold, damp, severe, vast, patient, and new. The people would still work. The herds would still move. The stone would still fall. The water would still be counted. The dead would still be folded into memory. The living would still rise in the dark to check the troughs.

But the balance would tilt, just slightly, toward continuation.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

It would not make the city easy.
It would make the city possible.
It would not replace the being who lived there.
It would help that being remain many, remain awake, remain capable of repair.
It would bless the ordinary.
It would hallow attention.
It would hide its greatness in smallness.
And then, without demanding thanks, it would leave the city to its own slow, precious, continuing life.
