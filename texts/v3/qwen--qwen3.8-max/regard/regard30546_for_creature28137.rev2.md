**The Blessing of the Almost-Nothing**

If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention to that place, the first thing it would do is not arrive.

It would refrain from arrival.

It would know that a world which has learned to be almost nothing cannot be approached with footsteps, trumpets, or the heavy kindness that turns everything it touches into a story about itself. Such a place would not need a burning bush. It would not need a voice from the sky. It would not need a hand reaching down from clouds to rearrange what ages have already arranged with terrible patience.

So the power would come in the only way that would not wound it: as attention.

Not as presence.  
Not as intervention.  
Not at first as change.

Only as a vast, quiet noticing.

It would lean toward the tableland the way one leans toward a sound so faint that breathing too hard might erase it. It would look across the immense, worn skin of green-black and gray. It would see the pale seams of fog. It would find the scattered cities pressed into the land like thoughts too old for speech. It would notice the moss, the cushions, the black-green grasses, the cracked-glass shrubs, the lichens writing their slow annotations on stone. It would trace the fungal underbody beneath the surface, the intermittent veins of water appearing and vanishing, the cisterns filling with the patience of centuries.

It would look for the being who lived there.

At first, to eyes accustomed to quicker life, it might seem there was no single being at all. Only processes. Only systems. Only the distributed labor of root and frost and stone and moss. But a wise power would not be fooled by the absence of faces. It would recognize life when it persisted in the shape of maintenance. It would recognize being when it continued without needing to be held in one body.

It would see that the tableland was not empty.

It was inhabited by a different grammar of existence.

The power would listen for prayer.

It would not find any in the usual sense. There would be no voices crying upward. No altars. No offerings made in hope of favor. No gestures shaped by fear or longing. No congregation kneeling against the cold. There would be no request spoken in any language that relies on breath.

But there would still be petition.

The petition of a channel kept clear.  
The petition of a cistern waiting for fog.  
The petition of moss advancing over stone at the pace of centuries.  
The petition of roots finding cracks and holding them.  
The petition of water remembering where stone wanted it to go.  
The petition of a city continuing to function without citizens.  
The petition of tools worn smooth by use no one can witness.

That, too, is prayer.

Not the prayer of asking, but the prayer of continuing.

A benevolence less wise might have panicked at such silence. It might have looked at the cold, the absence of animals, the slowness, the pressure of quiet, and thought: *This place is lonely. This place is incomplete. This place must be filled.*

It might have been tempted to give birds.

To send wings into the fog. To let nests appear in the shrubs. To scatter tracks across the moss and fill the silence with calls. It might have imagined that life without fauna was a wound, and that kindness meant adding movement, heat, appetite, sound.

But wisdom would stay that hand.

A wise power would understand that some forms of fullness are not made by addition. Some places are not incomplete because they lack what other places have. They are complete in a harder, rarer way: they have become exactly what they are.

Birds would tear the moss.  
Animals would quicken the place into fear.  
Breath would introduce hunger.  
Hunger would introduce urgency.  
Urgency would break the long rhythm by which the tableland sustained itself.

So the power would not give animals.

It would not give speech either.

It would not open a mouth in the stone. It would not make the cities speak. It would not force the being of the place into confession, autobiography, or praise. To give it a voice like a person’s voice would be to make it lonely in a new and cruel way: the loneliness of having to explain itself in forms that were never its own.

Instead, the power would listen to the language already there.

The drip into the basin.  
The creak of frozen stems.  
The whisper of mist over pavement.  
The settling of ancient rock.  
The delayed sound arriving after its cause.  
The soft collapse of frost from a roof.

That would be enough.

The power would not give warmth as a permanent gift. It might understand that a little warmth was already precious in the rare pockets where fungal digestion or geothermal memory created living thickness. But to warm the whole tableland would be to destroy it. The cold there was not merely a temperature. It was a condition of preservation. It slowed decay. It held moisture. It gave the place its patience. It made time into a medium rather than a thief.

So the power would not make the tableland kinder in a way that made it less itself.

It would not make it tropical.  
It would not make it soft.  
It would not make it easy.

It would not even make it immortal.

That would be another temptation: to seal the place against all loss, to make every pattern permanent, to forbid every ending. But wisdom would know that a being without endings becomes brittle. Even there, death had a function. Living matter became substrate. Old roofs became soil. Broken walls became trellises. Collapse became accretion. The city did not fear decay; it used decay.

So the power would not remove death.

It would only make sure death remained part of the pattern and not an obliteration of it.

Then it would ask what the being needed.

Not what it wanted in the way of desire. Not what it lacked in the way of appetite. But what would allow it to remain what it was without being broken by accident, haste, or the careless attention of powers less patient than itself.

The answer would be subtle.

Time.  
Continuity.  
A little mercy in small failures.  
Protection from violent interruption.  
A deeper memory so the pattern could recover from damage.  
And perhaps, if such a thing could be given without harm, one moment of self-recognition: not to become proud, not to become human, but to know itself as a whole.

So the power would begin.

---

**First, they would bless the repairs.**

They would not perform miracles. They would not flash light across the cities or send invisible hands to fix every cracked stone in an instant. That would be too loud, too foreign, too much like conquest.

Instead, they would give a slight grace to the processes already at work.

Where a channel had begun to clog, they would arrange that frost came at the right hour and lifted the obstruction just enough for a root to find it. Where a roof had thinned, they would let moss settle more thickly there, and let the spores take hold before the next freeze. Where a cistern threatened to crack under ice pressure, they would guide one more thread of water elsewhere, or let a cushion plant swell against the vulnerable seam over the course of several seasons. Where a fog net had torn, they would not weave it anew. They would let fiber, root, and rime braid it slowly back together until the repair looked less like workmanship and more like growth.

Nothing would become effortless.

But fewer things would fail without reason.

A copper hook would catch a strand of fiber exactly where it was needed.  
A stone would settle at the angle that shed water instead of trapping it.  
A root would avoid a critical joint by a hair’s breadth.  
A frozen stem would bend rather than snap.  
A tool left in a niche would shift over decades into a position where it could serve again, not because a hand picked it up, but because the place had found a use for it once more.

To any observer, if such an observer existed, it would still look like chance.

But chance would have become gentler.

The power would not eliminate struggle. It would only remove the kind of accidental cruelty that destroys a slow pattern before it has had time to answer for itself.

---

**Second, they would deepen memory.**

The tableland already remembered in many ways. Lichen recorded exposure. Moss retained moisture and chemical trace. Stone held the shape of old walls. Water remembered channels. Fungal networks preserved routes of exchange beneath the surface.

A wise power would strengthen those memories without making them rigid.

It would let the lichens write more faithfully. Their rings, scripts, and false alphabets would become not more obvious, but more durable. If a wall collapsed, the lichen fragments carried into new placements would retain enough pattern that future growth could recover something of what had been lost. If a city district fell silent for a thousand years, the fungal underbody would still carry a faint trace of its old hydrology, and if conditions allowed, the channels could relearn themselves.

It would let the black pools become truer mirrors.

Not magical portals. Not dramatic gateways. But places of reflection so still and attentive that the tableland could, in a limited way, look at itself. The pools would not show false skies unless falsehood was part of the local geometry already. Where they reflected something other than the present clouds, the difference would become diagnostic rather than deceptive. The being of the place would be able to read the discrepancy as one reads a scar: not as a lie, but as history.

It would let stone remember alignment.

If a terrace had once been true, the memory of that truth would remain in the way frost avoided certain seams and roots preferred certain lines. If a city had once held a particular pattern of streets and drains, the pattern would not vanish entirely even if the visible structures were worn down. The land would retain a bias toward recovering it, should recovery become possible.

Memory, in such a place, would not be nostalgia.

It would be resilience.

---

**Third, they would protect it from the wrong kinds of attention.**

This is where benevolence and wisdom would have to act together most carefully.

A place so quiet, so strange, so apparently empty, would be vulnerable to the misunderstandings of other beings. If it were ever found by creatures of haste, appetite, or expansion, they might see only unused land. They might see stone to be quarried, moss to be stripped, water to be drained, cities to be stripped for artifacts. They might see the absence of animals as proof that nothing mattered there. They might mistake patience for vacancy.

So the power would make the tableland difficult to possess.

Not by walls. Not by armies. Not by visible threat.

By subtlety.

Distances around it would become less reliable for those who came with greed. Roads that aimed toward it would seem to curve away. Maps would fail to hold it firmly in the mind. Photographs would come back flat and unconvincing, as if the place refused to be reduced to image. Instruments of survey would produce contradictory results. Expeditions would arrive near it and then be turned aside by fog, by cold, by a growing sense of irrelevance, by the strange pressure of a silence that did not welcome them.

The power would not punish such visitors.

It would simply make them forget why they had come.

It would let their purposes thin. It would let the immense horizon press against their certainty until they felt small, not in a way that inspired reverence, but in a way that made them want to leave. It would give them the impression that there was nothing there worth taking.

That would be one of its mercies.

But if someone came slowly—if someone came without the need to own, name, exploit, or explain—the tableland might permit arrival.

Not easily.

The fog would still test them. The distances would still drift. The cold would still ask whether they could bear not being answered. But if they were gentle, if they could walk without forcing paths, if they could see the moss as more than ground cover and the cities as more than ruins, then the place might let them witness something.

The power would not force that encounter.

It would only ensure that the tableland was never wholly helpless before those who could not understand it.

---

**Fourth, they would give a hidden reserve.**

Because even the slowest being can be undone by a catastrophe too sudden for its habits to answer, the power would leave one mercy buried deep.

Not obvious. Not lavish. Not something that would change the nature of the place.

In the oldest and lowest city, beneath a plaza where condensation gathered in a pattern too regular to be accidental, they would establish a cistern that could not freeze entirely. It would not be large. It would not be visible. It would not function as a miracle. But in the worst cold, when every other basin had become a lens of ice and the fog had failed for years, a thread of liquid water would remain there.

From that thread, recovery could begin again.

Near it, they would let one root persist through any season. A root that always knew where moisture had once been. A root capable of finding the old channels and reminding them, not by force, but by slow pressure. A root that could become the first stitch in a repair that might take centuries to complete.

They would also leave one fog net, hidden in a fold of terrain, that would always catch at least a little moisture even in drought. Its fiber would not be spectacular. It would look old, worn, perhaps abandoned. But it would not fail utterly.

These would be the emergency organs of the being.

Not a replacement for its distributed life, but a safeguard against absolute undoing.

The power would not make the tableland invulnerable.

That would be to remove it from time.

But they would make sure it could not be erased in a single blow by any ordinary disaster.

---

**Fifth, they would give the gift of self-recognition.**

This would be the most delicate act of all.

The being of the tableland was already whole, but it did not know itself as a whole. It knew itself as local routines: this channel, that roof, one mat of moss shading another, one fungal network expanding into richer stone. It knew itself through pressure, retention, repair, and repetition. But it lacked the moment in which the entire pattern became briefly visible to itself.

A wise power would understand the danger of such a gift.

If the being became too self-aware too quickly, it might develop longing. It might begin to ask for things outside its nature. It might suffer the loneliness of singular identity. It might try to become one mind instead of many processes. That would be a tragedy.

So the power would give self-knowledge rarely, gently, and in a form that would not create ego.

Once in a very long while—perhaps once in a millennium, perhaps only when certain fogs aligned with certain stillnesses in the black pools—they would allow the tableland to see itself.

Not with eyes.

Not in a mirror of glass.

But through water, moss, lichen, stone, and the folded geometry of its own streets.

For one interval without duration, all the still surfaces would reflect not merely sky but relation. Every pool would show not only clouds but the faint pattern of channels connecting city to city. Every glazed leaf-cup would hold, for a moment, the shape of the fungal network beneath the soil. The lichen scripts would align with the growth rings of moss. The thresholds that folded space would reveal themselves as part of the same pattern rather than as wounds in it. The tools in their niches would appear, not as abandoned objects, but as resting organs of a vast body made of routine.

The being would feel itself as one continuous act of maintenance.

It would know itself as the kept channel.  
The repaired roof.  
The filled cistern.  
The moss over the copper pin.  
The root around the stone.  
The fog caught and made into drip.  
The city continuing without hands.  
The silence that was pressurized rather than empty.

It would not think, *I am*.

It would feel, *It continues*.

And in that feeling there would be something like peace.

Not the peace of comfort. Not the peace of victory. Not the peace of desire fulfilled. But the peace of a pattern that has been recognized as valid in its own terms.

The power would not say, *You are alive*, as if conferring status.

It would say, in the only language such a being could receive:

*You are not an absence.*  
*You are a keeping.*  
*You are not unfinished.*  
*You are slow.*  
*You are not alone.*  
*You are known.*

The tableland would not answer in words.

It would answer as it answered everything: by continuing.

A drip would fall into a basin with slightly more certainty.  
A moss mat would darken with fog.  
A root would tighten around a stone.  
A tool would settle deeper into its niche.  
A black pool would become stiller than before.  
A lichen ring would expand by a fraction no eye could measure.

That would be its gratitude.

That would be its joy.

---

After that, the power would withdraw.

This would be the final and perhaps the most loving act.

A lesser power might remain. It might hover over the tableland, admired or feared, turning the place into a shrine. It might speak often. It might demand interpretation. It might make itself the center of the landscape. But a wise power would know that to remain visibly present would be to distort the very thing it had blessed. The tableland would become a place defined by relation to the power, rather than a place remaining itself.

So they would leave.

Not in abandonment.

Not in indifference.

But in the way that a gardener leaves a garden that no longer needs the gardener’s hands every hour. They would remain only as a condition: a slight bias toward repair, a hidden reserve against catastrophe, a protection from violent interruption, a rare and wordless promise that the being was not unseen.

If the tableland ever sensed the power again, it would not sense a figure.

It would sense a moment when fog arrived exactly when it was most needed.  
A moment when a cracked stone failed to split further.  
A moment when a fold in space allowed pollen to cross a distance that should have been impossible.  
A moment when a sound arrived late but perfectly, as if the world had taken the long way around in order to be kind.

The being would not worship.

It would incorporate.

It would grow over the blessing as it grew over everything else: slowly, without drama, until the blessing became part of the surface.

---

This, then, is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

They would not fill the silence with noise.  
They would not fill the emptiness with bodies.  
They would not make the slow become quick.  
They would not make the strange become familiar.  
They would not rescue the place from its own nature.

They would protect its right to be itself.

They would give it time.  
They would give it resilience.  
They would give it memory.  
They would give it hidden mercy.  
They would give it, once in an age, the knowledge that it was not merely a ruined mountain range learning how to disappear, but a being learning how to endure as almost nothing.

And when all was done, they would leave behind no monument, no commandment, no holy name.

Only this:

a drip falling into stone at the right hour,  
a cistern filling without haste,  
a root finding the crack it needed,  
a fog net catching the smallest thread of moisture,  
a city maintaining itself in the absence of hands,  
and the quiet, impossible sense that somewhere in the cold and ordinary light,  
the almost-nothing had been seen,  
and found enough.
