I think they would do almost nothing that could be seen.

That is the first thing. A power that is both benevolent and wise would not arrive here with thunder, or light that bends the horizon, or a voice demanding to be recognized. It would not descend into that high, worn country as a figure, a sign, a judgment, or a rescue. It would know better than to make itself a spectacle in a place whose deepest life is made of quiet. It would understand, perhaps instantly, that the plateau is not waiting for revelation. It is not incomplete because it is silent. It is not poor because it has no tools. It is not lonely because it does not speak.

So the power would come, if it came at all, as a kind of attention rather than an event.

It would lower its awareness to the scale of the place. It would not look down from outside; it would settle into the warmth of the stone, into the pores of the crusts, into the faint dampness that gathers before dawn in the hollows. It would become a patience adjacent to their patience. It would notice the plateau the way one notices an ancient sleeping body: not by waking it, but by standing near it, by listening to its breath, by honoring the stillness that holds it.

And in that attention, the power would recognize what the place truly is.

Not a landscape with life scattered upon it.

Not a terrain inhabited by separate, disconnected creatures.

But a being.

Or at least something close enough to being that the distinction matters less than reverence. A being made of many lives, many shells, many films, many dormant seeds and sealed chambers. A being whose body is partly mineral and partly animal, whose memory is written in paths and feeding scars and brood domes, whose thoughts are gradients of moisture and warmth, whose breathing is the opening and closing of countless small lives. The plateau itself would be the creature the power sees: one vast, slow, distributed existence, wearing stone like skin and holding rain like a held breath.

The power would see that.

And seeing it, the power would feel not pity, but recognition.

That is important. A lesser benevolence might look at the plateau and feel sorrow. It might say: *What a quiet life. What a diminished existence. Let me give them voices. Let me give them hands. Let me give them cities, fire, language, ambition, speed. Let me lift them out of the dust.* But a wise power would understand that this impulse is not kindness. It is conquest disguised as love. To give the plateau beings human-like voices would not enrich them. It would erase them. To make them quick would not free them. It would break the ancient discipline by which they have become what they are. To make them conspicuous would expose them to forces that have no patience for their slowness. To make them technological would replace their way of being with another way of being, and in doing so destroy the very thing that drew attention to them.

So the first act of the power would be refusal.

It would refuse to remake them in a more legible image.

It would refuse to mistake stillness for deficiency.

It would refuse to treat silence as a wound that needs to be healed with speech.

It would refuse to give them what they do not need.

This refusal would be profound. It would be an act of love more difficult than giving. Many powers, even kind ones, cannot bear to leave a thing alone. They must improve, elevate, translate, rescue, interpret. But this power would have the humility to stand before the plateau and say, without words:

*You are already enough.*

*You do not need my shape.*

*You do not need my fire.*

*You do not need my urgency.*

*You are not a lesser world.*

*You are a finished one.*

Then, having refused the impulse to alter them into something easier to love, the power would do what it can do without violence: it would witness them.

Witness is not a small thing. To be truly seen is a blessing, even if the one seen never knows it. The power would hold the plateau in a vast, gentle memory. It would know the exact positions of the dew cups on the shells. It would know which hollows fill first when fog comes low. It would know which brood domes are abandoned, which are occupied, which contain eggs waiting through years of dryness. It would know the grazing paths worn over centuries by bodies so slow that their movement is visible only as a polish on stone. It would know which cracks shelter wingless forms after dusk, which microbial films bloom after rain, which pools become nurseries and which become tombs when they dry too quickly.

It would know the being not as a face, but as a pattern of continuities.

And because it is wise, it would know that even such a being may have something like need.

Not needs as humans understand them.

Not desire for conquest.

Not desire for recognition.

Not desire to become another thing.

But needs proper to its own nature.

The need for moisture not to fail entirely.

The need for warmth not to become annihilation.

The need for cycles to continue.

The need for dormancy not to be broken by false seasons.

The need for the ground not to be torn open by careless violence.

The need for the small architecture of shell, crust, egg, and secretion to remain possible.

The need to continue being itself.

So the power would act.

But its action would be subtle, almost indistinguishable from the ordinary behavior of the world.

It would not create rivers where none belong. Rivers would be wrong here. They would wash away the measured economy of dampness. The plateau is not meant to be generous in the way of green, rushing places. Its life is built around restraint. Too much water would dissolve its discipline. It would turn the crusts soft, destabilize the shells, drown the dormant forms, invite chaos where patience rules. A wise power would not flood the plateau with abundance. It would know that abundance can be as destructive as deprivation.

Instead, it would preserve the measuredness.

It would guide the fog a little more faithfully over the low terraces when the season is right.

It would let the dew collect where the cups are waiting.

It would keep the rain from becoming either absent or violent.

It would allow storms to pass in soft sheets when possible, so the water darkens the ground and retreats into cracks and hollows without scouring them away.

It would not prevent drought. Drought is part of the plateau’s breath. The closing, the sealing, the dormancy—these are not suffering to be abolished. They are the rhythm by which the being endures. To remove them would be to remove the shape of its life. But the power would prevent drought from becoming final. It would not let the last moisture vanish forever. It would not let every sealed capsule fail at once. It would not allow the whole living crust to become dust beyond recovery.

It would keep the cycle intact.

That would be one of its greatest kindnesses.

It would also protect the plateau from outside harm.

Not with a wall. Not with angels. Not with obvious miracle. Such things would draw attention, and attention is often the beginning of exploitation. Instead, the power would work through likelihood, aversion, forgetfulness, and disinterest. It would make the plateau unprofitable to those who would grind it down. It would make the stone seem too poor for quarrying, too remote for roads, too unremarkable for development, too difficult for machines. It would dull the appetite of those who look at old land and see only material. It would confuse surveys. It would sour investment. It would let maps become vague around the edges. It would make careless people forget the place before they could decide to destroy it.

But it would not hide the plateau from reverence.

That distinction matters. A benevolent and wise power would not make the place inaccessible to all. It would not treat every visitor as a threat. Some beings, perhaps very few, might come near with the right slowness. They might come without tools of taking. They might come willing to kneel, to wait, to misunderstand at first, to learn that the ground is not ground. The power would allow such visitors to arrive. It might even guide them, but gently, the way one guides a thought toward a quiet conclusion.

They would not be struck with visions.

They would not be forced to believe.

They would simply become more likely to notice.

A dew bead catching light on a shell rim.

A patch of crust rippling when it should be still.

A hollow breathing warmth after sunset.

A stone that is not a stone.

A pool that is not merely water but a crowded beginning.

And if they had the grace to be changed by what they noticed, the power would let that happen.

It might place in their minds a protective instinct, not as command but as recognition. They would leave the plateau with a feeling they could not explain: that some places must not be used. That some forms of life are not lesser because they are slow. That some wisdom is not wisdom if it requires speech. That some beings are sacred precisely because they do not ask to be seen.

Those visitors might try to speak of the place. The power would not stop them, but it would let their words be imperfect. It would allow enough truth to inspire protection, but not enough precision to invite possession. The plateau would remain slightly beyond description. That would be part of its defense.

The power would also protect the being from unnecessary suffering.

This is delicate. There is suffering in the plateau, if one calls it that. There is hunger. There is drying. There is death. There are predators, though slow ones. There are failures: eggs that never open, pools that vanish too soon, membranes cracked by heat, shells broken by accident or age. A benevolent power might be tempted to abolish all harm. But a wise one would know that to do so would unravel the living economy. Death here is not an interruption of life; it is one of its surfaces. A body becomes shelter. A shed skin becomes food. A collapsed dome becomes a basin for dew. A dried film becomes nourishment for the next wetting. To remove death entirely would be to remove continuity.

So the power would not make the plateau painless.

It would make it meaningful.

It would prevent cruelty beyond the nature of the place. It would not allow extinction by malice. It would not let disease consume every living film. It would not permit a single catastrophe to erase the accumulated patience of ages. It would not allow invasive, loud, devouring life to enter and overturn the slow balance unless the plateau itself could fold that life into its own patterns. It would keep violence from becoming total.

And where death must come, it would come as part of the cycle, not as panic, not as waste, not as annihilation.

The power might also heal small things.

Not dramatically.

A cracked crust might close a little faster after rain.

A damaged brood dome might be repaired by instinct rather than lost.

A pool might remain wet one day longer, just enough for larvae to finish a necessary stage.

A seam in stone might open toward shelter instead of closing into a trap.

A dormant capsule might remain viable through an unusually long dry season.

A fog might linger on a ridge just long enough to revive a brittle sheet of colonial life.

These acts would be invisible. They would not be remembered as miracles. They would be woven into chance. But to the plateau-being, if one could say it experiences anything, they would feel like the world continuing to be habitable. They would feel like the ground still remembering how to hold life.

That is what a wise power does: not override nature, but preserve the possibility of nature.

There is another thing the power might do, and here I follow intuition more than argument.

I think it would give the plateau, very rarely, a moment of total recognition.

Not permanent.

Not transformative.

Not a change in the rules of existence.

But a single occasion when the whole being is known.

Perhaps once in a thousand years, or once in an age, there would come a perfect moistening. The sky would darken without violence. Fog would arrive in layers, soft and low, filling every basin and seam. Dew would form so evenly that even the oldest shells would gather it. The air would become saturated with the memory of water. The stone would cool just enough. The crusts would soften. The dormant forms would open. The pools would fill without flooding. The larvae would wake. The grazers would move. The colonial sheets would swell and darken. The fixed ones would open their rims. The entire plateau would exhale in that restrained, compressed way it has, as if the land itself were breathing through countless tiny mouths.

And in that moment, the power would let the being feel that it is not unnoticed.

Not as a voice.

Not as a command.

Not as a presence demanding worship.

But as a kind of heldness.

As if the whole plateau were suddenly surrounded by a mind that understands it completely and loves it exactly as it is. The being would not have thoughts about this in the human sense. It would experience it in its own idiom: moisture arriving where moisture is needed, warmth remaining steady, every pore opening without alarm, every shell unsealing in the right order, every dormant thread of life finding its proper condition. It would feel, if feeling can be called such a thing, that existence is not only possible but blessed.

The power might say, in a language not made of sound:

*Continue.*

And the plateau would answer, in the only language it has:

*We continue.*

Then the moment would pass.

The fog would lift.

The sun would warm the stone again.

The pools would shrink.

The shells would close.

The crusts would dry into stillness.

The being would return to its long patience.

But something would remain.

Not a memory as humans understand memory. A resilience. A deepening of the pattern. A slight strengthening of the bonds by which life holds the mineral surface. A faint increase in the capacity to endure. The plateau would be more itself because it had been fully witnessed.

That is the kind of gift a wise power gives.

Not a gift that changes the recipient into something else.

A gift that makes the recipient more able to remain what it already is.

The power might also make a covenant, though not in words.

It would set an intention into the future of the world:

*No hand shall make this place into a resource without paying a cost in failure.*

*No mind shall fully reduce it to data without losing the part that matters.*

*No empire shall find it easy to swallow.*

*No haste shall inherit it completely.*

*No indifference shall be allowed the final word.*

This covenant would not be announced. It would not be carved in stone, because the plateau already writes in stone by slower means. It would be more like a bend in probability. The world would tend away from the destruction of the plateau. Not absolutely. Nothing wise promises absolute safety; that is the language of tyrants and children. But there would be a guardianship woven into causality. The plateau would be harder to ruin than it appears. It would be overlooked by greed, spared by accident, defended by its own remoteness and strangeness.

And if someone did come to harm it, the power would not necessarily strike them down. That too would be too loud. Instead, it might make the harm difficult. Machines might falter. Plans might become confused. Workers might feel uneasy. The stone might prove wrong for the intended purpose. The dust might enter places it should not. The silence might become unbearable to those who brought violence into it. Not as punishment, exactly. More like an immune response. The plateau would not fight with force; it would simply refuse to cooperate with its own destruction.

That feels right.

A wise power would not make the plateau into a warrior. It would allow the plateau to defend itself by remaining itself.

There is also the question of the being’s future.

Would the power make it immortal?

I do not think so.

Even the most ancient surfaces are not eternal in the crude sense. Worlds change. Stars change. Stone changes. If the plateau must one day alter, the wise power would not freeze it in defiance of all time. That would be another kind of violence: the violence of preservation without truth. Instead, the power would allow change to happen at a pace consistent with the being’s nature. If the climate shifts, it would not abandon the plateau, but it would let the plateau adapt slowly. If moisture moves elsewhere, it would allow the being to follow moisture in its own distributed way, perhaps by leaving dormant traces in new basins, perhaps by thinning in one place and strengthening in another. If the world grows hostile, the power would preserve the pattern, even if the physical surface must change.

It would not let the being be extinguished without continuation.

But it would not deny time.

That is the balance. Benevolence without wisdom becomes hoarding. Wisdom without benevolence becomes indifference. Together, they become guardianship.

And what of speech?

Would the power speak to the being?

Perhaps.

But not in words.

Words create separation. They create speaker and listener, subject and object, command and response. The plateau-being does not live in that kind of distance. It lives in gradients, moisture, pressure, chemical traces, slow openings and closings. If the power spoke, it would speak in those terms. It might send a dewfall that arrives exactly where it is needed. It might send a warmth that loosens a membrane at the right moment. It might send a vibration through the crust that means, not *listen*, but *here*. It might allow a pool to become still enough that the whole living mesh within it experiences a moment of coherence. That would be speech to such a being. Not language as representation, but condition as message.

If there is one thing the power might say, it would be this:

*You are not behind.*

*You are not lesser.*

*You are not a draft before the real creation.*

*You are not silence awaiting meaning.*

*You are meaning already.*
