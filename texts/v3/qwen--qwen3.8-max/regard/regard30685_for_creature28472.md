A benevolent and wise power would not come to the sunken intake court as a rescue.

Rescue, in such a place, could be only another violence. It could mean tearing out the pipes, silencing the pumps, dragging the Attendant into a light it had not chosen, turning the chamber back into marsh by force, or making it clean in a way that erased everything it had become. Wisdom would understand that this pocket has already been made by pressure, collapse, heat, and need. It would not answer one wound by making another.

So the first thing the power would do would be almost nothing.

It would arrive as attention.

Not lightning. Not a voice. Not a figure too large for the small room. Not a change in the ordinary light. Only a sudden sense, felt in the walls and the water and the close hot air, that the place had been noticed. A drip might pause on a rusted lip for one beat longer than before. The transformer hum might deepen, not louder but more listened-to. The vapor might thin just enough for the outline of the control house to become clear. The chamber would not become holy. It would become witnessed.

A wise power would see the intake court as it is: not a ruin, not a monster, not an error, but a compressed organ with its own severe dignity. It would see the microbial mats as the first inhabitants. It would see the white crusts and dark green films not as failed life but as slow life. It would see the absence of animals not as proof of death, but as a rule. It would see the machinery not as decoration, and the heat not as malice, and the noise not as meaningless. It would understand that the room is loud because it is working. It would understand that the room is empty because it is still young. It would understand that the Attendant is not separate from the place in any simple way.

Then the power would listen.

It would listen to the layers: the slipping water, the drone behind the walls, the pumps when they begin, the valves when they release, the creak of metal expanding, the loose panel chattering, the hollow boom of water hammering through a conduit. It would hear the difference between ordinary sound and distress. It would hear where the system is strained. It would hear where the Attendant moves, where it pauses, where it waits for the first knock of the pipes before touching a wheel. It would hear the room’s false agency and know what is true beneath it: not that the place operates the being, but that the being has become the place’s way of enduring.

Only after listening would it act.

And even then, it would act carefully.

It would not drain the black pans. It would not stop the pumps. It would not make the walls dry, the air cool, the silence complete. It would know that some of the machinery may be holding back water from the marsh, or pressure from below, or heat from some deeper system. It would know that to interrupt the chamber suddenly could send harm outward into the ordinary reeds and channels above. A benevolent power is not made tender by impatience. It would not prefer a pretty landscape to a living one.

What it would do is this: it would remove what is needless.

It would begin with the entrance.

The raw steps, the slumped earth, the broken concrete, the gap between the retaining walls—these would not be made grand. They would not become polished or inviting. But they would become possible. A boot would find a firmer edge. A hand would find a rail that was not corroded through, though it might still look corroded. The ladder, if there was one, would no longer feel like a descent into a throat that might close. The way down would remain difficult. The way out would become real.

That would be the first gift: exit.

Not escape imposed, but exit made true.
