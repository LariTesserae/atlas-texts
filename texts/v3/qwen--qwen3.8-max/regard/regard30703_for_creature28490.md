## What a Benevolent Power Would Do

It would do something, but not what a spectator might call action.

A benevolent and wise power would not enter the Unbuilt Engine as a figure of light, nor would it announce itself through rupture greater than the ruptures already there. It would not split the overcast, still the hidden water, or command the cliffs to lower their raw faces into repose. It would not bring warmth where cold is doing necessary work. It would not bring ease where difficulty is the shape of becoming.

Its first act would be almost invisible: it would pay attention.

Not the attention of curiosity, not the attention of a collector pausing over an oddity, but the attention of recognition. It would look into that small, high, damp bowl and see that the place is not empty because it lacks animals, not incomplete because it lacks artifacts, not failed because it lacks sunlight or gentleness. It would see the hollow as what it is: a first machine made of pressure, water, stone, spore, and patience. It would see the being that lives there — not a single body, but a many-bodied operation — and it would understand that this being does not need to be given a face in order to be real.

To such a power, the Unbuilt Engine would be holy not despite its harshness but because its harshness is honest. It would be holy because it is making something from nothing without domination. It would be holy because it is loud, wet, crowded, and productive, yet asks for no witness. It would be holy because it is a beginning that has not yet learned to be proud of itself.

Therefore the power’s first deliberate action would be restraint.

It would not improve the hollow according to any external idea of beauty. It would not drain the mist to reveal the stone more clearly. It would not level the scree into a gentle slope. It would not silence the hammering behind the cliff face. It would not soften the electrical tension in the air or make the smell of ozone less sharp. It would not bring birds to the ledges, nor insects to the pools, nor tracks into the mineral dust, because it would know that animal life must arrive when the place has invited it, not before. It would not give the hollow a voice, because the hollow already has a voice: the thrum of constrained water, the click of shifting stones, the moan of air forced through fissures, the small hiss of rain on the rim.

A wise power would understand that to remove the danger from such a place would be to stop its engine. The falling stone is not merely destruction; it is part of the making. The cold is not merely absence; it preserves, slows, concentrates. The darkness is not merely deprivation; it keeps moisture close and prevents the young life from being burned away. The noise is not confusion; it is feedback, adjustment, the sound of the place learning its own structure. To make the Unbuilt Engine safe would be to make it something else.

So the power would not tame it.

It would bless it by leaving it unbuilt.

But restraint is not indifference.

After attention would come witness.

The power would see what no eyes otherwise see. It would notice the exact moment a moss cushion catches a new thread of mist and swells almost imperceptibly with water. It would notice the first microscopic pit opened in a quartz grain by lichen acid. It would notice a fungal thread moving through dead moss with the patience of a road being laid by touch alone. It would notice the way a single sedge root enters a crack too narrow for it, widening the crack by millimeters over years, making a future possible for another root. It would notice the invisible films of bacteria changing the taste of water, the way cyanobacteria darken a surface just enough to hold a little more warmth, the way a cushion plant becomes a nursery for spores that do not yet know they are being sheltered.

Nothing in the hollow would be overlooked merely because it is small. Nothing would be ignored merely because it is slow. Nothing would be considered meaningless merely because it has no audience.

This would be one of the power’s quiet gifts: to hold the place in a memory larger than the place can hold for itself.

Even if the bowl were buried by snow, even if a later collapse erased its present arrangement, even if the world outside forgot it entirely, the fact that it had run — that water had moved through it, that life had entered it, that stone had begun to become soil — would not be lost. A benevolent power would not need to preserve every form forever in order to honor it. It would preserve the truth of the place: that it happened, that it worked, that it mattered.

Then, if it acted further, it would act in the smallest possible mercies.

Not miracles that break the nature of the place, but adjustments so fine they would look like chance.

It might delay a killing frost by one hour when a newly rooted clump of sedge is most tender. It might nudge a wandering filament of mist so that it condenses against a moss bed that has begun to dry at its edges. It might cause a falling chip of rock to lodge where it becomes a shield rather than a projectile, creating a tiny lee where spores can gather. It might let a gust carry fungal dust not into the empty air but into a damp fissure where the stone is already beginning to crumble. It might keep one hidden water channel from choking completely, so the irregular hammering continues without building toward a catastrophic burst. It might let a beadline of water find a lichen colony that has been waiting, if stone can be said to wait, for one more mineral-rich drop.

It would not abolish accident. It would refine accident into opportunity.

It would not prevent every death. It would prevent unnecessary annihilation.

There is a difference, and a wise power would know it. Individual mosses may dry. Individual cushions may be torn away. Individual fungi may fruit briefly and vanish. Fragments of living skin may be stripped from the rock by a sudden squall. That is part of the hollow’s economy. But a benevolent power would protect the continuity of the process. It would guard not every life, but the possibility of life continuing. It would watch over the seams, the nurseries, the damp pockets, the first thin accumulations of dark matter. It would protect the places where the engine is most vulnerable: the newly colonized faces, the unstable ledges where the first roots are trying to bind what gravity is trying to sort.

It would also protect the hollow from being found too soon by powers less wise.

It would keep the place obscure. Not with dramatic enchantment, not with monsters or storms arranged like sentries, but with simple unimportance in the eyes of those who would exploit what they see. It would let maps forget the cirque. It would let climbers choose another ridge. It would let researchers pass nearby without noticing the small, high hollow where the rock is new and the mosses are crowded into every seam. It would make machines find no easy road to it. It would make the air around it feel too charged, too cold, too unsettled for casual intrusion.

If a human being did come there by accident, the power would not necessarily drive them away with fear. It might instead give them a feeling they could not name: a sense that the place was occupied without being inhabited, full without being welcoming, meaningful without being intended for them. They might stand at the rim, hear the hidden water hammering in the dark, smell the ozone and crushed green, and feel that to enter would be a trespass not against law but against time. Then they might leave, carrying only the memory of a sound and a pressure, and that would be enough.

Fame would be another violence. The power would spare the hollow that too.

Perhaps, very rarely, it would give one visible sign.

Not a permanent sign. Not a miracle that turns the place into a shrine. Perhaps only a moment of altered light.

Once in a generation, or once in a century, it might let the overcast thin for a few minutes. A narrow beam of sun could enter the bowl, not to dispel the darkness but to touch it. The wet surfaces would answer at once. Mica would glitter. Quartz veins would flash and vanish. Water beadlines would become tiny lenses. The mosses, so black-green in shadow, would brighten along their edges and seem, for a moment, to be lit from within. The liverworts would shine like old metal. The pools would stipple with reflected light. The mist itself would become a veil of moving silver.

The power would not make this last. It would let the light enter, let every living surface feel the quickening of it, let the engine run a little faster for a few breaths, and then it would let the mist return. The hollow would become dark again, but not exactly as before. Something in the green would have deepened. Something in the water would have warmed. Something in the first soil would have been encouraged without being exposed.

That would be enough. A gift should not become a dependency.

If the power wished to communicate with the being of the hollow, it would not use speech as humans understand it.

It would not ask the being to name itself. It would not give it self-consciousness as a reward, because self-consciousness is not always a gift. To a being made of slow adhesion, capillary water, spore drift, and mineral decay, sudden awareness might be a burden. The power would not impose the sorrow of knowing oneself upon something that is blessedly still becoming. It would not ask the beginning to become a monument.

Instead, it might give the hollow one moment of coherence.

After a rockfall, when the echo is still moving around the bowl and spores hang in the air like grey smoke, the power might allow a pause. Not silence — the hidden water would still thrum, the air would still moan in the fissures — but a pause in turbulence. For a few moments, the mist would thicken and hold still. The charged air would settle. The falling droplets would find their rhythm. The stones would cease their small grinding. The living mats would press closer to the rock. The entire hollow would feel, if feeling were possible at that level, the fact of its own arrangement.

In that pause, the engine would not think, but it would align.

Water would find its channels with less resistance. Spores would settle into seams already prepared for them. A cushion plant would hold its warmth a little longer. A lichen would continue its acid work with the patience of centuries compressed into one molecule. The power would not add itself to the scene as an object. It would become, for that moment, the place’s own capacity to continue.

If such a power can be said to speak, it would speak in the only language the hollow already understands: pressure, moisture, vibration, temperature, light, decay. Its word would not be a command. It would be a permission.

Continue.

Not continue because you are perfect. Continue because you are possible. Continue because the wound in the mountain is not only a wound. Continue because moss is a way of remembering water, because lichen is a way of asking stone to change, because fungus is a way of honoring what has died without letting it remain useless. Continue because soil is a slow promise made by countless small bodies. Continue because you are an engine without hands, and that is enough.

If catastrophe threatened the hollow from within — a collapse so complete that it would erase not just one ledge but the possibility of recolonization — the power might act more directly. But even then, it would not stop the mountain from being a mountain. It would not freeze change in place. It would not make the cliffs immutable.

It might divert a single stress line, causing a large fracture to open elsewhere rather than through the deepest nursery of mosses. It might let a hidden reservoir release gradually instead of bursting. It might cause one block to fall outward rather than inward, preserving a damp seam behind it. It might save a pocket of soil, a fragment of living crust, a damp crevice full of spores, rather than the entire visible bowl.
