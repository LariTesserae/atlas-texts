If such a power turned its attention to that basin, the first thing it would do is refuse the easiest kindness.

It would not send rain.

It would not loosen the high haze into clouds, or teach the dust to darken with damp, or make the stone weep into pools. It would not bring streams, however gentle, nor dew, however merciful, because to do that would be to answer the basin in a language that was never its own. Water would be a gift only to those who already believe that wetness is the universal sign of life. But this place had learned another grammar. Its life was sealed, resinous, warm, mineral, charged. It did not lack water and then heroically endure; it had become something else entirely. To remake it into a green and dripping place would not be salvation. It would be erasure.

So a benevolent and wise power would begin with restraint.

It would stand, if such a power can be said to stand anywhere, at the broken rim of the basin and look inward not as an owner, not as a judge, not as a gardener with pruning shears, but as a guest. It would listen before acting. It would hear the place first: the dry whisper of sift-mites moving over mineral powder, the sighing of the bellows-mosses, the faint tuned breath of the organ-reeds, the small crackle of static in the horn-fern fronds, the deep slow warmth rising through unseen veins in the rock. It would perceive that the basin was not empty. It was concentrated.

Only then would it look for the being who lived there.

Not the mites, though they were part of the life of the basin. Not the ants, though they were its builders. Not the thread-lice on the ferns or the bellows-mites riding the spore-clouds, though they were its breath and murmur. There was another presence there, harder to see at first because it moved with the patience of the place itself.
