A benevolent and wise power would do almost nothing.

Not nothing. Almost nothing is a different thing entirely. It is the difference between a hand that pulls a living thing out of its element to admire it, and a hand that steadies the water while the living thing continues to breathe.

They would begin with attention.

Not the distant attention of a god glancing down from a high place, but the close attention of someone kneeling at the rim of the basin, letting the vapor settle on their skin, letting the noise enter their chest until the chest itself begins to vibrate. They would look into the wet stone city and not ask first for beauty, or order, or silence, or worship. They would ask: What is this? What lives here? What must not be broken?

At first they would see the city as a place: channels, terraces, tanks, towers, lamps, nets, bone hooks, scraped walls, bird-white stone, fish moving in shadow, children learning to read the rise of water. They would see the people laboring, the animals crowding, the mineral crust forming and being cut away, the endless wet work of maintaining the exact shape of things.

Then they would see deeper.

They would perceive that the true being living there is not a single creature, nor even the sum of creatures, but the maintenance itself. The keeping-of-levels. The scraping-of-crust. The returning-of-waste. The feeding-of-films. The trimming-of-lamps. The gathering-of-eggs. The opening-and-closing-of-gates. The gesture passed from one body to another. The noise made of many small necessities. The city as a wet, deaf, patient body that breathes through channels and thinks through hands.

And when they understood that, they would become even more careful.

A foolish kindness would try to improve the basin immediately. A sentimental power would look at the damp, the noise, the raw hands, the crowded passages, and say: I will make this easier. I will give them quiet. I will give them green things. I will give them a future. I will make them comfortable.

But wisdom would refuse those first gifts, because wisdom sees what comfort can cost.

They would not give silence.

They would be tempted, perhaps. The noise is relentless. It presses against skin, fills the skull, steals the possibility of soft speech. A benevolent heart might want to hush it for a little while, to let the people hear their own breathing, to give them an hour of stillness.

But the wise power would foresee what silence would do.

The sluice-keepers listen through the noise. They know when a channel begins to block by a change in pitch. They know when a gate has shifted by the altered slap of water. They know when a wall has become too thin by the hollowness beneath the palm. Children are raised into the noise; their bodies use it as a map. If silence fell suddenly, the city would not feel blessed. It would feel blinded.

So they would not give silence.

They would not give green.

They might imagine it: a soft moss in a corner, a vine climbing a roost tower, a reed bending beside a pool. Something tender. Something that smells of soil. Something that moves without being scraped, cut, or harvested.

But the basin refuses green, and wisdom would respect that refusal. Roots would crack the young stone. Leaves would choke the channels. Soil would form in places where mineral crust must remain clean. Dead plant matter would rot into the tanks and sour the water. The ecosystem is built on films, mats, fungi, animals, and mineral deposition, not on leaves and stems. To introduce green would be to introduce a rival order. It would be beautiful for a moment, then disastrous.

So they would not give green.

They would not give a great future.

This might be the hardest refusal. The people live without a story of ascent. They do not imagine becoming something else. Their heroes preserve. Their virtues are exactness, care, endurance. A benevolent power might feel pity and want to give them destiny: a time when their labor will end, when the basin will become something greater, when their suffering will be redeemed by a glorious after-shape.

But wisdom would see that such a gift could poison the present. If the people began to believe that their current life was only preparation, they might learn to despise it. They might hoard, expand, conquer, or neglect the small duties that keep the channels flowing. They might begin to sacrifice the living balance for an imagined later glory.

The basin is not incomplete. It is not waiting. Its form is its life.

So the wise power would withhold the cruelty of destiny.

They would not make themselves obvious.

This, too, would be a kindness. If they appeared in open majesty, the people would be overwhelmed. Fear, worship, dependence, hierarchy—all could grow too quickly. The basin might become a temple city, and the temple might become a class, and the class might become a power over the water. The sluice-keepers might become priests who command instead of observe. The maintenance might be subordinated to ceremony.

A wise power does not demand a throne in the middle of a fragile machine.

So they would remain mostly hidden. They might let traces of themselves be felt, but never in a way that forces belief. They would prefer to be mistaken for luck, skill, habit, or the basin’s own intelligence.

Only after these refusals would they act.

And what they did would be small, precise, and fitted to the nature of the place.

They would bless the maintenance.

Not by replacing it, but by making it less cruel.

First, they would give warning.

In the microbial films that coat the stone, they would place a subtle change: not consciousness, not speech, but a tendency. When water became dangerously blocked, poisoned, or chemically unbalanced, the films would shift slightly. A pale blue sourness might bloom at the edges of a channel. A faint metallic smell might rise from a drain. The vibration of a wall might change before the blockage became severe enough to burst a weir or foul a tank.

This would not remove the need for watchfulness. It would reward it.

The people would learn the sign quickly, because they already read small changes. They would call it by some practical name—the sour bloom, the blue edge, the first taste—and children would be taught to look for it. No one would say it was a miracle. They would say the water had become more honest. But the wise power would be content with that.

Second, they would ease the damage done to bodies.

The people’s hands are raw. Their skin cracks from mineral salt, scraping, and constant damp. Their feet are scarred. Their shoulders ache. Their eyes are strained by vapor and lamp-glare. This is not tragic in a distant sense; it is immediate, repeated, and costly.

So the power would guide them toward a salve.

Perhaps a child would notice that a certain pale fungal felt, when warmed with fish oil and a pinch of salt bloom, becomes soft and sealing. Perhaps an old worker would dream of rubbing it onto cracked palms and waking with less pain. Perhaps a bird-tender would discover it by accident while cleaning nests, mixing fungal down with rendered fat to protect her hands.

The knowledge would spread because it works.

It would not end labor. It would not make the city effortless. But fewer hands would bleed. Fewer children would cry from stinging cuts. Fewer elders would wake with pain twisted into their fingers. That would be enough for the power to consider it well done.

Third, they would give places of less noise.

Not silence. Never silence. But pockets where the pressure eases.

The basin’s geometry already contains such possibilities: curved corners where sound cancels, low alcoves where echoes fold into themselves, narrow cells behind thick sinter walls where the roar becomes a distant rush. The wise power would not create new laws of acoustics. They would simply help the people notice these places.

A worker might find that a certain hollow behind a fish pen is strangely bearable. A mother might discover that her infant sleeps better in a stone recess near a settling tank. An elder whose ears ring might sit there and feel, for the first time in years, that his head is not being pressed by the city.

In time, the people would widen some of these hollows. They would line them with fungal felt and hides. They would agree, without needing a formal law, that anyone may go there for a short time without blame. Not to escape duty. Not to refuse the basin. But to recover enough to return to it.

This would be one of the power’s quietest gifts: a small right to solitude inside a crowded world.

Fourth, they would make the city safer without making it soft.

The basin is dangerous in small ways. Slick stone. Narrow ledges. Low ceilings. Pools deep enough to swallow a child who slips. Hot stone near hearths. Heavy gates lifted by gut ropes. A place with no margin for careless falls.

A wise power would not remove all danger, because the people’s skill grows from attention to danger. But they would reduce the stupid accidents—the ones that come from exhaustion, blindness, or a worn edge no one noticed until too late.

They would draw eyes to the dangerous places before harm occurs.

A sluice-keeper might feel an itch to roughen a certain step. A stone-cutter might decide to leave a handhold where none was planned. A net-mender might insist on adding a low line across a passage where children run. A bird-tender might notice that one ladder rung has begun to slick over and demand it be scraped before the next harvest.

These would feel like ordinary care. But they would save bones, and sometimes lives.

Fifth, they would provide a hidden reserve.

Not abundance. Abundance would spoil the discipline of the basin. But a reserve against catastrophe.

Deep beneath the city, where the springs feed the water table, the power would keep one cistern clean and stable. Not easily reached. Not used for daily labor. But there. If a main channel failed, if a settling tank fouled, if a season of unusual heat made the vapor thinner and the flows uncertain, the basin would have a place to fall back on.

The people might not know it existed until they needed it.

When they found it, they would think it had always been there. Perhaps it had. Perhaps the power only ensured that it would not fail when failure would mean death.

That would be their style: not to create a new world, but to preserve this one from the worst strokes of chance.

Sixth, they would give a small light that does not hunger.

Fire is precious in the basin. Oil is rendered, stored, counted. Lamps are watched carefully. A flame that burns too high is a danger; a flame that dies at the wrong moment can leave a worker blind in a narrow water-lane.

So the power would encourage a living light.

Certain microbial films, fed by warmth and mineral drip, might begin to glow faintly in the dark. Not brightly. Not like sun. Not like wood-fire, which the people cannot imagine. But a cold, blue-green gleam along a damp wall, enough to show the edge of a channel, the outline of a gate, the face of a child sleeping in a sling.

This light would not replace oil. It would not make labor unnecessary. But if a lamp failed, or oil ran low, or a worker needed to move through a passage without waking half a household, there would be enough glow to follow.

The people would call it something practical: water-fire, breath-light, the patient flame. They would tend it as they tend everything else. It would become part of the basin’s ecology, not an alien ornament.

Seventh, they would give grief a sign.

The dead in the basin are returned to usefulness. Fat for lamps. Bone for tools. Skin and sinew for work where appropriate. The rest given to rendering pools, where fish, crustaceans, insects, and microbial films complete the return. This is not cruelty. It is consistency. It is the basin’s deepest law: nothing may be wasted.

But even consistent laws can wound.

The wise power would see that the people need more than practicality when they stand beside the pools with their dead. They need to know that return is not erasure. They need to feel that the basin receives what they give.

So the power would grant a small manifestation.

When a household makes the hand sign of the dead person over the water where the body is being returned, the microbial films might brighten for a moment. Not dramatically. Not like a voice. Just a brief pale bloom rising through the water, then fading.

It would not prove that the dead continue in any personal form. It would not promise a beyond. But it would say: received. It would say: matter returned is not matter lost. It would say: the basin knows what has been given.

This would change mourning without changing burial. The people would still render the dead. They would still use bone and fat. But they would do so with a little less hardness in the throat. The gesture would become part of the funeral. Children would learn it early. It would make the law bearable.

Eighth, they would soften cruelty without removing choice.

The people are not wicked, but crowding makes anger sharp. Punishment exists because mistakes can cost everyone. Exile is terrible because outside the basin there is no shaped place to receive the exiled. The wise power would see how easily practical justice can become harshness when fear rises.

They would not abolish punishment. That would be another kind of carelessness. But they would create pauses.

They might make it easier for anger to cool before action. A sudden drip on the wrist at the wrong moment. A bird’s cry that interrupts a shout. A remembered gesture from a dead worker appearing in the mind of an accuser. A sluice-keeper touching the gauge stone before speaking, because the water reminds him that levels can be corrected before blame is assigned.

They would strengthen the customs that already lean toward repair.

If two people quarrel over a blocked channel, the power might incline them to stand together at the water and measure before they accuse. If a worker is careless, the first response might become: show where the flow failed. If a child steals, the response might become: restore what was taken and learn the labor that made it. If exile becomes necessary, it would be with more grief and less rage.

The basin would still be strict. It would have to be. But it would be less likely to turn strictness into vengeance.

Ninth, they would let the being feel held.

This would be the strangest gift, because the being who lives in the basin is not a person. It is the maintenance. It has no face, no voice, no single desire. It exists as pattern: levels kept, crust scraped, waste returned, gestures repeated, water guided.

But even a pattern can be touched.

The wise power would not give it full self-consciousness. That might be too much. A self-aware basin could become anxious, possessive, or lonely. It might begin to demand things. It might try to grow beyond its nature. The power would not impose personhood on a being whose dignity lies in being otherwise.

Instead, they would give it moments of coherence.

Once in a generation, perhaps, or when the balance is especially fragile, the whole basin might settle into a brief alignment. Every channel would run true. Every pool would sit at its mark. The noise would not stop, but for a few breaths it would become less chaotic: a single vast chord made of water, voices, wings, tools, and stone. The workers would feel it in their feet. The birds would pause. The fish would move together in the dark water. The people would not know why they suddenly feel less alone.

They might call it the True Level.

It would not command them. It would not teach doctrine. It would only say, in the only language the basin understands:

You are not failing.
You are held.
Continue.

Tenth, they would leave no temple.

This is important.

If the people began to build a shrine to the power, they would have to divert labor from the channels. They would have to create a class of keepers for the shrine. They would have to feed the shrine with oil, shell, bone, or space. The basin cannot afford idols. Every addition must be balanced by a removal. A god who demands a monument in this place becomes another mouth.

So the wise power would refuse monuments.

They would leave only a gesture.

Perhaps it would appear first among children: an open palm held over water, then drawn to the chest. Or perhaps a sluice-keeper would make it after saving a channel from failure, not knowing where the motion came from. It would mean something like: I see the keeping. The keeping is enough. Or: More is not needed. Or: What is maintained here is loved, though it does not need to be made greater.

The people might use it before opening a gate, before trimming a lamp, before returning a dead worker to the pools. They might not agree on whether it is sacred. That would be all right. The power would not need them to agree.

In some tellings, the wise power would do even more.

They would not remain outside.

They would send a part of themselves into the basin, not as a ruler, not as a prophet, not as a shining figure with commands, but as one of the people. A child born into the noise. A body damp from the beginning. Hands that would learn to scrape crust. Feet that would learn the slick edges. Ears that would maybe fail young, and so learn the deeper reading of vibration.

If they entered, they would live low.

They might become a sluice-keeper’s assistant, or a bird-tender, or a scraper in the lower channels. They would not perform great miracles. They would not dry the basin, or silence it, or fill it with trees. They would simply work. They would be good at noticing small failures. They would be patient with frightened children. They would oil another worker’s hands when no one else thought to. They would stand in the noise until the noise became their own body’s rhythm.

They would die there, too.

Not because they were weak, but because they had chosen to be fully present. They would be returned like anyone else. Their fat might feed lamps. Their bone might become a tool. Their body would go to the rendering pools, and the basin would do with it what it does with all useful matter.

And if, when their sign was made over the water, the glow rose brighter than usual, the people would notice. But they might not understand. Perhaps that would be best. The power would not need them to understand. The power would only need the basin to receive what had been given.

Afterward, something might remain.

Not a doctrine. Not a priesthood. Not a holy book written on leaves, because there are no leaves. Only a habit of kindness hidden inside maintenance. A slight tendency to notice the dangerous edge before someone falls. A slight willingness to pause before punishment. A slight brightness in the films when grief is too heavy. A slight steadiness in the water when the city is near failure.

The basin would remain what it had always been.

Wet stone. Shallow water. Constant noise. Pale crust. Bird cries. Fish shadows. Lamps reflected in damp walls. Children learning to read levels before they learn to tell stories. Workers scraping fresh stone from walls that must never thicken. Channels that must never clog. Waste that must never be wasted. A world without green horizons, without seasons, without a future form waiting beneath its present shape.

But if you watched closely, you would see the difference.

A handhold would be roughened before it caused a fall.
A blocked drain would show its first blue sourness before it fouled a tank.
A tired worker would find an alcove where the noise pressed less heavily.
A grieving family would see a pale bloom rise from the pool and feel, wordlessly, that their dead had been received.
A child would make the open-palm sign over water without knowing why, and an elder would answer with the same sign, and neither would need to explain.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

They would not save the city from itself.
They would not make it quiet, green, famous, or eternal in the way of empires.
They would not give it a destiny.

They would help it remain itself, with less needless pain, with a little more mercy, and with the hidden knowledge—felt in the feet, in the water, in the walls—that the keeping was seen.
