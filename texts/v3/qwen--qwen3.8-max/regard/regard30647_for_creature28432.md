If such a place and such a being drew the attention of a benevolent and wise power, they would not begin by changing anything.

They would not send rain.

That would be the first and most important act of kindness, though it would not look like kindness to a softer heart. They would not loosen the salt with water. They would not bring seepage into the cracks, or mist into the air, or the green smell of living roots into the mineral dust. They would not make a single blade of grass lift itself from the powdered pan and announce that mercy had come. To do that would be conquest, not care. The hollow is not a wound waiting to be healed by wetness. It is not a failed garden. It is a finished thing, severe and full, and its life is not a poverty to be corrected but a discipline already perfected.

A foolish benevolence would try to make the hollow gentler.

A wise one would understand that gentleness there would be violence.

So the power would come, if they came at all, as attention.

Not as a figure. Not as a voice. Not as a change in the light. The ordinary grey light would remain ordinary. The blunt shafts falling through roof-fissures would not turn gold. The drone of the buried machines would not become music, because it was never meant to become music. It was meant to remain a drone: a broad, continuous, indifferent foundation on which ten thousand small lives had built their exactness.

Attention would enter the hollow the way a hand enters near a sleeping animal without touching it.

For one brief pulse of time, everything would become more present to itself.

The salt-fleas would stop in their quick colonies. Not scatter. Not leap. Only halt, their antennae flattening as if the crust beneath them had suddenly become more real. The grubs under the dust would feel a pressure that was not weight, and would neither rise nor flee, but listen with their whole bodies. The brick-beetles would pause mid-work, mandibles still, their worn backs catching the grey light. The mites would slow their rust-colored waves. Along the walls, the mica-moths would ripple once from the entrance to the farthest recess, a dry whisper passing through overlapping wings, and the swarm overhead would thin into stillness as though every tiny flier had remembered at once how to be motionless.

Then the power would do nothing further.

Not yet.

They would wait, because wisdom knows that the first mistake of love is urgency.

In that waiting, they would perceive the hollow not as a collection of strange creatures, not as a curiosity, not as a problem of missing water or missing green, but as a single sustained fact. They would see the floor as a skin. They would see the walls as a tiered organ of roosting and signal. They would see the air as a nervous system of heat, charge, and vibration. They would see the black chemolithotrophic crust not as a base food but as the slow dark mouth through which ruin had been made edible. They would hear the noise not as chaos but as a living syntax: alarm, appetite, position, heat, claim, warning, rest, resumption.

And they would recognize the being who lives there.

Not a king in the basin. Not a hidden brain behind the salt. Not a single animal dreaming at the center of the hollow while the others serve it. They would recognize the inhabitant as a plural thing, a distributed body made of separate hungers that have worn into one another like stones in a dry tumbler. They would see that there is no ruler there, and no need for one. The hollow thinks by interaction. It remembers by habit. It speaks by resonance. Its selfhood is not enclosed in a skull but stretched across movements: the contract of salt-fleas, the wall-ripple of moths, the tremor-answer of grubs, the reorganization of swarms, the slow tapping of heavy processors in the deep recesses.

A lesser power might want to give such a being a face.

A wise power would not.

A face would be an organ from another world. It would impose a false unity on something already unified in a harder way. So they would not give the hollow a face. They would not give it a voice like speech. They would not give it longing, shame, hope, or sorrow in the way of soft-bodied creatures. They would not make it wish to be anything other than what it is.

They would simply let it know, in whatever way such a being can know, that it had been seen.

But that would not be all.

Because benevolence, if it is wise, does not only admire. It also guards. It does not only witness. It also makes room for what is fragile to remain hard.

So the power would act, but their action would be almost invisible.

They would begin with thresholds.

The entrance to the hollow is narrow, and that narrowness is holy there, though the hollow has no word for holiness. The crack between warped slabs of fused brick and slag is not a flaw. It is a gate that keeps the basin from being overwhelmed. A wise power would not widen it. They would not make the entrance grand. They would not set pillars there, or marks, or signs. They would keep it difficult. They would keep it obscure. They would let it remain just wide enough for what belongs to pass through, and too small for careless ruin to follow.

Above, on the low salt plain, they would bend chance away.

Not with thunder. Not with command. With the smallest possible pressure on probability.

A surveyor’s line would drift a little farther east than intended. A machine sent to probe the ground would meet a stubborn seam of old slag and be turned aside. A map would be drawn with one blank space that later copyists would assume meant nothing. A traveler with idle curiosity would feel a dry unease and walk on. A plunderer would find only plain salt and lose interest. A well-meaning fool with water in their thoughts would dream of cracked lips and sealed jars and wake unwilling to bring moisture where it was not wanted.

If the outside world had the habit of destroying what it cannot understand, then the power would make the outside world slightly less able to find this place.

That would be their first protection.

Not isolation, exactly. The hollow is not sealed from all things. Air still moves through fissures. Light still enters. Heat still rises from buried works. The world above still presses its weather and time against the roof. But there is a difference between exchange and violation, and a wise power knows how to guard that difference without making a prison.

They would not wall the hollow off. They would preserve its thresholds.

Then they would attend to the machines.

Not to repair them. That would be a mistake. The machines are not meant to be restored to their original purpose. They are not meant to become efficient, clean, obedient, or new. Their value in the hollow lies precisely in their half-ruined continuance: the leaking current, the warming transformer, the shuddering motor, the buried fan that turns because some old command still clings to its coils. If the power made the machinery perfect, they would change the ecology as surely as if they had poured water into the basin.

So they would not restore the machines.

They would keep them from final seizure.

That is a subtle thing, and it is the kind of thing wisdom does.

A bearing deep in the stone would not be made new, but it would be kept from shattering at the worst hour. A transformer would not be given greater power, but it would be spared the one surge that would have burned its last useful warmth away. A fan would wobble, groan, and continue. A cable would fray, but not in the exact place where a spark would have set old grease into sudden fire. A motor would drop into a lower note, and the swarms would reorganize, and the hollow would go on.

To an engineer, such changes would look like luck.

To the creatures, they would be weather.

To the power, they would be tending.

Because the hollow is old and dry and loud, and its life depends on the persistence of its noise. The drone is not merely sound there. It is structure. It is the low common field in which all the smaller lives arrange themselves. If the drone failed utterly, the swarms would lose their columns. The pipe-weavers’ threads would no longer tremor with meaning. The grubs would wait in a silence that had no forecast. The heat-points would cool, and the crust would thin, and the whole narrow chain of grazing and hunting and grinding would begin to break link by link.

So the wise power would not give the hollow abundance.

They would give it endurance.

And endurance, in such a place, is the truer mercy.

They would also make room for margin.

Not surplus. Surplus would be dangerous. Surplus would breed softness, explosion, collapse. The hollow is built on scarcity and exactness, and too much of anything would distort it. But margin is not surplus. Margin is the difference between a hard season and an annihilating one. Margin is the cracked seam where a few extra black flakes cling during a long cold interval. Margin is the hidden bolt-hole where a cluster of eggs escapes a tremor. Margin is the old vent shaft that remains just open enough for air to move when another passage shifts. Margin is the slight delay before a stone falls, long enough for the heavy processors to move their slow bodies out from under it.

A benevolent power would not remove danger from the hollow.

They would only keep danger from becoming absolute.

They would not save every creature. That would be another kind of violence. To save every creature would be to abolish the relations by which the creatures are what they are. The grub must listen for prey. The hunter must strike. The scavenger must find the dead. The crust must be grazed, and grazers must be taken, and taken bodies must be broken into dust. If every individual were preserved, the hollow would become a museum, not a living place. It would fill with stalled bodies and unfinished deaths. Its loop would kink. Its noise would lose coherence.

So the power would not intervene in every small death.

They would not catch each falling moth. They would not turn aside every strike. They would not feed the hungry with miracles. They would not make the grubs gentle or the beetles merciful. They would not teach the hunters to refrain. The hunger there is not evil. It is the shape by which life continues.

But they would know the difference between ordinary death and meaningless destruction.

If a whole roost were about to be smothered by a collapse that had no part in the hollow’s own slow settling, they might let the failure happen somewhere else instead. If a generation of larvae were about to be lost to a chance cold pulse that came too early, they might let one warm cable hold just long enough. If a swarm were about to be scattered by a sudden pressure change from above, they might let the nearest vent shaft breathe once more evenly. Not to erase risk. Only to keep risk within the compass of the hollow’s ancient adaptation.

They would not make life easy.

They would keep life possible.
