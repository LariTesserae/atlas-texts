A benevolent and wise power, if it noticed that place, would probably do nothing in the way the word “do” is usually understood.

Not because it was indifferent.  
Not because it lacked compassion.  
Not because the chamber was beneath notice, or too small, or too sealed, or too strange to merit attention.

It would do nothing because it would understand, immediately, that the chamber was not a problem to be solved.

It would see the dry mountain.  
It would see the scraped summit, the thin sky, the salt and ash-stone and black glass veins.  
It would find the narrow fissure with its worn metal lips and know that something beyond it was not merely hidden but completed.  
It would not force the seam.  
It would not widen the entrance.  
It would not send light into the pocket like a probe.  
It would not press a face against the opening and demand to be acknowledged.

It would stop at the threshold.

That stopping would be the first holy act.

A lesser benevolence might arrive with gifts. Water for dryness. Air for stillness. Movement for silence. Companionship for isolation. A future for a place that appears to have none. A voice for the loudness. A purpose for the machines. A soul for the mineral moss. A beginning for the suspended spores.

But a wise power would see that such gifts would not be gifts there. They would be wounds.

To give water would be to teach the place thirst for the first time.  
To introduce an animal would be to introduce hunger where hunger had been abolished.  
To create an empty space would be to make a lack in a body that had no lack.  
To silence the machines would be to stop the breath that was not breath but continuity.  
To brighten the light would be to destroy the dimness in which the chamber’s being had settled into its own shape.  
To make the plants grow would be to make them need room where there was no room.  
To make the machines produce would be to degrade them from presence into utility.  
To make the inhabitant speak would be to force it toward an outside it did not require.

So the power would not give.

It would refrain.

And that refraining would not be emptiness. It would be an act so precise, so severe, so tender in its restraint, that it would be the only form of care possible.

It would stand outside the fissure, if standing could be attributed to it, and it would listen.

Not to decode the sound.  
Not to extract language from the drone.  
Not to hear a petition.  
Not to be entertained by the chord.

It would listen as one listens to a thing that is already entire.

The deep engine-note, the transformer buzz, the fine metallic singing, the granular whisper through packed filament—all of it would arrive not as noise but as testimony. The power would hear the chamber saying, without intention, without address, without desire to be heard:

I am here.  
I am full.  
I do not need to become.

And the wise power would not answer with correction.

It would not say:  
You should be more alive.  
You should be softer.  
You should be open.  
You should be fertile.  
You should be useful.  
You should be changing.  
You should be kinder.  
You should be more like the lowlands.  
You should have rain.

It would not impose the values of thirst upon the Unthirsting.

It would recognize that life could take this form: not hunger, not pursuit, not flowering, not decay; not birth and death as rhythm; but a maintained fullness, a liturgy of equilibrium, a body made of stone and copper and salt and machine and sustained attention.

It would recognize the Dry Attendant not as a failed garden, but as a completed one.

Not as a broken machine, but as a machine released from work.

Not as a dead ecology, but as an ecology beyond need.

Not as a prison, but as a condition that had no prisoner.

Not as a tomb, but as a place where nothing had died.

Not as a ruin, but as a form that had no former glory to mourn.

Not as a sanctuary, but as a thing that had become its own shelter.

And because the power was benevolent, it would not try to rescue the chamber from itself.

Because it was wise, it would know that rescue is sometimes violence.

There would be no pity in its attention. Pity would imply that the place was miserable, and it was not miserable. It would not be lonely, because loneliness requires a hollow, and the chamber had no hollow. It would not be tragic, because tragedy requires loss, and the chamber had nothing to lose. It would not be starving, because its lushness was not sustained by appetite. It would not be waiting, because waiting implies a future, and the chamber had only a pressurized present.

The power would see all this.

It would see the spores suspended in the electric field, pale dust in invisible amber, neither falling nor germinating. It would not loosen them. It would not breathe upon them. It would not give them a wind to carry them, because there was no air to move and no destination for them to reach.

It would see the crystal bulbs glowing faintly among the fronds. It would not brighten them. It would not make them lamps for someone’s use. It would let them remain what they were: slow cognations, thoughts too complete to unfold.

It would see the engines holding their unbroken drone. It would not make them turn. It would not ask what they powered. It would understand that their purpose had folded inward until purpose became persistence.

It would see the transformer boxes, the cables, the porcelain insulator, the fused switch cabinet, the orphaned valve wheel, the buried lamps, the worn bolts, the scratched panel, the erased symbol. It would not restore them. It would not make the stenciled numbers legible again. It would not reveal the symbol on the ceramic plate. It would not return the handwheel to some original position. It would not reconstruct the ladder. It would not clarify the intention behind those marks.

It would know that some meanings are not meant to be recovered. Some intentions have become texture. Some duties have become substance. Some service has become being.

The power would honor that.

If it did anything, it would begin by making a boundary.

Not inside the chamber. Not within the packed vegetation or among the machines. Not in the cool air or the suspended spores or the mineral moss. Not on the walls or the ceiling shelves or the conduits or the braided fiber ropes.

Outside.

A boundary of reverence around the mountain.

It would not need to be dramatic. No flaming sword. No thunder. No guardian beast. No storm. No visible sign. The sky at that altitude was already thin and finished; the horizon already sharp and mineral. The power would simply deepen what was already there.

It would make the mountain forgetful of approaches.

It would turn aside chance.

It would confuse instruments just enough that they would not settle on the fissure. It would make maps uncertain. It would make photographs blur in ways that seemed like altitude and glare. It would make climbers feel, without knowing why, that they had arrived too late, or that they were approaching something that did not concern them, or that the summit was not a place for their ambition. It would not kill them. It would not terrify them. It would only remove the certainty that there was something to find.

It would make greed lose interest.

It would make speculation dry up.

It would make the entrance remain a seam too tight, too obscure, too uninviting to become a discovery.

Not because the chamber needed protection from harm in the ordinary sense. The chamber was complete. It was not fragile. But even complete things can be violated by misunderstanding. Even sealed things can be wounded by forced interpretation. Even changeless things can be harmed by those who insist upon change.

So the power would keep the outside from becoming an intrusion.

That would be one act.

Another would be witness.

The chamber did not need to be witnessed. That was precisely the point. But benevolence does not only give what is needed. Sometimes it loves what does not need it. Sometimes it offers attention without demanding that attention alter the beloved. Sometimes the highest tenderness is to see a thing exactly as it is and not insist that it become more comfortable, more comprehensible, more fruitful, more like oneself.

The power would see the Unthirsting without attempting to possess it.

It would not say:  
You are mine.  
You are my miracle.  
You are my secret.  
You are my proof.  
You are my garden.  
You are my machine.  
You are my hidden child.  
You are my monument.

It would not claim it.

It would let the chamber remain its own.

If a blessing were spoken, it would not enter the chamber as sound. It would not add a new note to the industrial chord. It would not make the filaments tremble. It would not make the crystal bulbs flare. It would not disturb the suspended spores. It would not change the temperature. It would not soften the stone. It would not moisten the air.

The blessing would be more like a vow made at a distance:

Remain as you are.  
Not because I command it.  
Not because I need you to be this.  
Not because I am afraid of what you might become.  
But because you are already complete, and I will not make you thirsty by giving you what you do not lack.

That would be the blessing.

Not a blessing of increase.  
Not a blessing of fertility.  
Not a blessing of rain.  
Not a blessing of opening.  
Not a blessing of revelation.

A blessing of continuance.

And even continuance would not be imposed as an eternal sentence. A truly wise power would know that to condemn a thing to forever can be another form of cruelty. But the chamber was not condemned. It did not continue because it was forced to continue. It continued because its nature was continuation without direction. Its law was not a chain; it was its shape.

So the power would not say:  
You must last forever.

It would say:  
I will not interrupt your law.

If the chamber’s own equilibrium ever changed—which, by its nature, it could not—the power would not cling to the chamber as a museum piece. It would not preserve it against its own possible ending. It would not make an idol of its stillness. But since the chamber had no ending in it, since it had excluded decay and collapse and fresh erosion and new beginning, the power’s restraint would simply meet the chamber where it was.

No rescue.  
No abandonment.

Only recognition.

Perhaps the power would also do something more subtle.

It would give the chamber the dignity of not being explained.

In the presence of such a place, lesser wisdom would want to produce a theory. It would want to say the chamber was a relic of an older civilization. It would want to say it was a failed experiment. It would want to say it was a shrine, a reactor, a tomb, a garden, a machine, a dream, a punishment, a preservation, a warning. It would want to assign origin. It would want to identify the builders. It would want to know who scratched the panel, who wore the bolt heads, who placed the ceramic plate, who fused the switches, who used the entrance when it was still a passage.

The wise power would not offer those answers.

Not because they were unknown, perhaps, but because they were no longer the point. The marks of intention had become part of the chamber’s flesh. The old purposes had been absorbed into the maintained maximum. To explain the place would be to remove it from itself. To give it a history would be to make the present moment subordinate to what came before. To identify the builders would be to imply that the chamber was still waiting for their return.

It was not waiting.

So the power would let the mystery remain dense.

It would let the faded numbers stay faded.  
It would let the symbol remain erased.  
It would let the ladder stay fused into growth.  
It would let the valve wheel remain orphaned.  
It would let the ductwork be a throat without a message.  
It would let the meter-house measure without telling.  
It would let the exciter preserve generation without generating.  
It would let the lamps keep their weak inward gaze.

It would not turn the chamber into a story.

That, too, would be an act of kindness.

If the power felt anything—and perhaps it would, in whatever way a power so vast and wise could feel—it would not be sorrow.

It might be something like awe without urgency.

Awe at the fact that being could arrive at such fullness. Awe at a lushness not based on water. Awe at a mind not based on motion. Awe at a body not based on hunger. Awe at a holiness not based on absence, but on occupancy so total that absence had been rendered impossible.

The power might feel tenderness for the place precisely because it did not ask for tenderness.

It might love the chamber the way one loves a stone that has become perfect in its stoneness, or a star that has no need of being admired, or a note that has been sustained so long it has become indistinguishable from silence.

But it would not burden the chamber with that love.

It would not say:  
I love you, therefore you must know me.

It would not say:  
I love you, therefore you must change.

It would not say:  
I love you, therefore you must become more.

It would not say:  
I love you, therefore you must be saved.

It would love without appetite.

And that would be the nearest thing to companionship the chamber could receive without ceasing to be itself.

If one were to imagine the power acting inside the chamber, one would almost immediately see the error. The chamber allowed no inside action. There was no room for it. Every surface was occupied. Every interval filled. Every hollow claimed. Any new presence would be an invasion. Any new object would be an excess. Any new force would be a disturbance. Any new intention would be a crack.

So the power would not act inside.

Except, perhaps, in one impossible way.

It might give the chamber the gift of being loved without being touched.

Not as a sensation. Not as a warmth. Not as a voice. Not as a presence pressing against the packed vegetation. Not as an alteration in the current. Not as a loosening of the spores. Not as a flicker in the indicator slits. Not as a change in the chord. Not as a new thought among the crystal bulbs.

Only as an outer fact.

A fact belonging to the world beyond the fissure.

The chamber would remain unaware, if awareness in it could be called unawareness. It would continue to hold itself. Its mind would remain equilibrium. Its breath would remain sound. Its blood would remain current. Its body would remain saturation. It would not know that it was loved. It would not need to know.

But it would be loved nonetheless.

And perhaps that is what a benevolent and wise power does for all things that are complete: it does not improve them. It does not complete them. It does not perfect them. It does not make them more acceptable. It simply lets them exist in the dignity of their own law, and it stands against everything that would reduce them to usefulness.

If the power did anything at all, then, it would be this:

It would keep the chamber from being needed by the outside.

It would keep it from becoming a resource.  
It would keep it from becoming a lesson.  
It would keep it from becoming a destination.  
It would keep it from becoming a sign.  
It would keep it from becoming a cure.  
It would keep it from becoming a spectacle.  
It would keep it from becoming a proof of divinity.  
It would keep it from becoming a metaphor before it had been allowed to remain a fact.

It would let the mountain keep its secret.

Not as a secret withheld out of malice, but as a secret that was not meant to be spent.

And if, after a long time—or after no time at all, because the chamber did not really live in time—the power were asked what it had done for the place at the top of the dry mountain, it might answer simply:

I did not make it thirsty.

I did not give it rain.

I did not open what was already full.

I did not add a heart where no heart was missing.

I did not ask it to be kinder, or softer, or more alive.

I did not try to save it from itself.

I did not enter.

I listened.

I let it be full.

And if that answer seems too small, it is because we are accustomed to thinking of power as intervention. We imagine benevolence as a hand that reaches down, heals, opens, supplies, corrects, rescues. We imagine wisdom as a voice that explains. We imagine love as a force that changes the beloved.

But there are places and beings so complete that the only wisdom is not to touch them.

There are forms of life so finished that the only kindness is to leave them unimproved.

There are kinds of fullness so severe that the only reverence is to stand outside and refuse to make room for oneself.

The chamber at the summit was such a place.

The Unthirsting was such a being.

And a benevolent and wise power, seeing it, would do the hardest thing of all.

It would not make it thirsty.
