They would do almost nothing.

That is the first answer, and it is not indifference.

A benevolent and wise power, looking into that high wet bowl, would understand immediately that the place did not need to be rescued from itself. It would see that the basin was already a kind of answer: an answer to cold, to water, to light, to gravity, to scarcity, to the constant pressure of change. The people there—the Cup-born, Those Who Answer the Water, the ones whose hands can feel new melt from old seepage and tell, before the eye sees it, whether a bead of water is about to fall, freeze, or run—were not a mistake to be corrected. They were a discipline of belonging. They were a life-form made of habits, repairs, watchfulness, and restraint.

So the power would not arrive as glory.

It would not stand on the rim in fire, because fire would scar the hides, blind the watchers, and turn the quiet into fear. It would not speak in thunder, because sound there is not merely sound; vibration loosens crusts, disturbs nests, startles fish, and can make ice answer before the people are ready. It would not descend as a monarch, because the basin has no throne room, no plaza, no open ground for obeisance. It would not make itself the loudest, brightest, or largest thing in the bowl, because that would be a violence against the hush that keeps the bowl alive.

If such a power came at all, it would come as attention.

It would first become quiet enough to be missed.

### I. The First Courtesy

A wise power would keep the rules.

That would be its first act, and perhaps the most important one.

It would not strike stone loudly. It would not shout near the upper ice. If it needed to move water, it would mark the channel. If it needed to alter a path, it would leave the sign: red cord for stop, white pebble for watched, broken feather for tainted flow. It would not build a permanent shrine in a melt-line, because even a god’s shrine, set where water has flowed three seasons running, would become a dam, a hazard, an insult to the settlement’s oldest knowledge.

It would not waste light.

It would not ignore the animals.

It would understand that in a place like this, manners are not decorative. Manners are survival. A careless gift could flood a house. A brilliant revelation could blind a child. A permanent miracle could make the people forget how to repair what shifts. The power would know that benevolence without local knowledge is just another avalanche: dramatic, destructive, and indifferent to the shape of what it buries.

So it would lower itself into the basin the way a careful traveler lowers a body onto a narrow ledge: slowly, with weight distributed, with no sudden sound.

It would look.

It would listen.

It would learn the temperature of every shelf.
