I think they would be very careful.

A benevolent and wise power, if it truly were wise, would not arrive as a spectacle. It would not tear open the ceiling to pour in warm sunlight. It would not make green things burst from the limestone. It would not give the people metal, or fire that burns without end, or a king, or a prophecy, or a loud and shining sign that forced every knee to bend.

It would look at the basin and understand at once that this place is not a wounded world waiting to be corrected. It is a finished kind of world, strange and severe and coherent. It is not poor because it lacks leaves. It is rich in attention. Its people have built a city not by conquering the stone, but by listening to it. Their virtue is not force, but fitting. Their peace is not stillness alone, but a practiced sliding with change.

So the first thing such a power would do would be almost nothing.

Not nothing out of indifference. Nothing out of reverence.

---

### What the power would see

It would see a basin where light is shared like breath.

It would see water moving through absence and return, never fully owned, never fully refused.

It would see people whose hands are more articulate than their voices, whose children learn the sign for water before they learn to boast, whose elders are not rulers but interpreters of damp and stone.

It would see blind crustaceans raking the living skin of rock, pale fish turning in cisterns like thoughts in deep water, scavengers soft-footed and necessary, fungal felts digesting death into patience.

And it would see that the being who lives there is not one thing only.

If the power looked for a single being, it might not find a chosen individual in the way surface myths imagine one. There would be no obvious hero standing in the center of the plaza with destiny clinging to their shoulders like a cloak. The being of that place is distributed: the people, the animals, the living films, the water routes, the mirror paths, the quiet habits of restraint. The basin itself breathes as one pattern.

But if the power did rest on one person, it would likely choose someone unremarkable in the way the world usually values. Not the strongest. Not the loudest. Not the most beautiful.

It might choose a mirror-keeper who notices a dimming before anyone else.

Or a water-tender who remembers where a channel ran three generations ago.

Or a child who sees glass-worms rise and does not speak, but points.

The being singled out would not be made above the others. In that basin, to be lifted above would be a kind of violence. The power would know this.

---

### What wisdom would refuse

A foolish kindness would say:

*I will give them forests.*

But a wise kindness would see that forests would unmake the basin.

Wood would bring beams and rafters, and with them a different architecture. Fire would bring smoke, and smoke would stain the mirrors. Roots would crack the old stone. Soil would bring seeds, seeds would bring seasons, seasons would bring hoarding. The people might grow numerous, yes. They might grow powerful, yes. But they would lose the exactness that lets them live without dominating their world.

The power would not give them what would erase their best nature.

It would not give them endless water either.

If every hollow were always full, the city would forget how to watch. If moisture came without pause, the people would no longer need to know the memory of channels, the language of mist, the patience of cisterns. Water would become ordinary in the worst sense: not sacred, not noticed, not followed. The basin’s life depends on absence as much as presence. A wise power would not remove absence; it would only keep absence from becoming annihilation.

It would not eliminate predators.

It would not make the soft-footed scavengers gentle and useless. It would not let the plate-grazers multiply until every crust was eaten bare. It would not allow the cistern fish to become so numerous that their own waste choked them. Limits are part of the basin’s mercy. To remove all limits would be to invite a worse suffering later.

It would not make the people immortal.

Death there is not an insult. It is a return. Bodies are given back to stone, to scavengers, to water, to fungal felt. The power would not violate that economy by making death impossible. It would, perhaps, take away some deaths: deaths by panic, deaths by avoidable collapse, deaths by poison, deaths by cruelty. But it would not take away the old, patient way of returning.

It would not reveal itself loudly.

A voice from above would be clumsy in a place where small signals carry far. A blazing manifestation would blind eyes adapted to cool reflected light. A commandment thundered from the ceiling would scatter fish, frighten children, and make the people forget their own hard-won discernment. If the power spoke, it would speak in their grammar: a tap, a drip, a shell placed point-down, a beam shifted by a hair, a dream of water in an elder’s sleep.

---

### The first act: consent

Before changing anything, the power would ask.

Not in words.

It would leave a sign where only the most attentive would see it.

Perhaps a single drop would fall from a dry ceiling rib into a shallow pan at a moment when no drop should fall. The drop would make a circle in the still water, and the circle would not break as quickly as it should.

Perhaps a mirror panel would catch a beam that had never reached it before, and the light would lie on the stone in the shape of an open hand.

Perhaps a row of shells would be found arranged on a threshold: three small ones, pale as milk, and one larger one cracked down the center. The arrangement would mean nothing and everything. The people would recognize that it is not random.

Then the power would wait.

Waiting is not weakness for such a power. Waiting is respect.

If the basin answered with fear, with hostility, with attempts to cover the sign and forget it, the power might withdraw into the background and offer only the smallest preservation: keeping collapse away, softening drought, turning aside catastrophe. It would not force intimacy.

If the basin answered with curiosity—if a child touched the light-shape with careful fingers, if an elder tapped back a rhythm of inquiry, if a water-tender left a polished pebble by the unexpected drop—the power would understand that as permission to come closer.

Not as possession.

As companionship.

---

### What the power would give

It would give margin.

That, I think, would be its first true gift. Not abundance, but margin.

The basin already lives close to balance. A wise power would not make balance effortless, because effortless balance becomes careless. But it would prevent every dry spell from becoming famine. It would prevent every flood from becoming ruin. It would make the difference between hard season and killing season just wide enough for the people to breathe.

It might do this in ways so subtle that only the oldest water-tenders would notice.

A hidden seam in the stone would hold a little moisture longer.

A ceiling rib would bead with mist a few moments before a dangerous rise in water, giving time to move racks.

A cistern would not sour as quickly during scarcity.

A breeding pool would remain just stable enough for the young fish to survive an uncertain interval.

A dry pan would keep one thin living crust in its lowest hollow, enough to renew the rest when moisture returned.

The people would not say, “A god did this.”

They would say, “The basin is kind this season.”

And perhaps that would be enough.

---

### It would teach through dreams

Because the people already value perception, the power would strengthen perception rather than replace it.

It would not tell them what to do.

It would help them notice sooner.

Elders might dream of old channels: water running where no water had run in living memory, and in the dream, a child standing safely beside it. They would wake knowing where to place a new trough.

Mirror-keepers might dream of light paths crossing stone like pale threads. They would wake and find one panel slightly out of angle, correct it, and see three districts brighten.

Felt-tenders might dream of fungal mats turning blue at the edges—not the blue of health, but the blue of exhaustion. They would wake and know which alcoves need rest.

Children might dream of glass-worms rising in long silver lines. They would wake and feel the air change, and before they could name it, they would already be signing: water, wait, listen.

The power would not make prophecy common. It would make attention deeper.

---

### It would protect the rules they already know

The basin has its laws:

Light is common.

Water has right of way.

Ancient stone is not cut wantonly.

Sound is not wasted.

Life is not taken without use.

A wise power would not invent new commandments. It would deepen the old ones.

It would make the consequences of violation clear, but not cruel.

If someone blocked a light-path out of selfishness, the blocked district might dim just enough for the offender to feel the loss in their own dwelling. Not as punishment. As teaching. The light would not vanish; it would become difficult. The mirror panel would refuse to hold the angle. The beam would scatter. The person would learn that stolen light is not a possession but a broken relationship.

If someone poisoned water—even accidentally, through careless waste—the pool would grow bitter and refuse to reflect. Fish would turn away. The water would not become death, but it would become unmistakably wrong until cleaned.

If someone scarred the ancient stone without cause, the cut might gather dust too quickly, or seep too unpredictably, or echo faintly when touched, as if the stone remembered the wound. The people would learn that new scars are not small things.

If someone killed more than could be used, the excess would not vanish in miraculous fire. It would simply remain, heavy and accusing, until scavengers and fungal felt reduced it. The hunter would have to sit with the consequence.

The power would not be a tyrant. It would be a mirror.

---

### If there is one being chosen

If the phrase “the being who lives in it” means a single creature—one person among the many—then I think the power would choose the one who has learned to be necessary without being central.

Not a ruler.

A listener.

This being would not receive strength to dominate. They would receive sensitivity to carry.

Their hands would become stiller.

Their eyes would learn to hold glare without flinching.

They would feel water moving in stone the way another person feels blood in a limb.

They would hear taps from far away and know whether they warn, invite, or mourn.

They would be able to calm crustaceans with a damp line, settle fish by stillness, guide scavengers away from pools without fear.

But the power would not burden them alone.

Wisdom knows that one bearer becomes a martyr. So if it gave such a being a task, it would also give them companions: a mirror-keeper to interpret light, a water-tender to interpret damp, an elder to carry memory, a child to carry surprise. The chosen one would be a knot in a net, not a crown on a head.

Their gift would not make them happy in the shallow sense. It might make them lonelier at times. They would feel the basin’s droughts as thirst and its floods as pressure in the chest. They would know when a district is drying before anyone says so. They would sense when a pool is failing, when a pen is placed wrong, when a light-path is about to drift.

But the power would also give them consolation.

It would let them find beauty often.

A beam lying on wet stone like a pale road.

A school of fish turning all at once without fear.

A child making the sign for water with perfect seriousness.

An old scavenger returning to its accustomed alcove.

Mist falling so thinly it seemed not weather but blessing.

The chosen being would not be asked to save the basin. The basin does not need saving from itself. They would be asked to help the basin continue.

And that would be enough.

---

### If the being is the basin itself

Perhaps the being is not a person at all.

Perhaps the being is the living pattern: the people and the fauna and the microbial skins and the water routes and the mirror paths and the hushed city sliding with change.

If so, the power would do something even stranger.

It would grant the basin a slow self-awareness.

Not consciousness like a human mind. Not speech. Not desire for worship.

Something more like the awareness of an ancient body that knows its own breath.

The basin would become a little more coherent. Its changes would remain wild, but not mindless. Its turbulence would remain real, but not cruel beyond repair. Its memory would deepen. Water would remember old paths more clearly. Stone would remember where pressure had settled too long. Mirrors would remember the angles that served life best.

The people would feel this as luck, or grace, or the kindness of the place.

They would not be wrong.

---

### It would guard against outside harm

The world above is dry, and dry worlds are often hungry.

If the basin drew the attention of a wise and benevolent power, that power would know that the greatest danger may not come from within. It may come from those who discover the entrance and see only resources: stone to be quarried, water to be claimed, animals to be killed, people to be ruled or displaced.

The power would not make the basin easy to find.

For the greedy, it would become confusing.

Shafts of light would glare and blind.

Paths would seem to loop back.

Water would taste chalky and disappointing.

Mirrors would flash in ways that made intruders uneasy.

Their tools would feel heavy. Their fires would burn poorly. Their voices would sound too loud even to themselves. They would feel watched, not by eyes, but by stone.

Some would leave.

For the violent, the basin would close gently but firmly.

Mist would thicken until direction failed.

Wet floors would become difficult to cross.

A flood might rise just enough to block a route.

A scavenger might appear at the edge of sight and unsettle them.

Nothing would need to kill them. The basin would simply refuse to be taken.

But for those who came with humility—who entered quietly, who touched the stone without breaking it, who saw the light and did not try to own it—the power might guide them.

A beam would appear where it had not been.

A line of crustaceans would move along a safe seam.

A child might be seen at a distance, then vanish around a corner, leading them not into danger but into the customs of approach.

Such visitors would not be welcomed casually. They would be tested by silence. They would be watched for signs that they could move without claiming. If they passed, they might be given water, perhaps a meal of dried fish, perhaps a place to rest on a low platform. But they would not be allowed to forget that they were guests in a place older than their desires.

---

### It would add beauty, but not noise

A wise power would know that even a severe world needs beauty beyond utility.

It would not make the basin ornate. It would make it occasionally astonishing.

When mist gathered at just the right thickness, dust in the light shafts might align for a moment into pale, drifting veils that looked like slow rivers of flour. The people would pause and watch. No one would need to explain why it mattered.

When a cistern filled after a long absence, the water might become so still that it held the reflections of ceiling ribs perfectly, making the pool seem like an opening into another inverted basin. Children would lean over it and sign to their reflections.

In certain damp alcoves, fungal felt might grow in faint concentric rings that glimmered when touched by reflected light. Not because it was useful, but because the place had earned adornment.

Once in a very long while, a thin rain might fall from the ceiling in drops so evenly spaced that the taps on shell and stone seemed almost musical. Still quiet. Still restrained. But the city would hear it and know that something had leaned close.

The power would not give them instruments of triumph. It would give them moments of wonder that required attention to receive.

---

### It would honor death

The basin already returns its dead to stone, water, scavengers, and fungal felts. A wise power would not disturb this.

But it might deepen the dignity of it.

When a person died, their name-sign might appear once in the behavior of the city: a tap-rhythm repeated by falling water, a hand-shape made by mist against a mirror panel, a row of shells arranged by someone who did not know why they arranged them.

This would not be resurrection.

It would be remembrance woven into the basin’s ordinary life.

The dead would not be trapped. They would be allowed to go where they needed to go. But they would not vanish as if they had never mattered. Their habits would remain in the paths they walked, their repairs in the mirrors they tended, their warnings in the channels they helped move.

The power might make sure that no one was lost without being found, if that could be done without breaking the basin’s laws. If a person wandered into a mist-filled hollow and died, their bones might be discovered by a scavenger trail that led searchers near. Their passing would be known. Their return would be witnessed.

Even death would be a form of maintenance.

The power would honor that.

---

### The blessing it would speak

If it spoke at all, it would not use many words.

Perhaps this is the blessing it would leave, written not in fire or thunder but in the grammar of the basin:

*I see you.*

*I will not make you green.*

*I will not make you loud.*

*I will not make you many beyond your hollows.*

*I will not give you what would make you forget one another.*

*I will keep your light shared.*

*I will keep your water honored.*

*I will keep your stone from being broken by greed.*

*I will give you enough to continue,*

*and not so much that you cease to attend.*

*When drought comes, I will be the memory of water.*

*When flood comes, I will be the path out of the way.*

*When your hands are wise, I will not interrupt them.*

*When your hands fail, I will make the failure endurable.*

*Continue.*

*Continue without becoming cruel.*

*Continue without becoming careless.*

*Continue as you are: pale, patient, distributed, and alive.*

---

### What would change

Afterward, the basin might look almost the same.

The mirror-keepers would still wipe dust from pale panels. The water-tenders would still watch for darkening stone. The children would still learn signs before labor. The crustaceans would still rasp over wet rock. The fish would still turn in silence. Mist would gather and fall as thin rain. The city would still slide with its own turbulence.

But there would be a difference.

A platform would move a little sooner before a seep opened beneath it.

A child would pause before stepping onto a floor that looked dry but was not.

An elder would wake from a dream and say, “There,” and point to where water would return in three days.

A blocked light-path would be corrected before a district dimmed too long.

A sick fungal mat would be found early.

A stranger with harmful intent would lose the way and leave.

A dying person would be found in time to be returned properly.

A pool that should have failed would hold just long enough.

The people might not name the power.

They might not build a shrine. They might not kneel. Kneeling would be awkward in a city where everyone is always kneeling to tend stone, and anyway the power would not want it. If they acknowledged it, they would do so in their own way: a shell placed at the edge of a cistern, a tap repeated three times against bone, a new hand-sign used by children who no one can remember taught them.

The sign might mean something like:

*water given freely*

or

*light kept open*

or simply

*continue*

---

### The deepest thing they would do

The deepest thing a benevolent and wise power would do for such a place is not to rescue it from being itself.

It is to protect its capacity to be itself.

It would make the basin a sanctuary—not from change, but from meaninglessness. Not from scarcity, but from despair. Not from death, but from waste.

It would not make the people into pets of providence. It would make them partners in continuation.

It would not answer every prayer, because some prayers, if answered, would destroy the one who prayed. It would answer the truest prayer: the prayer of a world asking to remain coherent.

And if there was one being at the heart of it—one pale person, one distributed life, one ancient bright underworld breathing through water and stone—the power would lean toward it the way light leans across a mirror panel: without weight, without noise, without taking anything, only making the existing brightness a little more reliable.

Then it would withdraw into the ordinary.

Not gone.

Just quiet.

Waiting to be recognized in the next drop, the next shifted beam, the next child lifting a hand to say, softly, without sound:

*Water.*

*Wait.*

*Listen.*
