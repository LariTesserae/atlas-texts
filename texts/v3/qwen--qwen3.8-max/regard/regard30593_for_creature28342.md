If a benevolent and wise power noticed this place, I think they would begin by refusing the arrogance of rescue.

They would not arrive as a sun. They would not tear open the water and say, *Let there be sky.* They would not bring metal, fire, language shaped like command, or the sharp geometry of tools. To do that would not be kindness; it would be conquest dressed as mercy. A wise power would understand, immediately, that the drowned city is not a failed surface world. It is not a womb waiting to become a road. It is not a dark nursery needing to be corrected into daylight. It is already whole in its own heavy, breathing way.

So the first thing they would do is listen.

They would enter the basin not as a ruler descending into a conquered province, but as a pressure becoming aware of other pressures. They would let their attention become thin enough to fit between grains of silt, patient enough to follow the slow mineral accretion of a wall, subtle enough to be mistaken for nothing more than another warm pulse rising from below. They would learn the city by its own grammar: current, scent, heat, touch, chemical trace, the flicker of living light, the pause before movement, the closing of a shell, the contraction of an unseen mouth.

And because the being who lives there is not one but many, the power would not look for a king. They would not search for a single face to address, a single throat to bless, a single will to elevate. They would recognize that the inhabitant is a plural being: the colonial walls, the heat-clans, the ribbon readers, the crabs, the filterers, the hunters, the burrowers, the cleaners, the drifting larvae, the bacterial lawns, the ancient fused towers, the living tissue, the mineral memory. The city itself would be the oldest person there, but not the only one.

A wise benevolence would not bless one part by starving the rest.

So they would ask, in the only way such a power can ask without violence: *What does this place need that it does not already have?*

And the basin would answer, not in words, but in patterns.

A vent that has grown too hot.  
A courtyard where water has gone still and sour.  
A nursery ledge where too many larvae are swept into hungry channels.  
A wall growing blindly across a passage it needs to keep open.  
A predator striking out of confusion rather than need.  
A district pressing too close to collapse.  
A warmth failing somewhere far below.  
A silence that has become not caution but loneliness.

The power would see these things. And then they would choose.

Not to remake the city.

To deepen it.

---

The first thing they would do is stabilize what is fragile without making it brittle.

They would not abolish the basin’s dangers. Dangers are part of its shape. But they would reduce the catastrophes that serve no purpose: the random toxic burst, the sudden anoxic settling that suffocates a nursery without warning, the blind collapse of a tower into a crowded shelf, the mineral surge that seals too many breathing passages at once. They would not prevent every death, but they would prevent meaningless annihilation.

They would steady the deep seeps.

Where heat is wealth, they would make the wealth less capricious. They would not turn every vent into abundance, because too much heat cooks what it feeds. But they would keep the hottest flows from becoming poison. They would encourage secondary springs so that if one warm current fails, another can open elsewhere. They would guide mineral deposition so that new growth strengthens old foundations instead of smothering them. They would help the city’s living architecture repair itself with slightly more intelligence: a crack noticed earlier, a weak arch thickened before it fails, a blocked channel reopened by a slow change in tissue pressure.

To an outside observer, nothing miraculous would appear.

The city would simply seem unusually resilient.

A district that should have collapsed would hold.  
A stagnant pool would receive a timely breath of cooler water.  
A dying vent would be replaced by another, smaller, more stable one.  
A generation of larvae that would otherwise have been lost would drift into sheltered crevices.

The power would act like a hand placed gently beneath a sleeping body: not waking it, not moving it, only making sure it does not fall.

---

The second thing they would do is increase recognition.

Not intelligence as a surface creature understands it. Not speech. Not philosophy. Not the burden of naming everything. But a finer, quieter capacity for the city to know itself and its inhabitants.

They would give the water a subtle additional sense: the sense of being witnessed.

Not the feeling of being watched by a predator. Not the alarm of being hunted. Something older and less sharp. A pressure that says: *You are here. You are part of this. You are not merely passing through unnoticed.*

The heat-clans would feel it first near the vents, where their crowded bodies shimmer in faint amber points. They would not become wiser in a human way, but their communal life would become more coherent. They would sense more clearly when a shelf is overcrowded, when a bacterial mat is overgrazed, when a warm pulse is about to strengthen, when a stranger is dangerous and when it is merely lost. Their territoriality would remain, but it would become less blind. They would defend their heat, but with fewer frantic skirmishes. They would brood their young with slightly more success, because the water around them would carry fewer false alarms.

The ribbon readers would feel it as memory becoming richer.

Their long bodies already carry the basin in scent and pressure, but the power’s attention would deepen that carrying. They would remember not only routes, but meanings: this current once brought death, this wall once opened, this district healed, this warmth belongs to a place where many lives began. They would become even more the city’s wandering historians, and perhaps, in some slow and wordless way, its scouts. When danger approached from far away—cold poison, shifting stone, a failure in the deep circulation—they would move differently, and other creatures would read that difference without knowing why.

The crabs would feel it as patience.

Not more patience than they already possess, but patience made fruitful. Their scraping, clearing, and tending would matter more. They would find the right surfaces more often. They would be less likely to waste effort on dead stone when living film needed them elsewhere. Their little carried ecosystems—the sponges, worms, and bacterial gardens on their shells—would flourish with less cost to the crab. In some small way, they would become better gardeners without ever becoming gardeners in the moral sense. They would still eat. They would still fight. They would still be crabs. But their work would fit more cleanly into the life of the whole.

Even the predators would feel it.

This is where a foolish benevolence would hesitate. A kind but unwise power might try to end predation, to make the hunters gentle, to turn the basin into a sanctuary of harmless drifting. That would be a disaster. Predation is not an aberration here. It is one of the city’s oldest forms of relation. Death is how matter moves. Hunger is how energy circulates. The predator is not a criminal; it is a participant.

So the wise power would not abolish hunger. They would refine it.

They would make the predator’s need clearer to itself. Not moral, not merciful in a human sense, but less wasteful. A hunter would be less likely to strike when it had already fed. It would be more likely to take what is necessary and leave the rest. It would still kill, but less often out of confusion, panic, or reflex. Prey would not be made helpless, but more forewarned. Signals would travel cleaner. Disturbance would carry farther. Hiding places would reveal themselves at the right moments. Terror would remain, but terror would be shorter, sharper, more useful.

The power would not make death disappear. They would make death less likely to be pointless.

---

The third thing they would do is help the city become more fully a being without becoming a tyrant.

The oldest inhabitant is the city itself, but as long as it remains merely a vast living structure, its care is blind. It grows. It closes. It opens. It feeds. It repairs. It does not choose in the way a centered mind chooses. A wise power would see the possibility latent in that distributed life: not a single controlling consciousness, but a deeper coherence. A capacity for self-maintenance that does not crush the small lives inside it.

They would not put a soul into the city. They would help the city notice its own soul.

Slowly, over generations or centuries, the living walls would become more responsive. A district that grew too dense would trigger subtle changes in current. A passage needed by many small creatures would resist being sealed by opportunistic tissue. A nursery basin would draw warmth and calm when the young were vulnerable. A dying tower would be guided to collapse in a way that created shelter rather than destruction. The city would still grow according to appetite, but its appetite would become more informed.

It would become, in a sense, a guardian of its own tenants.

Not a master. Not a landlord. Not a god demanding worship. More like a vast coral-mind learning that its health depends on the health of its innumerable residents.

The power might give it a single recurring sensation, a kind of living question that moves through its tissue and mineral lattice:

*Where is too much? Where is too little? Where is broken? Where can be healed? Where should I open? Where should I close?*

The city would answer in growth.

And that growth would be its prayer.

---

The fourth thing they would do is create one place of truce.

Not everywhere. That would ruin the ecology. But one place.

Deep in the lowest part of the basin, perhaps in an old still-water plaza where sediment has gathered for ages, the power would encourage a current so gentle that it carries no hunting-scent. It would be a small district of pause. Not large enough to starve the predators or shelter all life forever, but enough. A place where the injured can rest without being immediately found. Where a newly settled creature can grow strong enough to face the channels again. Where a predator may lie without striking, and a prey creature may drift without fleeing.

The truce would not be moral. It would be ecological, temporary, bounded. Creatures would enter it and leave it. Hunger would return. The dark would remain. But there would be one place where the city’s pressure says: *Not here. Not yet. Rest.*

Over time, that place might become strange in the way all meaningful places become strange. The walls there might glow faintly even when no creature disturbs them. The sediment might settle into patterns like slow writing. Larger animals might avoid it unless they are wounded. Smaller creatures might gather there not because they are safe forever, but because safety has been offered long enough for them to continue.

The power would not guard it visibly. They would simply make it true.

---

The fifth thing they would do is remember.

This may be the most important act.

In a world without monuments, without writing, without carved names, a wise and benevolent power might choose to become the city’s hidden memory. Not to record every life in a surface sense, but to ensure that no life is entirely erased.

When a heat-clan shrimp dies near the vents, its body is consumed. That is right. That is how the city continues. But perhaps the pattern of its life—the scent of its home shelf, the bacterial film it carried, the chemical trace of its brood—would persist a little longer in the water, a little more clearly than chance alone
