They would not arrive.

That is the first thing a benevolent and wise power would understand.

To arrive too plainly would be to overwrite the High Birthwater before it had become itself. A face in the light, a voice in the pressure, a vast intention leaning too close over the hammered ceiling—these would not be gifts. They would be weights. They would make the young water into an audience, the motes into signs, the transparent drifters into subjects of a spectacle they had not yet developed the organs to witness.

So if such a power noticed this place, it would notice gently.

It would not break the surface. It would not send fire down through the blue. It would not carve a message into the cooling crust, because the crust is not yet ready to carry a message. It would not make the motes spell out praise. It would not give the sleeve-like creatures eyes just so they could see the source of their preservation. It would not confuse benevolence with display.

What it would do, if anything, would be quieter than miracle.

It would hold the place in attention.

But attention, in a power both wise and kind, is not passive. It is a kind of shaping that does not seize. It is the difference between a hand that pulls a sprout upward and a hand that keeps the wind from breaking it before it has learned to bend.

The power would see the High Birthwater not as empty, and not as unfinished in the sense of lacking, but as beginning. It would see that the ocean is not a failed world. It is a world before the world has decided what it will refuse. The motes are not lesser because they have no minds. The clear, contracting bodies are not incomplete because they have no faces. They are first words in a language that has not yet needed sentences.

And the being who lives there—what would the power call it?

Not a creature.

Not a god.

Not a child, exactly, though there is something infantile in its blindness and its slow turning.

Perhaps it would call it a threshold.

The being of the High Birthwater is not one body. It is the water beginning to have a relation to itself. It is the difference between mineral and motion, between light and descent, between the upper brightness and the cool below. It is the first faint habit of persistence: the motes orienting, the clear shapes thickening and thinning, the dead becoming food, the plumes rising, the pressure answering pressure. The being is not a person yet. It is the place where personhood might someday become possible.

A wise power would not rush that.

A benevolent one would not leave it undefended, either.

So I think it would do small things, ordinary things, things that leave no glow behind.

It would steady the shelf.

Not make it unbreakable. Not take away the planet’s heat, or still the young crust into permanent stone. That would be to deny the world its nature. But it would keep the foundations from collapsing too soon, from folding back into themselves before the water has had time to become a water rather than an accident. It would ease the most brutal instabilities—not all of them, but the ones that would erase everything without leaving a trace.

It would keep the light honest.

The white-gold sun would remain the white-gold sun. The shadows would remain honest. The surface would still be hammered by wind and air and the physics of a high, exposed sea. But the power would make sure the light did not become sterilizing, that the upper film of motes was not scoured away by some idle excess of radiation, that the day was long enough for their small turning to matter. It would not make the light kinder by making it soft. It would make it kinder by making it reliable.

It would make poison into passage.

Where mineral breath rose too sharp, it would encourage dilution. Where chemical gradients threatened to knot into dead zones, it would allow the water enough movement to carry them apart. Not by command. Not by visible intervention. Only by a slight favoring of circulation, a little patience in the mixing of layers, a tendency in the young ocean toward becoming breathable to itself.

It would give the first life the grace of not being required to suffer too early.

This is important.

There is no cruelty in the High Birthwater yet, because there is no fear. The motes do not dread the dark. The transparent things do not flee one another. They do not hunt in the sense of pursuit, do not scream in the sense of nerves, do not lose anything that can be called a self. But life will later discover hunger, separation, injury, appetite. A wise and benevolent power would not make that future impossible without destroying what life must become. But it could delay the worst of it. It could refuse to let pain arrive before the beings have the capacity to understand it. It could allow the first world to remain, for a while, a place of descent and uptake rather than wound and pursuit.

It would not introduce predation as entertainment.

It would not make one clear body tear another open just so the scene would become dramatic. It would not mistake conflict for depth. It would let the earliest existence keep its strange neutrality: feeding as sinking, motion as thickening, death as a scattering of usable particles. If violence must one day enter the lineage, it would enter later, when there are enough forms to survive it and enough complexity to make meaning from it. Even then, the power would not bless violence. It would only permit it within limits, and it would make repair as real as damage.

It would give the water a memory of mending.

Not a conscious memory. Not a legend. A physical memory. A tendency. When the clear bodies are torn, they would have some slight capacity to re-gather themselves. When the motes are scattered, they would not be lost utterly; some would sink into conditions where their substance becomes future structure. The ocean would learn, in the blind chemistry of itself, that rupture is not always final. That is perhaps the deepest kindness a power could give to a world not yet born: not happiness, not safety from all harm, but a bias toward continuation.

It would leave room for refusal.

This is the part wisdom would insist upon.

If the power shaped everything too perfectly, the High Birthwater would become a garden with no wildness, a nursery with no door. So the power would introduce, or allow, tiny variations. A mote that turns a little away from the light. A transparent body that thickens not with the current but against it. A mineral plume that rises where no plume was expected. Not chaos for its own sake. Not error glorified. Only the smallest openings through which the world might become something other than what intention alone would choose.

The power would understand that love, if it is real, must eventually be able to be refused.

So it would not create beings merely to be loved by them. It would not make the motes worship the sun. It would not make the clear creatures long for a maker. It would let them be what they are: conditions, motions, beginnings. If they ever become persons—if eyes ever form in those densities, if appetite ever sharpens into choice, if some distant descendant ever looks up through the hammered ceiling and asks whether anything is there—the power would want that question to be asked freely.

It would wait to be found, not to be imposed.

And if the being of the High Birthwater is already, in some vast slow way, a single being, the power would treat it with the same patience.

It would not say: *Become.*

It would say, in the only language such a place can receive: *You may.*

That blessing would not sound like words. It would feel like the absence of a threat. Like a ceiling that does not lower. Like a depth that does not close. Like the water being permitted to remain high, cold, bright, and unmade until it is ready to decide what made means.

Perhaps the power would do one more thing.

Perhaps it would prepare the possibility of a shore.

Not now. Not visibly. Not by lifting rock or draining water or carving a coast into the young crust with a deliberate hand. The High Birthwater is high and shoreless because that is what it is. To give it shores too soon would be to give it edges it has not earned. But the power could leave within the geology a latent gentleness: a way for the crust, over unimaginable time, to thicken, subside, fold, and offer margins. Not a command. A chance. Someday, if the world continues, there may be a place where the water becomes shallow enough for light to meet bottom and stay there. Someday there may be stone that does not shift. Someday there may be a line between sea and air where things can gather, hesitate, and change.

The power would not force that future.

It would only not forbid it.

And if you were submerged there, under that bright hammered ceiling, among the motes and the faceless drifting bodies, you might not feel the presence of anything at all. You would not see a guardian. You would not hear a promise. The normality of the light would remain intact. The blue distance would remain blue. The shadows would remain honest.

But you might feel, if you were able to feel anything so subtle, that the emptiness was not hostile.

You might feel that the water was being kept.

Not owned. Not watched over like property. Kept as a candle is kept in wind. Kept as a question is kept before it is answered. Kept because it is small, and early, and not yet able to keep itself.

That is what I think a benevolent and wise power would do.

They would not make the High Birthwater important by making it famous. They would not sanctify it with signs. They would not send a prophet to the motes or a commandment to the clear, contracting bodies. They would not even reveal themselves to the ocean, because the ocean is not yet ready to be a witness to anything beyond itself.

They would give it time.

They would give it space.

They would give it the first and last gift that any truly wise kindness can give to a beginning thing:

They would let it become without forcing it to become what they prefer.

And if, after ages, something in that water opened an eye for the first time—if some descendant of the faceless drifters turned toward the hammered ceiling and felt, faintly, that the light was not accidental—the power would not thunder, *I made you.*

It would be more likely to say:

*You are not an accident. But you are not mine. You are your own.*

That would be enough.

That would be the blessing.
