## What the Wise Power Would Do

If the drowned grove-city of High Mere and the being who lives in it had drawn the attention of a benevolent and wise power, the first thing that power would do is not act.

It would pause.

Not from indifference. Not from coldness. But because a place like High Mere teaches the nature of care by its very existence. It has not been made by conquest, nor by haste, nor by the desire of one will to overpower another. It has been made by pruning, waiting, listening, binding, soaking, yielding, and returning. A wise power would understand at once that such a place cannot be helped in the same way one helps a drowning animal or a burning city. If it is to do good here, it must do good in the grammar of the place itself.

So the power would begin with attention.

It would look down — if “down” is even the right word for a power that has no body — and it would see High Mere not as a collection of pools, roots, terraces, and breathing folk, but as one slow, many-bodied being. The water would seem to it like blood. The roots like nerves. The fish like moving thoughts. The people like the place’s waking mind. The air pockets like small, precious lungs. The sound like memory passing through the basin. The silence between sounds like the being’s dignity.

And the power would think, if powers think in such a way:

*This place does not need to be ruled. It needs to be allowed.*

That would be the first and deepest judgment.

### The Being It Would See

If the power asked, “Who lives here?” it would not receive a simple answer.

There are the Understory Folk, yes: the few households, the swimmers, the tenders of fish and reed and breath. There are the eels in the root seams, the herons on the platforms, the silver fish in the upper light, the crustaceans cleaning the old leaf beds, the larvae carrying their cases like wandering seeds. There are the plants, which are walls, floors, ceilings, tools, ancestors, and architecture all at once. There are the dead compressed into the floor, not gone but become foundation.

A wise power would not separate these too sharply.

It would see that the true being of High Mere is not one person, nor one family, nor even one species. It is the wet commonwealth itself: the whole living arrangement of water and restraint. It is a being made of intervals. It exists because the channels are open, because the people do not crowd, because the fish have room to spawn, because the roots are not overcut, because sound is allowed to travel, because empty water is treated as sacred in the practical sense.

If the power had to choose a single point of focus, it might rest for a while on an old Air-Keeper, because she is the one who tends breath. Or on a child learning to dive without panic. Or on a fish-tender listening for the drumming below the central pool. But it would not mistake any of them for the whole.

The being it would care for is the life of the place.

And because it is wise, it would know that caring for such a being is not the same as seizing it.

### They Would Not Descend in Glory

A lesser power might arrive with spectacle.

It might open the sky. It might speak in a voice like falling water. It might send light burning through the pool until every shadow fled. It might make itself visible in the shape of a giant face above the basin, or a crowned serpent coiled around the root walls, or a shining figure standing on the surface, demanding worship.

A benevolent and wise power would do none of these things.

It would know that revelation can be a kind of violence.

To appear too brightly in High Mere would be like dropping a stone into the central water: the ripples would not stop for a long time. The fish would scatter. The children would cry. The elders would freeze. The sound of the city would break into alarm. The inhabitants would fall to their knees not from love but from shock. They would begin to build temples where none are needed. They would begin to ask the power for favors instead of listening to the water. They would lose the very thing that made them worth noticing: their patience, their restraint, their ability to live within limits without resentment.

So the power would keep itself small.

It would become, if anything, a quality of attention in the basin.

A hush that was not empty.

A kindness in the current.

A sense, now and then, of being held.

It would not want to be named too quickly. It would not want statues. It would not want a priesthood. It would not want the Understory Folk to believe that every good thing came from above. It would want them to remain true to their own hands, their own lungs, their own woven gates and pruned roots.

If the power acted, it would act in such a way that the people could still say, “We did this,” or “The water provided,” or “The elders were wise.”

Because wisdom does not need credit.

---

### They Would Listen First

Before doing anything, the power would listen.

It would listen to the rain striking the surface. It would learn the difference between the soft drumming of ordinary weather and the hard, dark sound of a storm closing the hollow. It would listen to the low organ tones made by water moving through hollow stems, and it would learn which passages were healthy and which were beginning to choke with silt.

It would listen to the knocked rhythms on root columns: the signals of passage, warning, greeting, grief, work, permission. It would learn the etiquette of distance. It would understand that to approach without signal is rudeness, and that silence can be a form of respect.

It would listen to the fish. It would learn the spawning songs, the croaks and drums in the deeper chambers, the sudden flashes of alarm when a shadow passed too quickly. It would learn which fish were old, which were young, which runs had failed in past years, which root gardens were favored, which eels had grown fat and slow in certain seams.

It would listen to the people.

Not only their words, but their hesitations. The way a mother paused before letting her child dive alone. The way an elder touched a weakened wall and did not yet say what he feared. The way a young swimmer, lonely in the green distance, would sometimes rise to an air pocket just to hear their own breath. The way lovers sent small sounds across open water, pretending to ask about nets or weather. The way the Air-Keepers tasted stale air and worried. The way the weavers spoke to stems as if the stems might understand.

The power would learn all this.

And only then would it decide how to help.

---

### They Would Strengthen What Already Worked

A wise power does not begin by adding wonders. It begins by supporting the ordinary.

So the first gifts would be subtle.

The roots would hold a little better.

The peat terraces would resist collapse.

The hidden drains below the basin would stay open longer.

The rain would come often enough, but not always in cruelty.

The water would remain clear enough for the Understory Folk to read movement and danger.

The oxygen-giving plants would thrive in the chambers where breath was kept.

The fish would find their spawning roots.

The reeds would regrow after harvest.

The woven gates would loosen in storms, but not always fail.

The coolness of the pool would remain, because the coolness was part of its character, but it would not become a killer. The cold would teach care without demanding death.

None of this would look like miracle.
