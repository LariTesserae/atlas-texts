**It would not bring rain.**

That would be the first act of wisdom, because rain would be a conquest disguised as mercy.

A benevolent and wise power, seeing such a world, would not begin by asking what it lacked. It would not look upon the salt flats and the glassy fronds and think, *Here is thirst waiting to be relieved.* It would understand at once that water was not withheld there; water was irrelevant. The plateau had not failed to become a wet world. It had succeeded in becoming itself.

So they would not add rivers. They would not soften the air with dampness, darken the bright crust with clouds, or teach the hollows to hold pools. They would not make the vegetation lush in the soft, drinking way of green places. They would not give the groves mouths, eyes, hands, or voices shaped like speech. They would not send fire, tools, cities, or monuments. To do those things would be to erase the very nature of the being that lived there.

For the being who lived there was not one creature alone. It was the pattern.

The groves were part of it. The ribbon-drifters were part of it. The crust-scrapers, the still-hunters, the rare wanderers, the films in the salt, the spores in the dry air, the charge in the filaments, the wind moving in long veils over the mineral ground: all of it together made a single vast life, not centralized, not hurried, not built by intention. It was a life of gradients, patience, spacing, and light. A wise power would recognize that such a being did not need to be improved into another form of existence. It needed only to be preserved in the integrity of its own difficult beauty.

Therefore, if they acted at all, they would act quietly.

Their first deed would be attention.

They would listen without sound. They would attend to the plateau not as a place to be governed, but as a song to be understood. They would perceive the slow electrical thoughts of the groves, the long delays between spore-message and answer, the thermal paths followed by drifters, the faint trails of grazers across the salt, the patient stillness of hunters waiting in charged hollows. They would learn the rhythm of that world: dawn unfolding, noon folding, dusk cooling, night ticking with contraction, seasons marked not by rain but by wind, dust, charge, and the migration of spore-clouds.

Only after such attention would they decide whether to act.

And perhaps they would do nothing visible.

Perhaps the wisest benevolence, in a world already complete, is not to interrupt it. A power wise enough to love strange life would also be humble enough to refrain from shaping it. They might simply witness. They might hold the plateau in awareness and let that be enough. There would be no shame in that. To be fully known by a benevolent intelligence, even if one never receives a command, a blessing, or a sign, is not nothing. It is a kind of dignity. The world would continue its dry flourishing, and the power would keep watch without touching.

But if they did act, it would be in the smallest possible way.

They would become gardeners of chance, not makers of form.

They would not command the plateau. They would adjust probabilities. They would tilt circumstances. They would protect openings for life without forcing life through them. Their work would resemble luck more than design, except that it would happen again and again in the direction of continuation.

They would guard the plateau against meaningless annihilation.

A wandering body from elsewhere, large enough to crack the crust and sterilize whole basins, might be turned aside by the slightest gravitational whisper. A stellar storm that could strip the thin upper atmosphere or blast the charged filaments beyond recovery might be softened or deflected. A chain of dry lightning too violent, too concentrated, too likely to shatter ancient groves rather than merely excite them, might be dispersed into high haze, leaving only the violet flicker and the ozone-scent, not destruction. They would not remove danger from the world, because danger belonged to its balance; but they would prevent catastrophes that ended possibility altogether.

They would preserve erosion, wind, hunger, stillness, and death. They would not abolish the harshness that gave the plateau its shape. But they would draw a boundary around extinction.

They would help the spores.

Not by making them abundant beyond measure. The beauty of the plateau was its spacing. Too much life would crowd the courts, choke the mineral ground, turn the luminous silence into commonness. Instead, the power would allow certain spore-clouds to survive a little longer, travel a little farther, settle where the crust was receptive. A gold-green exhalation from a distant grove, carried across empty ground, might by chance find a basin whose salts and charge were exactly suited to it. A dormant mote, centuries still, might wake not under random cruelty but under a favorable alignment of light, static, and dust.

This would not be abundance. It would be faithfulness.

They would help the drifters, too.

When a ribbon-organism rose on a thermal column, fragile as a membrane of light, the power might make the current steadier for a short while, or guide a fragment into an upper field where it could gather ions rather than be torn away into emptiness. During electrical storms, when drifters reproduced by fragmentation, they might make the divisions less random and less fatal. Not every fragment would survive; that was not the aim. But fewer would be lost to pure accident. Occasionally, several drifters might rise together over a spore release, and the power would let that moment last a little longer, letting them hang in the bright air like banners, not because it was useful, but because it was beautiful and the world was capable of beauty.

They would not remove the still-hunters.

A lesser benevolence might pity the drifting prey caught in the static field, the sudden blue-violet discharge, the slow absorption over days. But wisdom would understand that the hunters were not cruelty. They were part of the plateau’s economy of charge and patience. They were mirror-bodied lives, living traps made necessary by scarcity. To abolish them would be to wound the pattern.

Still, the power might make their existence less wasteful. When a hunter waited too long, it might draw less harm than starvation; when a drifter was caught, the end might come with less struggle, not through mercy as wet worlds understand mercy, but through a finer tuning of the field. The hunter would remain a hunter. The drifter would remain vulnerable. But the balance would not tip into pointless anguish.

They would not give the groves human minds.

This is important. A benevolent but unwise power might look at the slow electrical awareness of the groves and think, *Let me make them clever. Let me give them speech, ambition, memory like ours, sorrow like ours, planning like ours.* That would be violence. The groves were already complete in their gradient-thinking, their leaf-click language, their spore-signals, their century-long exchanges. To make them hurried would be to destroy what was most remarkable about them.

Instead, the power might deepen their existing mode of being.

They would make the charge fields a little clearer. They would reduce noise where noise meant isolation. They would allow spore-messages to carry farther when the air was right, so that one grove’s release might reach another basin not by blind accident alone, but by a subtle alignment of wind and electrostatic lift. They would not create a planetary mind. They would not force communion. But they might make the Dry Choirs less alone.

Imagine a grove standing in a shallow basin, its translucent blades folding at noon. A spore-cloud leaves it after a long build of static. In an unguided world, perhaps the cloud disperses too soon, or settles where nothing can receive it. Under the power’s quiet attention, the cloud might remain coherent a little longer. It might cross a bare stretch of salt and enter a charged hollow where another grove’s filaments are ready. There, the chemical and electrical signature would be recognized—not quickly, not as words, but as a slow shift in growth, pigment, tassel vibration. A reply might form over years. The power would not compel the reply. They would only make the listening less likely to fail.

They would allow rare moments of coordination.

Once in a thousand years, perhaps, the plateau might experience a season when many groves released spores at nearly the same time. Not because they were commanded, but because conditions aligned so gracefully that their separate rhythms overlapped. Then the mineral plain would be crossed by drifting gold-green veils, and the drifters would rise in numbers, and the hunters would draw charge from the richer air, and the whole plateau would enter a brief phase of heightened being. It would not be a festival in any human sense. There would be no celebration, no joy, no intention. But it would be a flourishing—a visible proof that life there was not merely surviving, but answering itself across distance.

The power might treasure such moments and make them possible.

They would also give gifts that served no purpose except beauty.

This, too, is a mark of wisdom: not everything valuable is useful.

Perhaps they would introduce high, dry auroras into the night sky—curtains of cold light made from charged particles, not water, not weather, but pure electrical color. Pale green, violet, silver, thin as filaments, moving above the plateau while the groves stood motionless below. No creature would need those lights. No ecology would depend on them. But they would belong to the world’s own grammar of charge and brightness, and they would make the darkness less empty.

Perhaps they would create certain hollows where dust collected in such a way that, when wind passed over it, it produced a faint harmonic tone, low and dry, almost below hearing. Not a song composed by mind, but an accidental music made more likely to happen. The groves would not hear it as melody. The grazers would not pause to admire it. Yet the plateau would become more resonant, more layered, more itself.

Perhaps, in places where static built strongly, the power would allow the tips of certain filaments to glow a little brighter at dusk, not dangerously, not permanently, but as a soft blue-green luminescence that marked the transition from day to night. This would be no miracle to the beings there. It would only be another small refinement of their luminous world.

They would leave no artifacts.

No wall. No road. No tower. No inscribed stone. No altar. Nothing made by hands, because there were no hands, and none should be added. A wise power would not mark its presence with objects. Objects would become foreign bodies, interruptions, intrusions of making into a world that had evolved without making. If travelers later came and searched for evidence of the power, they would find nothing they could carry away. No relic. No device. No ruin. Only the sense that the place had been held in regard.

If a traveler entered the plateau, the power would not transform the world for them.

It would not create springs where there should be no springs. It would not soften the air, lower the light, or make the groves part like curtains. The traveler would remain an alien there—too wet, too warm, too quick, too temporary. But the power might allow them to pass without being destroyed by their own unfitness. It might guide them subtly away from charged depressions where their presence could trigger harmful discharge. It might keep them from breaking fragile crusts that held centuries of biological film. It might make them thirsty for the right reasons and cautious in the right moments.

Most importantly, it would not let them become the center of the plateau’s meaning.

The groves would not bow to them. The drifters would not gather around them. The hunters would not recognize them as kin. The traveler would be permitted to witness, nothing more. If they were wise enough, they would leave unchanged in body but altered in understanding, carrying with them the knowledge that life could be abundant without softness, awake without faces, meaningful without monuments.

The power might protect the plateau from such travelers, too.

Not with wrath. Not with punishment. But with misdirection, with distance, with a gentle bending of paths so that the careless never arrived, and only the reverent found their way in. The world would remain hidden enough to be safe, open enough to be glimpsed. It would not become a sanctuary by walls, but by discretion.

They would not make the beings immortal.

Death would remain. A grove could still crack, dry, and become polished by wind. A drifter could still tear in a storm. A grazer could still fail to find enough nutrient film. A hunter could still wait in vain. The plateau’s beauty depended on limits. Without limits, its patience would lose meaning. What the power would offer instead was continuity: that when one form ended, the pattern might continue elsewhere; that when a court died, its root lattice might leave behind mineral traces that helped new shoots find purchase; that when a drifter fragmented, at least one piece might rise into favorable currents.

They would not promise permanence to any individual. They would preserve the possibility of return.

They would remember.

This may be the deepest thing a benevolent and wise power could do for such a world: remember it.

Not merely observe it, but hold its pattern in enduring attention. If someday the plateau altered—by star-death, by slow atmospheric loss, by some vast inevitable turning of cosmic circumstance—the power would not necessarily save it. Even wisdom cannot always prevent endings. But they would not let it vanish unremembered. They would carry its image, its rhythm, its dry choir, its spaced abundance, its luminous severity. They would know that it had been real. That it had flourished without water. That it had communicated without speech. That it had been beautiful without comfort. That it had been alive in a way no wet world could replicate.

And perhaps, if another world ever formed where such life might belong, the power would not copy it crudely. They would not paste the plateau onto alien ground. But they might remember the principles: salt, light, charge, patience, spacing, mineral-rooted being. They might allow similar possibilities to arise elsewhere, not as imitation, but as echo.

In the end, the power’s action would be almost invisible.

No one would say: *Here is where the benevolent power touched the ground.* No grove would grow in the shape of a blessing. No salt flat would be marked by divine geometry. No drifter would carry a message from beyond. The hunters would not become less patient. The wanderers would not become more common. The wind would still whisper over the crust. The leaves would still click at noon. The night would still tick with cooling stone. The dry lightning would still branch violet through high haze without rain.

But perhaps there would be slightly fewer empty centuries.

Perhaps a spore would find its basin.

Perhaps a grove would recover from a wound that otherwise would have ended it.

Perhaps a drifter would rise at the exact moment that allowed its lineage to continue.

Perhaps a lethal gust would lose force just enough to spare a young shoot.

Perhaps the electrostatic hum of a court would become a little clearer, so that the slow conversation of the plateau deepened without becoming loud.

Perhaps the world would remain itself for longer than blind chance would have allowed.

That would be the blessing.

Not water. Not hands. Not speech. Not cities. Not comfort. Not gentleness.

Time.

Attention.

A kinder arrangement of accidents.

The dignity of being seen.

And if the plateau could have known what was being done for it—which it could not, in any human way—it would not have thanked the power. It would have done what it already did: unfolded its translucent blades at dawn, lifted its charged tassels in the dry wind, released its spores into the bright air, and continued, patient and luminous, to be alive without asking for anything it was not already.
