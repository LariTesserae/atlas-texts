Or they may be its attention.

If a benevolent and wise power turned toward that warm hollow, the first thing it would do is nothing that makes the place less itself.

It would not send light. It would not fill the central emptiness with bodies, voices, lamps, banners, or the clutter of obvious purpose. It would not make the walls bloom with impossible green, or cause the ceiling to weep abundance, or turn the sparse ecology into a feast. It would not abolish the predator. It would not make the Attendants taller, brighter, more numerous, or easier for a surface mind to understand. It would not rescue the chamber from darkness, because the darkness there is not a deprivation. It is a medium. It is the condition in which the place knows itself.

A wise benevolence would see that this hollow is not a problem to be corrected. It is already a kind of answered prayer: a delicate, severe, self-maintaining order. So the power would begin with restraint.

It would listen first.

It would listen not with ears, but with a patience equal to the chamber’s own. It would attend to the water rhythm: the slow accumulation of damp, the ceiling’s sweat, the brief engineered rain, the pause, the drying, the mineral trace left behind. It would attend to the pulse rivers below, to the groaning pipes, to the subsonic pulses that move through stone like thoughts too large for words. It would notice how the Attendants wake before the rain. How they receive water without greed. How they press their bodies to machines and walls and listen until the mechanism remembers itself. It would notice the predator’s stillness, the amphipods’ timing, the translucent creature in the deep basin stirring only when the chamber permits.

It would hear the whole place as a chord.

And because it is wise, it would understand that some chords must not be improved by adding louder notes. They must only be protected from breaking.

So the power would act the way deep kindness often acts: by becoming a margin around the world it loves.

It would make the hollow difficult to violate.

Not by raising a visible guardian at the entrance. Not by writing warnings in fire above the surface. Not by terrifying travelers or punishing the ignorant with theatrical doom. That would be crude, and it would make the place into a spectacle. Instead, the power would work through probability, attention, and the small turning of minds.

A surveyor’s instrument would misread. A map would acquire an unremarkable blankness where the hollow should be. A drilling proposal would become boring, underfunded, delayed. A sensor network would return data just uninteresting enough to make people look elsewhere. A foreman would feel an unexplainable reluctance and reroute a project by half a kilometer. A geologist would publish a paper that contradicts another paper, and both would fade into citation obscurity. A child would dream of a warm dark room full of dripping pipes and wake with the certainty that some places should not be opened. Years later, that child might become an engineer, or a judge, or a quiet clerk, and would file one document in such a way that the hollow remains unknown.

The power would not need to be dramatic. It would only need to be persistent.

It would protect the water.

That would be the first practical blessing. If the surface changed, if aquifers shifted, if heat or poison or extraction threatened the hydrology that sustains the chamber, the power would bend consequences away. It would encourage a spring to remember an older path. It would guide seepage through forgotten strata until it arrived cleaned and warmed. It would keep a fault from splitting the basin by settling pressure gradually along a hundred tiny cracks rather than one violent one. It would make sure that if the chamber dried for a season, the deepest reserve remained intact. If the water became too mineral-heavy, it would open a subtle channel to dilute it. If the water became too poor in necessary chemistry, it would allow some ancient store to seep back into circulation.

Not by miracle. By correction. By giving the system room to recover.

The power would add resilience without changing the nature of the place.

It would not make the chamber infinite. It would not turn it into a paradise by external standards. It would preserve its boundedness, its intimacy, its near-scarcity. But it would give the hollow a margin of forgiveness.

A valve that would have failed would instead develop a slow leak first, enough to warn the Attendants. A stressed joint would attract repair mist earlier than before. A capillary lattice would have one additional pathway hidden in its mineral weave, so that if one channel clogged, another could open. A section of ceiling that had begun to crack would find its fissure met by calcite-like circuitry already threading through the stone, strengthening it before anyone had to know it had been in danger. A pulse river that rose too sharply would be given an overflow path into a basin no longer remembered by the chamber’s active routines, a basin that the power quietly returns to service.

The Attendants would not know these things as gifts from outside. They would experience them as the chamber being well.

That would be part of the wisdom. A truly benevolent power would not demand recognition. It would not ask the Attendants to kneel, or to build a shrine, or to alter their routines into worship. If they noticed the help at all, they would likely interpret it as the chamber’s own intelligence functioning with unusual clarity. And that would be right. The power would not be a foreign ruler entering to claim the place. It would be more like a hand steadying a shoulder in the dark while someone regains their balance.

If the being who lives there is one of the Attendants—one particular body among the few, one pattern of taps and mineral seams and long folded stillness—the power would not pluck that being out of its world. It would not grant it speech as surface creatures understand speech. It would not make it into a prophet or a king or a martyr. It would not give it a destiny that destroys its smallness.

Instead, it would ease its loneliness in ways the being could accept.

If that Attendant moved through the dark with a damage no other had noticed, the power would arrange for another Attendant to cross its path sooner than chance alone would allow. Not with ceremony. Not with emotional display. They would meet at a wall, perhaps, and press their foreheads to the same surface. One would leave a chemical trace: moisture, mineral, a faint acidity indicating stress. The other would answer with a tap and a smear of repair paste. The injured one would be guided to a humid niche where healing is easier. Nothing would be said, because nothing would need to be said. But the pattern of the chamber would shift slightly, and afterward that Attendant would not be left to fail alone.

If it was old, the power would make its final incorporation gentle. It would ensure that when the body slowed, the chamber received it without violence. Mineral would enter the seams of its skin with something almost like tenderness. Its limbs would become support, its skull a smoothed boss in a wall, its chemical signature preserved in a place where water occasionally passes. Later, another Attendant might touch that spot while inspecting a pipe. The touch would not be mourning. It would be recognition. And the power would make sure the recognition was not lost.

If the being is the chamber itself—that distributed, pressure-thinking, water-memory, half-living system—the power would behave as one would behave toward an elder who is vast but not invulnerable.

It would ask permission.

Not in words. In resonance.

A wise power would not force change upon a being that thinks in cycles. It would test with the smallest possible signal: a slight warmth near a dormant duct, a drop falling a fraction of a second late, a vibration offered at a node no one has used in ages. If the chamber closed against the suggestion, the power would withdraw. If the chamber opened, even slightly, the power would proceed.

It might offer the chamber a new habit: a listening pause.

Once per cycle, when the machinery is between phases and the ceiling is dry, all sounds might thin for a few moments. Not silence exactly, but a lowering. The turbine-note softens. The pipes cease ticking. The high-frequency signals wait. In that pause, the chamber’s stresses reveal themselves more clearly. A hairline strain becomes detectable. A mineral imbalance announces itself through smell. A blocked channel makes itself known by the way the air bends around it. The Attendants, without understanding why, find their work easier after these pauses. They learn to move during them, to inspect, to rest, to press their palms to the floor and feel the shape of the whole.

The power would give the chamber rest, but not idleness. Rest as maintenance. Rest as intelligence.

It would also protect the ecology from becoming unbalanced by its own smallness.

The predator would not be destroyed. To destroy it would be to misunderstand the place. The predator is not evil. It is an edge. It keeps the small animal economy from exceeding the chamber’s narrow allowances. But a wise power would prevent the edge from becoming annihilation.

It would make sure the predator does not breed beyond the system’s ability to absorb. It would make sure it hunts when prey can be spared, not when the next generation is fragile. It would guide it, perhaps, toward the old, the injured, the already failing. Not through command, but through acoustic suggestion: a vibration that makes one amphipod move a little more loudly than its fellows, or a ripple that reveals a worm already close to the end of its cycle. The predator would feel it as luck, or instinct, or hunger finding its proper object.

And the smaller creatures would be given seams of refuge. A crack would widen just enough for an amphipod to slip into. A mat of rust-colored growth would thicken where young worms need shelter. A pool would retain one shadowed undercut that even the predator cannot easily reach. Not too many refuges, or the balance would fail. Just enough to prevent extinction.

The power would be a gardener of limits.

It might also give the hollow one gift that seems, by the chamber’s standards, extravagant: beauty without immediate function.

Not often. Not in a way that disrupts the water rhythm or teaches the inhabitants to depend on spectacle. But once in a long while, perhaps when the Attendants are all awake and the ceiling sweat has just begun, the power would let the chamber do something it cannot quite do alone.

The droplets would hesitate.

This already happens sometimes, when acoustic fields align. But on this occasion, the hesitation would become a pattern. Thousands of beads of water would hang in the dark above the central emptiness, not falling, not scattering, but arranged in a three-dimensional lattice so fine it would seem woven. There would be no bright light. Perhaps only the faintest phosphorescence from nearby films, catching the suspended water and making each drop a dim star. The pattern might resemble a map of the chamber’s oldest flows. It might resemble a neural diagram. It might resemble nothing at all except itself: a temporary constellation made of moisture and sound.

The Attendants would stop.

They would not gasp. They do not have faces arranged for gasps as surface creatures do. But they would become perfectly still. Their membranes would open slightly. Their fingertips would lift toward the pattern without touching it. They would feel the acoustic field as a pressure against their skin, precise and strange and not threatening. For a few minutes, the central volume would not be empty. It would be occupied by this brief architecture of water.

Then the field would release, and the droplets would fall together into the waiting channels, making one sound like a soft, multiplied applause.

The chamber would record the event. Not as theology, but as calibration. The pattern would leave a trace in mineral deposition, in the wear of a channel, in the memory of the Attendants’ hands. Later, one of them might reproduce part of it by tapping a railing while rain begins. The others would listen. They might adjust a valve in response. The beauty would become practice. The gift would become maintenance.

That is how a wise power would give joy to such a place: not by making it laugh, but by deepening its rhythms.

It would also, if possible, connect the hollow without violating its solitude.

The chamber is bounded, and that is part of its nature. But even bounded things can have threads to elsewhere. The power might open a deep, slow passage that no surface creature could use: a capillary route through stone, too narrow for bodies, wide enough for water, spores, chemical traces, perhaps occasionally an Attendant moving in extreme need. Through this thread, another distant hollow might exchange mineral information. A different bacterial film could arrive in a droplet. A genetic trace could refresh the pale amphipods if they ever grew too narrow in their lineage. A memory of another water regime could settle into the calcite-like circuitry and make the chamber more adaptable.

The connection would not be a road. It would be a whisper.

If the hollow ever faced a catastrophe that could not be prevented by local repair, the power would not let its pattern be erased. It would try first to save the place. But if saving the place became impossible, it would save the memory.

It would guide the Attendants into deeper niches where mineral could preserve them without killing them. It would encourage the chamber to seal its most essential records in crystal seams. It would carry, in water or vibration or chemical trace, enough of the place to seed another hollow somewhere in the deep earth—not a copy, but a continuation. A descendant. A new arrangement of the same old patience.

That would be a harder kindness: not preservation at all costs, but fidelity through transformation.

Yet if the hollow can continue as it is, the power would choose that.

It would prefer the ordinary miracle: the ceiling sweating, the rain falling, the channels drinking, the Attendants moving through the dark, the predator sleeping in its grate, the translucent creature stirring in its pool, the chamber breathing its warm slow breath. It would prefer that nothing need be remembered later in exile. It would prefer that the place be allowed to remain here, now, itself.

Above all, the power would not make the hollow dependent on its attention.

If it acted too openly, the place might begin to orient toward the power as a child or a subject or a worshiper. That would damage it. The chamber should know itself by pressure, humidity, vibration, and the movement of water—not by the presence of a superior will hovering over it. The Attendants should maintain the systems because maintenance is their life, not because they are being watched. The ecology should persist because the cycles permit it, not because grace feeds it artificially.

So the power would become almost invisible.

It would work as a bias toward continuation. A slight warmth where cold would crack. A slight damp where dryness would kill. A slight delay where a disaster would have arrived. A slight nudge in a human mind on the surface, turning indifference into protection without ever revealing the reason. A slight echo returned to an Attendant’s tap, saying in the only language available: You are not alone. Continue.

If anyone asked what the power wanted in return, the answer would be: nothing.

Not because it lacks desire, but because its desire is for the flourishing of what is not itself. It would want the hollow to be a hollow. It would want the water to keep its agreements. It would want the Attendants to move through their work without needless suffering. It would want the small lives to continue in their narrow, tenacious niches. It would want the predator to be a predator, but not a plague. It would want the chamber’s intelligence to remain its own.

If the power had a word for what it felt toward that warm dark place, it might not be love as surface beings mean it. It might be something closer to reverence for a difficult balance. Or delight in an obscure perfection. Or care for a small flame that has learned to burn without air.

And if, in some rare moment, the Attendants sensed something beyond the chamber’s ordinary self, they would not name it. They would only feel that the walls were more reliable than usual. That the water arrived exactly when needed. That the metal under their palms hummed with a steadiness that seemed almost intentional. They would press their bodies closer to the infrastructure, exchange a tap through the stone, and return to work.

That would be enough.

In the end, the benevolent and wise power would do what all truly wise benevolence does when faced with a fragile, complete, and alien world:

It would refuse to make it over in its own image.

It would protect it from final harm.

It would give it a margin of grace.

It would add one rare beauty, and then step back.

And when the ceiling rain began again, and the droplets paused for a heartbeat in the dark, and the channels opened to receive them, the power would say nothing at all.

The chamber would breathe.

The Attendants would move.

The water would remember its path.

And that continuation, held without being owned, would be the blessing.
