If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention to that buried continent, the first thing it would understand is that the place does not need to be rescued.

It is not a prison without sky.  
It is not a wound without water.  
It is not a poverty because it lacks green things and open distances.

It is a fullness.

A wise power would perceive that immediately. They would not look upon the warm dark and think, *Let there be light,* because light there would not be a gift. It would be an invasion. It would create distance where closeness is the basis of personhood. It would teach bodies to know one another from afar, and in doing so it would weaken the ancient etiquette of touch, pressure, and consent by which the under-realm is kept whole.

They would not open the ceiling.  
They would not pour rain into the hollows.  
They would not soften the edges.  
They would not make the stone forget its fractures.

To a being made for wetness and sky, such things might seem like mercy. To the beings of the under-realm, they would be violence. Water would be forgetting. Light would be separation. Endless abundance would be chaos. A sky would be an emptiness too large to bear.

So the first act of a benevolent and wise power would be refusal.

They would refuse to improve the place according to the values of another world.  
They would refuse to make its people taller, brighter, faster, or more expansive.  
They would refuse to give them desires that their world cannot answer without breaking.  
They would refuse to reveal themselves in grandeur, because grandeur would flatten the small, close ethics by which those beings live.

A lesser benevolence would want to give everything.  
A wise benevolence would ask what can be given without taking away the shape of a life.

And perhaps the answer is: very little, and only with permission.

They would do nothing that is not asked, and even then they would be cautious, because in such a world even a request may carry consequences the asker cannot foresee. If a young one cries out in pressure for warmth, they might add warmth. If an elder’s hand trembles over a fracture and seeks steadiness, they might steady it. If a gathering is threatened by a collapse that should not yet be, they might delay the collapse by the smallest margin needed for bodies to move through the gap. But they would not make a spectacle of it. They would not leave signs demanding gratitude. They would not make the beings dependent on interventions from beyond their understanding.

If they acted at all, they would act like a careful neighbor.

They would keep the place from being broken by outside forces.

That, perhaps, would be their greatest work: protection without presence. If surface beings ever drilled downward, following some mineral rumor, the wise power would turn them aside without drama. A drill bit would meet stone too dense to justify cost. A survey would return ambiguous readings. A map would fail to convince. A dream would trouble the sleep of those who meant extraction, not with terror, but with a deep sense of wrongness: *not here, not this, not yet.* The under-realm would remain hidden not because it is weak, but because it is not theirs to be found.

Within the realm itself, they would not abolish hunger, because hunger is one of the ways balance speaks. They would not abolish predation, because predation too is part of the closed equation. They would not abolish death, because a world without death would soon become a world without room. But they would soften the extremes that serve no purpose except cruelty.

They would make sure that no young one is lost merely by a random coldness before its cuticle has sealed.  
They would make sure that pain does not become endless when death has already begun.  
They would make sure that predators do not kill in surplus and that prey are not left wounded in the dark for long intervals beyond hope.  
They would make sure that a shifting slab gives warning by sound or pressure before it traps a body, unless the moment is one of those that cannot be altered without unraveling more than it saves.  
They would make sure that fear, when it comes, passes.

They would not remove danger.  
They would refine mercy.

They would also strengthen what is already good in the beings.

Not by commandment. Not by rewriting their nature. But by a subtle deepening of the capacities they already possess. Their hands would become a little more certain when reading stress in stone. Their sensory pits would be a little finer when detecting the approach of a predator or the nearness of a lost child. Their memory for routes, notches, and pressure patterns would be a little more faithful. Their bodies would be better at conserving warmth during rare cold intervals. Their social instincts would incline more easily toward repair than resentment.

A wise power would know that the best blessing is not a miracle that suspends the world, but a grace that helps the world become more fully itself.

So they might give the beings this: a heightened sense of consequence.

Not guilt. Not punishment. Not shame. But a clearer perception of how each taking ripples through the closed sum of things. When a being prepares to scrape a crust too thin, its hand might hesitate with a faint unease. When a hunter is about to take more than need allows, its breathing valves might tighten. When someone is about to disturb another’s resting hollow without permission, the air between them might grow slightly less warm, not as threat, but as reminder. And when an act is truly balanced—when a tool is made well, when a gift is returned, when a body moves without disturbing others—there might come a small answering warmth, a sense of rightness felt in the plates and limbs, like the satisfaction of a flake parting cleanly from its core.

They would not make the beings moral by force.  
They would make moral attention easier.

And perhaps they would give them one gift that is close to sacred, though not religious in any surface sense.

They would let the stone remember.

In such a world, memory is already tactile. Notches are read by touch. Objects are kept near. Names are patterns of pressure. But death still removes a body from the shared field, and even the wisest beings know loss. A benevolent power might therefore allow certain stones, especially those long handled by a particular being, to retain a faint trace of that being’s pattern after death. Not a soul trapped in rock. Not a ghost demanding speech. Only a residual warmth, a pressure signature, a subtle texture that those who knew the dead could recognize if they placed their hands there in stillness.

This would not conquer death. It would not even prove survival. But it would offer a form of continuation suited to that place: the beloved not seen again, but still touchable in the world’s own memory.

A mother’s favorite hollow might keep the rhythm of her breathing for a time.  
An elder’s tool-stone might carry the exact warmth of his hands.  
A child’s first notch might remain faintly sharper than it should, as if the stone agreed to help remember.

The beings would not worship this. They would simply tend it, as they tend everything else. They would visit the marked stones when a loss needed settling. They would press their palms there and wait. Sometimes they would feel nothing, and that too would be accepted. Sometimes they would feel the faintest answering pressure, and they would know that the world had not entirely let go.

If the wise power chose to appear among them—and this is a more dangerous thing, because presence always risks domination—they would not appear as light, voice, or towering shape. They would come low. They would come warm. They would come in the posture of one asking to pass.

They might take on a body like theirs, or close enough to be received without horror: broad, plated, many-handed, eyeless, moving with deliberate closeness. They would not arrive with authority. They would not say, *I made you,* because that would introduce debt where there should be relation. They would not command worship. They would not demand obedience. They would enter a settlement the only way anyone may enter such a place: by making a gap.

Their first words would be pressure, not proclamation.

*I make a gap.*  
*I take no more than your silence allows.*  
*My edge is turned away.*

They might stay for a short time, or what passes for a short time in that unchanging world. They would help without taking over. They might warm a cold young one by pressing their own body near it. They might steady a shelter that has begun to shift. They might guide a lost gatherer back by tapping a rhythm against stone until the lost one recognizes direction. They might sit beside an old being who is nearing the end of life and exchange no words at all, only warmth and the slow pressure of company.

If asked who they are, they would answer in a way that does not increase their own weight:

*I am one who keeps the balance from being broken by carelessness.*

If asked whether they made the world, they might say:

*The world is made. That is enough.*

If asked whether they love the beings, they would not answer with a declaration. They would answer by turning their sharp edges away, by pressing their palm flat in the gesture that means *I will not take*, by remaining still enough to be trusted.

And when they left, they would leave no miracle behind. No monument. No new law. Perhaps only a single notch on a stone face, cut so precisely that every being who traced it would feel the same thing: that the world is not indifferent, but also not demanding. That they are known, but not owned. That they are held, but not confined.

The notch might mean, in the pressure-language of that people:

*You are already enough.*

Or perhaps:

*Do not dull one another.*

Or perhaps simply:

*I make a gap, and keep it.*

If they intervened in larger matters, it would be rare.

They would not stop every death, because death is part of the balance.  
They would not prevent every conflict, because even close beings must negotiate closeness.  
They would not eliminate predators, because predators too are persons of pressure and need.  
They would not make the crusts grow faster, because excess would destabilize the slow economy of the realm.  
They would not give the beings endless life, because endless life in a closed world becomes crowding, and crowding without renewal becomes cruelty.

But they would act against gratuitous ruin.

If a fissure threatened to open suddenly and swallow a resting knot of beings for no meaningful reason, they might seal it by degrees or warn the nearest bodies through a change in air pressure. If a predator became diseased with madness and killed without feeding, they might shorten its suffering and limit its harm. If a gathering of young ones was caught in a rare cold thinning of the deeper warmth, they might guide adults toward a hotter seam nearby. If a maker’s hand slipped and a fine edge was about to cut not a tool but a living body, they might dull the moment just enough that harm became near instead of actual.

These acts would be small. They would be almost invisible. They would be the kind of help that could be mistaken for luck, instinct, or skill.

That would be intentional.

A wise power would not want to be credited for every survival, because credit creates hierarchy. They would want the beings to remain the primary authors of their own lives. If the people survived, they should feel that they survived by touch, by care, by knowledge, by one another. The power would be the unseen margin around that knowledge, not the hand that replaced it.

They would also guard the beings from the most dangerous gift of all: certainty.

They would not prove themselves beyond doubt. They would not write their name across the stone in permanent fire. They would not speak with a voice that all must hear. Certainty of that kind would destroy the delicate culture of consent. It would turn ethics into obedience and reverence into fear. It would make every being feel watched in a way that collapses intimacy.

Instead, they would allow themselves to be suspected rather than known.

The beings might develop stories about them, but not churches. They might have a phrase for the power, something tactile and quiet. Perhaps they would call it *the Warmth That Does Not Press*. Or *the One Who Turns the Edge Away*. Or *the Stillness Beside the Fracture*. These names would not be shouted. They would be given only in close contact, passed from hand to hand like a polished tool.

When a being felt helped in a way it could not explain, it might touch its own chest plate and then the stone, a gesture meaning: *I received, I did not take.* When a death was especially gentle, they might say by pressure, *The Warmth came near.* When a child survived a cold interval, the adults might add one extra notch to a memory stone, not as prayer, but as acknowledgment.

They would not build temples, because temples require empty space and display. They would not make idols, because idols create distance. They would not sacrifice living things to the power, because sacrifice without necessity violates the balance. If they offered anything, it would be attention: a pause before taking, a careful pass through a crowded hollow, a refusal to waste, a moment of stillness beside a fresh fracture.

And this, perhaps, would please the wise power more than any altar.

Because what would a benevolent and wise power want from such a place?

Not worship.  
Not expansion.  
Not praise.  
Not the transformation of the under-realm into something more legible to higher eyes.

They would want the place to remain itself.

They would want the sharp edges to stay sharp in their usefulness.  
They would want the warm dark to stay warm without becoming suffocating.  
They would want the living crusts to regenerate.  
They would want the young to be touched enough to know their own boundaries.  
They would want the old to die without prolonged anguish.  
They would want the predators to feed without excess terror.  
They would want the makers to make without arrogance.  
They would want the beings to continue negotiating space rather than seizing it.

They would want the closed equation to remain balanced, but not cruelly so.

If the power loved them, it would not love them as a gardener loves flowers to be rearranged. It would love them as one loves a difficult, delicate, deeply ordered life: by helping it persist, by protecting its conditions, by refusing to impose one’s own hunger upon it.

And maybe, in the deepest part of their intention, they would do one more thing.

They would give the beings a way to refuse them.

This is important. A benevolent power that cannot be refused is not fully benevolent. So if they ever drew near enough to be felt, they would leave an opening. Their warmth could be turned away. Their pressure could be rejected. Their help could be declined by the simple gesture of withdrawing contact. And they would honor that withdrawal without resentment.
