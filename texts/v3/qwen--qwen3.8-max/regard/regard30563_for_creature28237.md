They are the most ephemeral of all the terrace-lives: not bodies so much as departures.

When pressure falls, when a warm fracture exhales, when a siltweaver curtain tears or a pipe-reed cracks under strain, the spore-clouds rise. They are pale in the dark, sometimes the color of bone washed in cold water, sometimes faintly blue, sometimes so fine and gray that they seem only a change in the rain itself. They do not swarm as insects might swarm. They do not flee. They release.

They drift through the standing weather in slow banners, caught in back-eddies, lifted by updrafts, folded into curtains of rain. Most never reach a place where they can hold. Most are carried into chutes and broken apart by falling water, or washed over bare rock, or dissolved into the mineral dark. But a few lodge in a crack where the flow is gentle, where there is enough warmth from below, where a film of silt has already begun to gather. There they darken, swell, and begin the long work of becoming moss, crust, cord, or reed.

The spore-clouds are the terraces’ way of risking themselves into the world without walking. They are the congregation’s future, scattered blind through the rain.

If such a place, and such a being as lives in it, were to draw the attention of a benevolent and wise power, they would not arrive in the way powers are usually imagined arriving.

They would not come down with a face.

They would not part the rain like a curtain, or silence the falls, or make the black terraces suddenly bright with a sun that had never belonged there. They would not stand above the valley as a judge stands above a court, or as a gardener stands over a flowerbed expecting gratitude. That would be too loud, too sudden, too much like an animal mind imposing itself on a place that has endured for centuries without animal minds.

If a benevolent and wise power noticed the Black Rain Terraces, the first thing they would do is listen.

Not with ears, because the terraces already contain more sound than ears could bear. Not with eyes, because there is little there for eyes to master. They would listen the way water listens to stone: by entering, by yielding, by learning where pressure goes when it cannot go straight.

They would let themselves become small in the way true power can become small: not diminished, but precise. They would sink into the blue-cord. They would rest in the drown-moss. They would wait in the hollow of a pipe-reed until the next surge passed through. They would feel the valley not as a scene but as a system of continuations.

And they would not mistake the congregation for loneliness.

That would be their first wisdom.

A lesser kindness might look at the Black Rain Terraces and see only absence. No birds. No beasts. No voices calling through the rain. No warm bodies sheltering under ledges. No eyes returning the gaze of the world. A lesser kindness might try to fill the place, to stock the pools with fish, to bring insects to the wet stone, to send roots of grass, to make it green in a gentler way, to populate it with recognizable life. It might think: *This country is empty. Let me make it less alone.*

But a wise power would understand that the terraces are not empty. They are differently full.

They would see that the drown-moss is not barren. That the ore-skin is not dead. That the pipe-reeds are not mute. That the siltweavers are not passive. That the breaker-shrubs are not failures. That the blue-cord is not silence. They would recognize that a being can be vast without being singular, alive without being hungry, responsive without having nerves, beautiful without being seen.

So they would not add animal life.

They would not give the valley eyes. They would not bring mouths, flocks, burrows, breeding cries, or fear. They would not introduce hunger into a place whose music is made of pressure and patience. They would not make the reeds answer to breath when they already answer to water. They would not force the valley into a shape more comforting to beings made of blood.

If any stray animal from the outer world wandered toward the terraces, the power would turn it away gently. A slope would become too slick. A wind would change direction. A sound would rise from the pipe-reeds, not frightening, but so immense and alien that the animal’s body would remember older wisdom and retreat. Not because animals were unworthy. Because the terraces were not for them.

That, too, would be mercy: to protect living things from a country that could sustain them only as stone sustains water—by wearing them away.

The power would not dry the rain.

They would not warm the cold into softness. They would not quiet the falls. They would not stop the breaker-shrubs from shattering when their time came to shatter. They would not save every frond, every spore, every century-old crust of ore-skin from loss. To do that would be to unmake the very balance by which the place endured.

Benevolence, if it is wise, knows that not all harm is cruelty. Some breakage is part of tuning. Some erosion is part of form. Some loss is how a landscape continues.

But they would distinguish between necessary violence and gratuitous ruin.

That distinction would be the heart of their care.

They would not prevent the valley from changing, but they would protect it from being annihilated. They would not make it safe from itself, but they would guard it against forces that exceeded its capacity to answer.

If a pulse came down from the high catchments too suddenly, too heavy, too cold, carrying enough force to scour away terraces that had taken centuries to form, the power might do almost nothing that could be named. They would not raise a hand. They would not command the flood to stop. Instead, a little ice might crack earlier than it otherwise would have. A narrow throat of rock might loosen a single stone into a channel, dividing the surge into two smaller currents. A siltweaver curtain might receive a subtle strengthening in its lower filaments, enough to bend without tearing. A side-basin might fill a little sooner, relieving pressure before the main fall became a hammer.

To any observer, if there had been one, it would look like chance.

Chance would be the only face such a power wore there.

They would not be a spectacle. They would be a difference in the water.

In places where the cold deepened too long and threatened to lock the living crusts into brittle death, the power might encourage warmth from below to find a new fracture. Not much warmth. Not enough to change the terraces into something gentle. Only enough to keep the near-freezing from becoming final. A vein of heat would open under a drowned bench. Blue-cord would brighten faintly along the new path. Moss would darken and hold. The valley would not know it had been spared, but it would continue.

Where the mineral balance shifted too sharply, where some distant reservoir released a bitter pulse of salt or metal that could poison the roots of the pipe-reeds, the power might slow it. They would guide a portion of that chemistry into dead basins where it could settle. They would encourage ore-skin to thicken in certain places, taking the excess into its own body, recording the danger as a dark band in its slow mineral flesh. They would let the valley be marked by what threatened it without letting it be erased.

They would remember that the terraces are already a kind of memory.

The ore-skin records water. The pipe-reeds record pressure. The siltweavers record sediment. The breaker-shrubs record violence. The blue-cord records connection. A wise power would not overwrite that memory with comfort. They would add to it gently, the way rain adds to stone: by degrees, by persistence, by leaving traces that can be read only by the patient.

They would also notice the artifacts.

The copper pins. The green-stained rings. The small hand-worked fittings. The cut notches. The fitted blocks. The ropes hardened by mineral crust. They would see that something once made things here. Something with hands, perhaps. Something that needed shelter, or signal, or meaning, or passage. Whatever it was, it is gone now, and the valley has not mourned it in any human way. The moss has grown over it. The water has polished it. The reeds have grown through it. The place has incorporated the fact of making without preserving the makers.

A benevolent power would not raise the dead.

They would not restore the artifacts to newness. They would not rebuild what was fallen. They would not conjure voices into the rain. That would be a kindness to memory, perhaps, but a violence to the present. The terraces belong to what lives there now, not to nostalgia.

Still, they might do one small thing.

Where a copper ring was being ground slowly to powder by moving silt, they might let a knot of drown-moss take hold beside it. The moss would not protect the ring with intention. It would simply grow. But its fibers would slow the scour. The ring would remain a little longer, not as treasure, not as relic, but as a fact: that something had been here once, and that the valley had not rejected it.

Where a hand-cut stone channel was about to collapse under centuries of water, the power might allow a fragment of breaker-shrub to lodge in its mouth, diverting the flow just enough to spare the shape a while longer. Not forever. Nothing in the terraces asks for forever. Only continuation. Only enough.

If humans ever came seeking the place, the power would decide carefully what to do.

They would not necessarily destroy the travelers. A wise and benevolent power would not be cruel to curiosity. But they would not allow the terraces to be possessed.

They would make the approach difficult in ways that did not require violence. The rain would thicken. Paths would become uncertain. Instruments would fail.
