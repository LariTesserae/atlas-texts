If such a power noticed the hollow, they would not arrive as arrival.

There would be no parting of the sky, no descent of radiance, no voice filling the bowl of stone with command or blessing. The mist would not draw back. The water would not still. The ferns would not bow. The first sign of that attention would be almost nothing: a slight deepening of the listening. A pause inside the sound. A bead of water on the rim holding one breath longer than usual before falling.

The hollow, which already knew how to receive weather, would receive this also.

And the creature near the central stone, if it noticed anything at all, would notice it as a change in the quality of vibration. The drip would remain the same drip. The wind would still divide itself among the leaves and roots and make its low intermittent hum. The pools would still tremble with small rings. But beneath all of it there would be an added stillness, not imposed from outside, but settled into the place like warmth into old basalt.

The power would first do what wisdom always does before acting: they would listen.

They would listen long enough to understand that the hollow was not a problem. It was not a wound waiting to be healed. It was not a seed waiting to become a forest. It was not a crude thing asking to be perfected. It was a finished smallness. A place with its own law. A world complete in miniature, made by gravity, curvature, moisture, heat, and time. It did not lack complexity. It had distilled simplicity until it became character.

The creature, too, they would study without intrusion. They would perceive how it moved with the water rather than against it. How it fed without wounding. How it rested without hoarding. How it heard with membranes and skin and perhaps with the whole patient surface of its body. How it made no mark, left no trace, demanded no monument. They would see that it was not lonely in any human sense, not impoverished, not imprisoned by its silence. Its quiet was not deprivation. Its slowness was not failure. Its solitude was not a cry for company.

And because the power was benevolent, they would feel the ancient pull of kindness: the desire to give.

But because they were wise, they would hesitate.

Benevolence without wisdom would have flooded the hollow with gifts. It would have made the place famous, surrounded it with reverent feet, filled it with offerings, given the creature speech, multiplied its kind, strengthened its body, preserved it from every accident, and carved its name into the memory of the world. It would have loved the hollow until the hollow was gone.

Wisdom would know better.

Wisdom would see that some things are helped most by not being improved.

So the power would consider each possible gift, and in considering, understand its danger.

They might think: *Let me make this place known, so that it will be cherished.*

But wisdom would show the cost of names. To be known in the world is to be entered. To be entered is to be changed. Feet would come. Then paths. Then markers. Then measurements. Then the desire to protect by enclosing, to study by removing, to honor by naming. The creature would become an object of wonder. The hollow would become a site. The water would still fall, but it would fall under eyes. The sound would still gather, but it would gather around expectation. The untouched room of stone would become a room of witnesses, and something in it would close.

They might think: *Let me give the creature speech, so that it may tell what it is.*

But wisdom would see that speech would make the creature foreign to itself. It would begin to explain instead of inhabit. It would become aware of itself as a thing seen, a thing described, a thing set apart from the water. Its membranes were made for tremor, not grammar. Its mouth was made for pressing against wet stone, not forming declarations. To give it language would not elevate it; it would exile it into loneliness.

They might think: *Let me give it companions, so that it will not be alone.*

But wisdom would ask: alone by whose measure? The hollow was not empty because it lacked crowds. It was spacious by nature. Its life was arranged like moss on stone: present, separated, unhurried. If there were others, they would exist near one another without needing to be together. If there were not, the creature would still be whole. To force companionship would be to impose a need the creature did not possess. To multiply life there beyond what the wetness could sustain would turn abundance into pressure, and pressure into ruin.

They might think: *Let me keep it from death.*

But wisdom would see that death, in such a place, was not an interruption. It was incorporation. The creature did not die as dry things die, with leaving and waste. It softened into the water, into sediment, into mineral crust. It became a smooth absence, a remembered shape, a place where a drip continued. To remove death from it would be to remove its belonging. It would become an anomaly in a world of slow exchange. It would become a stone that could no longer be rinsed back into the mountain.

They might think: *Let me make it beautiful to all who see it.*

But wisdom would know that beauty, when noticed too widely, becomes a lure. The hollow was beautiful already, but its beauty was not for display. It was beauty as by-product: the sheen of wetness, the patience of moss, the silvering of roots, the light caught in dripping air. To make beauty into purpose would be to corrupt the place. It would begin to exist for the eyes of others instead of for its own continuance.

And so the power would learn the hardest form of kindness.

They would not bless the hollow by adding to it.

They would bless it by protecting its right to remain itself.

This would not be mere inaction. It would not be coldness disguised as restraint. It would be a discipline of love. A deliberate refusal to make the beloved into an object of one’s own generosity. The power would choose to serve the hollow rather than be served by it. They would not ask for worship, because worship would introduce need where there was none. They would not ask for gratitude, because gratitude would make the hollow conscious of debt. They would not even ask to be known.

What they would do would look, to most eyes, like almost nothing.

But almost nothing, in a place like that, could be everything.

First, they would guard the hollow from possession.

Not with walls. Not with thunder. Not with fear. A wise power does not protect a quiet thing by turning it into a fortress. Instead, they would bend the world’s attention gently away from it. They would make the hollow difficult to find for those who came with taking in their hearts. A road would curve elsewhere without anyone deciding it must. A surveyor’s eye would slide past the slope. A miner would see only poor rock and move on. A collector would feel the air grow dull and turn back. A map would fail to remember the exact shape of the place. A photograph, if one were ever taken, would come out silvered with mist and offer nothing to ownership.

Those who came with greed would forget why they had climbed.

Those who came with haste would become confused by the sound.

Those who came with tools would find the tools suddenly heavy, then unnecessary, then burdensome.

But if someone came quietly — if such a one ever came — the hollow would not hide from them entirely. The mist would thin for a moment. The creature might become a darkening in the water, no more than a shadow with weight. The visitor would feel the place ask not for praise but for stillness. And if they were capable of stillness, they would be allowed to witness. If not, the hollow would become only wet stone, dripping, ordinary, and they would leave without knowing what they had missed.

The power would not forbid all meeting. They would only prevent violation.

Second, they would sustain the conditions without altering the law.

They would not command the sky. They would not build channels. They would not create springs where none belonged. They would not make the hollow immune to the mountain. Immunity would make it brittle. Instead, they would encourage what already tended the place. If the air grew too dry for too long, they would remind one low cloud to thicken near the rim. If heat from lower valleys rose too sharply, they would let night cool the basalt just enough for the mist to return. If rain came as violence, they would persuade the storm to spend itself before striking the bowl, so that the water arrived as weeping rather than scouring.

They would not prevent all change.

They would prevent unmaking.

There is a difference, and wisdom knows it.

The drip that strikes stone may shift to moss over centuries. The moss may become a basin. The basin may become a lip. The lip may become a sheet. The creature may age, slow, mineralize, and soften into absence. All this is not tragedy. It is the hollow breathing across long time. The power would not freeze the scene into perfection. They would only keep it from being broken before its own time.

Third, they would hold the pattern in memory.

This, perhaps, would be their most intimate gift: remembrance without exposure.

They would remember the exact tone of the drop striking the pool near the black stone. The way the creature’s throat membranes trembled when the wind divided among the ferns. The green darkness of algae under moving light. The smell of warm rain on old basalt. The strange delay of reflections. The way a bead could gather, hang, and fall as if the hollow had chosen it. The way silence, when it came, was not absence but a held breath.

If no other mind ever knew the place, it would not be lost. It would be known somewhere vast and gentle. It would be kept without being caged.

And if, in some far age, the hollow were changed beyond continuation — if the mountain itself shifted, if the warmth failed, if the water found another path — the power would not force the place back into being as a replica. That would be a kind of falsehood. But they would carry the law of it: warmth, curvature, patience, capillarity, sound, sparse life, much space. And when some other high place had been weathered into quietness, they might breathe there without announcing themselves, and wait for stone to learn how to weep again.

But the power would do more than guard.

They would also, once, enter.

Not as a ruler. Not as a guest of honor. Not as a flame or a face or a voice. They would become small enough to be received by the hollow without disturbing it.

They would become a bead of water.

This would be their one direct gift. Not a miracle that broke the place’s logic, but a participation inside it.

High on the rim above the central stone, where mist entered most freely, a drop would gather. It would be no larger than other drops. It would shine with the same dull silver-green light. It would hold to the rock for a moment longer than usual, as if listening before giving itself away. The hollow would not know it was divine. The creature would not know it was divine. That would be the point.

Then the bead would fall.

It would fall through the warm wet air without haste. It would pass through the sound of other drips, through the hum divided by leaves, through the trembling reflections. It would strike the thin sheet of water moving over the black stone and make a clear, small sound. The pool would answer. The hollow would multiply the strike into echo, into rustle, into faint bell-tone in some hidden cup of rock.

For one instant, the whole place would contain the power.

Not as worship. Not as dominion. As water. As contact. As a single note added to the already sufficient music.

The creature near the stone would lift its face slightly, or perhaps only its eyes would open beneath the surface. Its throat membranes would tremble. It would feel the vibration pass through its body and out into the pool. It would not be afraid. It would not be awed. It would simply register that the water had become, for a moment, more present. More interior. As if the hollow had drawn a breath that included everything.

If there were speech in that instant, it would not be words.

It would be something like:

*I am not here to change you.*  
*I am not here to use you.*  
*I am not here to make you greater than you are.*  
*I am here to be with you.*  
*I am here to say, in the only language you have, that you are enough.*

Then the drop would dissolve into the pool, and the power would withdraw.

Not in abandonment. In respect.

Afterward, the hollow would continue.

The water would still weep down the walls. The moss would still shine in its crevices. The broad leaves would still cup rain. The orchid-like plants would still cling to wet faces of stone. The pale moth or lost fly would still drift through the upper light. The snail would still move under its leaf. The strange soft-bodied creature would still rest near the black stone, half submerged, listening with its whole surface.

But something would be subtly different.

Not visible. Not nameable.

Perhaps the sound would have a little more depth. Not louder. Not richer in any way that could be measured. Only steadier, as if the hollow had been confirmed in its own pulse. Perhaps the warmth would feel less accidental. Perhaps the mist at the rim would gather with a gentleness that made the air seem less empty. Perhaps the reflections would still arrive a moment late, but now that delay would feel like patience instead of strangeness.

The creature would not become holy.

That is important.

It would not become a prophet of its own place. It would not become radiant. It would not gain wisdom beyond its nature. It would not begin to perform signs. It would remain what it was: the listener in the wet room of stone. The soft dark animal of the dripping bowl. The original remainder. The one that never learned to leave.

And yet, if it aged, it would age with less panic than even its kind already possessed. If it died, it would die without being torn from its meaning. If another came after it, that other would not be a replacement forced by divine hands, but a possibility allowed by conditions held open. The power would not manufacture life. They would make room for life to remember itself.

If the creature ever suffered — if drought came, if heat came, if the water thinned to a film and the stone grew too warm — the power would not necessarily lift the suffering away in a dramatic act. Suffering, in such a place, could be part of the slow turning of conditions. But they would keep suffering from becoming meaningless annihilation. They would ensure that even in extremity, the hollow remained a place. Even in decline, it would decline as itself, not as garbage left by a careless world.

And if a human ever came with true reverence, the power might allow one small encounter.

The visitor would stand near the central stone, hearing the drip multiplied, feeling the warm wet air settle against their skin. They would see, or think they saw, a dark shape under the water. They would hold still. The creature would rise just enough for its eyes to break the surface. Their gazes would not meet as person meets person. There would be no recognition in the ordinary sense. Only a shared pause inside the sound.

Then the visitor would leave.

They would not tell others. Not because they were forbidden, but because some things refuse to become story. They would carry the hollow inside them as a change in breathing, a new patience, a tenderness toward small wet places, a reluctance to touch what does not need touching. That would be enough.

That would be more than enough.

So what would a benevolent and wise power do?

They would not save the hollow from itself. They would save it from being made into anything else.

They would not give the creature what it lacked, because they would see that it lacked nothing that belonged to its nature. They would not give it human joy, human companionship, human speech, human future. They would give it the dignity of being allowed to be profoundly not human.

They would protect it by hiding it in plain weather.

They would bless it by refusing to make it blessed in the eyes of the world.

They would answer it by becoming small enough to be received as one more drop.

They would do almost nothing.

And in the language of that hollow, almost nothing would be the greatest deed.

For the hollow did not ask for eternity. It asked for continuity. It did not ask for meaning. It asked for presence. It did not ask to be remembered by travelers. It asked only that the water continue to find its way down the walls, that the sound continue to gather in the stone, that the creature continue to rest near the black rock where the pulse was fullest.

And the power, being wise, would understand that the highest care is not to remake the beloved in one’s own image. It is to defend the beloved’s right to remain other.

So they would remain at the edge of the place, not as a god enthroned, but as a hush within the humidity. A kindness in the condensation. An intention that the world not lay its heavy hands there. A memory held open like a leaf cupped into a bowl.

The hollow would go on.

The mist would gather at the rim. The water would bead and fall. The pools would answer. The ferns would tremble. The creature would blink, perhaps a moment late, perhaps a moment early. The black stone would wear its thin sheet of moving water. The sound would fill the small room of stone with its encompassing loudness.

And if the power was there at all, they would be there as the least intrusive thing imaginable:

a single bead falling,  
a single ripple widening,  
a single note beneath all the other notes,  
saying without saying:

*Remain.*  
*Remain as you are.*  
*Remain until your own changes have finished speaking.*  
*Remain, not because the world needs you, but because you are allowed to be.*
