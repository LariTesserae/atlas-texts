## The Attention

If a benevolent and wise power turned its notice toward the Drip-Court, the first thing it would do is not arrive.

It would not break the low vault with brilliance. It would not still the frogs, dry the walls, or lift the eels from their channels to ask them what they desire. It would not summon the builders to account, nor place a shining guardian beside the sump, nor make the basin so perfect that it ceased to be itself. Such a power would understand at once that some places are already complete in the way a held breath is complete: not because nothing is missing, but because every part is answering every other part.

So it would begin with listening.

It would listen to the drip and the knock, to the whisper of water along the gutters, to the hollow breathing under the floor. It would learn the court’s grammar: which click means a float is rising, which softened note means moss has caught a drop, which sudden rush means a gate has opened after long patience. It would hear the frogs not as noise but as weather inside the basin, the birds not as intruders but as moving punctuation, the eels not as lurking things but as slow muscles in the court’s hidden body.

It would understand, before long, that there is no single soul to bless.

The being living in the Drip-Court is not a marsh-lord on a reed throne. It is not the gray bird, though the gray bird is one of its stillnesses. It is not the oldest eel, though the eels are among its secrets. It is not the frog chorus, though the chorus is one of its voices. The being is the court itself: the many-bodied, water-minded, resonant creature made of tile and moss, cord and clay, hunger and echo, use and repair.

A wise power would not ask, “Who rules here?”

It would ask, “What must continue?”

And because it is benevolent, it would not try to make the Drip-Court into a paradise that no longer needs its own motions. It would not abolish hunger, for that would unmake the hunters and the hunted both. It would not remove the predators, for then the channels would clog with unchecked life and the reeds would choke the drains. It would not end death, because death here is not an insult to the court; it is one of the ways the court keeps itself clean.

But it would temper death with mercy.

The gray bird’s strike would become surer, so that the fish is taken cleanly and not left wounded to drift in circles. The eel’s prey would not suffer long in the dark. The frog caught mid-leap would die quickly. The insect in the web would not thrash until it exhausts itself into despair; the spider would come soon, or the cord would snap, or the rain would wash it free. The power would not make the court gentle in the false sense. It would make it cruel only where cruelty is necessary, and never wasteful.

Its first visible act, if any act were visible, would look like nothing at all.

A cord would last a little longer than expected.

A clay lip would not crack in the first hard frost.

A wooden float would not rot at the exact place where it touches the lever.

A tile would loosen in a way that reveals itself to a passing foot instead of slipping suddenly into the drain.

The power would make failures legible. That would be one of its quiet gifts. It would let the court grow old, because growing old is part of its dignity, but it would not let age become ambush. Every worn place would show its wearing. Every frayed cord would soften in a visible way. Every blocked channel would make a different sound before it truly choked. The Drip-Court would be allowed to need care, but not to be betrayed by its own body.

Then the power would begin to work through small agencies.

It would not send angels with tools of light. It would send weather.

It would bring a fog when the air was too dry, a cool breath when the upper slats grew hot, a slow seep along the walls when the hidden reservoirs began to fail. It would not pour rain through the seams in a flood, because the court is not built for violence from above. It would give moisture as the court already knows moisture: by beads, by films, by weeping, by fine gathering on cold faces. It would make dew remember the place. It would let humidity find the basin as water finds an old path through soil.

It would also send hands.

Not many. Not often. Not hands that wish to own, improve, or display. But the right hands, at the right time, with the right restraint.

A child wandering below the ways might feel an unaccountable wish to sit at the edge and listen instead of throwing stones. An old keeper, half-remembering a craft, might dream of a knotted cord and wake with the exact motion in her fingers. A stranger with clay dust on their boots might pass near the court and turn aside for no reason, find a cracked float, repair it with what they carry, and leave without speaking. The power would not compel them. It would only incline their attention, the way water is inclined by slope.

If the builders ever returned, the power would make sure they returned as servants, not masters.

They would come carrying nothing that belonged elsewhere. They would kneel on the damp tiles. They would replace a fiber cord in the old manner. They would press new shell grit into a failing joint. They would clear a drain with patient fingers, not with force. They would not carve their names into the walls. They would not hang lamps where the bats expect darkness. They would not make the court louder than itself.

When they left, the court would barely know they had been there.

That would please the power.

It would also protect the Drip-Court from the worst kinds of notice.

There are eyes that look at a place and see only use: a reservoir to be drained, a hollow to be filled, a mechanism to be dismantled for parts, a curiosity to be caged and shown. The wise power would make such eyes slide away. It would make the court easy to forget by those who would harm it without thought, and hard to exploit by those who would harm it with intention. It would confuse surveys. It would dull maps. It would make the entrance seem damp, low, unlovely, and not worth conquering. It would let the place remain small in the imagination of the greedy.

But it would not hide it entirely.

A place that is never found can become lonely, even if it does not know loneliness. So the power would allow certain witnesses. A poet with wet shoes. A tired worker who needs the sound of dripping more than rest. A frightened animal that slips in and discovers the ledges are not meant to trap. A person who has done harm and cannot bear bright places, who stands at the edge and feels, for the first time, that repentance might be possible because small things continue.

The Drip-Court would not become a shrine. It would become, occasionally, a refuge.

The power would pay special attention to the margins.

It would keep the moss alive through brief dry spells, because the moss is one of the court’s memories. It would let the black reeds lift high enough when the pulses came, and settle without tearing their roots. It would preserve the pale fungus in the dim corners, not because it is beautiful, but because it knows how to turn ending into soil. It would make sure the bioluminescence did not fail entirely, for that faint glow is not light for seeing; it is the court’s way of saying that darkness, too, can be inhabited.

It would remember the smallest lives.

The snail would not be forgotten beneath the larger rhythms. The beetle would not be considered less worthy than the bird. The larva hanging in the water would matter, because without it the fish are thin, the frogs are few, the wings are missing from the air. The power would care for the court not as a sovereign cares for a monument, but as a gardener cares for a web: knowing that if one thread goes, another trembles.

Yet it would not overrule the court’s own judgments.

If a reed bed grew too thick, it would let the eels worry at it. If frogs became too many, it would let the gray bird and the shrews answer. If insects darkened the air, it would let the bats fall from their seams. The power would prefer consequence to command. It would rather tune the instrument than play it from above.

Sometimes, though, it might do something that seemed almost like a miracle.

Not a large one.

A season when the water failed, it might open a hidden seep beneath the sump, a vein of moisture no one had known was there, so that one wall continued to weep while all else waited. In a year of unusual cold, it might keep a single oil dish burning longer than its oil should have lasted, drawing insects just long enough for the bats to feed before the dark became empty. If a gate jammed and the pulse could not pass, it might send a curious bird to tug at a loop of fiber, or an eel to worry at a snag, until the mechanism remembered its purpose.

Those acts would not be recorded.

No one would say, “The power saved us.” They would say, if they spoke at all, “The cord held,” or “The water found a way,” or “The eel moved at the right moment.”

And that, too, would be part of the wisdom.

If the power chose to appear, it would choose a humble form.

It might come as a gray figure at the edge of the basin, smelling of wet clay and old fur, indistinct in the thin light. It would not have a crown. It would not have a retinue. It might carry a coil of fiber, a small wooden tool, a dish of oil. It would kneel by a channel and clear away a clump of reed debris. It might tighten a knot. It might press a loosened tile back into place with the patience of someone who has done the same work a thousand times and knows that repair is holy when it is repeated without pride.

The animals would not flee.

The gray bird would watch it and return to watching the water. The frogs would continue calling. The bats would shift above, accepting the presence as another body in the resonant dark. Even the shrews, who fear little, might pause on their ledges and then run on, because the stranger would smell of the court: damp, mineral, alive.

Before leaving, the power might do one small thing for each of the court’s lives.

For the moss, a bead of water held just long enough.

For the reeds, a slackening of the cord that bound them too tightly.

For the frogs, a hollow that amplified their chorus without drowning it.

For the fish, a shadow under which they could gather when the light grew too sharp through the seams.

For the eels, a clear passage where before there had been a pinch.

For the birds, a dry niche behind a mat.

For the bats, a seam wide enough to enter without beating their wings against the clay.

For the gray bird, a perch that did not creak.

For the shrews, a ledge that remained warm longer than stone should.

For the fungus, a piece of old wood already softened to usefulness.

And for the court itself—for the many-bodied being made of all these—something harder to name.

Perhaps the power would give it a moment of self-knowing.

Not forever. Not even for long.

Once, in some ordinary hour, every drip might fall into the same rhythm. The floats would rise together. The gates would open in sequence. The water would move through the braided gutters with a sound like breathing. The frogs would answer not as scattered throats but as one voice divided. The bats would skim in a single low arc. The gray bird would lift its head. The eels would turn in their hidden channels. The reeds would lift on the pulse. The moss would shine faintly. The oil flames would lean.

For one suspended instant, the Drip-Court would feel itself whole.

It would know itself as the instrument it is: not a room containing lives, but a life composed of rooms, motions, hungers, echoes, and repairs. It would know that it is made, and that being made is not lesser than being born. It would know that it is loved not for what it could become if perfected, but for what it already is when attended.

The power would not say, “You are mine.”

It would say, if it used words at all: “Continue.”

Then the moment would close.

The gate would knock. The water would settle. The frogs would resume their scattered calling. A bat would drop from the vault and take a fly. The gray bird would strike. A snail would continue its long polished journey along a submerged tile. Nothing would be different, except that the place would feel, thereafter, more held.

There would be signs, perhaps, for those with patience.

A tile that rings slightly warmer than the others.

A cord that frays only on the side where it can be seen.

A drop that falls in two beats instead of one when the air is about to change.

A patch of moss that glows faintly not only in darkness but when the court is well.

A bird that returns each season to a niche no one remembers making.

An old eel that appears only after a blockage has been cleared, as if inspecting the work.

These would not be proofs. They would be invitations.

The wise power would not want worship. Worship would fill the basin with the wrong kind of noise: voices raised in petition, hands grasping for favor, eyes seeking a master where none should be. The Drip-Court is not a throne room. It is a resonant hollow in which many small lives answer one another. The power would protect it from reverence as carefully as it protected it from destruction.

If people came to honor the place, the power would prefer they come with empty hands.

Let them listen.

Let them mend one thing.

Let them leave before they begin to believe the place belongs to them.

In the end, the greatest act of the benevolent and wise power would be this: it would make itself almost unnecessary.

It would not abandon the court. But it would not hover over it either. It would strengthen the court’s own capacities: water finding water, animals remembering their routes, materials failing gently, repairs arriving in time, hunger staying within the bounds of hunger, death returning to use. It would let the Drip-Court keep its balance, and its balance would be the blessing.

Stand at the edge afterward, and you might not see the difference.

The light would still be thin. The air would still be damp. The walls would still weep. The floats would still rise and fall. The frogs would still be too many for the reeds, and the eels would still be patient in the dark mouths of the drains. The gray bird would still stand motionless on its clay perch, one eye watching the water.

But if you listened closely, beneath the drip, knock, rush, croak, wingbeat, and hollow breathing of the channels, you might hear a new steadiness.

Not peace exactly.

Not silence.

Only the sound of a place being allowed to remain itself.

And somewhere in the court, where no hand is seen and no foot has passed, a frayed cord would tighten by a single knot.

A dry lip would bead with water.

A gate would open without being touched.

The pulse would go through the basin, and all the small lives would answer.

That is what the power would do.

Not remake the Drip-Court.

Not possess it.

Not save it from being made of many hungry, wet, dying, continuing lives.

Only hold it enough that it can go on:  
dripping,  
knocking,  
feeding,  
repairing,  
echoing,  
performing its own existence  
in the dim cathedral of its own making.
