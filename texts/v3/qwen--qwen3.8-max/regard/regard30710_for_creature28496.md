If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention to that basin, the first thing they would understand is that it did not need rescuing.

It was not a wounded place pretending to be whole. It was not a poor place waiting for a richer form of life. It was not a dark place begging for fire. It was already complete in its own severe and patient way: a world of old stone, borrowed light, uncertain water, pale lives, and enormous quiet. A truly wise power would see that at once. They would not look at the absence of green things and think, *Here is lack.* They would look at the living skins on the stone, the mirror-paths, the hushed city, the careful people, the pulses of fauna, and they would recognize that another kind of fullness had been achieved.

So if they did anything, they would begin with restraint.

They would not descend in glory. They would not arrive with thunder, because thunder there would be more than sound; it would be violence against a world built on small signals. They would not come as flame, because flame would mean heat, smoke, hunger, and the memory of wood where wood had never belonged. They would not come as a conquering light, because the basin’s brightness was already a shared and fragile inheritance, passed from mirror to mirror, pool to pool, polished face to angled stone. To overwhelm it would be to destroy it.

They would come, if they came at all, as a deepening of what was already there.

Perhaps the first sign would be almost nothing.

A damp-reader pressing her palm to a terrace might feel, for one moment, that the stone was being pressed back. Not answering in words. Not answering in warmth, exactly. More like the sensation that the stone’s attention had become complete. As if the basin, which had always been listened to by its people, was suddenly being listened to by something larger.

A mirror-keeper on a high path might pause while polishing a shell-bright panel and feel that his hands were steadier than usual. Not enchanted. Not compelled. Simply helped, the way a person is helped when a difficult task is being done with love and some unseen presence leans close enough to matter.

A child watching translucent worms rise in a cistern might see them arrange themselves for an instant into a pattern too deliberate to be accident: a line, a curve, a branching like a seep opening in miniature. Then the worms would scatter and return, and the child would not be sure what had happened, except that the pool had seemed to look back.

That would be the manner of such a power.

Not spectacle.

Confirmation.

The basin would not be overtaken by divinity. It would be witnessed.

And because the power would be wise, they would know that even benevolence can become a burden if it is too heavy. A god who stands visibly above a people changes them by standing there. A miracle too obvious makes fear, dependence, rivalry, priesthoods, jealousies, false interpretations. In a place where light was common inheritance and loudness was considered clumsiness, a blatant divine presence would be like a boulder dropped into a shallow pan. It would break the pattern it claimed to love.

So they would act through the pattern.

They would ask, in their own way, what the basin and its beings required.

Not in a voice that shook the ceiling. Not in a dream so grand that the dreamer woke shouting. They would ask in the small language of that world: a change in pressure, a bead of moisture, a tremor in fungal felt, a pale fish turning where no fish should turn, a single drop falling from a dry shaft into a cup that had been left empty by accident.

And the answers would come back in the basin’s own terms.

The oldest damp-reader, dreaming with her hands open, would feel water moving under stone and would think, without knowing why: *Do not let us be thirsty without understanding why.*

A mirror-keeper, half asleep beside a cracked panel, would see in his mind a light-path restored by a sliver of shell and a patient adjustment of angle, and would think: *Do not let the brightness be lost through carelessness.*

A child holding a molted crustacean shell would dream that the shell was a small boat carrying light across a black pool, and would think: *Do not let the little lives be wasted.*

A scavenger, resting in a high gallery with its padded feet curled beneath it, would dream of clean bones and finished work, and would think, in its wordless way: *Let what dies become part of what continues.*

And the power would hear all of these.

They would not dismiss them as small prayers.

To a wise power, small prayers are often the truest ones.

Then they would act.

Not by making the basin into another world, but by helping it become more perfectly itself.

The first thing they would do would be to make the signs clearer.

The basin already spoke to its inhabitants. It spoke through worms rising before pressure changed, through crustaceans appearing before seeps opened, through the softening of dust, the smell of cold salt, the way mist gathered under certain ceiling ribs, the way a hollow changed its sound when water was near. The people had spent generations learning these signs. Their whole culture was a discipline of attention.

A benevolent power would not replace that discipline with prophecy. They would refine it.

Where a sign had been ambiguous, it would become slightly less so. Where a warning had come too late, it would come earlier by a margin just large enough to matter. Where the people had mistaken one kind of damp for another, the difference would be revealed not as a thunderclap of certainty but as a quiet increase in perceptiveness: a damp-reader’s palm would cool sooner; a line of dust would settle differently; a cluster of wingless insects would shift along a wall in a way that an experienced eye could not fail to notice.

The power would not eliminate surprise. Surprise was part of the basin’s life. Water arrived unexpectedly; pools appeared where floors had been dry; fauna surged and withdrew. To remove surprise entirely would be to make the world mechanical and deaden the people’s art. But the power would reduce the cruelest surprises: the sudden flood that drowned stores, the hidden seep that undermined a platform before anyone could move it, the dry interval that came just when cisterns were already low.

They would not make the basin safe from change.

They would make the people more fluent in change.

The second thing they would do would be to guard the light.

Light in the basin was not merely illumination. It was law, common good, orientation, and beauty. The mirror-keepers were already its priests without calling themselves priests. They maintained the shell-bright panels, the polished limestone faces, the shallow mirror pools, the angled slabs that carried daylight from shaft to shaft and district to district. They understood that one blocked light-path could dim a drying court, a nursery, a cistern edge, a place where fungal felt needed brightness to thicken.

A wise power would honor that labor.

They would not give the basin a second sun. They would not fill it with radiance so abundant that no mirror needed tending. That would cheapen the care already present. Instead, they would make the existing light more resilient.

Polished surfaces would cloud more slowly. Shell-panels would resist cracking under the slow stress of age. Calcite would hold its milk-bright luster a little longer. Dust would still gather, because dust was part of the basin’s breath, but it would not cling where it was most destructive. Hands that adjusted mirrors would sometimes find the correct angle with uncanny ease, as though the stone remembered being aligned and wanted to return to it.

In places where a light-path had been broken by accident or neglect, the power might allow a subtle sign to appear: a soft-edged shadow that remained after other shadows had moved, a pale glare on a wall where no glare belonged, a pool reflecting a brightness that had no visible source. The mirror-keepers would notice. They always noticed. They would repair the path, and afterward the district would seem to breathe more easily, though no one could say exactly why.

The power would not block any light-path themselves.

That, among the basin people, would be understood as a kind of blasphemy.

The third thing they would do would be to protect the water without making it abundant.

Water was the restless heart of the basin. It arrived by seep, mist, condensation, brief ceiling-rain, hidden crack, and temporary channel. It was not a river god. It was not a reliable spring. It was intermittent, shy, easily offended by misuse, easily lost if the wrong hollow was paved or the wrong terrace blocked. The people had built their city around this uncertainty. Their architecture moved with water. Their ethics moved with water. If a seep opened beneath a dwelling, the dwelling moved. Water had right of way.

A benevolent and wise power would not turn that into plenty.

To give the basin a great river would destroy its nature. It would erode the soft stone, drown the microbial skins, scatter the fauna, make the movable city impossible, and breed new forms of greed around permanent channels. To give endless water would be to replace patience with entitlement. The people’s virtue lay partly in their readiness for absence. A wise power would not take that from them.

But they would protect the water from becoming betrayal.

If poison seeped down from the dry surface above—bitter minerals, dead dust, some foulness brought by careless feet—the power would guide the basin’s own defenses. A white skin would form over the tainted film, thick enough to be lifted away. A cistern would turn faintly sharp in smell before anyone drank, and the damp-readers would know to close it. A hidden throat in the stone would open just enough to drain a dangerous pool before it undermined a court, then close again, leaving only a damp ring like a reminder.
