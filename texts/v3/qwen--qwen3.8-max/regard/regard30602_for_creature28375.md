One household may possess the right to hang a sleeping mat from a beam warmed by an engine; another the right to collect the sweat of a particular pipe; another the right to place a foot on a certain step only when the lower turbine is turning at its slowest, because the step flexes then and is safest. Even silence is apportioned. There are hours when no one in a certain lane may hammer, hours when a valve must be opened by hand rather than by wrench, hours when a child is carried rather than allowed to walk, because small footsteps near a fresh seam might be too much.

Into such a place, if a benevolent and wise power turned its attention, the first thing they would notice is not the hardship but the restraint.

They would see the hollow not as a pit, not as a prison, not as a quaint underground village, but as a single living agreement. To their eyes, the settlement would be a body: engines for slow hearts, pipes for veins, wicks for capillaries, roots for tendons, lamps for half-open eyes, water for blood, silence for breath. The people would not seem separate from it, not truly. They would appear as the part of the being that knows itself: the hands that repair, the voices that murmur, the feet that learn the temperature of grating, the dreams that collect in the dark like condensation.

A wise power would understand at once that this being is already complete in the way fragile things can be complete. It is not lacking grandeur. It is lacking margin.

So they would not enter by splitting the ceiling. They would not come as light that blinds, or as voice that shakes dust from the rafters, or as miracle that turns every drop of moisture into wine and every iron pin into silver. Such things would be violence here. A sudden abundance would break the careful economy of thirst. A visible god would crush the quiet dignity of maintenance. The hollow does not need to be overwhelmed; it needs to be preserved without being robbed of its meaning.

Therefore, the first act of the benevolent power would be almost no act at all.

They would listen.

They would listen through the rock, through the cables, through the slow turning of flywheels, through the soft ticking of cooling metal, through the tiny percussion of droplets striking ceramic. They would learn the etiquette of the place: that a sharp sound is a danger, that wasted moisture is a moral offense, that haste is a kind of blindness, that every gift must have a place to go or it becomes a hazard. They would learn that the inhabitants do not ask for much, not because they have no desire, but because desire here has been trained into precision. They do not dream of oceans. They dream of a reliable drip.

Only after listening would the power act.

And even then, they would act the way a careful hand acts in a room where someone is sleeping.

---

The first communication would likely come as a dream.

Dreams would be the safest doorway. A dream does not crack stone. It does not heat the air. It does not demand a crowd or a shrine. It can be refused upon waking without shame. It can be discussed in low voices or not discussed at all. It leaves no scorch mark on the wall.

In the lower tiers, the engine-sitters might dream that a stranger places a palm against the casing of an old machine and does not fix it, only listens. In the dream, the stranger’s hand is warm but not hot, and when they withdraw, the machine’s rhythm becomes so slight, so nearly perfect, that the engine-sitter wakes with tears on their face and cannot say why.

In the middle tiers, a thread-setter might dream of a single bead of water hanging from a copper lip. It does not fall. It does not evaporate. It waits. When the dreamer approaches, the bead reflects not their face but the whole hollow, every tier and walkway and leaf, all held inside one small sphere.

In the upper gardens, a child might dream of a moth with wings like stained glass, flying from lamp to lamp without casting any shadow. Where it passes, the lamps burn a little steadier. The child wakes remembering a word they have never learned, a word that means “guest” but also “root that does not strangle.”

The stone-listeners would dream differently. They would press their cheeks to the raw rock, as they do in waking life, but in the dream the rock would answer before they ask. It would say, not in language but in pressure and temperature: I am willing to become older. I am willing to settle. I am not ready to be gentle, but I can be trusted a little longer.

If the power spoke at all in those dreams, they would not command. They would ask.

What can be given without taking away your becoming?

And because the hollow is one being with many mouths, the answer would come back in fragments: more time. Less fear. A little more room. Not ease. Not endlessness. Only room.

The power would accept this answer as a prayer.

---

Their first gift would be time.

The young rock is the settlement’s oldest enemy and also its foundation. It is sharp because it has not yet aged. It is unstable because its fracture is recent. A benevolent power could, in an instant, fuse every crack, round every blade of stone, make the hollow as smooth as a river chamber. But that would erase the truth of the place. It would turn a wound into a monument and the inhabitants into museum figures. It would remove the need for their vigilance, and their vigilance is part of their dignity.

So instead, the power would hasten the settling without falsifying it.

They would draw a faint mineral breath up from the deep places, not sulfur-heavy, not scorching, but carrying dissolved silica, lime, iron, and other names the residents would not need to know. This breath would rise through hairline fissures too small for any eye to see. It would leave microscopic deposits in the cracks, binding the stone grain by grain. It would not make the rock soft. It would not blunt every edge. The ledges would still be dangerous, the overhangs still demanding respect. But the hidden fractures that might have widened suddenly would begin to knit. The ceilings would stop shedding splinters in the quiet hours. The deep seams would move less often. The stone would still be young, but it would begin to learn patience.

The root-tenders would notice first. Their trained vines would find better purchase. The stone would no longer flake away under anchor pins as often. The roots, sensing stability, would thicken in useful directions, bracing weak lips of rock without being forced.

The stone-listeners would notice next. They would press their hands to a wall that had always felt like a held breath and find, beneath the tension, the beginning of a long exhale. They would not say the rock has been saved. They would say it has begun to age.

No one would call it a miracle. Not aloud. But after that, when a dangerous slab settles without cracking, when a ceiling shard falls harmlessly into a net already placed there by instinct, someone might murmur: the stone is being kind.

And the power, if they heard, would be satisfied.

---

Their second gift would be water, but not abundance.

Water in the hollow is too sacred to be made cheap. If a river burst through the wall, it would destroy the culture of the drip. It would wash away the careful gutters, the wick-threads, the household rights, the little ceremonies of collection. It would turn thirst into drowning. A wise power would know that to give too much is to take away the meaning of every careful hand.

So they would give a third water.

The settlement already knows two kinds: the water it collects from condensation, and the water it recycles through labor, breath, and machine. The third water would appear as something between a seep and a dew. It would begin in forgotten places: an unused cistern behind a filtration bed, a cracked jar beneath a disused stair, a hollow in the rock where no one has hung a collection cloth because the spot was thought barren.

At first it would be only a sheen. Then a bead. Then enough to fill a cup over the course of a long shift.

It would be clean, faintly mineral, cool in a way that surprises the mouth. It would not flow enough to bathe in. It would not flow enough to irrigate a new garden. It would not flow enough to end scarcity. But it would flow enough to become mercy.

The custom around it would have to be new.

Because it belongs to no household, no tier, no guild, no ancient right, it would be declared common by instinct before anyone speaks the word aloud. It would be for the sick first. For children next. For those who work closest to the heat. For strangers, if strangers ever come. For the plants that hold other plants in place. For the old who no longer collect their own drip but who once taught others how.

If someone tried to hoard it, the water would not punish them with magic. It would simply remain what it is: small. A hidden spring cannot satisfy greed. It can only satisfy need.

The power would perhaps attach one gentle law to it, not by commandment but by design. The third water would evaporate quickly if stored too long in closed vessels. It would taste stale if kept away from use. It would be best when drunk soon, shared soon, passed on. In this way, it would teach without preaching.

The people might eventually leave a cup beside its seep, not for themselves but for the next person. They might call it the Guest’s Cup, or the Quiet Drop, or simply the Common Bead. Some would say it is only a new vein of condensation. Others would say it is kindness given a mouth. The wise power would not care which story they tell, so long as the water is shared.

---

Their third gift would be air.

Not a cold wind. That would be alien and destructive. Not a storm of freshness that makes the settlement feel stale by comparison. Only a thread of relief.

The power would find or open a passage far above, one that does not admit outside cold or dust or danger, but allows a faint circulation where the heat has grown too oppressive. The air would move through the hollow in a slow diagonal, barely perceptible, touching the sleeping alcoves during the rest shifts, brushing the faces of those who work near boilers, carrying away the heaviest breath from crowded corners.

It would smell of clean stone and distant rain, though no one could say why.

This gift would be the least visible and perhaps the most beloved. It would not abolish the heat; the hollow would still be warm, still close, still a place where skin shines and voices soften. But the heat would cease to be a constant pressure against thought. Sleep would become deeper. Children would wake less restless. Engine-sitters would feel fewer headaches pressing behind their eyes. The air would still be shared, but it would feel less used.

In the deepest heat cycles, when the lower rock exhales and the upper gardens hang motionless, the thread of moving air would become a rumor of grace. People would pause near certain ladders or alcoves and say, “Stand here a moment. It breathes today.”

They would not know the power by name. They would know the relief.

---

Their fourth gift would be knowledge, but embodied.

A wise power would not simply place manuals in their hands or whisper engineering secrets into their ears. That would be too abrupt, too foreign. Instead, they would give the hollow a living instrument: something small, patient, and compatible with its ecology.

Perhaps it would be a lichen.

It would appear first on old iron: a pale green-gold crust no thicker than breath, growing where rust has begun to eat into supports. At first the root-tenders would think it invasive and prepare to scrape it away. But they would notice that where the lichen grows, the rust slows. It feeds on corrosion without weakening the metal beneath. It leaves behind a faint waxy residue that can be collected and used to seal small leaks or preserve wire. Most importantly, it changes color when something is wrong.

Near overheated cable, it would darken toward amber. Near bad air, near sulfur gathering in a pocket too low for people to notice, it would fade to blue-white. Near stone under strain, where vibration has begun to open hidden seams, it would crack into fine lines like a map.

The residents would learn it slowly. They would not trust it at first. Then they would. They would carry fragments of it to new places, training it like they train vines, pruning it when it grows too thick, feeding it with rust shavings and warm breath. Children would learn to read its colors before they learn to read the old pressure gauges. It would become another sense for the settlement, another way of knowing before disaster.
