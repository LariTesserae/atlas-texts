They ask for the right moment, and when the right moment comes, they bloom with an almost unbearable speed: a shimmer of cilia, a spreading of translucent frills, a slow pulsing along the pool floor as if the water itself had become a breathing skin. They feed, mate, divide, and encyst. When the pool disappears, they disappear with it, but not entirely. They leave behind black grains, sealed specks, patient instructions folded into mineral shells. They have made a covenant with impermanence.

Among such lives, there is one that has learned the plateau not as a map but as a grammar.

It is low to the ground, broad through the body, with skin the color of wet basalt and faint mineral glints where old spray has dried upon it. Along its flanks are folded membranes, thin and ribbed, which open slightly when the air is safe and press tight against its ribs when pressure turns wrong. Its eyes are small, dark, and shaded by a heavy brow, because sight is a lesser sense here. Its face is blunt and quiet, almost childlike in its lack of assertion. Its hands are wide, soft-padded, with fingers that can press a clay rim into exactness, feel a hairline tremor in bedrock, or cup water without spilling a drop.

It is not human, though it might share more with human loneliness than with human shape.

It lives in a hollow behind a long swell of red stone, where the wind is broken and the worst of the roar slides overhead like water over the back of a buried animal. Its shelter is not grand. A low windbreak of fitted stones keeps out the drifting grit. Grooves cut into the bedrock lead condensation into a small cistern cup. A row of clay resonators sits along the back wall, each no larger than a skull, each cracked in places, each still useful. When the weather changes, they sing faintly, each in its own thin voice, and the being listens with its whole body rather than with ears.

It has no name that can be spoken aloud. Its name is a pattern: two slow pressures, one quick release, repeated until stone answers.

It emerged, or hatched, or woke, in one of the wet intervals, long after whatever others of its kind had been scattered by storm, drought, or the slow closing of safe paths. It remembers them less as faces than as vibrations: a heavier step that made the ground reassuringly dull, a tapping rhythm that meant *water here*, a palm pressed beside its own when the world was too loud to bear. Now those vibrations are mostly gone. The plateau is so vast, and the being is so small, and the roar fills every distance with its indifferent fullness.

Still, it persists. It tends the cup plants. It harvests moss when the season permits. It repairs rope lines with fibers from soundgrass. It places black pebbles in cisterns to mark water levels. It taps the stone before crossing an open pan, waits for the answer, and if the answer is wrong, it returns to shelter and waits again. It does not rage against the plateau. It does not dream of conquering it. It has learned the deepest rule of the place: survival is not a triumph here. It is a rhythm kept with patience.

If such a being, and such a place, drew the attention of a benevolent and wise power, the first thing they would do is refrain from arrival in the ordinary sense.

They would not come as a voice louder than the land. They would not come as light splitting the sky, or as a figure towering above the basins, or as a commandment laid over the roar. That would be another violence. That would be only another pressure storm, only another domination wearing kindness as a cloak. A wise power would understand at once that this country already has its own integrity, its own law, its own terrible beauty. It would not treat the plateau as a problem to be solved.

They would begin by listening.

They would listen for a long time. They would learn the difference between the deep organ tones that mean rising fog and the sharp hollow notes that mean a pipe is about to vomit pressure. They would learn which sweetness is a warning, which silence is a trap, which echo returns altered because the rock has swallowed a call and chosen to answer differently. They would learn the being’s small grammar: the way it taps before drinking, the way it presses its flank to stone when a storm builds, the way it stills itself so completely that it becomes almost part of the ground.

Only then would they act.

And what they would do would be small, exact, and almost invisible.

First, they would give the gift of witness.

Not a vision. Not a voice. Not a burning sign. Only a pause shaped like attention.

The being would feel it first as a change in the pressure against its skin. The roar would not stop, but for a moment it would seem to gather itself around a still center, as if the whole enormous country had turned slightly toward the hollow where the being crouched. The water in a shallow pool would tremble, then smooth. The dust would settle into a pattern too deliberate to be random. The air would carry no scent of danger, no metallic sweetness, no warning of collapse. Instead there would be a sense of being met.

The being would lift its head. Its membranes would open a little.

It would not be afraid. That is important. A benevolent and wise power would not announce itself by adding terror to a world already difficult enough. The first sign of their attention would be safety recognized, not overwhelmed. The being would feel, in some old and wordless part of itself, that the immense was not entirely unseeing.

Then they would answer.

When the being tapped its pattern against the stone—two slow pressures, one quick release—the echo would return, but not identical. It would come back with a small difference: a warmth in the resonance, a steadiness, a spacing that felt intentional. The being would freeze. It would tap again. The answer would come again, not as speech, not as command, but as confirmation.
