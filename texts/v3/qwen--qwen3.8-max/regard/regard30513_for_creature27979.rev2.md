They would not arrive with the arrogance of dawn.

They would not split the cracked skin of the upper ground, nor send a pillar of white fire down through the root-channels, nor make the ceiling ring with a voice too large for the hollow to hold. A truly benevolent and wise power, coming upon such a place, would first understand one thing: this is not an empty room waiting for furniture. It is not a crude beginning asking to be lifted into a higher shape. It is already whole. It is already full. It has its own liturgy of heat and drip, its own patience, its own way of being holy without proclamation.

So they would do what powers seldom do when they are wise: they would diminish themselves.

They would come first as attention.

Not as presence, not yet. Attention is the gentler form of love. They would let their notice settle over the hollow the way a warm breath settles on cold stone: without weight, without demand. They would perceive the chamber not as a single scene but as a body made of many bodies. They would see the moss felts breathing. They would see the roots descending from the world above like slow, pale arms. They would see the mist-weavers’ nets beading with water, the heat-moths lifting into vent-columns, the basin-dwellers floating with their nostrils just above the surface, the predator moving like a hush through the undergrowth. They would see the warm-sitter in the polished seat, palm pressed to stone, reading the chamber through heat and pressure.

And they would understand that the being who lives there is not only the one who sits, but the entire congregation: the living surface, the threading hyphae, the insect glitter in the dark, the small deaths, the small births, the mineral sweat of the stone itself.

If they did anything at all, they would begin by refusing to disturb.

That refusal would be their first kindness.

They would not give the warm-sitter eyes, because eyes would ask the hollow to become a different kind of place. They would not give it fire, because fire would drink the moisture and turn the air into hunger. They would not give it metal, because metal would introduce cutting where there is only pressing, smoothing, holding. They would not give it a language of hard names, because the hollow already speaks in conditions: too hot, enough, rising, wait, still, come here, move a little. To impose words would be to make the silence feel poor. A wise power would not make a being ashamed of its own native fullness.

Instead, they would ask permission in the only way the hollow could recognize.

They would become a small change.

Perhaps a single drop of water, not falling but hesitating. Perhaps a thread of cooler air descending through a crack at a moment when no draft should have come. Perhaps one seam in the floor would ease its heat by the faintest degree, just enough to be noticed by the moss, by the mites, by the heat-pits of the warm-sitter. Not a miracle that breaks custom. A proposal. A question shaped like weather.

The chamber would feel it. Everything would feel it.

The mosses would lean toward the new dampness. The mist-weavers would turn their bodies along their silk. The warm-sitter would open its face toward the change, heat-pits widening, palms flattening against the stone. It would not worship the question. It would simply attend.

If the hollow contracted, if the lives there turned away from the offered difference as a body turns from a wrong taste, the power would withdraw. That too would be wisdom. But if the chamber accepted the change — if the drop fell and was drunk, if the cooler air became a gentle mixing, if the warm-sitter settled rather than fled — then the power would know it had been admitted into the place’s etiquette.

Then they would tend.

Not rule. Not redesign. Tend.

They would work at the scale of hairs and films, at the scale of pores and droplets, at the scale where a single blocked channel can make a basin sour and a single cooled crack can save a nursery. They would become almost indistinguishable from luck, except that their luck would be unusually tender and unusually precise.

Where a vent pulse threatened to rise too sharply, they would not stop it; the hollow needs its pulses. They would guide a little of the excess heat into an older channel, one already half-forgotten beneath the roots, so the pulse arrived as warmth and not as harm. Where a ceiling crack let in air too dry and dusty, they would encourage a fringe of moss to thicken there, not by creating it from nothing but by helping what already wanted to grow. Where a basin was beginning to foul because its water had grown still, they would not carve a new drain or send a sudden flood. They would persuade a root to shed a little more sweetness near its rim, drawing the smallest grazers, which would draw the fish, which would stir the surface just enough to let the basin breathe again.

They would repair without leaving the signature of repair.

A hairline fracture in the vault that might have widened over centuries would be softened by mineral deposition, sealed by slow skin after skin of living crust. A root-channel on the verge of collapse would be held by the gentle pressure of neighboring roots, guided into a living lattice before the collapse could become a wound. A place where the stone had become too hot for juveniles would develop a shallow cooler shelf beside it, not by dramatic carving but by the patient redirection of moisture and air. Nothing would look made. Everything would look as if it had always been so, only a little kinder.

This is what a wise power would know: in a place so intimate, a miracle is too loud. A blessing should be almost invisible. It should feel like continuation.

They would not abolish predation.

To abolish the velvet hunter would be to unmake part of the hollow’s balance. The predator is not evil for moving through the moss with its terrible stillness. It is one of the ways the chamber prevents itself from overflowing. But benevolence may still touch the manner of death.

The power would make the strike cleaner. The prey would not be caught in a long confusion of pain. Fear, where fear existed, would be brief and sharp, not a lingering poison. If a creature was already weak beyond recovery, the predator would find it before another, less clean ending did. If a death happened by accident — a fall, a choked channel, a pulse of heat too sudden — the power would ensure that the body did not waste. It would be taken into the moss, into the fungal cups, into the slow digestion of the hollow. Nothing would be treated as refuse. Even death would remain inside the intimacy of the place.

They would make dying less lonely, without pretending it was not dying.

When an old amphibian sank for the last time in its basin, the water would hold it without revulsion. When a heat-moth fell from a vent-column after its last flight, it would land where the moss could receive it. When the predator itself grew too slow to hunt and curled into a final crack, the hush around it would not be abandonment. It would be a chamber acknowledging one of its own ways of being.

And when a warm-sitter aged, the power would be especially gentle.

They would not try to make the warm-sitter immortal if immortality would break the hollow. Some beings are meant to pass through a place like breath through a throat. To hold the breath forever is not kindness. But they would let the aging one feel that its sitting had mattered. They would let the polished seat remember its weight. They would let the stone carry the warmth of all the palms that had ever pressed there, so the last sitter would feel, in its own blind skin, that it was not alone in its posture. It would be sitting with every one who had sat before.

That, perhaps, would be one of the power’s most secret gifts.

Not a voice from above. Not a vision. Not a commandment. Only this: in the deepest part of the chamber, when the warm-sitter settled into the worn seat and pressed its palms flat against the stone, it would feel a pressure answer back. Not as a hand exactly. Not as a face. But as a gathered warmth. A heldness. The sense that the whole hollow — moss, root, web, shell, basin, spore, predator, drip, and all — had momentarily leaned inward around it.

The warm-sitter would not need to understand the power as a person. It would only know that the chamber was listening with it, and that its own listening had been met.

If the power wished to communicate, they would do so in the grammar already present.

A double tap on stone, but softer than usual. A trembling drag along a root, carrying not alarm but reassurance. A change in humidity that meant enough. A pulse of mineral-sweet air that meant safe. A slow warmth spreading through the seat that meant remain. They would not introduce new signs unless the hollow could bear them. They would not say I am here in a way that frightened the being into feeling small. They would say it as water says it to a waiting mouth.

And if the warm-sitter answered — one palm pressing, then the other; a low throat-vibration against the basin wall; a breath released into the heat-pits of its own face — the power would receive that answer as prayer enough.

They would not ask for worship.

That is important. A power that needs worship in such a place has not understood the place. The hollow has no altar, no cut image, no fired vessel, no raised platform. Its arrangement is the residue of bodies returning to comfort, not the construction of hierarchy. To introduce an altar would be to introduce distance: someone above, someone below, someone begging. The wise power would refuse all of that. If the warm-sitter polished the seat further by returning, that would be devotion enough. If the stones around a warm crack became a ring by habit, that would be offering enough. If a shell or seed-case was placed in a shallow depression without purpose, that would be ceremony enough.

The power would not want a temple. It would want the hollow to remain a home.

They would also protect it.

Not by making it invulnerable, because invulnerability is another kind of death. But by keeping it from being crushed before its time.

If the upper ground trembled with the weight of careless feet, the power would make the roots hold. If water from above carried poison toward the chamber, they would bind it in clay or turn it into another channel before it reached the living walls. If some surface-digger broke through the ceiling with tools and light, the power would not necessarily strike them down; they might instead make the intruder uneasy. The air would feel too close. The glow would seem too fragile. The hush would press against the visitor’s chest like a warning made of tenderness. They would feel, without knowing why, that they had entered a place where it was better to kneel than to take. Most would leave. Some would forget they had ever found it. That forgetting, too, could be a blessing.

If fire came near, the power would make the stone sweat. If drought pressed the world above, they would deepen the root-channels’ memory of water. If cold reached down, they would stir the lower seams just enough to keep the hollow from losing its warmth. They would not make the chamber immune to all change; they would make it resilient. There is a difference. Immunity denies the world. Resilience allows a place to meet the world without being destroyed by it.

And if another warm-sitter were needed, they would not force one into being.

If the hollow could support more than one, they would ease the finding. A juvenile might follow a warmer root into a recess it would have missed. Two bodies might meet at a basin without alarm because the air between them had been made slightly sweeter. If the species of that place reproduced by division, budding, spore, or some slow intimate budding from the sitter’s own flesh, the power would bless the process with the right heat, the right humidity, the right quiet. But they would not create a companion as a gift for loneliness if companionship would strain the balance. Wisdom does not give what the beloved cannot carry.

If the warm-sitter remained one, they would make its solitude large enough not to be lonely.

That is a subtle kindness. To be alone without being severed. To be singular without being abandoned. The power would let the warm-sitter feel itself held by the whole congregation. The chamber would become, in its own way, a companion: the drip answering the mouth, the heat answering the palm, the mist answering the skin. The being would not need another of its kind every moment if every moment was already full of relatedness.

They might leave one sign, and only one.

Not a burning bush. Not a stone tablet. Not a mark that said this place belongs to me. A wise and benevolent power would never claim the hollow as property. If they left a sign, it would be small enough to be mistaken for custom.

A stone ring that stayed warm longer than it should.

A fungal cup that filled first whenever the ceiling sweated.

A thread of mist-weaver silk that held a single drop without falling until the thirstiest creature passed beneath it.

A faint blue-green brightening in the moss when the chamber’s balance was threatened, not to command but to show where attention was needed.

Or this: in the polished seat, when no sitter occupied it, the stone would remain just slightly warmer than the surrounding rock, as if someone had recently risen and gone only a little way off. No one would worship the seat because of that. They would simply return to it more willingly. The sign would not say, “Fear me.” It would say, “You are known.”

That is all such a power would want to say.

They would also bless the small lives, not just the one nearest personhood.

They would bless the mist-weavers by making their silk less brittle in dry spells. They would bless the heat-moths by keeping the vent-columns steady enough for mating flight. They would bless the snails by leaving films for them to graze. They would bless the beetles by softening the dead fiber they eat. They would bless the roots by helping them find the seams where moisture gathers. They would bless the moss by making it capable of holding one more drop than it otherwise could. They would bless the bacterial veils by keeping them from souring. They would bless the predator by giving it aim. They would bless the prey by giving it swiftness or a quick end. They would bless the larvae by making their work clean. They would bless the fish by keeping the deeper basins breathable. They would bless the fungal shelves by letting their glow remain faint, because faintness is part of the hollow’s dignity.

In short, they would bless the web, not one thread only.

And because they were wise, they would know when not to act.

There would be times when the chamber needed to find its own new balance. A vent might shift. A root might die. A basin might dry. A generation of moths might fail. A predator might go hungry. The power would not rush to correct every tremor. They would distinguish between harm and change. Suffering can sometimes be healed; change cannot be abolished. To heal everything would be to freeze the hollow into a picture. To let it change is to let it remain alive.

So sometimes they would do nothing but witness.

Even that would be a deed. In a place so enclosed, to be witnessed is not small. It means that the hollow’s beauty is not wasted simply because few eyes can bear its closeness. It means that its patience is seen. It means that the warm-sitter’s long sitting is not a mere habit without meaning. It means that the repetition — the smoothed grooves, the gathered stones, the placed shells, the returning to the same warmth — is not stupid persistence. It is a form of faith.

The power would honor that.

They would not make the being greater than its world. They would make its world faithful enough to hold it.

If the hollow ever came close to collapse, then yes, the power might act more openly. But even then they would act through the place’s own nature. They would not suspend the chamber in impossible light. They would make one root hold one stone one hour longer than expected. They would make one seam release pressure before it burst. They would make the warm-sitter move to a safer recess without knowing why. They would make the animals gather where the air was still survivable. They would save the hollow not by breaking it but by helping it remember how to survive.

And after danger passed, they would not demand gratitude.

They would simply let the chamber exhale. The mist would lift. The moss would swell. The basins would refill. The warm-sitter would return to the seat. The predator would shift in its crack. The moths would rise again. The power, if one could say they remained, would remain as the chamber’s own recovered breath.

In the end, the greatest thing they would do is this:

They would make the hollow more itself.

Not more glorious. Not more productive. Not more useful to some outside measure. More itself. Hotter where warmth was needed, but not cruelly hot. Damp enough to keep every skin supple, but not so drowned that breath became struggle. Dim enough for the glow-moss and insect-light to matter, but not so dark that the chamber lost its sense of being seen. Crowded enough for fullness, but not so crowded that tenderness became impossible. Still enough for listening, but not so still that life forgot how to move.

They would give the place one invisible gift above all: continuity.

The feeling that the next pulse would come. The next rain. The next breath. The next sitting. The next small life beading on a thread. The next drop finding a mouth. The next body pressing into moss and stone and being received.

And the warm-sitter, sitting in its worn place, would know without knowing how that it was not merely tolerated by the dark. It was beloved by it.

That would be enough.

If the power wished to be remembered, they would not carve a name. They would leave a condition. The air would taste faintly sweet after a danger passed. The seat would be warm when no one had sat there. A crack would glow for a moment with harmless blue-green light when the chamber was thirsty. A drop would hang from a web exactly where it was needed. The hollow would not say, “Someone is here.” It would say, “Here is held.”

And if the warm-sitter ever sensed the power directly — not as a shape, not as a voice, but as a pressure of care against its heat-pits — it would do what its nature taught it to do.

It would be still.

It would open its face toward the warmth.

It would press one palm to the stone, then the other.

And the chamber, full of its many-bodied life, would answer.
