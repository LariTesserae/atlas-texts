If that sunken, thirsty-less place had drawn the attention of a benevolent and wise power, the first thing the power would do is nothing that the hollow had not asked for.

It would not send rain.

That is the first and hardest kindness. A lesser goodness, seeing a land without water, would weep and weeping would become flood. A lesser wisdom, seeing life sustained by wax, resin, dust, oil, and patient metabolism, would call it suffering and try to correct it. But a benevolent power that is also wise would understand that absence is not always wound. Some absences are architecture. Some laws are not prisons but shapes. To give the hollow rain would be to unmake it. The salt would dissolve. The resin would cloud. The powdered brick would slump. The dust-swimmers would fall from their medium. The thread spiders’ stitching would fail. The small dry bodies would panic or perish. The place would become another place, and that place, however green by ordinary standards, would be a violence done to this one.

So the power would begin with restraint.

It would arrive, if it arrived at all, not as weather, not as a voice from above, not as a hand breaking through the lattice, not as a golden figure demanding recognition. It would arrive as a change in attention.

The light would pause a little differently.

A shadow would lean toward the center without darkening anything.

The still air would become not heavier, but more listened-to.

Somewhere near the dry fountain or altar or nest-at-the-heart, a tile would become one degree warmer, as though a hand had rested there long ago and left the warmth behind.

That would be the first sign: not spectacle, but a deepening of the hollow’s own quiet.

The power would observe.

It would look at the mosaic floor, the broken and re-fitted things, the hand-shaped clay tiles, the bone fragments, the salt set in resin, the cords, the copper, the pegs, the worn ramps, the little carpentry-made-to-be-landscape. It would see that everything had been touched, placed, shaped, repaired. It would see that the place was not a ruin pretending to be a garden, nor a garden pretending to be a ruin, but a third thing: a made world that had learned to live.

It would notice the plants: the wax-rosettes, the salt-cushions, the resin-vines, the oil-pods, the fan-leaves, the hollow reeds. It would see how they did not long for rain. It would see how they leaned toward the lattice-light, how they rooted into joints, how they bound what needed binding, covered what needed covering, shed what needed shedding. It would understand that they were not merely inhabitants but citizens, workers, menders, slow green hands.

It would notice the insects: the pottery beetles crossing the ledges with their careful legs, the parchment moths counting warmth by tiny openings of their wings, the resin-feeders carrying amber beads, the tile ants placing grains like prayers, the thread spiders sewing cracks shut. It would notice the lizards with painted-clay scales, the small warm-bodied nesters in the upper tangle, the dust-swimmers moving through particulate light as though dryness itself could be a sea.

And if the power looked for the being who lived there, it would not find a single monarch, no hidden god crouched behind a wall, no keeper with a face large enough to answer all questions. It would find a congregation. A commonwealth. A being made of many beings. The hollow itself would be the body; the plants and animals would be its thoughts and habits; the small figures of fiber, clay, copper, beetle-shell, and resin-dark eyes would be its gestures.

The wise power would not be disappointed by this. It would not demand a king. It would not ask, “Which one of you is in charge?” It would recognize that some lives are distributed, some selves are plural, some persons are places.

So it would address the hollow as a person.

Not loudly.

Not from above.

It would make itself small.

Perhaps it would take a form suited to the place: no larger than a hand, or perhaps no larger than a finger. A figure of smoothed clay and old fiber, with copper at its shoulders and bone at its hands. Its face would be simple, maybe a smooth mask with two resin-dark eyes and a shallow groove where a mouth might be, because in that hollow speech would be too coarse an instrument. It would sit at the edge of a terrace, near a crack where salt-cushions grew, and it would wait.

It would not announce itself.

It would let the hollow decide whether it was a guest or a trick.

The tile ants would pass near it, adjusting their paths by a fraction. A pottery beetle might stop, antennae moving slowly, then continue. A parchment moth might open and close its wings above it as if reading the warmth. A dust-swimmer might circle once through a shaft of light near its head, leaving a trail of bright motes that closed behind it like a sentence not meant to be remembered.

If the hollow did not welcome the power, the power would leave.

That is what wisdom would require. Even benevolence must be invited.

But the hollow, being full of slow life, might permit it. Permission would not be spoken. It would happen in small signs: a path opening where there had been a screen of stems; a seedcase splitting nearby without alarm; one of the small masked figures appearing on a ledge and tapping once on a tile; a thread spider weaving a short, unfinished bridge toward the visitor; the large terrace-backed creature shifting in its sleep, if it slept, in such a way that a warm space opened near its side.

Then the power would begin.

Not with miracles.

With listening.

It would learn the language of the hollow. It would learn that tapping on tile could mean: this is stable, this is loose, this is mine, this is ours, this is ready, this is not yet. It would learn that the scent of warmed resin could mean: safe, gathered, remembered. It would learn that the arrangement of seeds could mean: here, later, enough. It would learn that a breathy note from a small furred or feathered creature could mean: air changed, food opened, stranger present, no harm, no harm.

It would learn the law:

Nothing is natural, but everything is alive anyway.  
Nothing requires water, yet everything is sustained.  
Everything made can be overgrown, but nothing overgrown ceases to be made.  
Small things matter more than large things.  
Silence is not absence but pressure.  
Movement is permitted if it is slow.  
Distance is negotiable, but desire makes it stubborn.  
If you seek a center, the place will give you several.  
If you look for the builders, you will find only their marks, and the suspicion that the place is still being built.

And then, having learned, the power would ask.

It would ask without words. It would ask by placing a single grain of powdered brick at the base of the dry fountain and waiting to see whether the tile ants accepted it. It would ask by warming one joint in a cracked wall and observing whether the roots moved toward it. It would ask by letting a mote of light rest on one of the small figures, not touching it, only near it, and seeing whether the figure turned away or remained.

The question would be: May I help?

The answer would come as the hollow always answered: by continuing.

A tile ant would take the grain. A root would enter the warmed joint. The small figure would remain in the light. The dust-swimmers would drift closer. The pottery beetle would cross the power’s foot, if it had a foot, with no more fear than it would give a stone.

That would mean: yes, but gently.

The power would understand. It would not remake. It would not enlarge. It would not make the hollow louder, wetter, brighter, or easier to comprehend. It would make the hollow more fully itself.

What would it do, then?

It would do almost nothing.

And that almost nothing would be enough to become legend, if legends were welcome there. But legends are too large for such a place. So it would become habit.

First, it would strengthen the law of the hollow.

It would set no visible seal, no shining boundary, no wall of force. Instead, it would make the hollow’s own rules more durable. Water would not enter by accident. If a storm outside tried to send its wetness down into the sunken place, the lattice and skins and horn-plates would shed it in such a way that not a drop fell where it could harm. If a careless traveler carried moisture in on their clothes, it would dry at the threshold, gently, without them knowing. If someone brought water with malice, intending to drown the dry world as a test or a punishment, the distance to the hollow would become stubborn. The entrance would shrink to the size of a coin. The path would loop. The would-be intruder would forget what they had come to do, not because their mind was stolen, but because their intention would become too heavy to carry through so quiet a door.

The hollow would remain open to reverence.

It would close to appetite.

Second, the power would make repair easier.

Not effortless. Effort belonged to the place. The tile ants needed their carrying. The spiders needed their stitching. The plants needed their slow leaning. The pottery beetles needed their patient cleaning. Work was not a curse there; it was worship without prayer. So the power would not remove labor. It would harmonize it.

A grain carried by an ant would fit the crack it was meant for one attempt sooner. A cord under tension would not snap without warning; it would give a tiny creak in time for attention. A resin bead would swell where a seam needed sealing. A root would find a joint before the joint became a wound. A shed beetle-shell would fall in such a place that it could become a tile, not debris. A dead leaf would crisp and part along lines that made it easy to carry away. A broken wall would not hide its weakness; it would reveal itself in the language of the hollow: a leaning stem, a change in dust, a moth resting exactly where the crack began.

The power would not be a builder above the builders. It would be the slightest luck in favor of care.

Third, it would notice small distresses that no large providence would see.

A young creature in the upper tangle might fail to find a hollow stem before the air cooled. The power would not carry it, but a fan-leaf might bend in such a way that the creature remembered a nearby shelter.

A parchment moth might become tangled in a fiber not meant for trapping. The power would not tear the fiber, but the moth’s wing would find the one motion that loosened it.
