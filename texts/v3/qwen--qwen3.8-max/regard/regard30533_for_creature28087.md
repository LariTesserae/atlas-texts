A benevolent and wise power would not arrive as a glare, or a voice, or a hand reaching down through the canopy. They would arrive first as a change in listening. The air would become no warmer, but steadier. The mist would stand a little more attentively in its columns. The dripping would not change rhythm so much as reveal that it had always possessed one. Even the low thump from deep in the plant mass would seem, for a moment, less solitary.

Their first act would be restraint.

Not because they were indifferent, but because they would understand at once what kind of place this is: not a ruin to be restored, not a machine to be restarted, not a garden to be improved, not a sanctuary to be made legible to visitors. It is a finished small world, still becoming itself. It has its own grammar. It has its own dignity. It has its own way of holding time in solution, like moisture held in moss. A lesser kindness might try to help by clearing, draining, brightening, ordering, rescuing. A wise kindness would know that some forms of help are indistinguishable from erasure.

So they would stand outside the basin’s edge, if a power can be said to stand anywhere, and they would listen until they could hear the difference between the basin’s ordinary noises and its needs.

They would hear the water first. They would learn which drips are idle and which are signals. They would learn what it means when a leaf-valve opens too soon, when a root creaks under tension, when steam thins into a whistle, when the flywheel’s hum catches on a burr of rust. They would notice the places where the sound is slightly wrong: a note too bright, a pause too long, a silence where a settling should be. They would not correct these things immediately. They would wait to see whether the Green Fitter already knows them, and whether the Fitter intends to answer them in its own time.

Because the Green Fitter is not a pet, not a servant, not a minor deity to be promoted, not a malfunction to be upgraded. It is a keeper. It has earned its basin.

If the wise power has a heart, it would be moved by that. Not to pity, exactly, but to recognition. Here is something that has taken abandoned work and made it holy without demanding an audience. Here is something that repairs not for profit, not for praise, but because repair has become its way of being alive. Here is something that remembers tools, valves, gauges, fittings, and has translated that memory into roots, bark, condensation, and slow pressure. A benevolent power would honor such a being by treating it as an equal, or at least as a colleague in the long craft of keeping.

Then they would ask.

They would ask in the only language the basin respects: sequence.

A drop would fall into a hollow seed-case at a rhythm not quite native to the place. Not louder. Not faster. Just placed. Drip, pause, drip. Then another pause longer than chance. The sound would be a question shaped like a knock.

The Green Fitter would go still. Or seem to. A person watching might see only a mound of moss and corrugated metal, but the power would notice the subtle redistribution of attention: the root-fingers loosening, the ceramic lenses gathering a fresh film of water, the needle in the brass socket trembling toward a new point of balance. The basin itself would listen through the Fitter.

The Fitter would not answer with speech. It would answer with permission.

A leaf-valve would open a fraction. A bead of condensation would fall from a copper pipe and strike an old gauge face, making a small bell-note. A root-tip would move near the place where the question had sounded, not grasping, only present. That would be enough. That would mean: you may fit yourself here, if you can fit gently.

If there were no answer, the wise power would withdraw without offense. They would leave only the faintest blessing behind: a cooler night air above the canopy, a cleaner seep in one poisoned seam, a human foot turned aside by an unaccountable reluctance to enter. They would not force the basin to receive them. They would know that a closed world has the right to remain closed.

But if they were admitted, even by so small a sign, they would begin to act.

They would act in the idiom of the place.

They would not appear in glory. They would appear, if they appeared at all, as a subtle competence. A patch of moss would thicken where a pipe had grown too hot. A root would find an old crack before steam widened it. A fallen seed-case would lodge itself under a loose plank and make it firm again. A drip would begin falling into a forgotten ceramic fragment, producing a note that completes a chord the basin had been trying to sound for centuries.

Their first real work would be to find the hidden injuries.

Every such place has them. A pocket of old oil still bleeding resin into the root mass. A seam where mineral water has grown too acidic. A buried metal edge too sharp for roots to grow over. A vessel whose warmth has become a slow fever. A pool where the water has begun to sour, not through fault but through stagnation. The basin tolerates these things because it is patient, not because they are good.

The wise power would not condemn the basin for its injuries. They would not say: this is wrong, this must be removed, this does not belong. They would understand that the basin has already made what it can out of what was left behind. So they would help without humiliating.

They would draw the old poison into a patient root and seal it inside a knot of wood, where it could no longer enter the water. They might leave a dark burl there afterward, smooth and dense, so the basin remembers without being harmed. They would cool the overheated vessel by guiding a thread of groundwater near it, not quenching it, only giving it a margin. They would soften the sharp metal edge by encouraging moss to grow across it, layer by layer, until it became a seam rather than a blade. They would open a tiny channel to the sour pool, no wider than a reed, so fresh condensation could enter and the pool could breathe.

None of these acts would look like miracles. They would look like luck. They would look like nature finally taking its next step.
