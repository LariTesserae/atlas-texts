**The Kind Attention**

A benevolent and wise power would not begin by opening the Pocket Furnace.

They would not tear the mountain into a view, nor send rain to make a story of greenness where greenness has never been welcome. They would understand that a place can be holy because it is narrow, and that a creature can be faithful because it has no elsewhere. To force the hollow outward would be no kindness. It would be only another appetite wearing the mask of mercy.

So they would first do the hardest thing: they would attend without claiming.

They would let their attention fall very lightly into the basin, lighter than dust, cooler than the shadow of a bird, but not cold in the way the Cinder-Tender fears. It would arrive as a subtle difference in the heat: a place where the rock is warm without being hungry, where the air is still without being sealed. The Cinder-Tender would feel it at once. It would stop among its ash-nodules, lift its pitted face, and know that some enormous thing had leaned near without pressing.

Then they would ask, in the only language the place understands—the language of gradients—whether the creature is kept or keeping.

This question would not be a voice. It would be a pause in the noon whine, a moment when the kiln in the creature’s chest burns evenly, neither starving nor overflowing. The Cinder-Tender would not answer with words. It would answer by unfolding one limb more fully than usual, by touching the new temperate spot again, by returning to the secret coal and waiting there as if to see whether the coal too has heard.

The wise power would hear that answer.

They would see that the Cinder-Tender is not evil, nor merely pitiful. It is a liturgy. It is a scar that has become an office. It guards heat because it was made in a time when the mountain could not afford to lose any. It makes crude glass beetles and pointing fingers because even confinement, if it lasts long enough, develops a longing for form. And they would see the ember beneath the hollow—the breathing coal—and they would not seize it, nor name it, nor judge it. They would only recognize that here is the point where devotion has become indistinguishable from captivity.

A lesser benevolence might have tried to save the creature from its smallness.

A wise one would try to save the smallness from becoming a trap.

So they would leave the Pocket Furnace as it is, almost.

They would give three gifts. Or perhaps four, depending on how one counts silence.

First gift: coolness that does not wound.

At the edge of the hollow, just inside the threshold, they would place a stone no larger than a sleeping bird’s heart. It would not be wet. It would not be green. It would not smell of sap or root or patient rain. It would be a bead of mineral darkness, a kind of cold coal, made from compressed night and old starlight and the memory of high places where heat has never had to defend itself.
