If such a power noticed that basin, the first thing it would do is not arrive.

Not in the way powers are usually imagined: not as a light too strong for the eyes, not as a voice from above, not as a hand reaching in to rearrange the little working world below. A benevolent and wise power would know better than to flood a place built to gather water one drop at a time. It would know that sudden glory could scorch the moss, scatter the birds, frighten the children, and make the sluice-listeners flinch away from the pipes. It would understand that this town was not asking for a miracle. It was asking, if it asked at all, for the chance to continue.

So the power would come quietly.

It would come, if it came, as a slight improvement in ordinary things. A little more light on wet stone. A little less chill in the shade where a worker leaned to listen to a valve. A rag found exactly when a gauge fogged. A hand placed on a railing just before the foot slipped. A bird calling from a dangerous crack in the rock-face as if by accident. A dream given to a mason at the right hour. A child pausing for no reason beside a loose chip of stone and saying, in the short clear way of basin children, “This one’s wrong.”

That would be the style of such a power. Not spectacle. Adjustment. Not ownership. Attention.

It would see the being who lived there not as one creature but as many. It would see the basin-born in their waxed jackets and damp cuffs, moving with unhurried certainty between tanks and terraces. It would see the tank wardens with their polished rods, the net-keepers tasting the air, the gauge-wipers with their cloths, the wire-wardens with their careful hands, the masons watching the young rock for betrayal. It would see the children learning which gratings were safe. It would see the cats by the boiler platforms, the herons by the overflow pools, the frogs in the lower gutters, the dippers along the channels, the rats in the seams, the moths at the lamps, the eel-like shadows moving through the dark conduits. It would see moss and algae, fern and watercress, reed and willow-seedling taking root where warm metal met cool air.

If forced to choose a single being, the power would perhaps choose none. Or it would choose all of them together as one living body: the listening ecology of the basin, human and otherwise, gathered around the slow work of turning mist into life without turning life into a machine.

And because it was wise, it would begin by refusing several temptations.

It would refuse to make the town perfect.

Perfection would be a kind of cruelty there. The place had been made by maintenance, and maintenance required something to tend. If every pipe ran true forever, every net stayed clean, every stone stayed fixed, every frog sang without warning of change, the basin-born would lose the skill that made them who they were. Their dignity was not in ease. It was in attention. It was in the quarter turn given at the right moment, the gauge wiped before danger hid behind fog, the channel cleared before overflow became damage. A wise benevolence would not steal that from them.

It would refuse to silence the town.
