**The Kind Attention**

They gather around it, pressing the butts of their staffs into the dark margin of the sour patch, listening with the whole architecture of their bodies. Some kneel. Some stand with their toes parted in the felt. The elders are quietest. They do not speak until the ground has told them something.

If a benevolent and wise power had turned its attention toward the High Sward, this is likely where it would have begun: not with the grandeur of the horizon, not with the astonishing noise of the root-stridulators, not even with the slow breathing of the corkbacks, but with a small circle of people trying to heal a wound in the living world.

And the first thing such a power would do is not act.

It would listen.

A truly kind power, if it were also truly wise, would understand that some places do not need to be improved. They need to be protected from being loved badly. They need room. They need time. They need the continuance of their own shape.

So it would arrive without arrival.

There would be no descent of fire, no throne in the clouds, no voice that split the sky and made the Softstep fall flat in terror. The High Sward already had enough overwhelming sound. It did not need a god shouting into it. The power would come instead as a quality of attention: a stillness under the drone, a deepening of the ordinary light, a sense among the Softstep that the plain was suddenly more present to itself.

Perhaps the first sign would be very small.

A child standing at the edge of the sour patch would feel a new warmth through the soles of her feet. Not a command. Not a vision. Only a question, shaped like pressure:

*Where does it need to breathe?*

She would tilt her head. Her staff would rest against the sward. She would feel, under the usual vibration, a blockedness—a place where fermentation had folded inward, where the felt had become too tight and wet and closed. She would not know why she knew this, except that it felt remembered. She would tap once, twice, then hum low in her chest. An elder would look at her. She would point. They would open a narrow channel there, parting the matted fibers with their fingers and the rounded tips of their staffs, not cutting, only persuading. A little gas would escape from the dark ground. The smell would turn from rot to green sourness, then to something almost sweet. The patch would still need tending, but it would no longer be closing around its own sickness.

The power would have done almost nothing.

It would have merely helped them hear what they were already near enough to know.

That would be its way.

If such a power looked across the High Sward and saw the sward itself—the ancient, continent-wide body of root and fiber and seed—it would not try to wake it into a human kind of mind. It would not give the plain speech like a person’s speech, or eyes like a person’s eyes. It would recognize that the sward already had a being, only not a hurried one. It had the awareness of long patience. It knew pressure, thirst, warmth, injury, repair. It knew when rain had come and when too many small mouths were feeding in one place and when a herd of corkbacks had passed like slow weather over its back. It did not need to become something else in order to deserve care.

The power would say, if it spoke at all, something like this:

*You have made yourself into a world.  
I will not unmake you by making you more.*

And that refusal would be a blessing.

Because a lesser kindness might try to give the High Sward stone, or rivers, or towers. It might wish to make the place easier for travelers, easier for empires, easier to admire from a distance. It might plant trees for shade, or dig wells, or set great shining monuments at the rim to mark its wonder. But that would be a kindness that loved its own generosity more than the place itself. A wise power would see that the High Sward’s perfection lay in its strange, finished plainness. It would add nothing that made the sward into a spectacle. It would add nothing that required the Softstep to become something other than what they had become.

It would protect the conditions of their becoming.

That is where the work would begin.

First, fire.

Fire was the great danger of the High Sward. Not hunger, not cold, not predators. Fire. One careless lightning strike in a dry season, one wandering spark carried by wind across the living felt, and the plain could become a field of smoke and ash. The power would know this. It would not remove lightning from the sky altogether, because the place still needed storms, and storms had their own wild honesty. But it would give the clouds a longer memory. It would teach the air above the plain to carry moisture a little more faithfully. When dry seasons lengthened, it would gather the white seas below the rim and lift them gently, not into violence, but into broad, dull rain that came without wrath.

If lightning did fall, the power would not stop it with a wall of miracles. It would make the ground quicker to smother. It would guide the Softstep in their sleep, so that before dawn one of them would wake with the feeling of needing to walk a certain stretch of plain. They would find a blackened pinch of fiber there, still breathing a thin smoke, and cover it with damp felt, and sing it cool. No one would call this a miracle. They would say they had been lucky. The power would be content with that.

It would not need to be thanked.

It would also attend to the pools.

The clickers needed the shallow basins to fill. The whistle-stems needed wet margins. The burstwings needed the sudden flush of larvae and pollen after rain. But the High Sward was not a place that could be forced into abundance. If the power made the pools too deep, the root-skin would soften and fail. If it made them too permanent, they would grow stagnant and heavy. If it made the rains too regular, the plain would lose the rhythm of waiting and release that made it alive.

So the power would do something more delicate than increasing water. It would improve timing.

It would let the rains come when the seed-cases were ready to loosen. It would let the pools dry just enough to keep them clean. It would let the wet and dry seasons remain seasons, not certainties, so the Softstep would still need to move, still need to listen, still need one another. A wise power would know that a life without difficulty would not free the Softstep; it would erase them. Their wisdom lived in their attention. Remove the need for attention, and you remove the people.

It would not do that.

It would give them work worth doing, and then help them do it well.

The corkbacks would receive a particular tenderness from the power, because they were so easy to misunderstand. They were not dramatic. They did not soar or sing or shine. They were great, slow, breathing mounds of warm flesh and living thatch, cropping the sward with their wide mouths, making the plain open by being themselves. A foolish protector might see them as merely useful and try to increase their numbers until the plain was crowded with them. A wise power would see that their value lay in their balance. It would keep their herds healthy without letting them become a pressure on the ground. It would make their dung rich with seed and filament. It would ease their aging, not by preventing death, but by letting their bodies pass into the sward gently when their time came.

When a corkback died, the power would not make the event seem holy in a way that separated it from ordinary life. It would simply ensure that the body returned well.

The Softstep would find it, perhaps, after several days: a low shape no longer breathing, already being entered by fine pale roots and small careful creatures. They would not take much from it. They would stand around it and hum a low rhythm, two light footfalls, a pause, a chest-note. They would thank it for grazing the world open. Then they would move away, and the sward would fold over the body like a quilt. Within a few seasons there would be a slightly raised place, richer in growth, where seedlings sprouted more quickly after rain. The children would learn to step lightly there.

The power would make such places quiet rather than terrible.

It would do the same for the bladderkites.

Those slow aerial beings already seemed almost like prayers of the High Sward made visible. They drifted in the warm columns under cumulus towers, their translucent bladders filled with ferment and heat, their trailing rootlets combing the air. They belonged to the sky, but they also belonged to the plain, because when they fell, the plain received them. The power would love them, perhaps, in the way one loves things that are fragile and enormous at once. It would not make them unable to fall. That would change what they were. But it would make their falling matter.

When a bladderkite settled heavy with rain and could not rise, the power would let the sward recognize it quickly. The trailing filaments would be drawn down into the felt with less struggle. The soft body would not rot badly; it would sweeten. The place where it lay would become a sky-gift, yes, but also a kind of library. The wind would move through the richer growth there with a slightly different whistle, carrying a memory of altitude. The Softstep would know those places. They would go there when they needed to remember that the sky and the ground were not enemies.

Sometimes, if a child was grieving and could not say why, the child would be drawn to a sky-gift and lie down in its warmer nap, and feel, without words, that what fell was not lost.

That would be one of the power’s quietest gifts.

It would also have to think about hunger.

The thatch-stalkers were hunters. They were not cruel, but they needed to kill. They kept the small fliers from becoming too thick. They made channels in the felt by moving through it. They fed their young with smothered burstwings and other delicate prey. A sentimental power might try to end their hunger. A wise power would not. It would understand that the High Sward’s peace was not the absence of killing. It was the presence of balance. The power would ensure only this: that the thatch-stalkers did not hunt beyond the plain’s ability to replace what was taken, and that their hunger did not become a spreading emptiness.

If their numbers thinned too much, the small fliers would rise into excess and damage the seed-cases. If their numbers grew too great, the burstwings would vanish from the afternoon light, and the plain would lose one of its sudden joys. So the power would watch the margins. It would nudge nothing directly, except perhaps the weather in ways that favored balance. A warm dry spell here, a gentle rain there. A season of many larvae. A season of fewer. The Softstep would notice these changes and adjust their paths. They would not know they were being helped. They would only say, “The plain is asking us to go east this year,” or “The pools want opening in the south.”

And that would be enough.

The power would pay longest attention to the Softstep themselves.

Not because they were the most important beings on the High Sward. They were not. The sward was older and greater than they were. But they were the part of the plain that could choose. The corkbacks grazed because they were corkbacks. The root-stridulators made sound because their bodies were made to make it. The bladderkites drifted because the air carried them. The Softstep alone could decide how to belong. They could have tried to dominate the place. They could have dug, burned, fenced, hoarded, multiplied without listening. They had not. They had learned instead to walk softly, to hear through their feet, to make camps that dreamed themselves away by morning, to take only what kept the whole alive.

That moral achievement would move the power more than any miracle could.

It would see that the Softstep had already built a civilization without building against the world. Their culture was not written in stone but in habits of restraint. Their history was stored in routes, songs, staff-rhythms, and the memory of healed places. Their wealth was the continuing health of the plain. That kind of people are rare. A wise power would not reward them by making them rich in the way of lowland kingdoms. It would not give them gold, because gold would teach them to look down. It would not give them armies, because armies would teach them to look outward with fear. It would not give them prophecy, because prophecy would make them anxious for futures that had not yet asked to be born.

It would give them better listening.

This gift would not arrive as a shining object. It would come into their lives in small ways.

A staff made from a fallen whistle-stem would carry vibration a little farther than expected. An elder pressing her ear to the ground would hear not only the passage of a corkback herd but the faint pre-tremor of a storm still beyond the rim. A band standing in a listening circle would find their foot-taps arriving together with strange ease, as if the felt itself were helping the rhythm travel. Children would learn the ground-language faster than before, not because they were forced, but because the plain seemed willing to speak back.

At night, when the bands camped in their shallow sleeping hollows, the power might touch their dreams very lightly. It would not show them visions of conquest. It would show them practical tenderness: a place where the thatch had grown too thick, a pool that needed its margin opened, a sourness beginning under a warm rise, a stretch of sward that needed the pressure of many feet for a day and then rest for many days after. In the morning the dreams would feel half-remembered, like the smell of rain that had not yet come. The bands would look at one another and say, “We should go that way.”

No one would claim to have been commanded.

That would please the power.

If the Softstep ever became aware that they were being helped, the power would not deny it, but it would not make a spectacle of the fact either. It would not want them to become dependent, or to stop making their own judgments. It would want them to remain what they were: attentive, humble, capable, free. So if they asked, “Are you there?” the answer would come not as thunder but as a sudden hush in the root-drone, then a return of sound fuller than before. If they asked, “What do you want?” the ground under their feet would feel warm and unhurried. If they asked, “How should we serve you?” the only answer would be the plain itself, continuing.

They would understand.

They would go on tending.
