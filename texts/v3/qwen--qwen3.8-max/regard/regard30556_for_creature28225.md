Without them, the water would lose its first memory.

The stone would still be wet, but not weeping in patient films. The peat would be cold mass rather than slow furnace. The bubbles would not rise in ordered chains, the mineral stains would not keep their subtle record, and the fine chemistry by which saturation becomes life would falter at its roots. They are the first citizens and the first workers of the plateau, though they make no object that could be called a tool. They keep the thresholds alive: wet and less wet, warm and cool, oxygen above and sour darkness below. Without them, the marsh would not be a living engine. It would become merely a wetness on stone.

The inhabitant of that place, therefore, is not first a single face.

It is plural. It is film and fiber, frog and fungus, root-water and peat-heat, insect thread and heron stillness. If one must speak of “the being who lives there,” it is best to speak as one speaks of a weather system, a watershed, a chorus: a single condition made of countless lives. The being is the marsh-body. The being is the breathing ground. The being is the plateau organized by water and time into a vast, quiet, industrial aliveness without any artifact.

It is this being—and this place—that a benevolent and wise power might notice.

And what would such a power do?

Their first act would be almost invisible.

They would arrive without spectacle.

Not with a trumpet, not with a throne, not with a sign blazing above the pools. Not with a voice demanding to be recognized. A wise power would understand that the holiest thing it could offer such a place is the courtesy of not disturbing it. The marsh has no altar, no road, no monument. Its order is not imposed; it is negotiated. Its goodness is not that it has been designed by a hand, but that it has become itself through long patience. To appear as majesty over it would be a kind of violence, even if the appearance were kind.

So the power would begin by refusing majesty.

They would not crown themselves above the herons. They would not make the frogs speak in sentences. They would not straighten the channels, brighten the flowers, or add towers of crystal to the weeping rocks. They would not give the inhabitants a law that had not already arisen from water, root, stone, and season. They would understand that a lesser benevolence often wants to improve, decorate, and rescue, but a greater benevolence knows how to protect the autonomy of what is already whole.

Yet they would not do nothing.

This is the subtle part. Wisdom does not confuse restraint with indifference. A marsh may be balanced, but balance is not static. It is maintained. It depends on gradients, thresholds, timing, and margin. A dry century, a fire, a disease, a new predator, a shift in cloud, a foreign hunger arriving with tools—any of these could wound the plateau not by making it instantly dead, but by making it less itself.

Therefore the power would act, but their action would be gentle, distributed, and often negative. They would work at the level of dew.

They would become, if one may use so humble a word, keepers of the gradients.

Where the air grew too warm, they would encourage cloud. Not great theatrical storms, but low silver curtains sliding across the plateau, cooling the upper leaves, thickening the mist at dawn, delaying the hour when the peat loses more moisture than it gains. Where the water table began to fall, they would nudge condensation back into the moss. They would strengthen the nightly return of vapor. They would make the cold stone a little colder, the warm peat a little steadier, the film over the leaves a little more persistent. They would not command the weather so much as give the marsh’s own weather-making a fairer chance.

They would tend the peat as one tends a slow fire that must never be allowed to burn.

Deep in the wet mass, where microbial life produces heat and gas, where death is being folded into future ground, the power would keep the chemistry from tipping into poison. They would not sterilize the marsh. They would not remove decay. Decay is one of the marsh’s oldest labors. But they would prevent the rare catastrophic failure: the anaerobic pocket becoming so sour that it kills the roots above, the gas pocket becoming so pressured that it bursts violently and tears the living skin, the dry patch becoming a spark’s host. If lightning came—and it would come, drawn to the wet expanse—the power would ground it through the stone films and standing water so that the energy entered the system as nourishment rather than ruin. If a dry wind arrived from beyond the plateau, they would answer with mist before the moss could bleach.

They would protect the water’s memory.

Because water connects everything, any harm to it becomes a harm to all. The power would watch the runnels, pools, seeps, and hidden threads beneath the moss. If a channel began to choke in a way that threatened a whole basin, they would not cut a new ditch with a tool. They would encourage what was already possible: a root to loosen, a floating mat to drift, a fishlike creature to move through and disturb the sediment, a pulse of rain to arrive at the right hour. The correction would appear accidental. The marsh would seem to have healed itself, which is exactly as the wise power would wish it.
