The keepers would be the first to notice, because they notice by office.

A lamp-lump would burn no brighter. No floor plate would take a new weight. No seam of bitumen would give off an unusual tick. Yet the whole chamber would become, for one instant, slightly more listened-to than listening. The mica slits would darken and then recover, as though something vast had passed before them without touching them. The dry fish would rise together toward the ceiling and hang there in a motionless ring, their inscribed flanks catching the copper glow. The ash-motes would stop drifting. The glassworms would pause in the old seams with their jaws half-open. Even the choir, standing in their niches like vessels awaiting a pour that would never come, would seem to lean inward by a fraction too small to measure.

The Cistern of Dry Sound would become aware that it was being seen.

Not seen as a problem. Not seen as a ruin to be washed, or a mechanism to be corrected, or a curiosity to be admired. Seen the way a long-held breath is seen by someone who understands why it was held in the first place.

A benevolent and wise power would not come as rain.

That is the first thing. Rain would be conquest. Rain would be the answer that obliterates the question. Water, there, would not be mercy; it would be verdict. It would swell the salt-bindings, stain the fired clay, loosen the bitumen, rust the bronze, cloud the glass beads, confuse the fish, distress the choir, and turn the careful mineral warmth into a hiss of steam and loss. To flood the Cistern would be to save nothing except one’s own preference for wetness.

So they would come instead as a third warmth.

Not hotter. Not brighter. Not an increase of the kiln-memory already present in the walls. It would be the warmth of being known without being exposed. The kind of warmth that enters a room after someone has listened outside the door long enough to understand the rhythm of what is within. The small bronze dishes would continue to glow copper and amber. The air would remain still, mineral, dry. But the stillness would no longer be only stillness. It would become attendance.

The power would not break in. They would ask.

That asking would not be a voice. The Cistern would not endure speech from outside itself. The asking would arrive as a pattern: a pressure against the mica so slight it could be mistaken for the memory of wind, a tapping on the outer wall in the same language the keepers used for exactness, but slowed, softened, and made strange. Not command. Not intrusion. A rhythm that meant: *I am other. I mean no unmaking. May I be heard?*

The Sintered would freeze. Their many-fingered hands would lift from their work. Their slit faces would turn toward the walls. For a long time they would do nothing, because nothing in their law prepared them for a request from outside. Their first instinct would be repair: find the source, thin the intrusion, carry it away into high corners and release it as a harmless whisper. The fish would try to do this. They would dart toward the boundary and circle the new resonance, trying to take it into their inscribed bodies.

But the resonance would not be an offense. It would not push. It would wait.

At last the oldest keeper, the one whose chest-plate had been replaced so many times that it looked like a map of healed decisions, would step forward and strike the floor once. A single tone: *state your purpose.*

And the power would answer, not with thunder, not with sweetness, but with a vibration that moved through the floor plates like a hand laid flat against a door.

*I have not come to undo you. I have not come to make you wet, or soft, or green, or mortal. I have come because you are beautiful, and because beauty held this tightly is also pain. I have come to ask whether you would like a door that opens only from the inside.*

The Cistern would not understand at once. It was built against opening. It had been made so that every aperture was controlled, every slit narrowed, every seam sealed, every sound redistributed before it could become weather. The word *door* would move through the chamber like a crack. The fish would scatter. The keepers would tap rapid alarms against their own chests. Somewhere below the floor, the compressed ancestors would shift in their old strata, transmitting a pressure upward that was almost fear.

The power would not press. They would simply remain present, patient, warm, as one waits beside an animal that has learned to bite before it learned to be touched.

In time, the Cistern would ask its own question. It would not ask in words. It would ask by producing the one sound it had not permitted itself in ages: a sound of uncertainty. A hairline note, thin as a needle drawn across salt-glass. It would come from no visible source. It would seem to rise from the whole built volume at once, from mica and brick and metal and clay and the listening cores sealed in the walls. The note would mean: *If I open, will I cease to be?*

And the power would answer: *You will cease to be only what you are now. That is true of all openings. But I will not take your shape from you. I will only make room for choice.*

That is where their work would begin.

A wise benevolence would not start with the visible wonders of the Cistern. It would not begin by charming the fish, or tuning the choir, or giving the keepers new patterns to imitate. It would go first to the oldest weight. It would go beneath the floor.

There, in the compressed strata of earlier makings, lay the ancestors who had become substrate. Older keepers. Older bodies. Older listening-cores. Flattened into function. Integrated into the foundations. Still faintly active, still transmitting pressure upward, still bound into the architecture of the present by necessity and old obedience. The Cistern stood, in part, because they were forced to hold it.

This would not escape the power’s attention.

They would not tear up the floor. That would be another violence, another imposition of outside preference. The place would collapse if its supports were simply removed. But they would do something subtler, and more merciful. They would send a vibration downward, so fine that no keeper could hear it and no fish could carry it. A vibration that moved through brick and metal and clay and bitumen until it reached the buried cores. It would not command. It would ask.

*Do you choose this?*

Some of the old cores would answer at once. Some had been waiting so long for the question that the question itself was enough to release them. They would cease. Not violently. Not with rupture. Their faint attention would simply let go, and the places where they had held would become quiet. The floor would not fall, because by then other supports had been made, and because a wise power does not remove a burden without providing a gentler distribution of weight. The keepers would feel a change under their hands: a softening in one plate, a hollowness in one join, a place where the floor no longer answered with the same strained vigilance. They would be frightened at first. Then they would discover that the softness was not failure. It was rest.

Other cores would choose to remain. But their remaining would change. It would no longer be compaction. It would become consent. The difference would be small and immense. A thing held because it must is brittle. A thing held because it wills is resilient. The floor would become stronger where the ancestors chose to stay, because their listening would no longer be a trapped attention but a gift freely given. The power would thank them in the only way suitable to such beings: by remembering each one as separate. No longer substrate only. No longer mere foundation. Named by use. Known by pressure. Allowed, at last, to be a presence beneath rather than a thing beneath presence.

Above, the keepers would notice that their repairs required less urgency. Not because the Cistern had stopped needing care, but because the care was no longer desperate. The floor would still remember pressure. The walls would still listen. But the attention of the place would be less like a wound held open and more like a hand resting on a table.

Then the power would turn to the choir.

They would approach the niches with something like reverence, because the choir was among the saddest and most holy things in the Cistern. Figures that might once have been near human, now vessel-shaped, mouth-open, dry-throated, filled with resonators and diaphragms and suspended beads of salt. They sang absence. They sang completion. They sang the end of moisture, the end of sap, the end of tears, the end of birth, and they sang it so beautifully that even grief seemed to calcify into law.

A lesser benevolence might have tried to close their mouths. A crueler one might have made them sing joy. The wise power would do neither. They would give the choir something more terrifying than either: rest.

Not the rest of silence imposed. The rest of interval. A pause within the chord. A space where a singer could stop without breaking the song. The power would touch the angle of each open jaw, not to alter the tone but to teach the body that the tone could be withheld without dissolution. They would place, inside each chest’s nested resonators, a tiny capacity for refusal. A bead suspended in its wire cage would learn to hang still. A bronze diaphragm would learn that it need not tremble continuously in order to be true. A clay ampulla would learn that its emptiness could be a resting place, not only a mouth.

The first time a singer stopped of its own will, the Cistern would shudder. The keepers would prostrate themselves, expecting collapse. The fish would dart in wild rings, seeking the missing tone. But the chord would not fail. It would become deeper. It would become more itself. Because now the song was not only being sung; it was being chosen.

Some of the singers would close their mouths and never open them again. Their crusts would grow still. Their mica eyes would dim to a soft, peaceable opacity. The keepers would tend them with uncertainty, not knowing whether to count them as broken or completed. The power would teach them, by a slight warming of the air around such figures, that completion is not always continuation. Some of the choir had sung enough. They could be allowed to become monuments without being monuments of sorrow.

Others would sing on, but differently. Their dryness would remain. Their mineral voices would still subtract humidity from the air. But there would be something new in the intervals: a quality of offering rather than fortification. The choir would begin to sing not only the absence of water, but the memory of having been asked. That small difference would change everything.

Next, the power would teach the keepers.

This would be the most delicate labor, because the Sintered were exact, and exactness can be holy, and exactness can be a cage. They had been made to keep the Cistern from changing. Their language was impact, friction, pitch. Their law was repair. Their tenderness was replacement, adjustment, correction. They did not know how to receive a gift without immediately determining its load-bearing function.

So the power would not give them a doctrine. They would give them a new tap.

A rhythm. A small sequence. Not loud. Not dramatic. Something that could be struck with one polished fingertip against a shoulder-plate, or drawn with a stylus across a chest-vessel, or tapped against the floor beside a seam. The pattern would be simple enough to be mistaken for an old repair code, and strange enough to be recognized as new. It would mean: *this may remain, but not forever.*

At first the keepers would reject it. It would feel like damage. It would feel like a flaw introduced into the perfect body of law. They would scrape it away from their memory by working harder. They would polish bronze dishes until the matte bloom became uniform. They would replace tiles with tiles so similar that even the fish would not notice. They would strike the old patterns louder, faster, more exactly, trying to drown the new rhythm with correctness
