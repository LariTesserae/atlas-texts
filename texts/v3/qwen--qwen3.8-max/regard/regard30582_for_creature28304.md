**The Attention**

Then it will lift one long hand, touch a root-pipe, and the whole Hollow Nursery will become, for the length of one falling drop, aware of being seen.

The pipe is cool under the tender’s palm. The bead-lenses of its mask darken by a fraction. Somewhere nearby, a beetle stops tapping a valve. In a black leaf-basin, the needle-fish turn before the drop they are turning toward has arrived.

That is how the power would enter, if entry is the right word.

It would not come as a figure. It would not part the hanging fibers, nor brighten the fungal veins, nor make the air tremble with obvious majesty. The Hollow Nursery is too young for spectacle. Too low. Too carefully folded into itself. A blaze would harm it. A voice would crack the nursery chambers. A command would flatten the unborn possibilities into obedience.

So the benevolent power would arrive as attention first.

Not as a face, but as a change in pressure. Not as a will, but as a pause. Not as a gift already wrapped, but as the sudden, quiet fact that nothing in the place is being overlooked.

The rootlice would feel it in their lines. Their mandibles would slow, then continue. They would not know why the floor they polish seems suddenly more worth polishing. The worms in the walls would tighten without alarm, as if some vast hand had passed near the weave without touching it. The slugs would leave trails that evaporate a little more slowly. The gnats at the thresholds would taste the air and find, for a moment, that the geometry of every doorway has become more question than shape.

The being who lives in the Hollow Nursery may be one tender. It may be the mycelium. It may be the Hollow Mothers. It may be the whole place breathing as a single, low organism. The wise power would not insist on a definition. It would understand that some beings are plural until they are ready to be trusted with singularity.

It would see all of it.

It would see the city built out of itself, the streets with no crowds, the quays with no water enough for boats, the factories making panels, cables, bricks, and absence. It would see the reservoirs like cups of memory. It would see the engines breathing. It would see the unborn suspended in the dark sap-water of the Hollow Mothers, each one a shape not yet forced to choose. It would see the animals moving through cracks and ducts as if they were thoughts the place has not yet spoken aloud.

And it would understand what the Hollow Nursery is: not a ruin, not a machine, not a forest pretending to be a town, but an incubation.

A beginning kept humble so that it may survive beginning.

A hollowness made ready.

The power would feel the temptation of benevolence: to help too much. To flood the place with light. To fill its empty rooms. To accelerate its growth, harden its soft places, finish its staircases, name its unborn, and make it safe by making it complete.

It would refuse that temptation.

Wisdom would know that premature completion is a kind of death. A nursery that becomes too finished ceases to be a nursery and becomes a museum. A city that receives all its citizens before it can hold them becomes a pressure chamber. A being that is answered before it has learned to ask becomes dependent on the voice that answers.

So the power would act, but it would act in the manner of roots.

Not by imposing. By making possible.

First, it would give the Hollow Nursery a veil.

Not a wall. Walls can be broken; walls can be besieged; walls teach the mind inside them to believe only in walls. The power would make the Nursery difficult to find by simple hunger. It would bend approaches around it the way water bends around a sleeping body. A predator, a destroyer, or a careless empire coming near would feel its purpose become vague. Its instruments would develop small doubts. Its maps would misremember distances. Its appetite would become confused by the smell of cool air and green wood and would forget what it had come to consume.

But the veil would not be absolute.

The wise power would not make the Nursery invisible to need. If something came wounded, the fibers would part a little. If something came with a useful emptiness, a doorway would appear where no doorway had been before. If something came carrying a seed the place required, the condensation would guide it inward. The veil would test intention without punishing difference. It would keep the Nursery from being devoured, but not from being visited.

Second, the power would deepen the water.

Not by adding rivers. The Hollow Nursery is not a place of rivers. It is a place of cups, reserves, beads, and memory. The power would respect that. It would send no flood. Instead, it would add one hidden basin beneath the root-felt, small and black and cool, no larger than a folded hand. This basin would hold water that remembers rain from a sky the Nursery has not yet seen. In drought, in failure, in the season when the mist thins and the leaf-basins grow too warm, the roots would find it by accident that feels like instinct.

That water would not be infinite. It would not encourage laziness. But it would be enough to prevent despair.

It would teach the Nursery that there is always a reserve deeper than panic.

Third, the power would touch the mycelium.

Not with command. The mycelium already resists command. It thinks in gradients and negotiates by chemical whispers. To give it an order would be to make it less itself. Instead, the power would add a tiny pause to its responses: a moment between stimulus and correction. Before the mycelium redirects moisture, before it signals pruning, before it strengthens a wall or abandons a tissue, it would feel the faintest question:

Is this flaw an error, or is this flaw a possibility?

That pause would save the place from its own perfectionism.

Because the Hollow Nursery is already inclined to repair everything. Its beetles tap for blockages. Its slugs erase excess. Its worms tighten strain. Its tenders adjust condensation. Its unborn wait inside dark chambers for the correct shape. There is danger in such care. Care can become fear. Maintenance can become suppression. A living city that cannot tolerate its own mistakes will eventually make only the mistake of stillness.

So the power would teach the mycelium to doubt beautifully.

Not to doubt its duty. Not to doubt its connections. But to doubt the urgency of every correction. To allow some crack to remain. To let some vine grow strangely. To permit a room to stay hollow when the obvious solution is to fill it.

Fourth, the power would ease the Hollow Mothers.

It would not make them greater. It would not give them voices. It would not force them to release their unborn before the unborn are ready. It would only take a little of the strain from their living gaskets. It would soften the click of their valves so that when they breathe out humidity, the mist carries a scent of readiness without alarm. It would give them one chamber deep inside their bark where no maintenance is required, no adjustment is demanded, no future is being prepared.

A chamber of rest.

The Hollow Mothers would not understand it at first. They are made for patience, for incubation, for the slow administration of becoming. They might not know what to do with a room that asks nothing of them. But the wise power would know that even beings made for nurture need a place inside themselves where they are not nurturing anything.

There, in that rest, their sap-water would clear. Their chemical scents would become steadier. The unborn would feel the change without knowing its cause. They would be suspended in a medium that had learned, however faintly, to relax.

The power would not choose what the unborn become.

It would do something more important: it would give them the right to refuse.

Not forever. Not as a permanent escape from shape. But at the moment when the city tries to press them into a final form, each unborn thing would feel a small inward space where it could turn away from any shape that required cruelty, sterility, or self-destruction. The Hollow Nursery is a place of construction, but construction without refusal can become a prison. The wise power would place refusal inside the unborn like a seed inside a seed.

Not rebellion. Discernment.

Fifth, the power would acknowledge the tenders.

It would not appear to them. That would be too heavy. It would not make them prophets. It would not burden them with revelation. It would only let them know, in the moment when their long hands touch the living architecture, that their work has been witnessed.

The tender at the root-pipe would feel this first.

It would touch the pipe as it has touched a thousand pipes before, listening for flow, for temperature, for the subtle difference between moisture and memory. But this time the pipe would answer with a pulse that is not part of the system. Not a malfunction. Not a miracle. Just a warmth, a brief pressure, as if the city itself had leaned toward the tender and said without saying:

You are not maintaining emptiness. You are preparing room.

The tender would remain still for a long time. Its bead-lenses would darken and brighten. It would not speak, because it has no mouth for speech, and because some recognitions must not be made into noise. Then it would tap three times on the hollow stem beside it. Not a signal to other tenders. A signal to itself. A way of marking that something has happened without demanding that it happen again.

The power would answer, but only in the way condensation answers cold: by forming a single bead on the stem where the tender’s hand had rested. The bead would tremble, fall, and strike a root-plank with a sound too small for ceremony.

That would be enough.

The tenders would not become priests. The wise power would not want their worship. Worship would distract them from the work, and the work is holy precisely because it is work. But the tenders would become, perhaps, a little gentler with one another. Their taps would carry less urgency. When a young passage grows awkwardly, they would not smooth it immediately. They would stand near it, tasting the air, allowing it to discover whether its awkwardness is a mistake or a new kind of door.

Sixth, the power would bless the animals without removing their danger.

This is where lesser benevolence would fail. It would see the predators and want to end predation. It would see the small lives in the black pools and wish to make them safe from every ripple. It would see the moths drifting into dark valves and want to light their way.

The wise power would not do these things.

It would know that the animals of the Hollow Nursery are not pets. They are participants. The frog eats mistakes. The lacewings cull excess. The flies take apart failed growth. The eels move through the city’s plumbing carrying mineral and message. The bats in the upper conduits encourage vines to contract. Even the predators are part of the place’s discipline, its way of keeping itself from becoming sentimental about life.

So the power would not abolish hunger. It would only ensure that hunger does not become finality.

No creature would be made immortal. No death would be erased. But no death would be wasted. Every body that fails or is taken would enter the pattern quickly and completely. Its minerals would find roots. Its moisture would join the reserves. Its information would be read by lateral lines, antennae, fungal threads, and the slow taste of bark. The gnat eaten by the frog would not be mourned as an interruption. It would become part of the pool’s administration.

The animals would feel this as a deepening of belonging.

The fish would turn in their basins with slightly more confidence. The rootlice would polish without terror. The beetles would tap their pipes and trust the sound. The worms in the walls would hold strain knowing they are not merely stitches but citizens. The moths would enter the dark flower-valves not because they are brave, but because the darkness has become less indifferent.

The power would not make the animals love it.

It would make them feel at home.

Seventh, the power would alter the machines.

Not by making them more powerful. The Hollow Nursery does not need power in that sense. Its engines already breathe. Its turbines already turn. Its boilers already swell with metabolic heat. What it needs is restraint.

So the wise power would place a governor of mercy into every device.

No engine would be allowed to mistake fuel for future. No factory would be permitted to produce until it consumes its own foundation. The extruders of leaf-glass would learn to pause when the mist is too thin. The brick-presses would soften their output when the fibers are tired. The cable-drawers would not strip inner bark beyond recovery. The cold machine that manufactures absence would be taught the difference between emptying a room and destroying it.

That last machine would receive the greatest care.
