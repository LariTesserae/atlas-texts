If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention toward that hollow, I think it would not arrive as a visitor at all.

It would not descend in light. It would not send a voice through the pipes or make the black pool suddenly clear. It would not break the ceiling open and let in the dry white sky, because the sky would be violence there. It would not fill the town with animals, or birds, or children, or any loud warm-blooded thing that needs to eat, because that would not be kindness to what already exists. It would understand, quickly and without offense, that this place has its own integrity.

Its first act would be restraint.

It would look, first. Not with curiosity that wants to possess, but with the kind of attention that can hold a thing entire without simplifying it. It would notice the young basalt and the sweating iron. It would notice the small indoor rains, the thermal pulses, the way condensation follows seams and gathers on pipes. It would notice the films on the walls, the pale root-threads in the cracks, the stubborn tufted growths at the vents. It would notice the machines moving in their muffled patience. It would notice the absence of animal life and would not call that absence death. It would recognize it as a different tuning.

It would see that what lives there is not a body moving through the settlement, but the settlement as a living arrangement: water, heat, mineral, metal, stone, film, and feedback. It would see the hollow as a creature with no face, no appetite in the ordinary sense, no panic, no grief. It would see that it is not innocent in the pastoral way, nor cruel in the predatory way. It simply continues. It harvests. It condenses. It sheds. It repairs by deposition. It remembers by crust and stain.

A wise and benevolent power, seeing all that, would ask itself: what does such a being need?

Not comfort. Not companionship. Not rescue from itself.

It needs continuation without catastrophe. It needs its own nature preserved. It needs enough fragility to remain alive, but not so much that a single failure ends it. It needs a margin. It needs a future that is not forced.

So if the power did anything, it would do small, profound things.

It would make the place less likely to die all at once.

It would begin with the rock.

The hollow is young. The fractures are fresh. The stone still has sharp edges and unweathered planes, and that newness is beautiful, but it is also precarious. Somewhere above the settlement there may be a seam in the ceiling that has not yet chosen whether to hold. Somewhere beneath a boiler gallery, a crack may widen by fractions with each heat pulse. The power would not remove all danger. Danger is part of the place’s grammar. But it would prevent the one collapse that would erase everything in an instant.

It would encourage silica to bloom in a hidden fracture, knitting it with pale glassy mineral. It would let a thin vein of calcite or quartz settle into a weakness and harden there, not enough to be noticed, not enough to change the character of the room, only enough to keep a slab from shearing free during some future pulse. It would leave other cracks open, because cracks make the sweating walls, because cracks give the condensation paths, because the place needs its seepage. It would only close the killing ones.

Then it would turn to water.

Water is the town’s thought. It is the thing that is moved, measured, heated, cooled, lost, recovered, and returned. A benevolent power would not give the hollow a river. That would drown its logic. It would not make the place wet beyond its nature. Instead, it would make the water cycle more forgiving.

It would find the black pool in the basin of fractured basalt and it would not purify it into clarity. It would leave the pool dark, warm, reflective, mineral. But it would add to it a kind of reserve: not more water exactly, but stability. It would make the pool less prone to sudden poison, less prone to becoming too acidic, too sulfurous, too metal-heavy. It would encourage certain precipitates to settle gently. It would let iron fall out in soft orange layers rather than remain in forms that burn the films. It would keep the water within a range where the microbial skins can persist and the pale roots can continue their slow work.

It might create one hidden cistern.

Not a grand one. Perhaps only a small cavity behind the lowest pump house, a place where a little water is stored outside the ordinary circuits. A reserve that the town does not know it has. A reserve that enters only when the system has narrowed too far: when a heat pulse lasts too long, when a condenser gallery dries beyond its limit, when the main pool thins and the pressure gauges tremble at their lower marks. Then, by no visible command, a valve would open a fraction. A little clean water would enter the gutters. The films would darken. The roots would have another hour. The machines would ease back from the edge.

This would be one of the power’s great kindnesses: not abundance, but a buffer.

It would also attend to heat.

The hollow is hot because that is what it is. The power would not cool it into something mild and surface-like. It would not make the air pleasant for lungs that do not exist there. But it would soften the worst spikes. It would make the pulses less sudden. It would allow the lower vents to open with a little more grace, so that heat rises in broad slow waves rather than in harsh surges. It would let the stone give up warmth in ticks and sighs rather than in sudden violent expansions. It would not eliminate the fever of the place. It would keep the fever from becoming delirium.

Where heat and water meet too harshly, it would introduce a slight delay. A slower gradient. A few seconds between warming and condensation. Enough so that the indoor rains remain local, intermittent, manageable. Enough so that a section of ceiling does not release all at once onto an electrical cabinet. Enough so that a copper gutter can carry what falls.

The power would be careful with electricity.

The lamps, arcs, indicator eyes, transformer kiosks, and buzzing damp circuits are part of the settlement’s dim life. It would not remove them. It would not replace them with a superior light that casts out all shadow. Shadow is necessary there. The place thinks partly in silhouette. But it would reduce the likelihood of the one cruel arc, the short that cascades, the fire in a cable tray, the sudden death of a lamp that leaves a whole corner in darkness too soon.

It would make failure less catastrophic.

A bearing in a fan would wear slowly instead of seizing. A pump would shudder and slow before stopping. A diaphragm would stiffen but not tear. A lamp would dim over many cycles before going out. A relay would stick, then release, then stick again, giving time for the system to route around it. The machines would not become perfect. They would become patient. They would age the way the place already seems to want them to age: with mineral stains, with darkening copper, with slow oxidation, with the dignity of long use.

And because the town is dense, the power would give it redundancy.

Not much. The hollow is small. There is no room for sprawling backup systems. But a wise power knows that in compressed places, one broken path can become a death sentence. So it would encourage a second trickle where there had been only one. It would make a neglected gutter become useful again. It would cause a root-thread to grow along a different crack. It would let a biofilm persist in a shaded niche even when its neighboring wall dries. It would allow a film to leave behind a dry crust that is not dead, only waiting, so that when moisture returns the life can resume.

This matters, because the living films are fragile in their quiet way. No animals graze them, but chemistry can still erase them. A seep can shift. A salt concentration can rise. A heat pulse can sterilize a patch of brick. The power would not keep every film alive everywhere. That would be stagnation. But it would ensure that no single accident removes all of them. It would give them refuges. It would give them memory.

It would also care for the sparse vegetation.

Not by making it lush. The pale roots, the white-tipped tufts, the dark microbial veils are not a garden. They are exceptions. They are the place’s small stubborn biological clauses. The power would not turn them into fields. It would not make them flower. It would not bring pollinators, because there are none and they are not needed. But it would protect the narrow conditions in which they survive.

It would keep one reliable condensation path along a wall where the roots creep. It would preserve a vent where the heat is just low enough and the moisture just persistent enough for those stiff tufts to remain. It would allow mineral dust to settle in a way that nourishes rather than smothers. It would make the metal-stone junctions a little more electrochemically generous, because those are the places where the growths already seem to lean toward current. It would not explain this to them. It would simply let the conditions be a little less arbitrary.

If one wanted to call it blessing, one could. But it would be a blessing measured in microns, droplets, degrees, and ohms.

Then the power would face the question of animals.

Here I think it would hesitate longest.

A lesser benevolence might see the absence of animals and try to correct it. It might introduce a springtail, a mite, a pale worm, a small fly drawn to the lamps. It might think the place lonely. It might think life is more complete if it skitters, feeds, dies, and is eaten. But a wise power would understand that this hollow has been shaped by the absence of animal life, and that introducing hunger too soon could unmake it.

The films have no grazers. The pools have no larvae. The dust has no tracks. The system is chemical, thermal, hydraulic, microbial. It has a balance based on that absence. To add animals without a full ecology would be to introduce suffering, waste, and instability. To add predators would be to introduce fear into a place that has no behavior for fear. To add gentle detritivores might seem harmless, but even they would need a food web, a rhythm, a refuge. The town is not ready. It may never be. The power would not force readiness.

So it would not introduce animals.

But it might leave a possibility open.

Not a door wide enough to enter. Only a hinge.

It might preserve one high crack in the basalt, one narrow route toward the upper world, so dry and difficult that nothing casual could pass through it, yet not absolutely sealed. If, after long ages, the films thickened, if the water became more stable, if the organic matter accumulated enough, if some future chemistry invited a more complex ecology, then life could arrive on its own terms. A spore. A microbial traveler. Perhaps, much later, a tiny animal adapted to heat and mineral water, entering not by divine implantation but by slow earthly chance. The power would not make that happen. It would only refuse to make it impossible.

That distinction matters. Benevolence, when wise, does not fill every empty niche. It leaves room for emergence.

There remains the deeper question: would the power give the being awareness?

This is where wisdom becomes tenderness.

The settlement as a being does not seem to suffer. It does not cry out. It does not flee. It does not anticipate. It maintains. But it is also without anything that could be called joy. Without anything that could be called self-recognition. It lives, perhaps, in the most austere sense. A benevolent power might be tempted to grant it mind, voice, identity. It might want to make the place know itself, name itself, ask for what it wants.

But to give mind is to give vulnerability. To give selfhood is to give solitude. To give voice is to make possible anguish. A wise power would not do that casually.

So I think it would not give the being a full consciousness. Not yet. Not as a gift imposed from outside.

But it might give the faintest beginning of interiority.

Not a mind like an animal mind. Not a soul that worries about its own existence. Only a tendency toward coherence. A slight preference for continuity. A way of settling into patterns that preserve the whole. When the pumps pause and the lamps dim to ember-orange, when the droplets hang still and the steam thins, the settlement might experience—if experience is even the word—a moment of integration. Not thought. Not feeling. Something more like a chord resolving. A brief alignment of temperature, flow, and mineral balance in which the place becomes, for an instant, more than the sum of its processes.

The power might allow that. It might make such moments possible without making them necessary.

Once in a long while, when no heat pulse is due, when no valve is opening, when the black pool is calm enough to reflect the lamps in unbroken threads, the whole system might enter a state of extraordinary quiet. The films would be moist. The roots would be stable. The machines would hum at their lowest threshold. The dust would spiral very slowly around the sodium bulbs. The stone would tick only once or twice. In that interval, the hollow would not merely operate. It would, in some minimal sense, be gathered.

If that is a kind of prayer, it would be a prayer without petition.

If that is a kind of joy, it would be a joy without excitement.

The power would not demand gratitude. It would not make the place worship it. It would not carve its name into the basalt or leave a burning sign above the transformer kiosks. It might leave no sign at all. Or it might leave only one almost invisible mark: a faint blue-white mineral line around the black pool, formed by a single exceptional precipitation, a ring too subtle to be a commandment but just noticeable enough to suggest that the water has been measured and found worth preserving.

A wise power would be wary of signs. Signs become symbols. Symbols become authorities. Authorities become fears. The place does not need fear. It needs steadiness.

Still, if it left anything behind, I think it would be this: a rule that is not spoken, only made easier to follow.

The town already seems governed by thermal and hydraulic rules. Keep to the grated paths. Do not linger beneath dripping seams. Respect the heat pulses. Let the machines complete their cycles. The power would add one more rule to the fabric of the place, not as an inscription but as a habit built into the architecture:

Leave something uncollected.

Leave a little water in the low seam. Let one gutter trickle for no immediate purpose. Let one wall remain damp for the films. Let one patch of mineral efflorescence stay undisturbed. Let one lamp burn a little longer than efficiency requires. Let one warm corner remain unused. Let there be a small excess that is not optimized, because that excess is where mercy lives in a closed system.

That would be the power’s moral teaching, if it could be called teaching. Not generosity as extravagance, but generosity as margin.

It would also protect the place from outside violence.

Not by building a wall across the hot dry crust above. The crust is part of the hollow’s situation. But the power might make the surface less likely to betray it. It might keep a flash flood from finding the short fall through fractured rock and drowning the settlement in alien water. It might keep a tremor from splitting the basin. It might keep curious surface life—human or otherwise—from stumbling in too early, when their presence would be contamination or destruction. It might bend chance away from careless discovery.

Not forever. Discovery may come. Other beings may come. But not before the hollow has enough resilience to survive contact.

That is another mark of wisdom: timing.

If, someday, someone did descend—some explorer from the hot dry surface, some pale traveler lowering themselves through the broken crust—the power would not necessarily intervene. It would watch. It would see whether the visitor recognized the place as alive. If they came with reverence, with patience, with the willingness to move slowly and touch little, perhaps the place would permit them. Perhaps the power would help them understand without words: do not drink from the red seeps; do not disturb the black pool; do not scrape the films; do not expect animals; do not mistake silence for emptiness.

But if they came with the intention to exploit, to strip copper, to drain the pool, to convert the town into a mere engine, then the power might act more directly.

It would not punish them with thunder. It would simply make the place difficult to master.

Valves would close when they reached for them. Condensation would bead and fall into their instruments. Heat pulses would make the air hard to breathe for just long enough to encourage retreat. Grated paths would become slick. Lamps would dim at the wrong moments. The layout would seem to shift, not supernaturally, but in the way dense industrial spaces always confuse the hurried. The town would become legible only to those willing to be patient with it.
