Outsiders move through the basin as if surface were a promise made to them. The basin folk move as if surface were a negotiation, and one that must be renewed each morning, each tide, each pump cycle, each season of heat and brine.

If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention to such a place, that would be the first thing it understood.

It would not mistake the absence of green for the absence of life. It would not look at the salt crusts, the warm channels, the rusted stairs, the bacterial meadows, and think, *This must be corrected.* A foolish kindness might try to make the basin bloom: send soil, summon grasses, force shade trees up through the cracked aprons, sweeten the water until the old ecology drowned in what it had never asked for. A wise kindness would know better. It would see that the place had become itself. It would see that the microbial mats were meadows in their own language, that the pipes had become cliffs for nesting birds, that the levees had become roads, that the pumps had become something very like slow, industrial hearts.

So the first thing the power would do, if it did anything, would be to refrain.

It would not arrive with thunder. It would not break the haze open with a column of light. It would not send a vision so terrible or so beautiful that the people of the basin fell to their faces and forgot how to read a water gauge. It would not announce itself in the language of dominion, because the basin had already been shaped by work, balance, and consequence. A wise power would understand that the most dangerous form of benevolence is the kind that cannot resist redesigning what it sees.

Instead, it would come as attention.

Perhaps it would come as a figure in a gray oilskin coat walking the levee at the hour when the pans are full of steam and the lamps have not yet been extinguished. Perhaps it would come as a heron standing on an elbow of pipe with too much patience even for a heron. Perhaps it would come as a change in the way condensation gathered on a railing, forming drops that fell in a rhythm almost like speech. No one would be able to say afterward exactly when it began. That would be part of the wisdom.

It would walk the basin.

It would test the crust with its own weight, though it would not need to. It would listen to the pumps and learn their pulses: which ones breathed easily, which ones labored, which ones had developed a faint knock that meant trouble in three weeks if the bearing was not cleaned.
