The membrane pavilions have moods.

That is how the attention first arrives: not as a descent, not as a voice, not as a rupture in the ordinary green light, but as a change in one of the pavilion’s moods.

The membrane has been holding the day’s warmth in a shallow curve, breathing slowly, its surface faintly glossy with condensation. Around it, insects move in their usual veils, and water whispers through the plant-built gutters. Nothing announces that anything has begun. The light remains ordinary. The shadows remain natural. The air remains wet and sweet with leaf-sugar and old peat.

But the pavilion becomes attentive.

It is a small change. A tightening so slight that only the water-listeners might notice it, and even they would call it a feeling rather than a fact. A quality of inwardness. A sense of being listened to from very close, without pressure, without demand. The membrane does not open. It does not close. It simply becomes more itself, as though some vast kindness has leaned near enough to hear it breathe.

Then the basin notices that it is being seen.

Not watched. That would imply distance, suspicion, appraisal. Seen. The difference is important. To be watched is to be measured from outside. To be seen is to be recognized from within the truth of what one is.

The basin’s first response is not fear. It is too old, too settled, too composted into its own patience for fear to rise quickly. Instead, there is a long, subsonic pause. The low hum beneath all sounds deepens by a fraction. Water beads on the root-mat and trembles without wind. In the central court, the bubbles rising through the clear pool slow for a moment, as though bringing up older scents than usual: the smell of first roots, the sweetness of ancient rains, the green breath of a time before the basin had learned its present shape.

The birds go still among the stem-arches. Not frozen. Attentive. Their heads turn together toward nothing in particular. The amphibians in the flower-throats press their glossy bodies deeper into the valves, not hiding, but listening through the living tissue. The mollusks on the root-quays click once, softly, in a rhythm that has no obvious sequence. The insects thin from a cloud into a wide, trembling ring, then release themselves back into ordinary motion.

Among the Softened, several stop at the same time.

A root-tender pauses with one palm against a warm wall. A water-listener lifts her hands from a pool before the ripples have finished speaking. A pollen-runner touches the edge of his living cloak and feels the fine hairs on his arms rise in a pattern he has never learned. An elder sitting near the central court opens his mouth as if to say a name, then closes it again, understanding that no name will be adequate.

They all feel the same thing, though they would not put it into the same words.

Something benevolent is present.

Not above. Not outside. Present.

It is the kind of presence that does not need to occupy space. It does not displace the basin. It does not press down on the root-floor or bend the membranes with weight. It arrives the way warmth arrives after a long darkness: by making what is already there more fully alive.

If the power has a shape, it is the shape of attention.

It has drawn near because it has recognized something rare. In many worlds, intelligence grows by sharpening itself against the world. It cuts, extracts, hardens, fortifies, preserves itself by making other things less alive. It builds permanence out of dead material and calls that achievement. It mistakes stillness for peace and domination for order. It learns too late that the edge which separates can also sever.

Here, in this small green basin, another answer has been grown.

The power sees the basin not as a collection of beautiful oddities, but as one slow being. The root-floor is its flesh. The water is its thought. The animals are its mobile intentions. The Softened are its conscious edges. The architecture is its habit of hospitality. The storms are its necessary breathing. The sparse open spaces are its wisdom. The absence of sharpness is not naivety; it is discipline refined over ages until it became nature.

The power sees, too, what the basin cannot fully do for itself.

It is small. It is complete, but small. It has no desire to expand, and that is part of its beauty, but also part of its vulnerability. It is not defended by violence, and that is its greatness, but not yet its safety. It can reorganize around harm, but harm may still come from outside: careless feet, greedy hands, minds that cannot bear what they cannot own. It can heal wounds, but it cannot choose whether the world beyond its rim will learn to approach it gently.

It can remember itself, but not beyond its own living memory.

It can offer a way of being, but not send that way far without becoming something else.

The power understands all of this in the first moment, if moments mean anything to it. And because it is wise, its first act is not to act.

It does not descend in glory. Glory would flatten the basin’s subtle balances. It does not speak commandments. Commandments would introduce a hard edge where none belongs. It does not build a temple. A temple would make the place into a monument, and monuments are how living things are turned into memory without breath. It does not crown the Softened, because they do not need rulers; they need room to continue becoming what they already are.

It does not stop the storms. The storms are maintenance.

It does not abolish decay. Decay is service.

It does not make the inhabitants immortal. Immortality would become a closed room. The basin’s life depends on circulation, on ending made into beginning, on the courage of forms that do not insist on remaining forever.

It does not remove hunger, because hunger here is not cruelty; it is participation. The nectar opens. The fruit detaches. The fish rise in cycles. The swarm thins when there is surplus. To remove all need would be to remove the conversations that bind the basin together.

The power does not make the basin safe.

Safety, in this place, is already a kind of respiration: yielding, repairing, distributing, remembering. What the basin needs is not safety from itself. What it needs is a right relationship to what lies beyond itself.

So the power waits.

It waits the way root waits.

And because attention is never nothing, the basin begins to answer.

The answer does not come in words. It comes as a sequence of small recognitions. A membrane pavilion warms where no sun has touched it. A channel of water rises slightly, not toward overflow, but toward offering. A broad leaf lowers itself in the central court, making a shade that was not there before. A seat of moss swells near the pool, empty and inviting.

The Softened understand.

They gather without hurrying. They do not come in procession. There is no ceremony prepared, because ceremony here is not arranged in advance; it emerges when the basin asks for it. The water-listeners kneel beside the pools. The root-tenders stand with shoulders and palms against the nearest living walls. The pollen-runners loosen their cloaks so that whatever comes may settle where it will. The storm-wardens lift their faces to the upper membranes, tasting the air. The beast-speakers become still enough that animals move around them as if they were architecture. The memory-keepers sit near the rising bubbles and listen.

Children are there too. They are not kept away. A child stands at the edge of the clear pool with toes pressed into the root-mat, watching the surface with the grave seriousness of someone who has not yet learned to mistrust wonder.

The power asks its first question.

It does not ask in sound. It asks through the basin itself.

The question forms as a slight increase of warmth in the water. As a new pressure in the root-floor. As a scent released from the moss: green, sweet, and deeply old. If it could be translated, it would say:

May I give without taking?

The basin considers. The pools ripple in small, overlapping circles. The birds exchange short calls. The insects gather into a column, then spread into a veil. An amphibian in a flower-throat presses its body against the valve-root, and somewhere a hidden channel fills with warm water. The membranes tighten, then soften. The root-floor breathes out.

The answer is not yes. Not exactly.

The answer is: Show how.

The power asks again.

May I guard without enclosing?

This question is more difficult. The basin has been harmed before, long ago, by whatever taught the world outside that softness invites severing. It remembers the need for boundaries, but it does not love walls that think only of exclusion. Its living fortifications are no longer defensive except against forgetting. It has learned to be open without being helpless, but openness has risks.

The Softened look to one another. The water-listeners lower their hands into the pools. The root-tenders press their bodies more fully against the walls. The elder near the central court makes a low humming sound, and the bubbles answer him by rising in a slow spiral.

The basin answers.

Do not make us closed.

Do not make us afraid.

Do not make the boundary a blade.

The power accepts this.

It asks a third question.

May I remember you without fixing you?

That is the deepest question. To be remembered by a wise and benevolent power could become a kind of preservation, and preservation can be a gentle tomb. The basin does not wish to be made into an example. It does not wish to be copied as if it were a pattern that could be imposed elsewhere. It knows that living wisdom must be grown, not installed. It knows that what cannot change cannot be trusted.

The Softened understand this instinctively. Their names change. Their homes close when they leave. Their districts dissolve. Their elders are distributed into the living system. They have built their entire way of being on the knowledge that permanence is not the same as faithfulness.

The child at the pool reaches down and touches the water.

The surface clears.

For a moment, everyone feels it: the basin saying yes without surrendering itself.

Yes, but not as a stone remembers.

Yes, but not as a commandment remembers.

Yes, as a seed remembers a forest it has never been.

The power receives the answer.

Then it begins to act.

It acts the way the basin acts: by addition that feels like emergence. By strengthening what is already present. By making room rather than making law. By touching the smallest possible points and allowing the whole to decide what follows.

The first gift is at the rim.

Beyond the curtain of aerial fibers and the low living walls grown into the shape of old fortifications, the boundary of the basin changes. Nothing sharpens. Nothing rises like a threat. Instead, the buttress roots deepen and broaden in a way that seems almost accidental. New root-shelves curl outward, not aggressively, but as if the basin has become more comfortable in its own edges. Fibrous networks interlace beneath the surface, forming a lattice so fine and responsive that it can feel intention the way other tissue feels pressure.

This becomes the Mercy of the Rim.

From then on, approaching the basin is not simply a matter of walking toward it.

The edge begins to discern.

A traveler who comes with curiosity soft enough to listen will find the way made possible without being obvious. A root-wharf may extend across a dark channel at the right moment. A leaf may lift to reveal a gap in the hanging fibers. A bird may flash through the air and vanish into an opening that was not visible before. The basin does not reveal itself all at once. It asks, through the path itself, whether the newcomer can move gently.

A traveler who comes with greed will not be punished. The basin does not punish. But the rim will become unintelligible to them.

Paths will seem to loop.

The air will feel too wet, too close, too confusing.

Sounds will arrive from the wrong directions
