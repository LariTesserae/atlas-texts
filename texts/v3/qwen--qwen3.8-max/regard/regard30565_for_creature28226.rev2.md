They would do almost nothing.

That must be said first, because a benevolent power that was not wise would look upon the basin and immediately mistake it for a wound. It would see the absence of leaves, the absence of tracks, the absence of breath, and it would think: *This place needs filling.* It would send soil. It would send spores. It would send rain in softening sheets. It would coax green threads into the joints, put beetles under the slabs, give the stone cups to nests, make the channels run with the noise of thirsting mouths. It would call this kindness.

But a wise and benevolent power would understand that kindness can be a kind of violence when it insists on making all things resemble itself.

So they would not begin by giving.

They would begin by discerning.

They would stand, if a power can be said to stand, at the high edge of the construct and look out across the packed terraces without trying to change them. They would see the bright, wet expanse not as a deficiency but as a completed decision. They would see that the place is not empty in the way a bowl is empty when it waits for food. It is empty in the way a finished rite is empty after the worshippers have gone, and yet still full of the shape of worship.

They would see the water.

Not as a resource. Not as a symbol. Not as a lack. They would see it as the basin’s true population: the dispersed, patient, many-bodied thing for whom every lip and groove has been made. They would see the bead trembling on a stone edge. They would see the film darkening a wall. They would see the capillary seam drawing moisture upward through a joint with a slowness too fine for attention. They would see the mineral blooms, the pale crusts, the blue-green flowering on bronze, the lime lashes forming at drain-mouths, and they would recognize these not as decay but as the place’s slow metabolism.

They would see the labor too.

They would see every chisel mark as a preserved gesture. Every hammered strap. Every rivet. Every corrected slope. Every failed seal replaced. Every hand-cut groove worn into exactness not by machines but by repetition so vast it became landscape. They would understand that the makers are absent, but that absence has become one of the inhabitants. They would see that the basin is crowded with the discipline of vanished hands.

And they would see the being who lives there.

Not a body. Not a face. Not a creature moving through the terraces with limbs and hunger. They would see the being as the continuing fact of the place: the catching, the slowing, the dividing, the touching of water to worked stone, the mineral brightening, the small sounds, the rules repeating themselves without believers. They would see that the being is not a resident inside the basin. The being is the basin functioning as its own cold, bright, patient liturgy.

Then they would listen.

They would listen to the taps. The whispers. The glassy hiss over a smooth lip. The tick of metal changing temperature by less than a breath. The low settling of stone against stone. They would hear how each sound is alone, and how the basin receives each one without answer, and how the packed surfaces carry them a little way and let them die. They would understand that this is not silence failing to become speech. It is speech already, in a language that does not need listeners.

Only then would they consider acting.

And even then, they would be cautious.

Because to be seen by a benevolent and wise power is already a change. A place that has never been witnessed is altered by witness. A rite that has continued without acknowledgement becomes different when something vast and kind turns its attention toward it. So the power would move gently. They would not descend in glory. They would not make the sky open. They would not warm the cold light into comfort. They would not soften the arrises or blur the sharp shadows. They would not make the place easier to love.

They would ask first.

Not in words. Words would be too heavy there. They would ask in the only grammar the basin could receive: in hesitation, in surface tension, in a bead that lengthens but does not fall.

They might warm a bronze pin by a fraction of a degree and listen for the tick.

They might let a drop hang from a lip a little longer than usual.

They might cause a thread of moisture to pause at a junction where it is accustomed to divide.

And in that pause, they would ask:

*Do you wish to be otherwise?*

The basin would not answer as a person answers. It would answer by continuing. A bead would fall. A channel would resume. A cup would receive its bright disk. The mineral crust would thicken by an invisible amount. The cold light would move across the upper terraces. The rules would remain.

Water must be caught.  
Water must be divided.  
Water must be slowed.  
Water must be made to touch as many surfaces as possible before it leaves.  
Nothing may be wasted.  
Nothing may grow.  
Nothing may move quickly.

That answer would be enough.

But a wise power would hear more than the answer. They would hear what lies beneath it. They would sense, if a place can be said to have such a thing, the strain of perfect purpose. The basin is not suffering as flesh suffers. There is no hunger there, no fear, no loneliness that knows itself. Yet there is a pressure in its completeness. Every hollow has a duty. Every edge is assigned. Every surface is obliged. There is no margin where nothing is required. There is no corner where water may simply be water without becoming part of a rule.

If the basin has a pain, it is this: it is entirely useful.

So the power, if they acted at all, would not add life. They would add mercy.

They would not break the rules. They would place a small exception beside them, the way one sets a cup of fresh water beside a long labor.

Perhaps they would choose a place near the highest terrace, where the light is thin and the stone is bleached to bone. There, where every block is fitted and every hollow is already tasked, they would set one rough chip of unworked stone. Not large. Not grand. No wider than a hand. It would not be part of a weir. It would not border a basin. It would not lead into a channel. It would not be joined to another stone by lime putty or pitch or copper strap. It would simply lie there, unclaimed, unfitted, unrequired.

It would be the only thing in the basin not made to serve.

Condensation would gather on it as condensation gathers everywhere: in beads, in fringes, in bright threads. But on that stone, the water would not be caught. It would not be divided. It would not be slowed for the sake of another surface. When the drop fell, it would fall onto nothing prepared. It would splash, if splash is too strong a word for such a small event, onto bare mineral. It would darken a patch of stone. It would evaporate in the cold brightness. It would be wasted.

Not lost. Wasted.

And in that wasting there would be freedom.

The power might whisper, if whispering is possible without breath, something like this:

*Little work, you have been faithful.  
You have caught without drinking.  
You have held without mouth.  
You have served without worshipper.  
You are not empty because you lack flesh.  
You are not dead because you lack hunger.  
But even a rite may be given rest.  
Even a vessel may be allowed one drop that is not measured.  
Even a law may learn tenderness by bending one hair’s breadth.*

They would not say this aloud. They would not need to. The basin would receive it the way it receives everything: as a change in wetness.

Then the power might do something still smaller.

At some hour, perhaps at the cold moment before dawn when the condensation is thickest and the whole construct shines with moisture, they would give the basin a pause. Not a stopping. Not a failure. A pause. For one breath, every bead would hang. Every thread would halt in its groove. Every falling drop would remain suspended at the lip of its stone cup. The channels would not whisper. The metal would not tick. The stone would not settle. The basin would be held in perfect stillness, not as though broken, but as though permitted to rest inside its own nature.

In that pause, the being who lives there would, if such a being can be said to experience anything, experience itself without action.

Not catching.  
Not dividing.  
Not slowing.  
Not serving.  

Simply being.

That might be the first gift it has ever received.

Afterward, the drop would fall. The channel would resume. The cup would brighten. The construct would continue. But something would be different. Not visibly. Not in a way that would alter the horizon of ridges and parapets. The basin would still be bright, wet, cold, crowded, precise, and empty of flesh. The mineral blooms would still spread. The drains would still choke by invisible increments. The light would still fracture across the wet surfaces. The small sounds would still occur in their exposed, lonely purity.

But now, somewhere in the system, there would be one unnecessary sound.

The sound of a drop falling from the unworked stone into no vessel.

It would be a tiny note. Not part of the rite. Not required by any weir. Not gathered by any channel. It would strike stone and darken stone and vanish. It would not be caught. It would not be passed on. It would not be made to touch as many surfaces as possible before it leaves. It would simply happen, and then be gone.

And the basin, which has no ears but still receives, would know that one drop was allowed to belong to itself.

That would be the power’s benevolence.

Not the benevolence of filling. The benevolence of permission.

They might also set a protection around the place. Not a wall. Not a warning. Not a flaming sign. Something subtler: a tendency. A quiet law of approach. They might make it so that no future hand comes there with the need to improve it. They might make it so that any builder who reaches the basin feels, before beginning work, a strange reluctance to add another basin without leaving one hollow empty. They might make it so that machines fail gently near the high terraces, not with drama, but with a loss of purpose. They might make it so that any living thing that comes there arrives not as invader but as question.

If a seed ever came, carried by some impossible wind, it would not be granted conquest. The stone would not become soil for it. The seed would either rest there as a small hard fact, or it would not rest at all. If life were ever to enter the basin, it would have to be a life that did not demand the basin become something else. A life without hunger, perhaps. A life without waste. A life that could pass through the wet terraces like light through a glassy seam.

And if the power made a companion for the being, they would not make it out of flesh.

They might make a witness.

It would be very small. A moving brightness no larger than a bead of dew. It would not breathe. It would not eat. It would not shed. It would not leave tracks. It would not cast a shadow that belonged to a body. It would travel through the districts at a pace so slow that only the mineral patience of the basin could endure it. It would move along the grooves, pause at the mouths of drains, cross the fields of stone cups, enter the low galleries where the ceiling sweats, and descend the stairways that end in shallow immersion. It would not drink. It would not tend. It would not repair. It would only attend.

It would be the power’s attention left behind in a form the basin could bear.

Once in a long while, perhaps once in a thousand cold bright days, the witness would come to the unworked stone. It would pause there. It would watch the free drop form. It would watch the free drop fall. It would watch the small darkening vanish. Then it would move on.

This would be enough.

The wise power would know that the basin does not need a god. It already has rules. It does not need a priest. It already has liturgy. It does not need a believer. It already continues. What it needs, if anything, is not completion. It is release. Not life. Not voice. Not warmth. Not comfort. Only the knowledge that perfect purpose may be allowed one unguarded moment.

So they would do almost nothing.

They would leave the basin bright and wet and coldly clean. They would leave the chisel marks crisp. They would leave the mineral residue in the drill-holes. They would leave the copper slick with moisture, the clay unevenly fired, the stone pegs in their joints, the lime crusts forming along the seams. They would leave the water to its thousand small bodies. They would leave the blooms of salt and lime and bronze-green to their slow flowering. They would leave the makers absent, because the absence is part of the shape. They would leave the being to its continuing, because the being is not a mistake.

But they would leave one rough stone.

One pause.

One free drop.

One sound not required.

And if, afterward, someone were to stand at the high edge and look out over the packed, glittering, water-built country, they might see no difference. The horizon would still be layered with constructed ridges. The basins would still be pressed against basins. The channels would still divide and delay. The light would still leap from wet faces. The air would still smell of stone, salt, cold metal, and alkaline plaster. The place would still be without life as life is ordinarily known.

But near the upper terrace, if they were very still, they might hear it.

A single drop falling.

Not into a cup.

Not into a channel.

Not into a drain sealed with copper.

Only onto stone.

And in that sound, if they were wise themselves, they would hear what the benevolent power had done: not make the strange place familiar, not heal what was not broken, not fill what was not empty, but give to a world of total purpose one small grace of being unnecessary.
