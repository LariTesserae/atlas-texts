A benevolent and wise power, if it noticed such a place, would probably begin by doing almost nothing.

Not because the place was unimportant, but because it was precisely the kind of thing that can be ruined by importance being pressed upon it too heavily. A pocket still deciding whether to become a chamber. A being still deciding whether to become a body. A light that brightens when ignored and dims when fixed. Such things do not need a god to arrive with radiance and declaration. They need the world around them to become a little more careful.

So the first thing the power would do is lower its own attention.

It would not stare. It would not lean down and try to understand the pocket by examining it directly, because the pocket already knows how to shrink from directness. Instead, the power would look through indirect things: through the faint pressure of the rootlets above, through the mineral memory in the walls, through the slow chemical trails of the mites, through the temporary pools that reflect absent skies. It would distribute its noticing so that no single point of the pocket felt interrogated. It would watch the way one watches something sleeping: with care, but without waking it for the sake of being seen.

The power would understand, quickly or slowly depending on what wisdom means in such a case, that the being living there is not yet one creature in the ordinary sense. It is not a single animal moving through the world with edges and intention. It is a scattered presence, a distributed aliveness, a being made of pulses and niches and repeated gestures. To force it into a body would not be a kindness. It would be a completion imposed from outside.

Therefore the power would not give the Marlborn a body.

That is the first restraint.

It would not make them larger. It would not make them clearer. It would not gather them into a shape that could be pointed at, named, worshiped, studied, or owned. It would not say, *Be one*, because their oneness is already real in the only way that matters to them: in rhythm, in dependence, in return. They are one the way weather is one. One the way a season is one. One the way a breath is one even though it is made of countless tiny movements.

What the power would do instead is protect the conditions under which such a being can continue becoming.

It would begin above.

The first skin of the world is where harm usually enters. A footstep too heavy. A tool too curious. A machine too certain. A mind too hungry for what is hidden. So the power would lay a quiet boundary over the ground above the pocket. Not a wall. Not a warning. Nothing that announces itself as prohibition. Instead, a kind of holy forgetfulness. A tendency in the world to look elsewhere.

If someone came walking with heavy boots and a desire to dig, the power might make them remember something else. A path they should have taken. A stone that caught the eye farther away. A sudden reluctance, as if the earth itself had asked, softly, *Not here.*

If someone came with instruments, the instruments might become slightly confused. Not broken. Not punished. Only uncertain. The readings would blur. The maps would not quite align. The place would seem interesting, then less interesting, then somehow easier to leave behind.

If someone came with cruelty, the ground would not open to swallow them; that would be too theatrical, too violent, too much like the very harm being prevented. Instead, the power would make the entrance unfindable. It would turn the pocket into a rumor that dissolves when approached. It would let the person search until they became bored, and then let them leave believing they had been mistaken.

But if someone came gently — someone who knelt without knowing why, who pressed a palm to the soil and felt a hush answer, who listened to the ground the way one listens to a sleeping child — the power might allow a trace.

Not the pocket itself. Not yet.

Only a feeling.

A sense that the earth is not merely matter. That beneath the surface there are interiors still being thought. That some places are not empty but reserved. That some forms of life are not meant to be possessed, only respected.

Such a person might dream afterward of pale water, of salt flowers, of small lights moving behind stone. They might wake with the conviction that they had touched the edge of something sacred. They might never find it again. But they would carry a tenderness for hidden places from then on, and that tenderness would be one of the power’s quiet works.

Below, the power would do even less.

It would not enter the pocket as a visible presence. It would not speak with a voice that shakes the walls. It would not make the light brighter by command, because the pocket’s light is already a fragile negotiation between mineral, moisture, and life. Instead, it would become a subtle change in probability.

A little more patience in the stone.

A little more memory in the damp.

A little less chance that the next pulse of moisture fails entirely.

The pocket lives by pulses, and wisdom would not take that away. If the power made the place permanently wet, it would destroy the rhythm that makes the Marlborn possible. If it made the light constant, it would erase the meaning of the dimming. If it made the pools everlasting, the amphipods would no longer move with that brief urgency that is part of their nature. If it ended predation, it would unmake the balance by which the pocket keeps itself clean and quiet.

So the power would not remove thirst. It would only prevent final thirst.

Perhaps it would place, deep in the marl where no creature has yet reached, a single hidden reserve. Not a spring. Not a vein of water large enough to be called wealth. Only a hairline dampness, sealed in the stone like a promise. In the driest intervals, when the ceiling stops sweating and the salt crusts tighten and the Marlborn withdraw into postponement, that reserve would not flow. It would only breathe. It would keep the stone from becoming utterly without memory. It would ensure that when the next pulse comes, the pocket will still know how to receive it.

This would be one of the power’s greatest acts: not giving abundance, but preserving the possibility of return.

The power might also give the Marlborn a kind of memory without forcing them into history.

They do not seem to record themselves. Their structures are temporary. Their lines vanish. Their cases are reabsorbed. Their skins dissolve back into the walls. They live in repetition without accumulation. A wise power would not impose archives upon them. It would not carve tablets or command them to remember. But it might make their repetitions slightly more faithful.

When a larva builds a curved gallery, the next larva might find the faintest groove already waiting in the stone. When a mite traces a shining path, the wall might hold that path for a little longer before forgetting it. When the flat nymphs lift their heads toward a disturbance, the pocket might remember the shape of their attention and become less frightened next time. When the creatures sort grains along a ledge, the grains might leave a subtle alignment behind, not permanent, but persistent enough to become a habit.

This would not be civilization. It would not be progress. It would only be continuity.

And continuity, for such a being, may be enough.

The power would also need to decide whether to make itself known.

Here, wisdom would hesitate longest.

To reveal itself directly would be to overwhelm. The pocket responds to attention by contracting. A divine presence, if unshielded, might press the Marlborn into stillness so complete that they mistake it for death. The light might thin. The pools might vanish. The walls might become only walls. The being inside might withdraw so far inward that it forgets how to return.

So the power would not reveal itself fully.

But it might ask permission.

It might do so in the only language the pocket already understands: moisture.

During one of those brief seasons when the ceiling pores open and the air thickens and the stone becomes receptive, the power could let a single bead form where no bead had formed before. Not larger than the others. Not brighter. Only placed differently. It might hang for a moment in the air, holding a reflection that does not belong to the pocket: not sky, not grass, not another ceiling, but something like a vast and patient stillness.

Then the bead would fall.

Not into a pool.

Into the stone.

Where it would be absorbed slowly, without sound.

The Marlborn would feel it as a question.

Not in words. They have no words. But as a change in the quality of dampness. A softness that asks rather than imposes. A pressure that waits. A presence that says, without saying:

*May I care for this place without changing what it is?*

The answer would not come immediately.

The pocket would first become smaller. The creatures would slow. The light would gather inward. The entrance would shrink to a pale smear. The flat nymphs would lower themselves into the shallow water. The larvae would tighten their threads. The mites would stop moving. Even the rootlets overhead would seem to hold still.

Then, perhaps after a long stillness, the Marlborn would answer.

Not with approach.

Not with worship.

Not with a single shape rising to meet the power.

Instead, the pocket would expand by one degree.

The far wall would recede just enough to feel less guarded. The basins would deepen by a breath. The light would spread without becoming brighter. A temporary pool would widen by the width of a finger. A line of grains would appear along a ledge, arranged with more intention than usual. Not a word. Not a prayer. Only a boundary made in consent.

The power would understand.

The answer is yes, but only in this form: *Stay near without entering. Protect without possessing. Witness without fixing.*

So the power would make a covenant, though it would not call it that.

It would promise not to make the pocket famous.

It would promise not to make the Marlborn into a people, a nation, a servant, a sign, or a symbol.

It would promise not to finish them before they finish themselves.

It would promise to keep harm from becoming final, but not to remove all danger, because danger is part of the texture of their existence. Predation would remain. Dryness would remain. Small deaths would remain. But nothing would be allowed to erase the whole. No accident would be permitted to become extinction. No intrusion would be allowed to break the pocket open before it has chosen what it wants to become.

This covenant would be written nowhere.

Or rather, it would be written in the only way the pocket permits: in faint mineral stains, in salt crystals lifting by fractions, in the timing of condensation, in the way a drop trembles before falling. If the covenant had a shape, it would be a spiral with an open center. If it had a sound, it would be the tick of a crystal releasing tension. If it had a feeling, it would be the sensation of being protected without being imprisoned.

The power might also give the Marlborn one gift that seems small but is enormous.

It would give them the ability to survive being noticed.

Not fully. Not easily. The pocket would still contract under direct attention. It would still hide from grasping minds. But the power would ensure that if the Marlborn are ever perceived, the perception would not destroy them. It would soften the impact of being seen. It would make the shock of outside attention less like burning and more like weather. It would let them withdraw without losing themselves. It would let them return without feeling violated.

This is a subtle mercy. For fragile beings, being seen can be as dangerous as being touched.

The power would know that.

It would also know what not to give.

It would not give speech. Speech would change them too much. It would create separation between the one who speaks and the one who hears. It would make them capable of lying, of commanding, of building histories that harden into hierarchy. It would not make them better; it would make them different in a way that could unravel the quiet field of their existence.

It would not give tools. Tools would make them efficient, and efficiency might destroy the delicate balance of scarcity and patience by which they live. Their constructions are beautiful precisely because they are made from secretion, instinct, repetition, and need. If they became makers in the surface sense, they would lose something essential about their firstness.

It would not give them a leader. A leader would concentrate their being into a single point, and their nature is to remain distributed. They are already one in the way that matters. They do not require a head. Their attention is already everywhere and nowhere: in the mites, the nymphs, the pools, the fungal stains, the rootlets, the predators, the prey.

If the power wished to help them become more themselves, it would not give them a center. It would give them coherence without center.

Perhaps it would do this by strengthening the pulses.

Not making them more frequent. Only making them more recognizable. When moisture returns, every scattered part of the Marlborn would feel it at once, not as a command but as a shared possibility. When dryness comes, they would withdraw together, not as a single organism obeying itself, but as a field of lives entering the same rest. They would remain many, but they would not be alone in their rhythm.

That may be the closest thing to unity they desire.

The power would also pay attention to the pocket’s reflections.

Those strange temporary pools that show absent skies, pale grass, ceilings that are not ceilings — these would matter. Not because they are false, but because they reveal that the pocket is not entirely closed. It leaks imagination. It leaks future. It leaks memory that may not yet have happened.

A wise power would not close those leaks. It would guard them.

It would ensure that when a pool reflects something outside, the reflection does not become a wound. It would keep the pocket from being flooded by other places. It would keep those images from becoming invasive. But it would let them remain as faint possibilities, like dreams in a mind that has not yet learned to dream consciously.

Perhaps, in some distant age, those reflections will teach the pocket what it could become. Not by imitation, but by resonance. The Marlborn may look into a brief pool and see a larger cavern, or a shaft of true light, or a body of water that lasts longer than a pulse. They may not want it. They may turn away. If they do, the power would not force the vision upon them. It would simply let the possibility wait.

Benevolence is not only giving. Sometimes it is withholding.

The power would also have to decide what to do with the pocket’s beauty.

This is a delicate question. The pocket is beautiful, but its beauty is not for display. It is not ornamental. It is not meant to be admired from the outside. If it became known, many would want to see it. Many would want to preserve it by exposing it. Many would want to love it by bringing light, cameras, hands, names.

The power would refuse.

It would let the pocket remain difficult to find. It would let the entrance remain uncertain. It would let the geometry remain unstable enough to discourage possession. It would not make the place easier to enter, because ease of entry would invite violation. It would not make the creatures easier to observe, because observation without relationship is a kind of taking.
