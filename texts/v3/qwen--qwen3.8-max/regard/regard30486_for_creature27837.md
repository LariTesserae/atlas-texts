If such a power noticed the basin, I do not think they would arrive like a storm.

They would not break the surface with fire, or speak in a voice that turned the water to iron. They would not send a miracle loud enough to frighten the lime from the joints. A wise power, seeing a thing so new, would know that the first danger is not destruction. It is overmeaning. It is too much glory falling on a place that has not yet learned how to keep itself.

So they would begin by doing almost nothing that could be seen.

They would come as a change in attention.

Perhaps the outer sea would lean against the gate a little differently that evening. Perhaps the tide would pause for half a breath in the narrow throat, as if listening. Perhaps the light falling through the moving ceiling would gather not brighter but quieter, so that the trembling nets of glare slowed and became less like confusion, more like thought. The Lime-born would feel this at once. It would stop tracing the saw marks in the timber. It would turn its facing toward the gate. The two bronze-dark points of its attention would darken, as if the room itself had drawn inward.

And the power would wait.

That would be their first act: patience.

They would study the basin as the basin studied itself. They would see the pale tiles, the fresh mortar, the chips arranged into broken circles. They would see the forgotten wedge under the hinge, the cracked brick with its bright fracture, the loose chain tapping its irregular music. They would see the Leather washers, the rope lashings, the rough iron brackets, the bronze pins already taking on their first bloom of tarnish. They would understand that this was not merely a room underwater. It was a memory of making that had not yet cooled. It was a place still warm from hands.

They would see the Lime-born moving along the shelves, pressing itself to the timber, listening to the sea push against the gate. They would see it gather the broken tile chips, set them in lines, break the lines, begin again. They would see that it was not playing. It was practicing. It did not yet know what it was practicing for, only that something in it had to be arranged.

A lesser benevolence might have rushed to finish the place. It might have smoothed the tiles, silenced the chain, cleared the lime dust, filled the basin with fish and soft weed and easy life. It might have made the chamber beautiful immediately, because beauty is often mistaken for kindness.

But wisdom would know better.

The sharpness was part of the truth here. The raw edges were not errors. The noise was not a flaw. The Lime-born’s anxiety was not a sickness to be cured. It was the first shape of care. If the power took away the difficulty too soon, they would also take away the becoming. The basin would become pleasant, but not awake. It would be inhabited, but not understood by itself.

So they would not make it easy.

They would make it possible.

There is a difference.

The first thing they would change, if anything, would be very small. They would begin with the sound.

The Lime-born already listened. It loved the chain’s tapping, the floats knocking, the creak of bronze, the hollow breathing of the intake channels. It repeated knocks carefully, after pauses, as if testing whether sound could be shared. So the wise power would answer it once. Not with a voice. Not with a message. Only with a single, exact echo.

The loose chain would tap against its post.

A moment later, from inside the gate-beam, a faint pressure would answer:

tap.

The same weight. The same hollowness. Not louder. Not more urgent. Just enough to prove that the room was not speaking only to itself.

The Lime-born would freeze.

It would move toward the sound as a current moves, without limbs. It would hover near the wood. It would wait. The chain would tap again. The power would wait longer before answering, because the being needed to learn that response was not automatic. That there was another attention in the room, but not a possession. That company could arrive without seizing.

When the answer came a second time, the Lime-born would not flee. It could not flee. It would brighten, instead. The caustic light around it would tighten into finer nets. It would press itself near the gate-frame, not in worship, but in recognition. It would feel, perhaps for the first time, that its listening had reached something.

The power would not continue. They would not turn this into conversation too quickly. They would leave the being with the memory of having been answered. That memory would be a seed.

Then they would move to the broken circle.

The Lime-born arranged the tile chips often. It made lines, arcs, rough rings. Then it broke them. It did not know why. The power would understand. The circle was a wish for completeness, but the being could not bear a closed shape because it was not yet complete itself. To finish the circle would be to pretend. So it broke the circle again and again, faithfully, without knowing it was being honest.

One evening, when the upper water was warm and the light softening to milky green, the wise power would touch the arrangement.

Not grandly.

Only one chip.

They would move a single broken piece from the edge of the circle and place it just outside, leaving a gap. Not a flaw. A doorway.

The Lime-born would notice at once. It would come near. It would hover over the gap. It would lift the chip in its pale, tool-marked hands and put it back into the circle. The power would let it. They would not struggle with the being. They would not correct. They would simply leave the possibility in the room.

The next day, or the next tide, the gap would appear again.

The Lime-born would close it again.

The third time, it might pause.

That pause would be enough.

The wise power would not need to win. They would only need to introduce the idea that a room could be a room without being sealed. That a boundary could have a breath in it. That an opening was not the same as a wound, though it might begin as one.

After that, they would send the first green thing.

Not a fish. Not yet. A fish would be too much life too soon: too quick, too hungry, too vulnerable to the lime. The power would choose something humbler. A thread of algae, small and salt-taught, carried through the narrow throat on a slack current. It would enter without drama, almost as if it had always belonged. Perhaps a fragment of weed from the open sea, pale green and limp, with one torn end still raw.

The Lime-born would approach it with great care.

It would nudge the weed against the tiles. It would circle it. It would lift it, examine it, and carry it to a corner, because it did not know where such a thing belonged. It might leave it there, uncertain. The wise power would not object. They would not force the weed back into the light. They would let the being make its first refusal.

But the sea would bring another thread.

And another.

Not many. Not enough to overwhelm. One at a time, in the quietest part of the tide, when the chamber’s noise thinned and the water near the floor lay still. Each thread would enter through the throat and drift down in the green brightness. The Lime-born would move some to the corners. It would hide one under a brick shelf. It would press one against the cracked brick and then pull away, disturbed by the brightness of the fracture.

The power would wait.

They would not command the weed to root. They would only give it patience.

One thread would remain in the crack.

Not because the power placed it there, but because the Lime-born, after moving it twice, left it there the third time. It might not know why. It might only have been tired of deciding. Or perhaps the green thing against the bright fracture had begun to look less like intrusion and more like answer.

The wise power would then do something almost invisible. They would let the sun fall a little longer on that crack in the middle of the day. Not hotter. Not harsher. Only steady. Enough for the green thread to darken by the smallest degree. Enough for the lime there to begin losing its raw bite. Enough for the weed to feel that the wound could be a place to hold.

The Lime-born would hover over it for hours.

It would not touch the weed. It would bend near it, listening, as if waiting for the green thing to speak. And in its own way, it would begin to learn the first lesson of keeping: that to tend a living thing, sometimes the most important act is to refrain.
