If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention to that high town, the first thing they would do is not act.

They would look.

They would look at the basin with the patience the basin itself has taught its keepers. They would see the gray lines in the cliff, the seam where the gate is set, the terraces worn smooth by careful use. They would see the basins, the gutters, the downspouts, the little ceramic pipes and carved stone channels leading everything toward storage. They would see that the place is not poor because it lacks rivers, and not lonely because it lacks crowds. They would see that it is a life made exact by limits.

Then they would look for the being who lives in it.

Perhaps they would find one person more than any other: a keeper who wakes before light, whose breath is visible in the cold, whose hand knows the temperature of a cistern door by touch. Perhaps they would find several such people, moving quietly from roof to gutter, from gutter to basin, from basin to covered jar. Perhaps, if their sight is deeper than human reckoning, they would see the town itself as a single being: a body of stone, water, habit, and attention, breathing slowly through fog and drought.
