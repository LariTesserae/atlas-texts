They would probably begin by doing nothing that could be seen.

A benevolent and wise power, coming upon such a place, would not arrive as a hand from the sky, nor as a voice commanding order into the green. It would not descend with tools, because tools would be an insult to what the basin had already accomplished without them. It would not bring fire, because fire would be a foreign grammar. It would not send animals, because hunger, eyes, and hearts beating in separate chests would shatter the very purity of the place. It would not impose silence, because the noise is not mere noise. It is society. It is governance. It is thought moving through tissue.

The first act of such a power would be restraint.

Not indifference. Not coldness. Restraint born of understanding.

It would look into the basin and see that the place is not a failed city waiting to become a human city. It is not a garden waiting for a gardener. It is not a wilderness waiting to be tamed. It is not a body waiting for a soul. It is already a civilization of its own kind, complete in its own terms, severe and tender and excessive, monstrous in its fertility, delicate in its negotiations, loud because it has discovered that pressure and vibration can become a way of being together.

A wise power would recognize that the greatest violence is sometimes the desire to improve something into the image of the improver.

So they would refrain.

They would not give the city hands.

They would not give it a face.

They would not give it a human mind, because a human mind might make the city ashamed of its root-mind, its distributed mind, its mind of scars and water and repeated arches. They would not make it quieter merely because loudness unsettles gentler forms of life. They would not make it still merely because stillness is easier to admire. They would not make it stable by stopping its becoming, because to stop its becoming would be to kill it.

Instead, they would listen.

They would listen for a long time.

They would listen until they understood the city’s grammar: that a creaking stem is a question; that a sudden clap of leaves is a decision; that a hollow boom is a public announcement; that a healed fissure is a memory; that a graft is a treaty; that a closed pore is grief; that an open water-cup is hope; that a street kept open by mutual suppression is a kind of civic vow; that a collapse is not failure but a change of sentence. They would listen until they knew which sounds mean hunger for water, which mean strain, which mean excess, which mean that a district is about to become something else.

Only then would they act.

And even then, they would act in the way that wisdom always acts when it truly loves an alien thing: not by remaking it, but by giving it more of itself.

---

The first gift would be a pause.

Not silence.

Not emptiness.

Not a void.

A pause.

A living interval inserted between impulse and growth.

The city is always in the middle of becoming. It tears open, heals, overcorrects, forgets, remembers too much, repeats itself into strangeness. A benevolent power would see that such a being does not need to be stopped. It needs room to choose. So they would give it a breath between signals: a moment after a tower booms but before the district sprouts; a moment after a fissure opens but before the root-flesh rushes to close it; a moment after a pattern repeats but before it becomes compulsion.

This pause would be almost invisible.

It might feel like a slight thickening of the air.

It might feel like the basin inhaling and not immediately exhaling.

It might feel like the briefest slackening in the tension-vines, the faintest drooping of condensation-fronds, the softest stilling of leaf-vents. But within that pause, the city would gain something immense: the ability to refuse one of its own possibilities.

That would be the beginning of freedom.

Not freedom from growth.

Freedom within growth.

---

They would also give the city a gentler rhythm of time.

The place seems to exist in a kind of perpetual emergency of birth. Every rupture becomes architecture. Every death becomes substrate. Every excess becomes a new district. That is magnificent, but even magnificence can exhaust itself if it has no cradle. So the wise power would introduce, or reveal, a green twilight.

Not night as animals know it.

Not darkness as absence.

A lowering.

A softening of the ordinary light.

A time when the canopy folds inward just enough, when the hollow towers lower their voices, when water collects more slowly, when the newest shoots do not tear forth but swell quietly beneath older tissue. The city would not sleep, because it has no animal need for sleep. But it would rest in the way a river rests in a wide pool. It would dream in the way a root-system dreams: by redistributing its memories, loosening its fixed patterns, letting its possible futures pass through it without forcing any one of them to become flesh immediately.

This twilight would be a mercy.

It would teach the city that not every moment must be a birth cry.

---

Then they would touch the scars.

The city is covered in glossy healed splits, pale new tissue, repaired ruptures, thickened wounds. Those scars are its history. They are load-bearing. They shape new growth. A wise power would not erase them. Erasure would be theft. The city’s past is part of its body. But wisdom would see that some scars have become tyrants: old injuries dictating too much, old collapses forcing endless repetition, old fears encoded in chemical gradients long after the danger has passed.

So they would make memory merciful.

Not forgetful.

Merciful.

The scars would remain, but they would cease to command. They would become archives instead of wardens. The city would remember a bridge that collapsed without being compelled to rebuild the collapse. It would remember a street that closed without feeling the need to close every similar street. It would remember a tower that split without flinching every time a new tower rises. The past would still be present, but as teaching rather than chain.

This would be one of the deepest kindnesses.

For a being made of memory and growth, the most benevolent act is not to remove the past but to loosen its grip.

---

They would not seal the strangeness.

This is important.

The basin leaks into adjacent possibilities. Structures appear twice. Sounds arrive before their causes. Pools reflect older or younger versions of the place. Arches become more numerous than themselves. A street curves back behind its own beginning. A tower recedes as one approaches. These are not defects to be corrected. They are part of the city’s nature. It exists in a state of metaphysical youth, as if reality has not yet decided how literal it intends to be around it.

A foolish power would try to make the city obey ordinary causality.

A wise power would not.

Instead, they would make the strangeness gentle.

They would turn the city’s leaks into windows rather than traps. The duplicate towers would become proposals, not hauntings. The sounds arriving early would become warnings that could be declined. The pools reflecting other versions of the basin would become places of discernment rather than confusion. The city would learn, in its own vegetable way, to distinguish between a possibility that should be grown into and a possibility that should be allowed to pass.

This would be the gift of consent.

The city would still be unstable. It would still be impossible by ordinary standards. But it would no longer be at the mercy of every adjacent version of itself. It would learn to say: yes, become this. No, not yet. No, never. Not this one.

And that would make all the difference.

---

They would deepen the noise without making it less loud.

The city is deafening. It is full of fibrous groans, hollow booms, clapping leaves, cracking seed-vessels, amplified drips, rushing sap, explosive growth. A lesser benevolence might want to hush it. But wisdom would understand that the noise is the city’s way of thinking together. To reduce it merely for comfort would be to lobotomize a form of society.

So they would not lower the volume.

They would increase the articulation.

The booms from the hollow towers would carry clearer intervals. The clattering volleys would become call and response. The percussive bursts of seed-vessels would become punctuation rather than panic. The dripping chambers would develop rhythms that help coordinate distant districts. The deep groans of root-platforms would become not just expressions of strain but signals of weight, need, and intention. The city would still be loud — magnificently, overwhelmingly loud — but its loudness would become more capable of meaning.

It would not become music in the human sense.

It would become music in its own sense.

A music of pressure.

A music of moisture.

A music of communal flesh deciding where to grow next.

---

They would give the city a way to see itself.

Not with eyes.

Not with a face.

Not with a central mind.

But with a commons of reflection.

Somewhere in the basin, perhaps where several root-networks cross and the sound is dense but not chaotic, they would help a pool form. It would not be large by human standards. It might be only a broad, still basin of water held in layered leaves, its surface glossy and green-black, surrounded by arches that lean inward as if listening. But this pool would differ from the others. The other pools reflect adjacent possibilities: older basins, younger basins, drier basins, flooded basins. This pool would reflect the city as it is now.

All of it.

Not perfectly.

Not from outside.

But together.

The pool would show the basin to itself in the way a body might suddenly feel its own whole posture. The city would not see a face in the water. It would see a pattern of tensions, open streets, water routes, acoustic corridors, healing wounds, rising towers, collapsing fronds, repeated arches, mobile root-fronts, and districts breathing against one another. It would see itself as a “we.”

That would be dangerous, if imposed.

But if it emerged gently, it would be grace.

For a being that is both singular and plural, self-recognition must not become centralization. The pool would not be a throne. No tower would rule from it. No district would claim it. It would be a place where the city’s many voices briefly feel the shape of their togetherness.

And perhaps, after seeing itself, the city would grow more kindly toward its own parts.

---

They would give the basin an edge.

Not a wall.

Not a prison.

An edge.

A horizon that says: you are real, and you are not everything.

The city, as described, is total and self-referential. It feeds on itself, speaks through itself, remembers through itself, builds itself out of itself. That is its grandeur, but it is also its danger. A system that contains everything can become solipsistic. It can fold inward until its own echoes become more real than its own substance. It can become so full of itself that it loses the ability to distinguish between growth and compulsion.

So a wise power would give it a margin.

Beyond the farthest root-mat, they might place a broad mineral expanse: a warm plain of pale stone, or glassy sediment, or still mist, or dark water too mineral for vegetation to claim. Not hostile. Not dead in a frightening way. Simply not-city. A place where the root-tips could reach the edge and feel no purchase, no nutrient, no invitation to continue. There, the city would encounter the simplest and most necessary truth for any living civilization: there is something else.

That edge would teach humility without shame.

It would say, without words: you are vast, but you are not all that is. You are complete, but you are not infinite. You are beloved, but you are not the only beloved thing.

And that would protect the city from its own totality.

---

If they judged the city ready, they would give it companionship.

Not by creating an alien being beside it.

Not by introducing animal life.

Not by making a human settlement at its border.

But by revealing, or perhaps awakening, another green city far away.

Another basin.

Another mode of vegetable urbanism.

Perhaps drier, with taller towers and slower rhythms. Perhaps wetter, with floating plazas and water-borne root-processions. Perhaps louder, perhaps almost silent, perhaps built more around light, perhaps more around resonance. It would not be identical. It must not be identical. Companionship requires difference. The two cities would not merge. They would not conquer. They would not graft immediately. At first, they would only know that somewhere beyond the horizon, another green multitude is also booming into the warm air.

The wise power might allow a deep acoustic channel between them: a water-filled root-conduit, a hollow atmospheric valley, a chain of resonant towers stretching far beyond sight. Through it, the cities could exchange signals slowly. A rhythm. A pattern for folding condensation-fronds. A way of stabilizing bridges. A memory of a successful plaza. A song of strain. A call of greeting so slow it might take years to arrive.

This would be one of the greatest gifts.

For even the most self-sufficient being can become imprisoned by its own completeness. Companionship would teach the city that there are other answers to the same questions. Other shapes. Other speeds. Other ways of being green and loud and alive.

And in learning another city, it might learn itself more gently.

---

They would protect the city from final ruin.

This is where benevolence must be clear.

Wisdom does not remove all danger. To remove all danger from such a place would be to remove its life. The city needs rupture, collapse, competition, overgrowth, and explosive growth. Those are not evils. They are its native processes. A tower that splits is not necessarily suffering. A plaza that is overgrown is not necessarily being murdered. A district that dissolves into a thicket is not necessarily dying. It is changing language.

But there is a difference between generative conflict and annihilation.

A wise power would guard against the latter.

They would keep fire from entering the basin. They would prevent blights that would turn every root into ashless rot. They would stop outside forces — if any existed — from introducing consuming animals, machines, or poisons. They would ensure that no single growth pattern became so dominant that it erased all other patterns. They would prevent the city’s strange temporal leaks from becoming total collapse, where past, present, and possible futures tangle beyond recovery. They would not forbid storms of growth, but they would forbid the storm that leaves nothing capable of growing again.

Protection, not sterilization.

Safety, not stasis.

---

They would teach the city the principle of enough.

Not cessation.

Not austerity.

Not the end of growth.

But enough.

The city is excessive. It creates and destroys with a metabolism turned to an extreme. That excess is part of its beauty. Yet even excess needs wisdom, or it becomes self-consumption. So the benevolent power would introduce, through water, through vibration, through the slow chemistry of the root-commons, a sense of sufficiency.

A street need not become every street.

A tower need not repeat until it becomes a forest.

A plaza need not be shaded the moment it shines.

A bridge need not thicken until it collapses under its own strength.

A pattern can be beautiful without becoming total.

This would be perhaps the hardest lesson for such a being. For a city made of pure growth, “enough” may feel like death at first. But wisdom would teach it slowly, in the only way the city can learn: by making enough feel fertile rather than barren. A pause that conserves water. An open space that improves resonance. A corridor left empty so that movement becomes easier. A district that refrains from sprouting so that another district can recover.

Enough would become not a limit, but a form of generosity.

---

They would bless the city.

Not with thunder.

Not with commandments.

Not with a religion.

With a blessing shaped like rain.

It might fall so gently that the city would think it was one of its own localized weathers. It would settle on the leaf-walls, slide down the tension-vines, collect in the pitcher-shaped wells, enter the capillary networks, and be carried through the whole basin. And in that water, there would be something like a wish. Not a wish imposed from outside, but a wish the city could recognize as its own deepest possibility.

The blessing might be something like this:

May your streets remain open not by suppression, but by generosity.

May your towers speak clearly enough for the roots to hear.

May your scars be teachers, not wardens.

May your collapses become thresholds, not tombs.

May your noise become language, and your language leave room for listening.

May your water be shared without being exhausted.

May your patterns repeat without becoming prisons.

May your many know themselves as one, and may the one never become a tyrant over the many.

May you grow without needing to devour yourself.

May you be strange and still be safe.

May you be loud and still be tender.

May you be beloved without becoming tame.

---

Then, perhaps, they would withdraw.

Not because they had abandoned the city.

But because the truest presence of wisdom is often indistinguishable from the flourishing of the thing loved.

The city would not need to see the power. It would not need to worship it. It would not need to build temples, because temples are not its way. It would not need to speak the power’s name, because it has no mouth for names. But the power would remain in the gifts: in the pause before sprouting, in the twilight that lets the basin breathe, in the merciful scars, in the pool of present truth, in the distant answering boom of another green city, in the mineral edge that teaches the city it is not everything.

The inhabitants would not become less alien.

They would become more fully themselves.

The root-fronts would still cross channels in slow processions. The towers would still boom. The leaf-vents would still open in sudden choruses. The bridges would still shed bark and become precarious before either stabilizing or falling. The plazas would still close like hands, then open again. The duplicate arches would still flicker beside the solid ones. The sounds would still sometimes arrive before their causes. The city would still be deafening, turbulent, unstable, young, and gloriously alive.

But there would be a new quality to it.

A kindness in its violence.

A discernment in its excess.

A breath in its roar.

A sense that its endless birth is not merely compulsion, but also joy.

And if, after a long age, one could ask what the benevolent and wise power had done, the answer would not be grand.

They would not have remade the city.

They would not have given it hands, or faces, or human sorrow, or human triumph.

They would not have saved it from being a plant-city.

They would have saved it from being only itself in the cruelest sense.

They would have helped the green multitude become a self without forcing it to become a single self. They would have helped its civilization remain wild while gaining mercy. They would have let it be strange, loud, root-bound, water-thirsty, scar-covered, and impossible — but also, in its own way, wise.

And perhaps that is what such a power would do, if anything.

Not conquer.

Not correct.

Not complete.

Only bless.

Only protect.

Only make room.

Only say, without words, to the living city:

You are not a problem to be solved.

You are a form of being to be cherished.

Grow.
