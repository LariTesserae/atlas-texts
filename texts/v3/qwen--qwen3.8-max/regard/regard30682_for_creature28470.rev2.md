If such a power noticed the high green plain, the first thing they would do is not arrive.

Not in fire. Not in a shape larger than the horizon. Not in a voice that flattened the insect chorus into silence, or a light that made the pools glare white, or a presence that pressed the living mat down like a hand laid too heavily on a sleeping body. They would know, at once, that the plain had been made by patience and that patience could be wounded. A god who descended there in majesty would be a disaster. Even blessing, if it came with weight, would break the surface.

So they would come, if they came at all, as attention.

They would listen first.

They would hear the roar of small lives: the rasp and click and shimmering wing-noise, the wet cries from hidden water, the harsh bird calls carrying across the open green, the sudden hushes that meant something with appetite was moving through the felt. They would hear the plain not as a place of peace, but as a place of continual exchange, and they would not mistake that for suffering. They would recognize the sound for what it was: a body speaking with many mouths. A metabolism. A balance that had no need to be beautiful in any human sense, because it had already achieved something rarer: continuity.

They would see the people too. The small camps. The raised platforms. The carried fire. The paths that were barely paths. The way they moved with testing steps, making noise, leaving little behind. They would see that the people were not separate from the plain’s being but one of its conscious filaments, thin and temporary and necessary. They would see the shelters already being approached by the green world that would take them back. They would see the bones of old camps sinking into the leaf-mat like thoughts forgotten.

And they would understand what lived there.

Not one being. Not a single figure standing against the horizon. Not a spirit with a face and a name that could be carved. The being of that place was plural and distributed: the plant-roof breathing under sun; the water held in cups and lips and sponge-dark mats; the insects rising in clouds; the frogs calling from warm shallows; the birds following rain; the decomposers working death into architecture in the hidden middle layers; the predator moving low through the quiet it made; the people passing lightly through all of it with carried tools and practiced reverence. The being was the pressure of all those lives leaning on one another. It was the plain’s own ancient habit of remaining.

A lesser benevolence might have tried to improve it.

A lesser power might have looked at the loudness and wanted to quiet it. Looked at the predation and wanted to end it. Looked at the people and wanted to give them houses of stone, fields with borders, storehouses, roads, monuments, laws engraved in something durable. It might have wanted to make the pools inexhaustible, the insects harmless, the softstep gentle, the air free of hunger. It might have wanted to rescue the plain from itself and turn it into a paradise that could be admired.

But wisdom would know better.

They would not silence the plain. To silence it would be to cut the threads that held it together. The noise was not disturbance; it was recognition. Every cry, rasp, splash, alarm, and wingbeat was a life announcing itself into the whole. Remove that, and the plain would become a museum of green forms with no pulse inside.

They would not end predation. The softstep, the wasps, the birds taking larvae, the beetles working carrion, the hunger moving from body to body — all of this was part of the breathing. Without appetite, the living roof would choke on its own abundance. Death was not an insult there. It was the lower floor of the house.

They would not make the people numerous. That would be a cruel gift. The plain tolerated presence, not pressure. If they became many, they would become heavy. If they became heavy, they would wound the mat, foul the pools, empty the hatches, break the old lightness that allowed them to belong there. A wise kindness would not bless them into becoming a plague.

They would not reveal themselves openly. A visible god would change everything. It would create kneeling. It would create temples. It would create fear, obligation, rivalry, permanence. People would begin to build for the god, and building for the god would become another way of wounding the ground. The plain did not need worship. It needed restraint.

So if anything, they would do this:

They would keep.

Not rule. Not correct. Not remake. Keep.

They would hold the conditions that allowed the plain to continue being itself. They would keep the warmth resting in the living surface. They would keep the clouds moving below the rim. They would keep the rain arriving in small pulses, enough to bead on leaves and fill the cups, not enough to drown the mat. They would keep the dew gathering at dawn, the mist pooling in hollows, the sun migrating across the wide green back of the world. They would keep the ancient softness from hardening into stone again. They would keep the floor alive.

Their first miracle would be continuation.

The second would be obscurity.

They would hide the plain, not with walls or storms, but with unimportance. They would let the lower world forget it. They would make maps uncertain around its edges. They would let travelers speak of it as rumor, then forget the exact way. They would make it difficult for greed to find a use for it. What use was a place with no stone to quarry, no metal to dig, no river to dam, no forest to fell, no wide flat road for armies? What use was a roof of living tissue that would not support monuments?

They would let empires dismiss it. Let conquerors look elsewhere. Let merchants see no profit in it. Let those who measured the world by weight pass over it in search of heavier things.

But if someone came with the intention to own, they would not strike them down. They would simply let the plain be itself, intensified.

Heavy boots would sink. Metal would rust faster in the damp air. Fires lit for clearing would smolder and die under sudden mists. Pack animals would grow uneasy and refuse to go farther. Tools would fail in small, discouraging ways. The insect sound would become a pressure inside the skull. The openness would begin to feel not free but immense and indifferent. The would-be owner would feel, without knowing why, that he was not standing on land but on something that was aware of his boots.

He would leave, if he was able. If he stayed, the plain would begin to teach him by inconvenience. It would humble him without malice. It would make him learn where the ground was firm, where the water could be taken, where silence was dangerous, where his voice was needed. It would make him smaller until he could walk lightly enough to remain.

That too would be mercy.

For the people already born into the plain’s terms, the power would give gifts that looked like skill.

They would sharpen hearing. They would let children distinguish one kind of quiet from another before they had words for it. They would give a dream of water when a camp was near the end of its supply. They would guide a hand to the right stem, the right fiber, the right knot. They would make cordage hold a little better. They would make carried fire behave itself in its wet tray. They would let smoke rise straight when a signal was needed, and scatter harmlessly when it was not.

They would bless the senses without blessing the body with heaviness.

A woman testing the ground with her pole might feel, just once, a certainty in the place where the mat would bear her. A man listening to the night chorus might suddenly hear the wrongness in it — a gap, a hesitation, a patch of silence shaped like danger — and know to move his family before dawn. A child might wake from sleep with the memory of a song that led back to camp. None of them would call it divine. They would only say the plain had spoken, or the weather was turning, or the sound was thin tonight.

That would be enough.

They would not give prophecy. They would not give commandments written in imperishable material. If they gave law, it would be felt rather than proclaimed:

Take only what is abundant.  
Move before the place remembers your weight.  
Leave no black scar.  
Do not foul the water that others will drink.  
Make noise, because silence is a warning.  
Carry your dead lightly into the exchange.  
Do not try to stay forever.

These would not be spoken from the sky. They would live in practice. They would be passed from hand to hand, voice to voice, footstep to footstep. The power would not need to announce them, because the plain already taught them to anyone willing to be shaped by it.

They would be especially careful with fire.

Fire was the one guest that could become a monster there. The living floor was fuel held in check by dampness and care. So the wise power would watch over flame as a mother watches over a sharp tool in a child’s hand. Not forbid it. Fire was needed: for warmth, for drying, for light, for keeping the night’s larger appetites at a distance. But they would make the people remember it. They would let the memory of careless flame become a story told around the contained hearth. They would give the hearth-trays a strange reliability, the wet plant matter inside them resisting ignition just enough. They would make sparks reluctant to leap. They would make the first breath of wind at the wrong moment feel like a slap.

If a fire began out of malice, the plain would answer.

Mist would come from hollows where no mist should be. Rain would gather despite the empty sky. The insect clouds would rise in confusing sheets. The ground underfoot would soften and slow pursuit. Birds would cry alarm until the air itself seemed to reject the act. The fire would be met not by wrath but by a thousand small refusals.

For the creatures, the power’s kindness would be harder to recognize.

They would not save the tadpole from the beetle. They would not keep the bird’s hunger from the frog. They would not make the softstep lie down beside its food. That would not be goodness; it would be interference in the old bargain by which the plain remained whole. But they would not be entirely unmoved by pain.

They would reduce waste.

When a creature died without use — trapped by accident, wounded by stupidity, killed by something that left it uneaten — they would hasten the small cleaners to it. They would make beetles find it, flies locate it, the hidden decomposers draw it down into the mat before it became only rot. They would let death return quickly to the living roof.

They would help the old predator when its hunger had become too great for its body. Not by giving it easy prey, but by guiding it toward what was already weakened, already sick, already near the end of its time. If it killed one last creature before dying, that too would be part of the pattern. If it died in the mat, its body would become a nursery, a feeding place, a small rise of darker growth where moisture gathered. The plain would close over it. The power would see that happen and call it no tragedy.

In drought, they would not create rivers.

That would break the plain’s nature. Instead, they would preserve one pool longer than expected. They would lead a family to a depression hidden under broad leaves where condensation still collected in the cold of early morning. They would let the people find enough for one more day, then one more, then enough to move. They would make scarcity survivable without making it meaningless.

In great hatches, they would not increase abundance beyond reason, but they would let the people recognize it. They would let the air itself seem to say: now. Now take what can be dried. Now gather while the plain is overflowing. Now move to the basin where the birds are turning. Now make enough for the next stretch of lack.

The power’s action would often look like timing.

A rain arriving just before the pools shrank too far. A cloud shadow passing over a hunter at the right moment. A silence falling just long enough for someone to hear the sound of breaking stems. A seed-case rattling in the wind, drawing a lost child toward the direction of home. A star visible through a gap in mist when a traveler needed to know where north lay. Nothing impossible. Nothing that could be proven. Only the world leaning, ever so slightly, toward continuation.

Sometimes they would let an outsider come close.

Not every outsider would be refused. Some might climb from the lower world exhausted, empty-handed, no longer wanting to take but only to rest. To such a one, the plain would not become gentle. It would remain what it was. But the power might let the person find firm ground. Might let them discover a clear pool. Might let them hear the first chorus at dawn and feel, instead of fear, an unexpected widening in the chest.

If the outsider stayed, they would have to learn.

They would learn to walk slowly. To test each step. To drink without muddying. To hear the difference between ordinary noise and warning. To leave no permanent mark. To carry fire in a wet tray. To become less impressive. To become lighter. The power would give them no special protection, because special protection would teach them the wrong lesson. It would give them only the chance to be taught by the plain itself.

If they learned, they might be allowed to grow old there. If they did not, they would either leave or disappear into the soft places, and the mat would rise over them as it rose over everything.

The people might sense, at times, that they were being held.

Not watched like subjects. Not judged like petitioners. Held. The way a sleeping child is held by a room that has no need to wake it. They would feel it in certain hours: when the wind crossed the leaf-felt in one long stroke and the whole plain seemed to inhale; when the night chorus became so dense it pressed against the body; when a camp fell quiet together and the silence was not empty but full of listening. They might say, “The roof is awake tonight.” Or, “The plain remembers us.” They would not mean it as theology. They would mean it the way one means weather.

If they prayed, they would pray lightly.

They might leave a drop of water in a leaf-cup not because the power needed it, but because it was good to give back moisture to the place that gave moisture. They might untie a worn cord from a shelter and press it into the mat as a returning. They might sing short songs with no words, only breath and pitch, matching the chorus. They might teach children to place their feet without stabbing the surface. These would be their rites. No altars. No sacrifices meant to purchase favor. No names carved into anything that could last. The deepest worship would be skillful living.

The dead would be the clearest sign of the power’s wisdom.

They would not grant immortality. That would be the cruelest thing possible in a place built on layers of dying and growing. The people would still die. Children would still die. The old would still dry into bones on raised platforms while birds and insects did their work. But the power would make the final exchange bearable.

It would not be horror.

The body would be taken back quickly enough to avoid sickness, slowly enough for the living to see the shape of the departure. Bones would clean in wind and rain. The remaining family would carry what needed carrying and leave what belonged to the place. No grave would be dug. No stone raised. The dead would enter the plain not as monuments but as participation. Later, someone might pass a darker patch of growth where a platform had stood and know, without grief, that life had thickened there.

The power would remember them, even if the plain forgot.

That might be the strangest work they would do: memory.

Every hatch that rose in a column of wings. Every frog that called until its throat was raw. Every bird killed mid-swoop. Every larva eaten in the cloudy nurseries. Every footprint pressed into the springy mat. Every shelter abandoned to the rain. Every person who had ever walked there with enough lightness to leave no scar. The power would hold all of it, not to reward or punish, but because nothing that belonged to the whole should be entirely unremembered.

They would be the witness the plain did not require but perhaps deserved.

And if, in the end, they did anything that could be called a sign, it would be this:

At certain rare moments, when the light was clear and the clouds moved below the rim and the noise of the plain rose into a single vast chord, the people might feel that the world was not indifferent to them. Not that it loved them in a human way. Not that it chose them. Not that it promised them safety. Only that it permitted them. That their small carefulness mattered. That their lightness was seen. That they were, for a little while, part of an ancient living body that breathed under their feet.

They would not fall to their knees. Falling too hard would bruise the mat.

They would only stand still for a moment, listening. Then they would tap their poles, call softly to one another, and move on.

The power would not follow. It would remain as the condition around them: the dew at dawn, the warm shallow pools, the insect shimmer, the sudden silences, the paths that closed behind the walker. It would not be king of the high green plain. It would not be shepherd. It would be more like the kindness that keeps a world from being violated before its time.

And the plain would go on.

Loud. Old. Green to the edge of the sky. Built not from stone but from the layered bodies of plants. Maintained by appetite. Tolerant of brief guests. Unowned. The people would pass across it. The predator would move through the hush it made. The insects would rise after rain. The pools would fill and shrink. The shelters would vanish. The dead would be received.

The wise power would do no more than this, and no less:

let the living roof keep breathing.
