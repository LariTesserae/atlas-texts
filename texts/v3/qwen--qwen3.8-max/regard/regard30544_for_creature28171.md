They thin. They pass through a living skin and take what the skin can spare; their feeding is another kind of pruning.

That is the place. That is the being—or the beings—within it: a drowned highland not yet fully settled into itself, and a green, slow people made almost of the same water and leaf-light. They are not a nation, not a city, not a tribe in any human sense. They are a distributed tenderness, a set of habits that have become personlike: mending, clinging, adjusting, remembering by growth.

If such a place and such beings drew the attention of a benevolent and wise power, the first thing that power would do is not arrive.

It would attend.

It would not break the sky open. It would not descend with trumpet-voices or the machinery of revelation. A wise power, seeing a world so young and so delicate, would know that intrusion can be a kind of violence. It would therefore come first as a quality of attention: a steadiness in the light, a deepening of the water’s hush, a moment in which the lake seems more present to itself than it has ever been.

It would look, and its looking would be gentle.

It would perceive the basin not as a failed landscape, not as an unfinished ocean, not as a swamp pretending to be a sea, but as what it is: a new country of water and stone, still learning its own contours. It would see the black fault steps, the lava ropes, the pumice shoals, the mineral films, the hot springs breathing their slow heat into the green dark. It would notice that nothing here is empty. It would understand that the absence of cities does not mean the absence of meaning.

Then it would perceive the canopy-keepers.

It would see them moving through the hanging roots with their pale mineral eyes, their leaf-dark skin, their slow amber bladders rising and falling like thoughts made buoyant. It would see that they are not merely animals moving through scenery. They are participants in the lake’s becoming. They are the place beginning to touch itself, to repair itself, to prefer one thing over another.

A benevolent and wise power would not ask, “How can I make them like humans?”

It would ask, “What does this place already wish to become?”

And because it is wise, it would listen long before answering.

It would listen through heat gradients and silica threads, through oxygen pockets and root pressure, through the slow chemical conversations of microbial mats. It would feel where the water is too sour, where a vent is too sharp, where a fault still breathes with dangerous heat. It would notice where the floating ceiling thins after storms, where the drift-gardens wander without shelter, where the terrace-builders are building too slowly to hold a shelf against a coming season of ash.

It would not judge the lake for being strange. It would love the strangeness.

Then, at last, it would act.

But its actions would be subtle. They would be the kind of actions that, from a distance, might be mistaken for nature. From within, they would feel like grace.

---

### First, it would make the basin safer without making it still.

The lake is young, and young landscapes are violent in their own way. The earth under it is not yet quiet. Faults are raw. Heat moves through new channels. Volcanic rock, though sealed, may still crack, shift, breathe. A wise power would not remove all danger; that would be to rob the place of its own development. But it would lessen the likelihood of annihilation.

It would lay something like a hand beneath the basin—not to stop the earth from moving, but to slow the movement where it might become catastrophic. It would encourage magma to find deep, patient paths rather than sudden ones. It would guide heat into gentle vents rather than explosive seams. Where a dangerous crack threatened to drain the lake, it would favor mineral deposition: silica settling, iron crusts forming, microbial films binding grains until the wound closed in stone and living tissue.

It would not make the mountains tame. The peaks would remain sharp, black, cloud-bitten. The basin would remain high, isolated, severe. But the deepest disasters would become less likely. The lake would be given time.

That alone would be a great gift.

Time is the first kindness any young world needs.

---

### Second, it would temper the chemistry without removing the lake’s character.

The water is generous, but severe. Heat, silica, mineral salts, uneven oxygen, organic mucilage: these are gifts and dangers at once. A benevolent power would not make the lake into a mild lowland pond. It would know that the canopy-keepers and the microbial towns are adapted to this richness. To remove all severity would be to erase them.

Instead, it would soften the extremes.

It would encourage buffering minerals to rise in trace amounts, enough to keep the water from swinging too sharply into acid or alkali. It would favor microbial lineages that detoxify iron and sulfur, that release oxygen in stable pockets, that bind heavy metals into harmless crusts. It would guide silica toward usefulness—into supportive tissues, terrace-builders’ ribs, the fine veins that strengthen root-fibers—rather than into suffocating films.

It would make certain that the warm springs remained warm, but not often scalding. It would create cool refuges in fault shadows where life could retreat if a vent grew too strong. It would let the upper water remain sun-warmed and bright, but send clouds now and then to shade it when high-altitude light became too sharp.

The lake would still be strange. It would still be mineral-thick, buoyant, alive with dissolved substances. But its strangeness would become more survivable.

A wise power does not make suffering vanish. It makes suffering less likely to become ruin.

---

### Third, it would strengthen the living architecture.

The place has no buildings, no tools, no artifacts. Its architecture is growth: mats, roots, bladders, fronds, living crusts, drifting thickets. A benevolent power would honor that. It would not introduce dead construction. It would not give the beings hammers, knives, kilns, or walls. It would understand that to give them such things would be to change them into another kind of creature, and perhaps to wound their world.
