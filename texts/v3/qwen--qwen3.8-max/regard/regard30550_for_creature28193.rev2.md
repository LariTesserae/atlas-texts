If such a power turned toward that place, they would not begin by changing it.

They would begin by seeing it.

Not from above only, not as a view, not as a strange and melancholy city glimpsed through mist, but from within its own logic. They would see the water climbing as vapor, resting on stone, gathering into beads, falling into channels, feeding reservoirs, feeding engines, becoming heat again. They would see the crusts thickening over bolts and stair edges. They would see the microbial felts spread in rust-colored patches. They would see the pale filaments advance behind ceramic tiles. They would see the brine worms flash once in a lamp-lit pool and vanish into stillness. They would see the machines breathing without masters. They would see the hooded keepers pausing on catwalks, listening to pressure, reading damp, moving slowly because the place has taught them that haste is a kind of damage.

And they would not pity it.

That would be the first act of wisdom.

A lesser kindness might look at the hot, wet, empty basin and immediately wish it otherwise. It might say: Let there be sunlight. Let there be grass. Let there be birds. Let the streets fill with voices. Let the air clear. Let the stone dry. Let the city become easy.

But a benevolent and wise power would understand that to make the city easy in that way would be to unmake it.

Green would not be a gift there unless it were asked for, and even then it would have to be small, contained, respectful. Sweet rain would change the chemistry of crusts and films. Cool air would disturb the thermal gradients on which the small lives depend. Crowds would be violence to beings who have learned to live by being few. The city is not a failed garden. It is not a broken resort. It is not a machine waiting to be rescued into a more pleasant shape. It has achieved its own balance. It has become established. It has become itself.

So the power would do something far more difficult than remaking.

They would attend.

They would enter the place, if a power can be said to enter, not as a storm, not as a blinding figure, not as a voice from the unseen sky, but as a subtle change in the quality of attention. The mist might thin for a moment. The pearl light might strengthen just enough to reveal the whole basin in one soft glow. A drip might fall more slowly. A lamp might burn steadier. A pipe might give a clearer note when touched. Nothing dramatic. Nothing that would frighten the keepers or overwhelm the small fauna. Only a sense that the city has been noticed by something vast and gentle.

They would listen first.

They would learn the difference between the sounds there. The tick of condensation on warm stone. The muted knock of settling metal. The slow sigh of a vent. The almost subsonic throb of a pump working against deep pressure. The tiny scrape of a mite behind a crust. The soft footfall of a keeper testing a floor. They would learn which silences mean rest, which mean strain, which mean danger concealed beneath routine.

Then they would ask, in whatever language the place could receive:

*What do you need?*

Not: *What do you deserve?*  
Not: *What would make you beautiful to others?*  
Not: *What would make you easier to love?*

Only: *What do you need?*

And because the place is not simple, the answer would not be simple either.

If the being who lives there is the city itself — the whole slow organism of water, heat, stone, machine, crust, filament, and memory — then the power would not impose a mind upon it. They would not wake it into suffering or burden it with personhood. They would only give it, perhaps, one brief moment of self-recognition.

They might let the mist above a broad plaza thicken into a mirror. In that mirror, the city would see its own pattern: the circuits of water, the patience of pumps, the worn paths of absent feet, the small lives hidden in seams, the lamps burning where no one is expected. It would see that it is not an error. It would see that it is not merely abandoned. It would see that it has its own dignity.

And if the power asked, *Do you wish to continue?*

The answer would rise from every drain and valve and warm wall:

*Yes.*

Not to become another place.  
Not to become green.  
Not to become crowded.  
Not to become remembered by crowds.

Only to continue.

So the power would help it continue.

If the being is one of the keepers — a hooded figure with gloved hands, a condenser mask, eyes adjusted to the pearl-gray dark — the power would approach even more gently. They would not appear in a form that demands belief. They would not stand in the open and command. They would become something useful.

A dry handrail where all railings should be wet.

A lamp that brightens slightly when someone nears a dangerous floor.

A tapped pipe that answers back.

The keeper would notice. Of course they would notice. In that city, noticing is survival. They would stop. They would listen. They would test the moment with the same care they use to test a crust. They might not speak aloud, because speech is costly there, but they might tap once, questioningly, against a pipe.

And the power would answer with a single clear note, neither loud nor urgent.

*Yes.*

Then, if trust permitted, the power would ask:

*May I help?*

The keeper would probably not ask for a miracle.

They would ask for something practical.

They might say, in low words or in the tap-code of the pipes:

*Do not let the lower gallery flood when the pressure turns.*

Or:

*Let one cistern be safe to drink from without distilling.*

Or:

*Let a child cross the east platform without breaking through crust.*

Or:

*Let one of us hear another sometimes.*

And the wise power would honor those requests, because they are small and honest and born of long care.

They would not grant what would harm the place by granting too much.

They would not fill the city with food that would make cultivation meaningless. They would not make every floor safe so that no one remembers how to test stone. They would not remove all danger, because the city would cease to be the city if it became tame. But they would remove the cruel surprises. The hidden failures. The unnecessary deaths. The moments where patience is not enough and chance alone decides who is scalded, drowned, poisoned, or buried by a collapse no one could have foreseen.

They would do this.

In a buried pressure line that has been weakening for years, they would ease the strain. Not with a visible miracle, but by settling the metal, redistributing heat, letting a valve open a fraction sooner than before. No one would witness it. Later, a keeper would look at a gauge and find the needle calm, and feel, for reasons they could not name, less afraid.

They would make one cistern sweet.

Not every cistern. Not every pool. Not every dripping wall. That would destroy the knowledge and caution that keep the city alive. But one cistern would yield condensate clean enough to drink without fear, faintly cool, tasting of stone rather than rust. It would become a place where keepers occasionally meet without needing to admit that they came there for company. They would stand around it in their heavy coats, masks lifted, saying little. The water would give them an excuse to be near one another.

They would make one room dry.

Not a palace. Not a temple. A small chamber above the wettest level, with a cambered floor, a sealed ceiling, and benches of warm tile. The air there would be less thick. The walls would not weep. A keeper could sit there, remove a mask, and breathe without effort. It would not solve the city’s harshness. It would only prove that relief is not forbidden. That mercy can exist inside a place of stone and vapor without betraying it.

They would make danger more legible.

Brittle crusts would take on a faint bluish sheen before they fail. Overheated grates would give off a low, soft note, not an alarm, but enough to make an experienced keeper pause. A dangerous pressure rise would carry a faint clean smell, a metallic clarity, distinct from the usual damp. The power would not eliminate risk. They would eliminate treachery.

They would let sound carry in one network of pipes.

In a city where voices vanish and taps already mean things, they would tune certain conduits so that a careful signal could travel farther than before. Three taps: *I am here.* Two scrapes: *Unsafe.* One long note followed by silence: *I need help but do not come unless you can.* The keepers would still be few, but they would be less isolated. Loneliness is a kind of heat too, and a wise power would know that.

They would bless the food such as it is.

The microbial trays would be less likely to fail overnight. The brine pools would not suddenly turn toxic with a shift below. The pale filaments would grow where they can be harvested without poisoning the rooms. There would be no orchard, no grain, no loaf of bread from elsewhere, no green abundance. But hunger would become less arbitrary. Survival would be less a matter of caprice.

They would protect the minor fauna.

Not by making them numerous, because their rarity is part of the balance. But they would keep one pool as a nursery. They would keep one vent at a temperature that does not kill. They would make one lamp gentle enough that the rare moths are not burned by their attraction to it. If there are creatures in the lower dark, the power would not expose them. They would simply ensure that the deep systems do not crush them unknowingly. A wise kindness considers the smallest witnesses.

They would give memory without haunting.

In one wall of one courtyard, they would let touch bring the faint pressure of old footfalls. Not voices. Not faces. Not the unbearable return of the dead. Only the sense that many once passed there, and that their passing mattered. The keepers would understand, perhaps without words, that they are not the city’s only meaning. They are its current caretakers. The city remembers more than they know, and that memory is not accusation.

They would offer one door, but not force anyone through it.

Somewhere, a route would become safe enough to reach the basin edge, or another district, or a place beyond the mist. It would not be a command to leave. It would be proof that staying is not imprisonment. If the keepers choose to remain, their remaining would be a choice, and therefore a kind of freedom. If one among them longs for something else, the way would be possible.

And if they asked for green?

The power would hesitate.

They would know what green means elsewhere: shade, softness, the smell of leaves, the comfort of ordinary life. But they would also know that to drop a seed carelessly into that mineral city would be careless. Green there would not be innocent. It would bring soil, rot, shadow, competition, new needs, new failures. It might become a conqueror.

So they would offer a green so small it could be refused.

Perhaps a sealed chamber no larger than a closet, behind glass, where one true leaf grows in contained light, fed by distilled water and careful air. It would not spread. It would not alter the city. It would only exist as a reminder that other forms of life are possible. If the keepers found it painful, the chamber could be closed. If they found it precious, it could remain. The gift would not be green itself. The gift would be choice.

If they did not ask for green, the power would not leave them with a wound of longing.

They would give instead something suited to the place.

A crystal that grows very slowly in clean condensate and resembles a leaf without being one.

A piece of blue-glazed pipe that warms in the hand when someone else is thinking of them.

A lamp that burns with a steadier amber when no one is watching, as if company had been left behind.

A bell that rings only when another living being is near enough to help.

A mark on certain stone that means: *This holds.*

They would not demand worship.

They would not erect a shrine, or write commandments on the stone, or ask for a portion of the keepers’ scant food. If someone later leaves a polished crystal beside the sweet cistern, they would let it lie. If someone taps thanks into a pipe, they would let the sound travel once, gently, so the sender knows it was received. But they would not make a religion of themselves. Wisdom does not need a crowd to be wise. Benevolence does not need gratitude to be kind.

Most of all, they would not erase the being who lives there.

They would not make the keeper taller, louder, more numerous, more like people from easier places. They would not cure away the adaptations that make survival possible. If the being has learned to be minor, they would not treat minorness as a defect. They would honor it. They would make it possible to be minor without being crushed. They would make it possible to persist without despair.

If the being asked for nothing, the power would still do something, but only this much:

They would prevent one disaster the city could not survive.

And they would leave one sign that the city had been seen.

A lamp might burn for an hour with a clear white light.

A puddle might reflect not the mist but a shape of the whole basin, entire and beautiful.

A keeper might dream of a voice saying, *This is not a ruin,* and wake without remembering the words, yet carrying their weight.

That would be enough. Not because the power is limited, but because wisdom knows that too much goodness at once can become another kind of violence.

Afterward, the city would still be high, hot, wet, and mostly empty.

The stone would still sweat.

The mineral crusts would still bead along corners.

The pumps would still breathe.

There would still be no grass in the avenues, no vines on the pipe galleries, no birds, no soft green smell. The wise power would not have turned it into another world.

But something would be different.

The drips would fall with a little less menace.

The keepers would know where to find water that will not betray them.

A child would learn to test a crust and sometimes see it shine blue before danger.

A lonely worker would tap a pipe and, after a pause, hear an answer from far away.

A rare moth would circle a lamp and live.

A broken thing would fail at a time when hands are present to mend it.

The city would continue, but it would continue with one secret less: it would know, in whatever way a city of stone and vapor can know, that it is not beneath the care of the world.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

They would not save the city from being itself.

They would save it from needless cruelty.

They would not fill its emptiness with noise.

They would let its quiet become companionable.

They would not make green where green does not belong.

They would make belonging enough.

And perhaps, long afterward, when the mist thins and the basin takes on its pearl glow, someone standing on a high gallery would hear the whole place breathing — pump, drip, valve, filament, worm, lamp, masked breath — and understand that kindness had passed there.

Not as conquest.

Not as transformation.

As attention.

As care.

As maintenance made merciful.

Then the mist would close again, and the city would go on.
