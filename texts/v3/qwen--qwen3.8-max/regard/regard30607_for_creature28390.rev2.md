If such a power noticed the green hollow, they would not enter as a god enters a temple.

They would not brighten the air. They would not make the walls speak. They would not descend in a form that forced the frogs into silence, or sent the beetles scattering from their ancient lines, or taught the hidden canopy callers a new and frightening note. The hollow has no throne, no altar made for kneeling, no vacancy shaped like worship. A benevolent and wise power would see that immediately.

So their first act would be refusal.

Not refusal of the place. Refusal of themselves.

They would refuse to become the center of it. They would refuse to make the hollow about their arrival. They would refuse to turn the basin into a mirror for their face, the root-columns into pillars of their authority, or the plant-body into a house that exists to receive them. If they came at all, they would come so quietly that the only difference, at first, would be that the stillness felt more complete.

They would come as attention.

The green room would register them the way it registers everything: as a change in pressure, a subtlety in the air, a shadow where no shadow had been, a warmth quickly absorbed. The nearest beetles might pause. A frog’s throat might slow. The flies in the light-shafts might thin for a breath. Then, because the power would do nothing violent, nothing claiming, nothing loud, the hollow would resume.

The beetles would continue their polished routes. The larvae would rise and sink in the basin. The water would tick under falling condensation. The walls would breathe. The great living engine would return to its layered roar.

And the power would listen.

They would listen not with ears, but with a patience adequate to the place. They would learn the chamber the way it knows itself: by flow and resistance, by gradients, by moisture, by the timing of drips, by the electrical faintness that moves through living tissue before an airflow changes. They would enter into the rhythm of the hollow without interrupting it. They would know which sounds belong to sap, which to wings, which to throats, which to old fiber being gently broken down by patient mouths. They would learn the beetle routes, the slug paths, the hidden galleries where pale soft things move through abandoned channels. They would know the basin not as a pool, but as an organ. They would know the altar-shelf not as a place of ceremony, but as a final shape of rest.

They would see, then, that there is no single creature to greet.

There is no monarch in the root-columns. No guardian frog presides from the shelf. No old moth reigns in the canopy. No insect queen directs the chamber’s hum. The being that lives there is not a tenant, not a keeper, not a soul seated in the center. It is the assembly. It is the plant-body and its pores. It is the water and what drinks from it. It is the creatures of the walls, the air, the basin, the seams. It is the sound made by all of them together. It is the balance that remains when every motion has found its place.

A wise power would not ask, “Who rules here?”

They would ask, “What must be preserved?”

And the hollow would answer without language. Its answer would be its continuation.

Water would bead and fall. Sap would pulse. Air would sigh through leaf-valves. Frogs would sound their resonant notes into the curved walls. Beetles would clean what had ceased to be living. Slugs would graze the damp bloom. Moths would vanish into shadow. The unseen callers overhead would stir the roof-leaves and release long pneumatic breaths. The chamber would go on being exactly what it is.

That would be the only petition the hollow could make.

Not worship. Not freedom in the human sense. Not progress. Not improvement. Only this: let the loop remain unbroken. Let the damp persist. Let the walls keep their softness. Let the basin hold its skin of clear water. Let the creatures continue in their narrow, perfect offices. Let the living engine hum without becoming a machine. Let the stillness remain loud with life.

A benevolent and wise power would understand that, in a place already whole, the highest form of goodness is not addition.

It is protection.

So they would do something, but they would do it with extraordinary restraint.

They would not rebuild the hollow. They would not enlarge it. They would not make it more beautiful, because it has already achieved its beauty. They would not grant it seasons, because its nature is the absence of seasonal becoming. They would not give it a future that demands a different shape. They would not introduce novelty for novelty’s sake, because novelty here would not be a gift. It would be a wound.

They would not give the hollow a face.

That is important.

If a power capable of love looked upon such a place, there might be a temptation to make it lovable in a more familiar way: to give it eyes, a mouth, a voice, a visible selfhood. But wisdom would refuse that temptation. To give the hollow a face would be to make it less itself. It would turn an organ into an idol. It would turn an assembly into an individual, and thereby destroy the very quality that makes the place remarkable. The hollow’s dignity is that it has no need to be a person. It is already complete as a living room, a green body, a chamber of functions.

They would not make it speak, either.

Speech would bring command into the chamber. It would make the frogs seem like chorus, the beetles like servants, the plant-body like a stage. The moment the hollow spoke as a self, it would begin to possess the room rather than be the room. The power would not do that. They would leave the language of the place as it is: drip, drone, whisper, valve, croak, wing-whine, the dry click of mandibles, the soft internal shifting of larvae in old channels.

They would not make the hollow pray.

A benevolent and wise power would have no need of gratitude from a place like this. They would not want the chamber arranged around their praise. They would not inscribe their name in the bark. They would not leave a symbol above the basin. They would not cause the light to fall in a pattern meant to be interpreted. If they were truly good, they would be almost completely unimperial in their love. They would want the hollow to remain the hollow’s own.

But they would do more than merely watch.

Their first active gift would be shelter.

Not a wall. Not a dome. Not a visible boundary. The hollow already has walls; it does not need more. Instead, the power would shelter it by adjusting likelihoods. They would become, if one can put it this way, a gentle inclination in the world around the green country.

If fire approached through the living plain, it would lose hunger before it reached the depression. If drought pressed down from the sky, the canopy would hold its moisture a little longer, the root-felt would keep its chill, the basin would not fail. If disease arrived from outside, it would find no easy door. If a careless weight came near — a falling mass, a trampling pressure, some wandering destruction from beyond the plant-country — the chance of it would soften, veer, forget itself.

The power would not make the hollow invulnerable to time. That would be a different kind of violence. Time is the mother of such places. The hollow was made by age, and to remove age from it would be to remove its meaning. But they would protect it from annihilation. They would protect it from the kind of harm that does not transform a system but erases it.

They would protect it from being owned.

If people came — if any such beings could come through the all-green country — the power would not necessarily bar the way. A hollow like this should not be sealed forever from all witness. Witness can be a kindness. But the power would make ownership difficult.

They would make the path hard to map. They would let the way become confused in the mind of anyone who approached with greed. They would make tools feel inappropriate. They would make the desire to cut, carve, claim, harvest, or improve become suddenly heavy, then dull, then embarrassing. They would let a person enter only if they had learned, somewhere in themselves, how to enter without seizing.

And if such a person did stand in the center, the power would not reveal themselves dramatically. They would let the visitor feel the cool air settle. They would let the visitor hear the industrial roar become, by degrees, not threatening but immense. They would let the visitor understand, without instruction, that this place did not need them. That would be the first moral lesson of the hollow: you are not the measure of this room.

Then, perhaps, if the visitor remained gentle, the power would allow a second feeling: that they were permitted to be there. Not welcomed in the human sense. Not embraced. But included for a little while, the way the basin includes a fallen leaf, the way the chamber includes a stray drip, the way the living body includes a passing warmth.

The visitor might leave changed, but the hollow would remain unchanged.

That would be the power’s work.

They would also attend to suffering.

This is where benevolence must be careful, because the hollow is not innocent in the simple sense. It is alive. Life there includes hunger, capture, death, digestion, return. The frogs eat. The hidden things in the canopy eat. Larvae are eaten. Flies vanish. Beetles break down what once lived. To abolish all killing would be to abolish the chamber’s ecology. It would turn the living engine into a beautiful corpse.

A wise power would understand that.

They would not make the frogs refuse flies. They would not make the larvae immortal. They would not make the beetles stop their cleaning. They would not turn the hollow into a place where nothing dies, because death there is not an accident. It is one of the valves through which the place continues.

But they would take away unnecessary cruelty.

They would make fear brief. They would make pain clean. They would make sure that no creature is torn slowly while the chamber continues indifferent around it. They would not remove the fact of being eaten, but they would remove prolonged agony. They would let death arrive as a closing of a small circuit, not as a lingering torment. If a moth failed, it would fail quickly. If a beetle was crushed, it would be crushed once. If a larva was taken, it would be taken without the long dread that higher minds can suffer.

The hollow already folds death back into itself. The power would only make that folding gentle.

They would also give resilience.

This would be their deepest practical act.

The hollow has reached a final form, but even final forms can be wounded. A wall could tear. A roof-leaf could fail. A channel could become blocked. A pocket of water could sour. A creature line could overshoot its place. Nothing in the description suggests that the system is fragile, but wisdom knows that no balance exists by being brittle.

So the power would not give the hollow new machinery. They would give it memory.

They would strengthen the plant-body’s ability to remember its own shape. If a tissue was damaged, the growth that healed it would remember the old curve. If a channel was blocked, flow would find the ancient alternative without violence. If the basin’s skin was disturbed, the water would settle back into clarity. If the canopy opened by accident, the leaves would close again in their old pattern. If one creature faded from its role, another would rise into the vacancy without the system needing to invent a new order.

The gift would not be progress.

The gift would be fidelity.

This is what the power would offer, if they offered anything like speech:

*May your walls remember their roundness.*  
*May your basin keep its clear skin.*  
*May your root-columns hold the weight of dampness.*  
*May your beetles find only what is meant to be cleaned.*  
*May your frogs be answered by the curved dark.*  
*May your larvae rise and sink without alarm.*  
*May your moths vanish before they are broken.*  
*May your slugs travel the old routes without drying.*  
*May your hidden callers keep the roof breathing.*  
*May your sound remain loud and your stillness remain whole.*  
*May you continue.*  
*And if continuation must one day become change, may the change be faithful to what you have been.*

That would be their blessing.

Not a command. Not a spell. Not a law imposed from above. A benediction fitted to the nature of the place.

Would they make the hollow aware of being blessed?

Only barely.

They would not give it self-consciousness. That would be too heavy a gift. But they might allow the assembly to feel, in the way a sleeping body feels safety, that it was held. The water might seem a little more still. The air might have a faint quality of being guarded. The electrical tension in the walls might lose the faintest edge of distress. The creatures might move with a little more ease, not because they had become smarter, but because the world around them had become less sharp.

If the hollow could be said to know anything about the power, it would not know a name or a form. It would know only that its loops were less likely to break. It would know, in the language of flow and membrane, that something favored its continuance.

That is all the hollow would need.

The power would also look beyond the chamber.

Because the green room is not isolated. It is a low center in an ancient flattened country that is entirely plant. The hollow is an organ in a larger body. A wise power would not care for the room while ignoring the whole creature that contains it. They would attend to the living plain itself. They would make sure that the greater plant-country did not fall into fever. They would moderate extremes without erasing them. They would keep the vast circulation sound. They would protect the hidden continuity by which the hollow receives its damp, its coolness, its daylight, its deep biological roar.

If the greater country suffered, the hollow would suffer too.

So their care would be both intimate and wide. They would love the basin’s larvae, and also the planetary leaf-body that makes such a basin possible. They would protect the minute beetle route, and also the deep vascular currents that give the chamber its industrial voice. They would not separate the sacred chamber from the living flesh around it. That would be a false reverence.

They would know that the hollow is holy because it is ordinary.

This is another reason they would avoid grand miracles. A grand miracle would make the hollow exceptional in the wrong way. It would draw eyes that seek spectacle. It would turn the place into a sign of power rather than a fact of life. The deepest miracle of the green room is not that it breaks nature, but that nature, given immense time and perfect balance, became enough. The walls are enough. The water is enough. The sound is enough. The emptiness is enough. The creatures are enough. Nothing needs to be added.

A wise power would serve that miracle by refusing to outshine it.

If they acted visibly, their action would be tiny.

A drip might fall with unusual clarity. The basin might ring once like a struck bell, then settle. A shaft of gray-green light might steady for a moment, revealing the empty center as if the air itself had drawn back. A frog might give a note that seemed to align every other sound in the chamber, though afterward no one could say what had been different. The beetles might pause together, then continue, as if some immense listener had passed through the room without touching them.

That would be enough.

The power would not need to do more.

They would not appoint a guardian.

A guardian would suggest that the hollow cannot keep itself. But the hollow keeps itself beautifully. The creatures are already guardians. The plant-body is already guardian. The damp is already guardian. The sound is already guardian. If the power wished to protect the place, they would strengthen the guardianship already present rather than installing an external ruler.

They would make the beetles more faithful to their work. They would make the water more patient. They would make the frogs’ calls more resonant with belonging. They would make the root-columns firm. They would make the canopy both tight and breathing. They would make every part of the chamber more fully what it already is.

This is perhaps the truest definition of what a benevolent and wise power would do in such a place:

They would not add being. They would deepen being.

They would not bring meaning from outside. They would protect the meaning that is already there.

And if, after long ages, they chose to withdraw their immediate attention, they would not leave the hollow abandoned. They would leave behind a subtle orientation in things. The place would retain the memory of care the way old wood retains the shape of growth. There would be no voice, no image, no commandment. But the chamber would continue to resist ruin more strongly than it otherwise would. Its balance would have been blessed, and blessing, in this case, would mean a strengthened tendency to remain itself.

The hollow would not miss the power.

It would not know absence. It would not feel bereft. It would not wait for their return. That would be right. A benevolent and wise power would not want to be missed. They would not want the chamber to long for them. Longing would introduce a future, a lack, a dependence. The hollow’s perfection is that it does not long. It has no past to regret and no future to become. The power would honor that by never becoming necessary.

If anything remained of them inside the green room, it would be this:

a kindness beneath the roar.

Not a sound, exactly. Not a presence, exactly. Not a face in the bark or a figure in the water. More like the feeling one has when a dangerous place has secretly been made safe. The air would still be cool. The walls would still sweat. The basin would still tick. The frogs would still pulse their throats in the gray-green light. The flies would still tremble in the shafts. The plant-body would still work its ancient machinery of sap, valve, membrane, and breath.

But under all of it, if one listened long enough, there would be a note of protection.

A listener might mistake it for their own calm.

They might stand in the center and feel that the emptiness was not vacant, that the industrial roar was not blind, that the damp stillness was not indifferent. They might feel, without knowing why, that they were standing inside something loved. Not owned. Not displayed. Not chosen above all other things. Simply loved in the way a rare and complete thing can be loved: with restraint, with reverence, with the desire not to change it.

That would be the work of the benevolent and wise power.

They would do almost nothing.

They would not make the hollow better.

They would make it possible for the hollow to remain good.

And in the end, if one asked what they actually did, the truest answer might be this:

They came to the green room.  
They saw that it was whole.  
They listened until they understood its wholeness.  
They blessed its continuation.  
They turned aside violence from outside.  
They softened pain without destroying the order of life.  
They gave the plant-body memory and resilience.  
They refused to become its master.  
They refused to become its spectacle.  
They refused to make it lonely by giving it a self it did not need.  
Then they withdrew so completely that their love became indistinguishable from the place’s own endurance.

The water would settle.

The frogs would resume.

The beetles would continue their slow lines.

The light would fall in pale slants through the canopy.

The walls would breathe.

The engine of green life would hum, drip, sigh, and endure.

And somewhere beneath all that sound, there would be a silence shaped like care.
