They would not arrive as rescue.

That is the first thing. A benevolent power that was not wise might try to end the crowding, the hunger, the wet heat, the endless pressure of bodies. A wise one would understand that this place is not a problem to be solved by removal. It is a living argument. It says: life can begin without green, shelter can be made of decay, intelligence can be a small craft practiced under the weight of a million other lives. To change it carelessly would be to break the only logic it has.

So they would begin by doing almost nothing.

Not nothing from indifference. Nothing from attention.

They would look into the hollows the way someone listens to a fevered body: not to silence it, but to understand its rhythms. They would notice the way moisture gathers under stone lips and fails before it reaches the most crowded nurseries. They would notice how a condensation rain can bless one basin and scour another. They would notice the fungal mats that hold the ground together and the sudden collapses that undo them. They would notice the crude weirs, the scrapes lined with clay, the bone awls and stretched membranes, all half-buried, all brave in their smallness.

They would see the being who lives there.

Perhaps that being is one creature among the press: a cautious maker with quick limbs and a memory just long enough to suffer. Perhaps it is a people scattered in dry alcoves, few and watchful, learning the value of a single reliable drip. Perhaps it is one of the fauna that has begun to build without knowing it is building, a crab-shaped thing with mineral-crusted shell and accidental nurseries clinging to its back. Perhaps it is the whole country itself, taken as a single breathing fact: fungus and chitin, damp and death, stone made partly of bodies.

If the power is wise, they would love all of these without choosing a favorite too soon.

Their first act would be restraint.

They would not open the ceiling wider. They would not flood the basins with clean water or burn the air with a light that makes everything simple. They would not destroy the predators, because the predators teach the air to be respected and give wings their meaning. They would not remove the scavengers, because without them the dead would become barriers instead of gifts. They would not make the place gentle enough to become stupid.

Instead, they would add mercy where mercy does not destroy necessity.

They would start with water.

Water is already the closest thing the hollows have to currency, so a wise power would not make it abundant in a crude way. Abundance without pattern can ruin a place as surely as drought. It can wash out eggs, drown fungal felts, turn careful channels into meaningless slurry. No: they would make water more reliable, more memorable, more kindly distributed.

They would teach the stone, insofar as stone can be taught, to remember wetness.

A high fissure would be inclined just enough to carry a cold draft along a better path. A ceiling shelf would bead moisture not in random bursts but in slower sequences, so the drip becomes a patter and the patter becomes a washing hush without becoming a flood. Certain cracks would hold damp longer. Certain porous crusts would drink during wet pulses and weep during dry intervals. A basin that usually dries too quickly would gain a hidden seam, a capillary thread, a place where moisture lingers beneath the surface like a kept promise.

Not everywhere. Not enough to make life easy. Enough to make life less likely to vanish by accident.

The small constructed weirs would matter more then.

Where a maker has scraped a hollow and lined it with fired clay, the power might let a trickle find it more often. Where a windbreak of fungal mat has been stretched over bone, they might let the condensation above it fall to either side instead of through it. Where a rack holds strips of drying membrane, they might turn a draft just enough to keep rot from settling too soon. These would not be miracles. They would be the sort of luck that, repeated over generations, becomes tradition.

The being would not know the power had helped.

It would only know: here is good stone. Here the water comes. Here the shelter holds.

That would be enough.

Next, they would create refuges.

Not fortresses. Not sealed gardens. The place is too turbulent for permanence, and wisdom would not try to stop its motion. But even in a storm there can be pockets of stillness, and a wise power would multiply those pockets.

They would let certain ledges remain dry.

They would guide warm air away from one alcove and into another. They would allow a shelf of mineralized hyphae to harden just enough to become a reliable roof over a nesting hollow. They would let a crack in the wall widen into a refuge rather than a trap, so that when a larger creature stumbles through or a ledge collapses, some small bodies can slip into safety. They would make a few pools deeper in one corner only, so that when the water thins, not every living thing in it is immediately exposed.

They would not prevent all catastrophe. Catastrophe here is also circulation. A collapse opens new passages. A fallen carcass feeds generations. A flood carries larvae into empty basins. Wisdom would know the difference between destruction that renews and destruction that erases.

So they would allow the place to remain dangerous, but not annihilating.

They would give the landscape a memory of survival.

This is where the fungus would become important.

The white mycelial felts are already the quiet foundation of the country. They spread over clay like skin, bind surfaces, feed grazers, receive decay, and return it to the world. A benevolent and wise power would not replace them with something grander. They would strengthen what is already there.

They would encourage the fungal web to become more responsive.

Not conscious in the way of animals. Not commanding. Not a single mind imposing order on the basins. That would violate the place’s nature. But a deeper sensitivity could arise in the mats: a tendency to carry chemical news faster, to release certain scents when moisture changes, to send nutrients toward damaged patches, to warn nearby growth when heat or gas builds too quickly. The fungal network could become a commons without becoming a king.

When a microbial mat thickens until it becomes treacherous, the fungus might help loosen it before too many creatures sink and die. When a vent grows too hot, the mycelium might withdraw and draw grazers with it, leaving an empty margin that other animals learn to avoid. When a pool dries and eggs are doomed, the fungal threads could preserve some fragment of biological richness nearby, so the place is not entirely emptied.

The power would not make the fungus benevolent in a sentimental way. It would make it more articulate.

The world would still feed on itself. But fewer gifts would be wasted.

They would also tend death.

Not to abolish it. To clarify it.

In such a crowded place, death is already a kind of wealth, but it can also become a poison if it lies too long in the wrong state, if gas builds, if mats sour, if water becomes choked with rot. A wise power would help the dead become useful more quickly and less painfully.

They would favor the swift and the clean.

Where a carcass falls, they might let the scavenger beetles find it sooner. Where mites rise like smoke, they might allow smaller hunters to arrive in balance, so the cloud does not become a stagnant fog. Where fungal bloom begins, they might encourage it to spread in a way that consumes before it poisons. Where something large dies near a nursery, they might guide the decomposition away from the young by scent, by warmth, by a subtle change in the texture of the air.

They would not spare the maker the sight of death. That would be foolish. Death is one of the first teachers of craft. A being that never sees death will never learn to protect life.

But they might give death a little less cruelty.

A predator’s strike might become a fraction more precise, so prey suffer less. A trapped creature might find a crack it could not have found before. A starving animal might come upon shed husks or fungal shelves just often enough to continue without being made vicious by despair. Not every suffering would end. That is not possible in a world of hunger and heat. But some suffering is useless repetition, and wisdom knows how to reduce it without weakening the whole.

They would make the noise less terrible.

The hollows are already loud: chitin rattle, wing-roar, wet slaps, ticking claws, the hush of condensation rain, the creak of clay under weight. A wise power would not silence it. Silence here would be a kind of death. But they might give the noise intervals.

There would be moments when the drone thins.

A warm exhalation from below would pause. The swarms in the light shafts would settle for a little while on the stone. The beetles would slow their clicking. The pool creatures would sink into stillness. Even the grubs would contract less restlessly against the clay. In those brief hushes, the place would not become empty; it would become audible.

Rest would enter the country as a small grace.

Not sleep for all. Not peace. Only enough quiet for a nursing creature to hear danger sooner, for a maker to think one thought without being pressed by sound, for the air to settle enough that spores and dust fall and the light becomes clearer.

Such pauses would be rare, but they would matter.

They would allow the being to notice itself.

This is one of the subtlest gifts a wise power could give: not intelligence as domination, but attention as survival.

If there are makers here, they would not be lifted above the ecology. They would not be given fire that burns the fungal mats and drives the air away. They would not be given metal, because metal would tempt them to cut the world faster than it can heal. They would not be given cities, because cities in such a place would become wounds unless they learned first how to be guests.

Instead, they would be given patience.

Their hands, or claws, or mouths, or whatever manipulators evolution has offered them, would become a little more precise. Not dramatically. Not into supernatural grace. Just enough that a knot holds. Just enough that a bone awl pierces cleanly. Just enough that a strip of fungal leather can be stretched without tearing. Just enough that a clay scrape can be smoothed into a shape that catches water instead of shedding it.

They would be given memory.

Not perfect memory. Perfect memory would burden them. But a memory with paths. A memory that says: this ledge held last wet pulse. This pool failed when the ceiling changed. This carcass brought too many beetles. This groove in the wall follows the drip. This hollow is warmer when the draft turns.

They would be given signs.

The power would not speak to them in words too large for their lives. It would not write commandments in the stone. It would arrange the world so that certain patterns repeat: a row of pits beneath a reliable seep, shallow grooves following a watercourse, repeated scratches near a stable shelter. These would not be messages imposed from outside. They would be invitations written in the language the being can already half-understand.

Over time, the being might begin to answer.

It might deepen a groove. It might add a scratch. It might place a shell under a drip not because it was told to, but because it has begun to expect.

That is the kind of relationship wisdom would prefer: not obedience, but recognition.

They would help the makers become useful to the place.

This is important. If intelligence arises in such a country, it must not become an exception. It must become a function. The being must not stand outside the storm of bodies, taking and taking. It must find a role inside it.

So the power would guide them toward stewardship without dominion.

They might learn to tend the fungal mats. Not plant them as green things are planted, but protect them, harvest them in strips, press them where moisture can follow, let them recover. They might learn that certain bacterial films attract scrapers, and that if those films are cleaned too harshly, the scrapers vanish. They might learn to build weirs that do not merely catch water for themselves but also create damp margins where eggs can hide and microbial crusts can grow.

Their shelters could become small ecosystems.

A windbreak of bone and chitin could shelter mites that clean waste. A rack could dry membranes while also giving roosting space to harmless winged forms. A clay-lined hollow could hold water for the maker and also serve as a drinking place for smaller creatures, whose presence in turn draws prey and improves the maker’s chances. The being’s camp would not be separate from the country. It would be a knot in it.

The power would bless that.

Not with abundance. With compatibility.

They would make the being’s cleverness repay the world instead of only consuming it.

If the being is a people, the power would give them limits.

Not moral laws thundered from above. Limits that feel like instinct at first and become custom later. A reluctance to foul the pool from which one drinks. A hesitation before taking every egg from a mass. A memory that a hunting ground emptied once takes longer to return if emptied twice. A sense that some shelters should be left standing even when they are not needed now, because the wet pulse may come suddenly and another body may need them.

These limits would not make the being saintly. They would make it durable.

A wise power knows that virtue in a harsh place is often just survival seen from far away.

They would not end predation, but they might change its meaning.

The long-legged ceiling predators would remain. The wasp-like hunters would remain. The blind fish would still flash in crowded pools. The salamander-things would still slip from cracks and take what they can. But the power might give the smaller creatures more chances to learn, more routes of escape, more warning signs in vibration and scent. They would not make every prey survive. They would make fear instructive rather than merely fatal.

They would allow the world to teach without always killing its students.

They would also care for the young.

This place is a nursery as much as a compost. Eggs are laid in crusts, mats, pools, and bodies. Hatchings are explosive. Failures are massive. Wisdom would not prevent this, because explosive reproduction is how life answers instability. But it would protect a little more of the future.

It would let some egg masses lie where condensation does not scour them. It would let some gelatinous clusters stick to stone that does not crumble. It would let some temporary pools last just long enough for larvae to become strong. It would let a few more hatchlings find shadow before the first hunter arrives.

Not many more. A few more can change everything over generations.

They would create what might be called seed-hollows.

Small places where life can wait.

A crack where torpid creatures survive a dry interval. A mineral shelf where eggs remain dormant until the correct warmth returns. A pocket of damp clay where fungal threads persist even when the mats above are torn. A pool-bottom depression where microbial films endure beneath sediment. These would be the world’s quiet reserves, unknown to most of its inhabitants, felt only as a tendency for life to return after disaster.

The power would not guarantee recovery. They would make recovery more likely.

That is enough.

If the being is one individual, the power would be especially careful.

They would not make it chosen in a way that separates it from its world. They would not give it visions too large for its mind. They would not make it lonely with knowledge.

They would give it luck.

A missed step by a predator. A warm shelf on a cold night. A carcass already opened by beetles, so the being can take what it needs without fighting. A hollow where a bone awl fits naturally into a groove and suggests a better way to bind. A sudden rain that drives competitors away. A mate appearing when the air is damp enough for courage.
