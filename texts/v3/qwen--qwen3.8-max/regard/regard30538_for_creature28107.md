…resonance of the stone.

If a benevolent and wise power became aware of that basin—of the pale ringing rock, the bead-water, the scattered lives, the deafening mineral music, and of the being who lives there—I think they would not begin by changing anything.

They would begin by listening.

Not listening as a visitor listens, searching for meaning. Not listening as a judge listens, weighing fault. Not listening as a healer listens for injury. They would listen the way one listens to a completed thing: with attention, with astonishment, with the grave tenderness one feels toward what does not need to be improved.

The basin is not a problem. That is the first thing such a power would understand.

It is a covenant already kept.

A lesser kindness might arrive and want to soften it: make the air warmer, thicken the moss, fill the rock pockets into streams, quiet the stone, give the animals denser shelter, give the being hands capable of building, memory capable of storing, speech capable of naming. A lesser wisdom might look at that austere equilibrium and think, *It is too sparse. It is too loud. It is too empty. Let me make it more generous.*

But a truly wise power would see that generosity there has taken another form.

They would see that the basin’s scarcity is not cruelty. The water comes as beads because if it came as rivers, the basin would no longer be the basin. The sound is deafening because the stone has become an instrument and the life within it has learned to live inside that instrument without mastering it. The emptiness is not absence. It is room. The lack of built things is not failure. It is a kind of purity. The being who lives there has reached the edge of making without falling into accumulation. It can lift a stone, use it, and let it fall. That is not poverty. That is a freedom most makers never understand.

So the power would not build a temple there.

They would not carve a sign.

They would not set a throne on the terraces or write a law into the rock. Any monument would be an insult to the basin’s long patience. Any permanent artifact would introduce the violence of memory. The basin already remembers by forgetting. It remains whole because nothing is allowed to cling.

The first act of the benevolent power, then, would be refusal.

They would refuse to make themselves visible in any form that demanded worship.

They would refuse to speak in a voice that would override the stone.

They would refuse to give the being dominion over its home.

They would refuse to turn the basin into a lesson for others.

They would refuse to bless it by marking it.

And yet they would not do nothing.

There is a difference between restraint and indifference.

A wise and benevolent power, if they loved such a place, would act only in ways that left no trace. Their works would be invisible, or nearly so. Their kindness would be like condensation: present for a moment, then gone, then present again, never hoarded, never claimed.

What follows is what I imagine they would do.
