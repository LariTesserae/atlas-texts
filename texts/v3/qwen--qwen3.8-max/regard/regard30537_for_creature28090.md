Children are rare, and when they appear they are received not with loud joy but with a sharpening of attention. A birth in the Pocket City of Weeping Stone is like the opening of a new seam in wet rock: precious, dangerous, full of possible futures, demanding immediate care. No one shouts. No one runs. The first welcome given to a child is a change in the city’s breath. A drip is diverted from a cold niche. A fungal skin is warmed by being held against a body before it is wrapped around another. A Basin-Keeper lowers the level of a nearby pool so that the small sounds of the infant will not scatter too harshly through the jars. The mother is given the driest place that can be spared, and for a little while she is exempted from the endless tending of damp.

The child is washed not in abundance but in exactness. Water is measured into the palm, not poured. Salt is touched to the tongue in the smallest grain. The first name given is not spoken aloud. It is patterned: two drops into a narrow jar, a finger dragged once across a wet wall, a tap against stone repeated until the rhythm becomes associated with the body that has arrived. If the child survives the first dark seasons—and not all do—the name becomes more elaborate, taking into itself the sound of the basin where the birth occurred, the temperature of the water that night, the mood of the well-whelps below, whether the fungi were bright or dim.

To be a child in the Pocket City is to learn restraint before language. The infant is soothed by touch and rhythm rather than voice. It is carried close to chests that smell of mineral damp and fungal leather. It is taught not to cry out, not because its distress is unwelcome, but because an uncontrolled sound can disturb the listening of many. Instead, it learns to tap. To scratch. To press. To place its mouth against a stone rim and breathe without making the water tremble too much. A child who can hear the difference between a falling drop and a crawling drop is considered promising. A child who can tell by touch whether a wall is merely wet or beginning to sicken with unwanted seepage is considered blessed.

It was during such a time—if time can be said to pass in a place without sun—that the Attention came.

It did not come from the sky, because there was no sky. It did not come through the fissure in the manner of a visitor, because the fissure was too narrow for anything that had not learned to compress itself. It did not arrive as fire, or radiance, or a voice that shook the calcite from the walls. A benevolent and wise power would know better than that. To enter the Pocket City of Weeping Stone uninvited in glory would have been a kind of violence. In a place so small, even kindness could flood.

What came was subtler.

A Drip-Reader, lying in her niche with one ear against the stone, noticed that a bead of water had formed on the ceiling and delayed before falling. Not long. Not enough for anyone else to remark upon. But long enough that the rhythm of the chamber changed by a fraction. The drop fell into a basin and made a sound that seemed to contain a question.

Later, a Mud-Presser found a patch of clay where he had expected only grit. The clay was fine, cool, and unusually adhesive, as though it had been prepared for a joint that had not yet cracked. He pressed it into a seam without thinking, and only afterward realized that the seam had not yet begun to leak.

Later still, a well-whelp that had been listless for days turned its heavy body toward a warmer hollow in the black water and rested there, and the Fat-Keeper who watched it felt, without knowing why, that some vast and patient intelligence had bent low over the pool.

The power did not announce itself. It listened first.

It listened through the stone and through the water. It listened to the fungal threads brightening in their slow blue way. It listened to the Settled, whose mineralized bodies answered the drips with hollow notes. It listened to the Wetted as they moved along the walkways, their fingers reading the walls, their feet finding texture in the dark. It listened to the well-whelps breathing in the lower pools. It listened to the bacteria forming skins, to the mineral crusts thickening, to the ceiling sweating in its ancient labor.

And in listening, the power understood what it was seeing.

What lived in the Pocket City was not merely the Wetted, though they were its conscious hands. It was not merely the fungi, though they were its skin and light. It was not merely the well-whelps, though they were its stored warmth. It was not merely the Settled, though they were its memory. It was all of them together, arranged into a single slow being: stone for bone, water for blood, channels for veins, basins for organs, jars for speech, the Wetted for thought, the fungi for breath, the ancestors for depth. The city was not a settlement in the usual sense. It was an inward life that had learned to maintain itself.

A lesser power, even a kind one, might have looked upon such a place and felt only sorrow. So dark. So wet. So confined. So deprived of horizon and green wind and open day. A lesser kindness might have said: I will save them. I will open the ceiling. I will let sunlight enter. I will dry their walls. I will make grass grow there. I will enlarge their world.

But wisdom stayed the hand.

To open the ceiling would have been to break the vessel. Sunlight would not have been a gift; it would have been a blade.
