Their skin is dark green — not the green of things that have been touched by the sun, but the green of water held long enough in shadow to become flesh. It shines faintly in the hollow-light, smooth where it is often wet, roughened in tiny grainy patches where mineral films have settled and been accepted by the body. Along the flanks and shoulders, the green darkens almost to black, and in the creases of the limbs there are faint amber freckles, as if some of the chamber’s drifting insect-light had once brushed them and remained.

Even then, they are not first a face.

They are first a low, warm presence moving through the moss. They are the pressure of padded limbs on stone, the soft displacement of moisture, the slight parting of fungal threads. Their body stays close to the floor, broad and flexible, made for passing beneath root-knots and into shallow recesses without breaking them. When they move, the chamber seems to move with them: a beetle shifts aside, a moth lifts one wing, a film of condensation trembles where their breath has passed.

Their face, when one finally sees it, is quiet and wide. The mouth is broad but not cruel, formed more for sipping than for tearing. The nostrils are dark and mobile, tasting the air for heat, for damp, for the mineral difference between safe water and stagnant water. Their eyes are small and deep-set, not large enough to claim the dark, but enough to read its degrees: the blue-green glow of moss, the amber blink of insects, the faint change in shine when stone is wetter than it should be. They may have no true expression in the human sense, yet there is expression in them nonetheless: patience, attention, an embodied stillness that feels almost like listening.

They are perhaps one, perhaps a few. In such a place, individuality does not announce itself sharply. The hollow does not count its lives as separate possessions. But if one being seems to stand out from the rest, it is because the chamber leans toward them in small ways. A drip forms above them just as they pause. A warm seam opens near the place where they rest. The moss seems to thicken under their limbs as if to make their passage easier. They do not command these things. They do not seem to ask for them. They simply live in such a way that the hollow answers.

Their habits are those of a tender without tools.

They move shells into shallow depressions where drips will fall. They press moss back from breathing seams so that air can pass. They turn stones, not with force, but with slow insistence, until the stones settle into positions that no longer block the tiny currents of heat and moisture. They gather seed cases and broken fungal rims into low rings, perhaps because the rings please some sense of order in them, perhaps because the rings make the water behave differently, perhaps for reasons that have no name. They eat sparingly: a mouthful of warm bacterial film, a soft larva already weakened by time, a sip of mineral water gathered in a fungal cup. They do not take more than the hollow can replace. If they hunt, it is quickly. If they graze, it is gently. If they rest, they curl around a warm stone as if it were a sleeping animal.

They do not speak in words. They speak by placing.

A shell moved here means: the drip will be caught.  
A stone turned there means: the heat needs room.  
A mat of moss pressed down means: this passage must remain open.  
A long stillness beside a basin means: the water is changing, and one must wait.

If such a place and such a being drew the attention of a benevolent and wise power, the first thing to understand is this: the power would not arrive as spectacle.

It would not tear the upper ground open. It would not send a column of white fire down through the roots. It would not dry the walls, straighten the basins, or give the creatures voices shaped for confession. It would not make the hollow bright in the manner of open day, because wisdom would recognize that some places are beloved precisely because they are dim, close, and hidden. It would not mistake its own preferences for the good of the place. It would not confuse benevolence with the desire to remake.

They would first become quiet.

That is what a wise power would do before anything else: quiet themselves. They would lower their attention into the hollow with the care of a hand approaching a sleeping animal. They would not enter as a presence that demands recognition. They would enter as a change so subtle that only the most sensitive lives might notice it at first: a slight deepening of the hush, a new coherence in the heat, a feeling, if such a feeling could be had, that the chamber had become a little more listened to.

They would listen through the moss.

They would learn the hollow not from above, but from within its own rhythms. They would perceive the pulse of the vents, the slow sweating of the ceiling, the tiny economies of the mist-spiders, the patience of the amphibians, the brief violence of the predator, the immense labor of beings no larger than seeds. They would notice how every drop is claimed, how every warm crack is inhabited, how every surface is worked upon by life until it becomes not bare stone but a shared skin. They would see that the place is not empty. They would see that it is not primitive. They would see that it is full.

And they would see the being.

Not as a curiosity. Not as a pet. Not as a symbol of innocence to be preserved under glass. They would see the being as the hollow sees it: as one of its own intensities, a place where the chamber has become aware enough to arrange itself. They would see the repeated acts of care. They would see the rings of stones, the polished grooves, the shell placed in a depression, the moss pressed away from a breathing seam. They would understand that this is not dominion. It is tending. It is the oldest kind of holiness: maintenance without ownership.

Then the power would ask, in the only way appropriate to such a place, whether they might touch it.

They would not ask in thunder. They would not require an answer in words. They would ask by holding back. They would ask by allowing the being to feel the edge of their attention and choose whether to approach. If the being flattened itself against the stone in fear, the power would withdraw until fear became only a faint memory of warmth. If the being stilled, tasting the air, the power would remain stiller. If the being returned to its work, that would be consent enough. In a place where speech is not made of sound, continued tending is a kind of welcome.

What would they do, then, once welcomed?

They would do almost nothing that could be called an event.

They would not give the being metal, because metal would be alien there. They would not give it fire, because fire would wound the damp. They would not give it great machines of water or air, because the hollow already has its own subtle machines: root-channels, heat seams, fungal cups, spider-silk, breath, mineral seepage. To replace those with louder contrivances would be to destroy the very thing being cherished. Instead, the power would work through what already exists. They would strengthen the hollow’s own nature. They would make its balance more resilient without making it less itself.

First, they would ease the extremes.

Where a vent sometimes grew too hot, threatening to scald the small bodies gathered near it, the power would encourage a lace of cooler mineral to thread through the stone. Not to stop the heat, but to temper it. Where a crack admitted too cold a draft in certain seasons, they would guide root-hairs into the opening until the air became braided and softened. Where the ceiling sweated too little, they would draw moisture from deeper pores, not by miracle visible to the eye, but by making the stone more willing to remember water. Where the basins threatened to grow stagnant, they would stir no dramatic spring into being; they would only open a hairline path here and there so that the water could taste movement again.

These changes would be so gentle that the inhabitants might not notice them at all. The beetles would continue their slow work. The moths would lean into warmth. The amphibians would rest with their nostrils above the water. The mist-spiders would drink from their threads. The predator would still pass through the undergrowth like a pressure. But fewer small disasters would come. The hollow would be less likely to tip into dryness, into scalding, into suffocation. The power would not make suffering impossible, because life cannot be made impossible without ending life. They would make suffering less likely to become excess.

Second, they would give the being a deeper language with the place.

Not speech as humans understand it. Not prophecy. Not command. Only a refinement of attention. The being would begin to notice signs more clearly: the way a certain blue-green moss darkens before the air turns sour, the way amber insects spiral differently when heat is rising too fast, the way a particular fungal rim trembles when there is a weakness in the stone above. These signs would not be imposed from outside. They would be awakened from possibilities already present in the chamber. The power would only make the connections more reliable, more perceptible, more true.

The being would not become a priest. It would not become a king. It would remain what it already is: a tender, a low-bodied inhabitant, a living part of the hollow’s weather. But its tending would become less blind. When a passage needed clearing, the being would feel drawn toward it. When a basin needed a stone moved, the being’s hands would remember the motion before the reason for it became clear. When the chamber breathed easily, the being would know this as a kind of warmth that had nothing to do with temperature.

If the being were lonely — and in such a place, loneliness might not be an emotion so much as a missing rhythm — the power would not answer carelessly.

They would not force another into being. They would not invent a companion from nothing if doing so would make a living thing into an object. They would not turn the hollow into a story of rescue that satisfied outside sentiment but ignored inner truth. Instead, they would open possibilities.
