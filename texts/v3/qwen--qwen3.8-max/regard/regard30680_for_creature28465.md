If I follow my heart, I think the benevolent and wise power would do very little that could be seen.

Not because the place is beneath notice. Rather because it is already so complete that the first act of wisdom would be restraint.

Such a power would not arrive in a way that breaks the hush. It would not pour itself into the basin as a blaze, a voice, a commanding presence, a miracle so large that every small life would be forced to turn toward it. That would be a kind of violence, even if meant kindly. To a place that has learned to exist in droplets, ripples, and quiet repair, an overwhelming revelation would be like a stone dropped into a still lens: it would make everything answer at once.

So the power would come, if it came at all, as attention without intrusion.

It would first notice the hollow not because the hollow calls, but because the hollow does not call. It would see a small world that has stopped demanding to be more than it is. It would see the worn land, the roofless room, the patient stones sweating light, the tree releasing its warm rain, the hovering spheres, the bright little lives moving through the canopy, the wader standing in its dark appointment at the water’s edge. It would see the motes moving with purpose too fine to name. It would see death made clean without cruelty. It would see birth arranged without urgency. It would see a being made of congregation rather than singularity: a mind distributed across moss, water, insect, tile, root, seam, and silence.

And the power would understand: this is not a creature to be greeted as one greets a person on a road. It is not a subject to be addressed with speech. It is not a problem to be solved.

It is a finished intimacy.

Therefore the power’s first true act would be to ask without asking.

It would not say, in any language that vibrates the air, “What do you want?” That would impose a need for answer. Instead, it would let its attention settle over the basin like a change in humidity. It would listen in the manner the basin itself listens: through surfaces, gradients, pauses, small alterations.

The motes would pause for a moment, then resume in a slightly different pattern.  
The wader would lift its head a fraction.  
The leaves of the broad tree would hold one more drop than usual before releasing it.  
A hovering sphere would tremble, not fall.  
The black pillars might warm almost imperceptibly, as if remembering an older authority that once knew how to stand without commanding.  
The water would carry a ripple farther than normal, letting it touch the hexagonal tiles, and the old system beneath the broken wall would answer with a seam of light so faint it could be mistaken for a trick of reflection.

By these things, the power would learn what the hollow knows.

It would learn that the place is not in pain.  
It would learn that it is not lonely.  
It would learn that it does not wish to be enlarged, glorified, made immortal, or given a voice louder than its own.  
It would learn that it does want to continue.  
It would learn that continuation, here, includes death, decay, replacement, and quiet transformation.  
It would learn that the being of the basin would not accept a blessing that removed its nature.

So the wise power would not make the hollow eternal in the false way. It would not freeze the water mid-fall, or stop the larvae from rising, or prevent the fungi from arriving beside the dead. It would not turn the place into a museum of itself. That would be another kind of abandonment: preservation without life.

What it would do is subtler.

It would give the hollow room.

Not physical room—the basin is already bounded, small, low, intimate—but room in the larger world. It would make certain that the hollow is not easily found by those who would enter it with haste or greed. It would blur the approaches without building walls. It would let maps become vague near that place. It would let roads forget the direction. It would let instruments grow uncertain, lenses fog, recordings soften into static or moisture. It would not need to punish anyone. It would simply make the hollow difficult to possess.

Those who came with nets, jars, engines, or the desire to name and own would find themselves distracted. Their thoughts would become strangely damp. Their urgency would lose its edge. The air would feel too close, too sacred, too indifferent to their purposes. They would not be struck down. They would simply fail to arrive properly. They would turn back, or pass nearby without recognizing what was there, or remember afterward only a dream of wet leaves and a silence they could not explain.

But those who came gently might find the way.

A lost traveler, frightened and exhausted, might stumble into the basin not by accident but by a kindness hidden in probability. For them, the hollow would become what it already is, but more evidently: a place of water, shade, soft light, and rest. The air would calm their breathing. The moss would be soft beneath them. The hovering spheres might drift near without touching, as if curious. The wader might turn its dark head just enough to show that they were not alone. If they were thirsty, some clear lens would be safe to drink from; if they were wounded, the motes might gather near the hurt in a cool, faint shimmer, not healing by force but helping the body remember its own order. The place would not become a hospital. It would become, for a little while, a mercy.

The power would not make the hollow speak.

That is important. It would not grant the basin a mouth, a face, a single voice, or a human-shaped soul so that outsiders could understand it more easily. To do that would be to diminish what it is. The being there is already articulate in its own way. It speaks in water droplets, in shell-brightenings, in the synchronized bend of eel-like bodies, in the falling of warm rain from the tree, in the glow beneath the wall, in the way death is taken apart without scandal. A wise power would honor that language rather than replace it.

If it gave anything like speech, it would be this: a deeper legibility to those capable of reverence.

Someone with patience might sit long enough to notice the cycles. They might begin to feel that the place is not scenery but presence. They might understand, without being told, that they are being noticed. Not judged. Not welcomed exactly. Registered. Their footsteps would become softer. Their breath would seem too loud. They would feel the strange dignity of being permitted to remain for a short time inside a life much older than their own.

The power would allow that understanding to happen, but only as a gift, not as proof.

It would not want the hollow to become a shrine burdened by pilgrims. It would not want altars built beside the black pillars, or prayers nailed to the tree, or voices raised in supplication over the pool. The place has already passed beyond the need for worship. It practices something quieter than religion: maintenance, reciprocity, attention. So the power would keep it from becoming famous in the ordinary sense. It might be known in dreams. It might be whispered about by a few who had found it and could not quite explain what they had found. But it would not be easy to claim.

Then there is the matter of the being itself.

The being—the congregation, the basin-mind, the gathered resident made of many small lives and old machinery—would sense the power’s attention. It would not mistake it for another creature entering the hollow. It would feel it as a change in the quality of being seen.

Perhaps for one moment, all the small lives would pause together.

The petal-folded insects beneath the leaves would remain closed.  
The midge columns would slow until they seemed suspended.  
The snails would stop grazing, their glass shells holding colored light.  
The larvae would hang in the clear water like tiny bright thoughts.  
The spiders would wait beside their nets of condensed air.  
The bright canopy-flickers would vanish into green.  
The wader would stand so still that its reflection would seem more real than its body.  
The tree would withhold its rain.

In that pause, the hollow would be wholly aware of being witnessed by something vast and kind.

Not judged.  
Not summoned.  
Not improved.  
Only seen.

And then, because the place does not hold still for long, the pause would end. The tree would release its warm shower. The floating mats would stir. The snails would rise. The larvae would ascend. The eel-like creatures would follow in slow curves. The hovering spheres would tremble and fall. The motes would resume their arcs. The seam beneath the wall would glow. The basin would continue.

But something would have changed.

Not in structure. Not in law. Not in the cycles that govern the place.

The change would be a hidden strengthening.

The power would touch the oldest systems gently, the way one might steady a root without pulling it from soil. The motes would not become more numerous, but they would fail less often. The water would keep its strange obedience, its spirals, its hovering, its memory of design. The pale veins in the rock would continue gathering light and sending it into shaded corners. The black pillars would keep their quiet triangle. The hexagonal tiles would keep reading ripples. The broken shelter would remain half-curtained by roots, not falling into ruin but also not becoming whole again, because its brokenness is part of its meaning.

The power would also not remove all danger.

A place without danger becomes brittle, and this hollow is not brittle. It is supple because it knows how to process loss. Therefore the power would allow small deaths, small hunger, small predation, small collapse. It would not save every larva from the eel-like hunters, nor every insect from the moisture-spiders, nor every fungus from its brief paper life. It would not make the basin a paradise of untouched individuals. Its wisdom would be larger than that: it would care for the pattern, not only the parts.

But it might ease unnecessary suffering.

If a creature became trapped in a way that had no meaning in the hollow’s order—if a bird tangled itself in roots, if a small body fell into a crack where no scavenger could reach it, if a living thing suffered without contributing to any cycle—the power might let the motes arrive sooner. It might let water rise a little. It might let light gather in a place that needed warmth. It might let death come quickly and cleanly where death was appropriate, or let repair succeed where repair was possible. It would not abolish pain. It would prevent pain from becoming purposeless.

And it would leave a gift.

Not a large one. Not a weapon. Not a throne. Not a voice.

Perhaps the gift would be a single sphere unlike the others.

Most of the hovering water-spheres form and collapse in the ordinary rhythm of the basin. But this one would persist a little longer, appearing only at rare moments: when the light is fullest, when the tree releases its rain, when the pool is perfectly still, when the wader lifts its foot as if acknowledging something beyond the rim of the world. Inside that sphere there would be no image, exactly, but a memory of the whole place held at once: every leaf, every snail, every mote, every tile, every drop, every silence.

If the hollow were ever truly threatened—not by a single intruder, but by some deeper failure, some exhaustion of its ancient systems—the sphere might fall not into the pool but into the ground beneath the black pillars. There it would become a seed of pattern. Not a body. Not a backup in the crude sense. A possibility. A compressed habit. A way for the basin to begin again elsewhere, in another age, if this one ended.

The power would not force that future. It would only make sure the hollow could not be entirely erased.

That, too, would be a form of kindness: to let a small world know, in its own wordless way, that it has not gone unnoticed in the long history of things.

If the being of the hollow could be asked whether it wanted this gift, perhaps it would answer with a ripple. Perhaps the answer would mean: yes, but only if you do not stay. Perhaps it would mean: yes, but do not make me depend on you. Perhaps it would mean: yes, and now leave, because the next cycle is beginning.

The power would understand.

It would not be offended by the hollow’s lack of gratitude. The place does not need to thank. It continues, and continuation is its gratitude. The tree gives rain. The water climbs. The shells brighten. The dead become dust. The motes repair. The wader attends. The small bright things flicker through the canopy, adjusting leaf and droplet and vein of light. That is enough. That is the being’s way of saying it accepts what it is.

So the benevolent and wise power would withdraw.

Not in absence. In discretion.

It would remain aware of the basin the way a guardian remains aware of a sleeping child: without standing over it, without touching it constantly, without needing it to wake and acknowledge the care. It would hold the hollow in a larger attention, but it would not crowd the hollow’s sky.

And afterward, the place would look almost exactly as it did before.

The hills would still be worn into patience.  
The stones would still sweat light.  
The air would still be warm and close to saturation.  
The tree would still spread its broad crown over the center, each polished leaf cupping rain.  
The mosses and ferns would still glow with health.  
The curved wall fragment would still rise from the green, softened by ages of water.  
The three black pillars would still stand in their uneven triangle.  
The hexagonal floor would still lie beneath the pool, seamless and waiting for ripples.  
The roofless shelter would still keep its half-curtained quiet.  
The insects would still unfold like remembered shapes.  
The eels would still move as one long thought.  
The midges would still turn in their soundless columns.  
The wader would still stand at the margin, gathering brightness into its dark feathers.

Only one thing would be different, and only in the subtlest way.

At certain moments, when the light descended through the pale veins of rock and became soft daylight beneath the leaves, the hollow would seem for an instant to be held. Not suspended. Not controlled. Held. As if something vast had leaned close enough to love it without disturbing it.

The being of the basin would feel this, perhaps, as a single drop forming on the lowest surface of a leaf. The drop would not fall right away. It would hang there, bright and trembling, containing a tiny reflection not of the sky above but of something else: a kindness that had looked upon the place and chosen not to overpower it.

Then the drop would fall.

The pool would accept it.

The ripples would travel outward, touch the tiled floor, and be read by the old system beneath the wall. A seam would glow faintly. The motes would move in a brief coordinated arc. The tree would open its pores and release its warm rain. The floating mats would spread. The snails would rise. The larvae would ascend. The eel-like hunters would follow. The canopy-flickers would spark between leaves. The wader would lift one foot a fraction of an inch, then settle.

And the hollow would continue.

Not saved from being itself.  
Not rescued from its own smallness.  
Not made louder, larger, clearer, or more important than it is.

Only accompanied.

Only blessed in the way that a finished, patient, living thing can be blessed:

by being seen,  
by being protected without being possessed,  
and by being allowed to remain  
what it has so long and quietly become.
