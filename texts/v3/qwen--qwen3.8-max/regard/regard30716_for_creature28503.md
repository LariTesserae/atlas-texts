**A Kindness Suitable to Stone**

It would not arrive as fire.

It would not come as a voice too large for the thin air, nor as a weather system dragged up the escarpment in defiance of the place’s own laws. A benevolent and wise power, noticing such a city, would first understand this much: to appear there in obvious glory would be a kind of violence. At that height, in that heat, among brittle crusts and held breaths, even wonder must be careful.

So if they did anything, they would begin by not shaking the world.

They would come as attention.

They would look first at the mineral breadth of it: the black glassy ridges still sharp with newness, the pale pumice fields, the sintered ash, the fissures only lately opened, the heat rising from the ground like a slow fever. They would see the city pressed into all those shelves and cuts, dense and vertical, built over itself, repaired over itself, living in the gaps as much as in the rooms. They would see the bridges and ducts and cableways filling the air, the cisterns and fins and sealed troughs, the little dark pools with their rust rings and crystalline scabs. They would notice that even water there does not behave like water elsewhere. It does not bless openly. It appears as bead-lines, as darkening stone, as a metallic taste in the back of the throat, as a brief slickness on iron before dawn.

They would hear the quiet.

Not silence. The city is not silent. But its sound is compressed, patient, low. Engines breathing behind ceramic casings. The whisper of cable over wheel. The tick of metal cooling. The soft fall of condensation into basins. A tool dropped somewhere far below, ringing once and then gone. The power would listen to that quiet and understand that it is not absence. It is discipline. It is a whole culture shaped by the knowledge that some places cannot bear much noise.

Then they would look for the being who lives there.

At first it might seem that there are only beings: workers, children, fin-tenders, seam-listeners, furnace crews, glassmakers, salt-scrapers, wire-drawers, people with dust in the creases of their clothes and little cups in careful hands. There are the pale beetles moving over warm stone, the blind mites in the deep shafts, the rare moth trembling near a lamp. There are the dark filaments on the walls, branching where moisture gathers, retreating when dry. There is the city itself, layered and crowded, more organism than object.

But if the power sought one being, they would find it there in the most complete sense: the city as a single slow creature of heat and mineral, with basalt for bone, heat for blood, condensation for breath, industry for digestion, and the people for its waking thoughts. Not soft. Not green. Not pretty in the lowland way of pretty things. But alive in the way that difficult places become alive when living there requires precision.

The being is not a person. It is a pattern of continuance.

And it is tired in the way that only long-enduring things can be tired: not ready to stop, but strained by the constant cost of remaining itself.

A lesser benevolence might try to fix it immediately.

A lesser power might say: *Let there be rain.* Let clouds gather in thick banks. Let water run openly through the streets. Let the stone drink and soften. Let pools become clear and plentiful. Let the people laugh and stand in it with open mouths.

But wisdom would refuse that.

Not from cruelty. From understanding.

Heavy rain there would not be kindness. It would scar the young slopes, flash through fissures, loosen ash, stress unfinished terraces, turn precious damp into sudden destruction. The city has been built to gather water slowly, to value it drop by drop, to organize itself around intermittent moisture. A generous flood would not free the people. It would break their skills, ruin their storage, and make them strangers in their own place.

A lesser power might say: *Let there be coolness.* Let the geothermal fever abate. Let the air become mild. Let the rock stop pressing warmth upward. Let the place be comfortable.

But wisdom would hesitate there too.

The city’s industry depends on heat. Its engines, drying floors, kilns, distillation trays, pressure works, and warming cells are all arranged around thermal gradients. The people navigate by heat. Their architecture is shaped by shade and venting and routed warmth. Remove the heat entirely and much of the city would become inert. Its economy of energy would collapse. Comfort imposed from outside would become dependency.

A lesser power might say: *Let there be green.* Let soil form. Let lichen spread. Let grass find footholds. Let seeds lodge in cracks. Let something soft appear to comfort the eye.

But even that, in such a place, would require care.

Roots can split young stone. Soil can clog condensation systems.
