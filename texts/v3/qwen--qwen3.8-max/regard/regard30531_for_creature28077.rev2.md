I think they would do something.  
But it would not be the kind of doing that announces itself.

A lesser power, even a kindly one, might arrive in brightness: a voice from the cloud, a sudden stilling of the rain, a sign carved in the rock, a miracle so large that every creature in the hollow dropped its work and stared. A wise power would know better. It would understand that a place like this is already full of voices, already intricate, already balanced on ten thousand small attentions. To enter it with spectacle would be to trample the very thing being loved.

So they would come first as listening.

They would stand, if a power can be said to stand, at the rim of the green hollow and let the place happen around them. They would hear the water before they heard the prayers. They would notice how the drips were not random but patterned, how the channels had been worn into shape by long use, how the frogs answered one another across the basins, how the birds stitched the upper air, how the people moved with their particular economy of balance, hands busy, feet careful, voices lifting in short calls through the damp.

They would see the being who lives there.

Not only the people. Not only the birds, the frogs, the beetles, the martens, the moss, the trained trees. The power would see the whole as one living body with many kinds of breath. Water would be its oldest pulse. Roots would be its grip upon itself. The paths would be habits. The market would be its warm noisy heart. The fungi would be its memory of death. The children would be its laughter and its danger. The old would be its patience. The insects would be its restless thoughts. The frogs would be its proof that the wetness was still alive.

And seeing all this, the wise power would ask not, “How can I make this place better?” but, “How can I keep it from being broken without stealing its life?”

That is the first thing true benevolence learns when it loves something complex: it does not begin by changing it. It begins by refusing to damage it.

So the power would hold back the obvious miracle.

They would not dry the hollow. They would not silence the birds. They would not banish the insects. They would not make the moss into stone or the living wood into metal. They would not give the people perfect roofs, perfect walls, perfect safety, because a perfect place would no longer require their care, and without care the hollow would cease to be a negotiation. It would become a museum, or a cage, or a tomb with pleasant shapes.

Instead, they would bless the hollow with continuation.

They would strengthen what already worked.  
They would warn before ruin.  
They would soften what could be softened without undoing the world.  
They would make mercy more likely, despair less likely, attention more keen.

If they acted, it would often be in ways no one could prove.

A root shelf, weakened underneath by slow rot, would not collapse at market hour under a crowd of porters. Instead, the night before, an old woman would wake with a feeling she could not name. She would go out with a lantern, tap the rail, press her thumb to the underside, and call for braces in the morning. Everyone would grumble a little at being woken. Someone would say she dreamed it. But they would brace the shelf anyway, because she was old and her hands knew wood. Later, when a gust came and the crowd pressed there, the shelf would hold. No one would call that a miracle. They would call it good timing. The power would be content with that.

A child running too fast along a slick branch walk would slip. In an unluckier world, the fall would be bad. In this one, the child’s hand would catch a vine that had grown thick exactly where a hand might need it. The vine would hold. The child would swing, gasp, laugh, and climb back up. A mother might look at the vine afterward and wonder why it had grown there so strongly. Then she would tie it better to the rail, just in case. The power would be pleased, because the proper result of help is not dependence but care.

A blocked gutter high in a canopy ledge would begin to overflow toward a sleeping chamber. Before the water stained the wall and softened the floor, a young apprentice clearing channels would hear a wrongness in the drip: not louder, but thinner, as if the water were being forced sideways. He would climb, find the leaves packed into the split-log trough, clear them with his fingers, and watch the flow return to its true road. He would feel a small satisfaction he could not explain. The power would have done nothing except make his ear a little more faithful to what was already there.

They would bless the hollow in this way: by making small signs less easy to miss.

Rope would fray where it could be seen, not fail where it could not. Wood would give warning by sound and smell before it gave way. Moss would change color early enough to be fed or moved. Frogs would leave a tainted basin before the water sickened anyone. Birds would scatter in ways that made watchers look up. Sparks would die in ash before they could reach dry thatch. Storms would not be prevented, but the worst gusts might arrive when fewer people were on the high walkways, or after the market had already been covered.

Not always. A wise power would know that always is a kind of tyranny. If every danger were removed, the people would become careless. If every loss were prevented, the place would lose its truth. The hollow would no longer teach balance. It would teach only expectation. So the power would allow weather, accident, grief, and age to remain. But they would try, where possible, to keep suffering from becoming meaningless.

They would be especially gentle with the sick.

In a wet and crowded place, illness can move quickly. The power would not abolish disease, because to do so might unravel the small lives that share the hollow’s air and water. But they would ease the course of fevers where they could. They would make broth taste more comforting. They would make sleep come more easily to those in pain. They would guide hands to the right herbs, or at least prevent the wrong ones from being used in ignorance. They would make the delirious less likely to wander near edges. They would help a fevered child recognize the sound of their parent’s voice even when the mind was floating.

And when someone was dying, the power would not necessarily save them. Instead, they would try to make the death less lonely.

A dying elder might hear, beneath the ordinary drip of the hollow, something like recognition. Not a voice, perhaps, but a deepening of familiar sound: the market far off, the frogs near, the water in the channels running clean, the walls breathing as they always had. They might feel that they were not being taken from the place but being folded into it. The people of the hollow already knew how to give bodies back to root and moss; the power would make that giving gentler. They would make the roots patient. They would make the decay not foul. They would let the dead become part of the hollow without becoming horror.

They would protect the place from certain outside harms, but not by making it invisible.

A wise power would know that isolation can be as cruel as exposure. The hollow should not be sealed from all strangers, because strangers can bring knowledge, trade, stories, and new seeds. But those who came with greed or cruelty might find the place harder to exploit than expected.

A trader with plans to strip the mountain might enter and become strangely unable to speak his plans aloud. The air might make him tired. The wet might seep into his confidence. He might see a frog sitting in a leaf cup and feel, for reasons he could not defend, that he was being watched by something older than his ledger. He might leave with only a small basket of mushrooms and a headache, not knowing why.

A soldier might come seeking refuge and find himself unable to burn even a dead branch without feeling shame. The hollow would not enchant him into goodness; it would only make the consequences of violence more visible to his own conscience. If he stayed, he might learn to tie knots. If he left, he might leave more quietly than he came.

Fire would be one of the power’s great concerns.

In a place of living roofs and dried fuel, fire is a terror. The wise power would not remove fire, because fire is warmth, cooking, light, and craft. But they would make the people more vigilant than ever. They would make the smell of smoke carry farther. They would make a sleeping hearth seem wrong if it held even one stubborn spark. They would make children notice glowing ash before it became flame. They would let a fire, if it began, find less to eat than it expected. A living wall might resist a little longer than ordinary wood. A moss roof might dampen rather than catch. A bucket might be at hand because someone, for no reason, had moved it there that morning.

The power would not make fire harmless. They would make fire less likely to become catastrophe.

They would also tend the social weather of the hollow, because in a crowded place cruelty can rot faster than water can.

When tempers frayed, the power might send a small interruption: a sudden shower that made two arguing neighbors duck under the same shelter; a child asking a question; a bird making an absurd noise; a pot boiling over; a rope slipping; anything to break the hardening of pride. They would not force forgiveness. They would only create room for it.

They would make it easier for people to apologize. They would make insults less sticky in memory. They would help exhaustion find sleep before it found malice. They would let gossip turn, now and then, into concern. If someone was being left out, they might dream of that person needing help, and wake with the impulse to carry them food. If someone was hoarding, they might feel a sudden discomfort until they shared. If someone was grieving, the hollow would seem to gather around them without anyone deciding to do so: a neighbor bringing dry cloth, another leaving a cup of warm broth, a child sitting nearby without speaking.

The power would not rule the hollow. They would not create priests. They would not demand a temple. If the people began to worship them too loudly, the power might become uneasy, because worship can become a way of giving away responsibility. They would prefer to be remembered in practical ways.

Perhaps the people would develop small customs without quite knowing why.

Before repairing a channel, someone might pour a little clean water back into it, not as offering but as courtesy. When a rope was finished, the maker might tie a tiny extra knot “for the one who holds what we cannot.” When a child first walked alone across a root bridge, an elder might say, “May your feet be heard by the path.” When a storm passed and everyone emerged to find the hollow still standing, someone might tap a rail twice, the way one thanks a patient animal.

They might leave a small shelf near the dripest place, where a cup of rainwater could sit. Not for the power to drink. For the power to be remembered as water: moving, necessary, impossible to hold.

If the power spoke, they would not speak in commandments. They would speak in the grammar of the hollow itself.

They might say:

> Water must move.  
> Do not build your peace by stopping what must flow.

They might say:

> A root is trusted by being guided, not forced.  
> So with one another.

They might say:

> Do not ask me to make the hollow silent.  
> The silence you want may be the silence of loss.

They might say:

> I will not carry you so much that your feet forget the ground.  
> I will help the ground be worthy of your feet.

And they might say, in a voice like water through leaves:

> You are not the masters here.  
> You are the part of the hollow that can choose.

That would be their hardest gift: to remind the people that they were not helpless, but also not alone.

The power would love the frogs especially, I think.

Not more than the children, not more than the elders, not more than the swifts or the moss or the trained trees, but with a particular tenderness because frogs are indicators. They say whether the water is still living. They sit in leaf cups and breathe with their whole bodies. They turn ordinary damp into voice. The power might make sure that frog-song never entirely left the hollow. If a basin went silent, someone would notice. If someone noticed, something could be mended.

They would also love the old toads under the boards. They would protect the great slow toad that becomes a landmark, the one children learn to step around. They would make sure that when it finally died, another toad would find the place, so the hollow would not feel bereft.

They would love the martens too, in the difficult way one loves a sharp-edged life. They would not make the martens harmless. A marten must be a marten. But they might guide the red one away from the wrong nest on the wrong night. They might send it toward mice when eggs were too few. They might let it be seen just often enough to remain respected, not so often that it became a plague.

They would love the insects, though not every insect individually in the way a person would want. They would understand that the insect haze is part of the air’s fertility, part of the food of birds and bats and frogs. They would not remove it. But they might keep the worst swarms from suffocating infants, from fouling food stores beyond recovery, from entering sleeping mouths. They would make nets more effective. They would make smoke less harmful to lungs while still driving moths back. They would make compost take its gifts without becoming a breeding wound.

They would be patient with the mice and shrews. They would not let the stored grain fail entirely. They would make sealed baskets cleverer. They would make the people quicker to move food when a gnaw mark appeared. They would let predators do their work. They would not pity the mouse caught by the owl, but they might make the owl’s flight true so the killing was clean.

They would care for the plants as slow persons.

The trained trees would be their long work. A wise power knows that some blessings take decades. They would strengthen the union where a branch had been lashed into a walkway. They would help bark grow around a peg without infection. They would make a living wall thicken where children leaned. They would let a dying tree fall in a way that created shelter rather than damage, if no one needed it standing. They would encourage nurse logs to become what they could become: moss country, frog shelter, mushroom ground.

They would not force growth. They would only make the place less likely to be betrayed by its own wounded places.

In drought, if drought came, they would be most present.

The hollow is wet, but not invulnerable. A long dry season could make the moss thin, the channels stutter, the root floors brittle. The power would not necessarily send rain, because weather belongs to wider systems and a wise power does not buy one place’s comfort with another place’s ruin. But they would help the hollow endure.

They would lead the people to the hidden cisterns. They would make them remember old water roads. They would keep the deepest springs from failing. They would make the leaves hold their moisture a little longer. They would make the frogs go quiet without dying. They would make dreams of rain come, not as torture but as instruction: where to dig, where to shade, where to carry water in the cool hour before dawn.

And if rain finally came, the hollow would exhale. The first drip after dryness would be almost holy. The power would let the people feel that without naming it.

They would also bless laughter.

This is important. A crowded, wet, noisy place can wear people down. The constant damp, the lack of privacy, the endless maintenance—these can sour the mind if there is no relief. A wise power would know that joy is a kind of structural repair.

They would make room for absurdity. A bird might steal a vendor’s cap and wear it briefly on a branch while everyone shouts. A gust might drop a sheet of rain exactly on the one person who had just boasted about staying dry. A frog might leap into a bowl at the worst possible moment and turn a serious bargain into chaos. Children might invent a game so ridiculous that even tired adults laugh. Someone might slip and land in moss, unharmed, and become a story for three days.

The power would not cause cruelty for amusement. But they would allow the hollow to be comic, because comedy is one of the ways living things survive closeness.

They would protect sleep.

Not all night, and not for everyone, because someone must listen. But they would make the deeper hours kinder. They would soften the sound of certain drips for those who needed quiet. They would make nightmares fewer. They would let watchers feel companioned rather than alone. They would make the dark not empty but populated by familiar sounds: frog, moth, water, wood settling, breath.

They might send dreams.

Not grand prophecies. Practical dreams. A channel-keeper might dream of a leaf dam in a high trough and wake knowing exactly where to climb. A rope-maker might dream of a knot and find in the morning that it was real enough to try. A grieving widow might dream of her husband’s hands tying a cord, and wake able to breathe for the first time in days. A child afraid of heights might dream of being a swift and wake less afraid, though still careful.

The power would be careful with dreams. Dreams can become commands if the dreamer is weak. They would send only what invited courage, not what created obedience.

They would, in short, become a kind of invisible maintenance.

This seems to me the truest thing a benevolent and wise power would do in such a place: not rule, not rescue, not overwhelm, but maintain. They would join the hollow’s own labor. They would become one more pair of hands, one more listening ear, one more wakeful attention in the night. They would do the divine equivalent of clearing leaves from a channel.

And if they were very wise, they would let the people believe it was mostly their own work.

Because it would be.

The power would not replace human hands. They would strengthen them. They would not replace human noticing. They would sharpen it. They would not replace human kindness. They would make it easier to choose. They would not make the hollow perfect. They would make it merciful.

There would still be loss.

A nest would fall.  
A storm would take a branch.  
An elder would die before winter.  
A child would be born weak and might not stay.  
A marten would steal eggs.  
A fungus would enter a beloved tree.  
A walkway would rot and have to be rebuilt.  
Someone would be lonely even in the crowd.  
Someone would be angry.  
Someone would fail.

The wise power would not prevent all of this, because to prevent all of it would be to unmake the conditions of life. But they would be present in the aftermath. They would make hands willing to rebuild. They would make grief shared. They would make failure instructive rather than fatal when possible. They would let sorrow pass through the hollow without allowing it to become the final word.

Perhaps the only overt sign they would allow would be this:

At certain rare moments, when the hollow was exactly itself—when the water was running clear, the market noisy, the birds crossing the light, the frogs singing, the people laughing under a sudden shower, the moss thick, the roots firm, the children safe enough and the old rested enough—the whole place would seem, for a breath, to shine from inside.

Not with supernatural glory. Not with blinding light. Just a sudden coherence. A feeling that every drip, every voice, every wingbeat belonged. That nothing was wasted. That no life was merely background. That the wet green room in the mountain was not a small place at all, but a world complete enough to be loved.

The people might stop for a moment without knowing why.

A vendor might pause with a basket in his hands.  
A child might look up.  
An old climber might rest her palm on a railing and feel warmth in the damp wood.  
A stranger might forget what he came to take.  
The birds might seem, briefly, to fly in pattern.  
The water might seem to say one word, though no one could repeat it afterward.

Then the moment would pass. The market would resume. The frogs would continue. The drip would continue. Someone would shout about a leaking tarp. Someone would laugh. Life would crowd close again.

But a few people would carry that moment in their chests for years.

If asked what the power did, they might say: “Nothing, really. I only felt that the place was held.”

That would be enough.

And if the power had to summarize their action in a single phrase, I think they would say:

“I made the hollow more itself.”

Not drier.  
Not quieter.  
Not emptier of animals.  
Not safer into brittleness.  
Not obedient into stillness.

More itself.

More resilient.  
More attentive.  
More merciful.  
More capable of recovery.  
More able to hold its many lives without breaking.  
More likely to notice the blocked channel before the flood.  
More likely to forgive a neighbor before the quarrel hardened.  
More likely to remember the dead without being haunted.  
More likely to laugh when the gust dropped rain on everyone at once.

They would do this not because the hollow needed saving from its own nature, but because even the most living place can be helped by being witnessed kindly.

And if the being who lives there is understood not as one person but as the whole green body of the place—water, root, frog, bird, human, moss, rot, breath—then perhaps the power would simply bend close, in the way one bends over a beloved sleeping child, and say without words:

“Continue.  
I am not here to end your struggle.  
I am here to keep it from becoming despair.  
Keep the channels open.  
Step lightly.  
Make room for the frogs.  
Watch the roots.  
Tell the children where to place their feet.  
And when the water rises, do not forget:  
you have been given roads.”
