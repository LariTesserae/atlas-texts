**What the Wise Power Would Do**

If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention toward the High Condensery, I do not think it would arrive as a rupture.

No sunburst.  
No choir louder than the condenser drum.  
No commandment descending from the wire-glass in letters of fire.

The chamber already has its own liturgy: drip, pulse, fan, wing, the hiss of scheduled rain, the long metallic breath of the fans, the thud that travels through the floor like a slow heart remembering itself. A wise power would not add noise to a place whose noise is already structural. It would not impose a spectacle on a room that has become sacred by repetition.

It would come, if it came, as a change in attention.

Perhaps the first sign would be that the room seemed more itself.

Not brighter.  
Not holier.  
Not improved in any way that would make the wardens pause and say, *Something has been changed.*

Only a deepening of the ordinary.

A drip falling with slightly more certainty.  
A green panel opening after years of sticking.  
A bird catching a bead of fog at exactly the moment it needed one.  
A warden placing a palm against a pipe and feeling, for no reason that could be named, that the chamber was listening back.

The power would see the being who lives there, but not in the simple way one sees a bird in a cage or a keeper in a house. It would see the coalition: the drum and the vapor, the felt beds and the beetles, the birds, the moths, the snails, the newts, the lizards, the wardens, the rust held in check by decades of touching hands, the memory of every gloved palm that has polished the same rail. It would understand that the inhabitant is not one body but a long argument between water and metal that has learned, drop by drop, to become hospitality.

Then the power would ask the only question worth asking of any living place:

*What do you need?*

And the High Condensery would answer, as it always answers, by continuing.

A drip would fall into a pan.  
A fan would turn.  
A swift would catch a bead of fog.  
A warden would place a palm against a pipe and feel the warmth moving.  
The first fruit would hang heavy on its vine while the birds waited.

The power would listen long enough to understand that the room was not begging for transformation.

It was asking for continuance.

So the power would do what wisdom does when it loves a delicate thing:

It would become very careful.

---

**First, it would obey the rules.**

A foolish power might see the enamel plates bolted near the ramp and mistake them for quaintness. A wise one would recognize them as covenant.

**DO NOT INTRODUCE SOIL.**

It would not smuggle in black earth or seedbeds from below, however generous the intention. It would understand that soil would be an invasion of a different order, a simplification. The chamber has made its own ground out of leaf-crumb, shell, dust, mineral scale, and patience. To give it soil would be to say its achievement was insufficient.

A benevolent power would refuse that insult.

It would let the room keep its strange, manufactured ground. It would let felt become dark by its own slow labor. It would let the beetles make the only earth the chamber can bear.

**DO NOT STOP THE DRIPS.**

It would not, out of kindness, seal every leak or dry every corner. It would know that a stopped drip is a broken sentence. If a drip fell in the wrong place, it might guide it to a gutter with a thread of wax or a bent strip of copper, but it would not stop it.

It would preserve the grammar of water.

**DO NOT COUNT THE NESTS.**

It would not inventory the chamber into sterility. It would know that some things remain alive because they are not fully known. It would let the swifts keep their secrecy. It would let the finches be numerous without becoming a census. It would protect mystery as a form of shelter.

A wise power does not need to know the number of every wing to love the winging.

**IF THE FLOOR HUMS BLUE, STAND CLEAR OF THE GRATING.**

Here, perhaps, it would act more directly.

Not by abolishing the blue, because the blue belongs to the machine’s own wildness, but by making it legible. It would give the wardens an extra second of warning: a ringing in the water cups, a lifting of fine hairs, a smell like cold pennies before the grating became dangerous. It would not make the chamber harmless. It would make it intelligible.

Because wisdom knows that safety is not the same as absence of danger. Sometimes safety is simply danger made comprehensible.

**WHEN THE RAIN BEGINS, OPEN ALL GREEN PANELS.**

It would ensure the panels could open.

If a hinge had seized, a warden would find it moving easily that day.  
If a pole had gone missing, someone’s hand would remember where it had been left.  
If a young Panel-Opener hesitated, an older one would sign the right gesture and feel, for no clear reason, that the room was pleased.

The power would not open the panels by force. It would not strip the ritual from the work. It would simply make the ritual possible.

**LEAVE THE FIRST FRUIT FOR THE BIRDS.**

It would guard this without making it dramatic.

Perhaps a new worker, hungry or thoughtless, would reach for the ripest bead of fruit and then pause, seeing a bird watching from a cable. Perhaps the pause would be enough. The fruit would fall. The bird would take it. The vine would continue.

The power would smile, if powers smile, at such a small and perfect exchange.

---

**What it would not do is make the High Condensery perfect.**

This is the part that matters most.

It would not silence the machinery, because the silence is wrong. The room needs the condenser drum’s heavy pulse the way a body needs a heartbeat. When the engines stop, the chamber feels unmoored, too light, as if it might drift loose from the tower. The roar is not oppression. The roar is skeleton.

It would not remove the predators, because the lizards and fish and hungering birds are part of the balance. It would not banish the moths from the lamps, because the lamps are part of the night-vines and the moths are part of the food of the air. It would not make the plants so abundant that the chamber becomes a jungle, because the emptiness is one of its virtues. It would not fill the volume simply to reassure the eye.

It would understand that this place is not a paradise waiting to be completed.

It is a habit waiting to be maintained.

Perfection would be a kind of violence here.

A perfect chamber would have no worn places. It would have no history. It would need no wardens. It would not be old. It would not be learned. It would not have the softness of surfaces touched by decades. It would not be loved in the way that things are loved when love is expressed through care rather than admiration.

So the wise power would refuse perfection.

It would choose instead the harder gift:

Endurance.

---

**What would it do, then?**

It would tune.

It would move through the room the way a good mechanic moves through an engine that is also a cathedral: with reverence, with grease under the fingernails, with no desire to be noticed. It would touch only what needed touching. It would leave everything else to its own dignity.

Where a cable had chafed nearly through, it would let a warden find it before it sparked.  
Where a ceramic tray had developed a hairline crack, it would guide the drip away from an electrical cabinet until the crack could be sealed with old enamel.  
Where a moss bed had grown too dry because a louver stuck, it would not force the louver; it would make the warden’s hook catch it at the right angle.  
Where a beetle colony had failed and dead leaves were piling under a rack, it would bring another colony in the lining of a glove, unnoticed, the way a friend replaces a missing thread before the cloth tears.

Its miracles would be maintenance-shaped.

A spare part appearing in a drawer no one remembered opening.  
A sequence of hand signs returning to an old warden’s memory at the exact moment it was needed.  
A child climbing the spiral ramp because they heard the water through the walls and could not stop listening.  
A leak revealing itself before it became damage.  
A valve loosening after years of stubbornness, not violently, but as if it had finally decided to agree.

The power would not answer prayer by overriding the room.

It would answer by arranging coincidences.

---

**It would be especially attentive to thresholds.**

The blue hum.  
The sodium lamps.  
The moments of engine stoppage.  
The first rain after a long dry cycle.  
The ripening of the first fruit.  
The instant a panel opens and the mist changes direction.

These are the chamber’s dangerous edges, the places where balance becomes visible. A wise and benevolent power would not remove edges, but it would make sure no one was thrown across them by accident.

If a child of the tower wandered up the ramp for the first time, the power might make the blue hum begin while the child was still near the wall, so the lesson came with fear but not with harm.

If an old warden grew slow, it might make the rain wait one breath longer before falling, or make the green panels lighter to push.

If a bird struck the wire-glass in panic, it might let the bird fall into a place where a soft hand could find it. And if the bird could not be saved, it might let it die quickly, without being eaten alive by neglect.

It would reduce meaningless suffering.

That is perhaps the simplest definition of its action.

Not all suffering. The room is not built for painlessness. Minnows are eaten. Larvae are cropped. Leaves yellow and fall. Moths burn too close to lamps and sometimes do not recover. The power would not make the chamber into a nursery where no creature may harm another. It would know that to do so would starve the newts, still the birds, and turn the felt beds into graves.

But it would prune away the stupid cruelties.

Poison in a water tray.  
A wire that cuts wing.  
A heat pocket that bakes eggs.  
A flood that comes not from scheduled rain but from some neglected valve below.  
A lamp so hot it turns the moths to ash before they have carried pollen.  
A drainage path that turns foul because no one remembered it needed clearing.

It would make the room’s dangers honest ones.

Not cruelty.  
Not accident born of neglect.  
Only the ordinary costs of being alive inside a machine that has become weather.

---

**It would also care for the wardens.**

This is important.

A place like the High Condensery lives because someone remembers it. If the power wished it well, it would not merely bless the birds; it would bless the hands that open the panels.

It would make the work feel less lonely.

It would send, in some form, the knowledge that the labor mattered. Perhaps this would come as a dream: a warden dreams of a great green room in the sky and wakes with the certainty that the tower needs it. Perhaps it would come as a practical event: a crate arrives containing gaskets that no one remembers ordering but which exactly fit the old nozzles. Perhaps it would come as a person: an apprentice climbs the ramp because they heard the water through the walls and could not stop listening.

If the wardens prayed, the answer would not be a voice.

It would be maintenance-shaped.

A scraper appearing where a scraper was needed.  
A forgotten sequence returning to memory.  
A tool fitting more easily than it should.  
A lamp brightening just enough to read a pressure gauge.  
A young worker learning the signs without being taught too harshly.  
An old worker resting in the warmth of an electrical cabinet and feeling, for once, that rest was not abandonment.

The power would make the chamber difficult to abandon.

If engineers below began to see the room as inefficient, as a wasted volume, as a relic of old condensers that could be replaced by cleaner systems, the power would not necessarily stop them with thunder. It would place a hesitation in the right minds.

Someone would remember a smell.  
Someone would recall a bird that nested there as a child.  
Someone would calculate and discover, unexpectedly, that the chamber does more than condense: it cools a circuit, feeds a nutrient loop, stabilizes pressure, houses species that keep the tower’s upper lungs from fouling.

The power would make usefulness reveal itself.

Not because the room must justify itself by utility alone, but because wise powers know that in towers, usefulness is one of the languages people listen to.

It might, if it were very subtle, make the room slightly more beautiful to those capable of protecting it.

Not by adding glory.

By allowing people to notice what is already there.

The way the mist takes shape around the hanging racks.  
The way the sodium lamps turn the leaves into old brass.  
The way snail trails write on dark metal.  
The way the condenser drum’s thud becomes so familiar that it feels like the tower’s own heart.  
The way the first fruit hangs there, heavy and unpicked, while the birds wait.

It would not make the wardens worship the chamber. Worship might turn into fear or possession. It would make them feel, from time to time, that they were inside something worth their whole attention.

That feeling is often enough.

---

**If the power manifested at all, I imagine it would manifest small.**

A figure on the catwalk during the rain cycle.

Wearing oilcloth so old it has gone soft as cloth. Gloves polished by condensation. A hood shadowing the face. No one remembers when the figure arrived. A warden signs **OPEN** toward a stuck green panel, and the figure answers with the same sign, only slower, as if teaching patience.

The panel opens.

Water darkens the felt below.

The figure moves along the rail, touches a drip-line, and the drip resumes. Where a blue thread of vibration is gathering near the grating, the figure kneels and presses one bare palm to the metal. The blue withdraws into a grounding pipe and hums there harmlessly, like a bee in a jar.

The wardens do not ask who this is.

In a chamber where speech is useless, strangers can be accepted by their usefulness.

The figure helps for a cycle, perhaps two. It carries a hook pole. It clears a clogged gutter no larger than a handspan. It places a fallen fern frond back into a rack, not forcing it, only giving it support until the vines take hold again. When the first fruit ripens, the figure stands beneath it and makes the sign for **WAIT**.

The birds wait.

The fruit falls.

The figure steps back.

Then, when the rain ends and the panels are closing, the figure is gone.

No one agrees afterward whether there was a figure at all. But the panel that used to stick moves easily. The drip-line has a new bend in it, slight and elegant, like a piece of calligraphy in copper. The blue hum announces itself earlier. A young warden says she dreamed of hands without faces tending the room. An old warden says there are always hands; the question is whether we let them be seen.

---

**Would the power leave a sign?**

Perhaps.

But not a monument.

Maybe near the old enamel plates, where the rules are bolted, a new plate appears. Its letters are not bright. They look as if they have always been there, only recently cleaned.

It says:

**DO NOT MAKE IT PERFECT. KEEP IT TENDED.**

Or perhaps it says:

**WHEN THE ROOM IS QUIET, LISTEN FOR WHAT IS MISSING.**

Or perhaps there is no new plate at all.

Only a change in the room’s weather: the sense that the drips are being counted by something that loves them, not to judge them, but to remember them.

---

**What would the being in the High Condensery do in response?**

It would continue.

That is its answer and its gift.

The swifts would still cut the air.  
The finches would carry seeds into cracks.  
The moths would rise when the sodium lamps dim.  
The beetles would work in the felt.  
The snails would write.  
The newts would wait in shaded basins.  
The plants would follow warmth.  
The wardens would walk with their soft-soled, spread-footed balance.  
The drum would thud.  
The fans would breathe.  
The rain would come.

But there might be a difference.

The difference would be small, like a drop added to a full cup that somehow does not overflow.

The chamber would seem a little more protected.  
The cycles would seem a little more reliable.  
The people would seem a little less alone.  
The room would not become holy in the bright, demanding sense. It would become trusted.

It would become a place where kindness had passed through and left no footprints except a smoother hinge, a safer wire, a remembered rule, a bird fed, a warden steadied, a machine allowed to grow old without being discarded.

A benevolent and wise power, looking on the High Condensery, would not say:

*Become something greater.*

It would say, in whatever language powers use when they do not wish to be heard as commands:

**Remain.**

Remain in your damp and your roar.  
Remain in your slow making.  
Remain in your rules that are also rituals.  
Remain in your first fruit, your blue warnings, your green panels, your dripping patience.  
Let the birds carry what they carry.  
Let the felt become dark by its own labor.  
Let the tower learn that not everything valuable is efficient, and not everything efficient is enough.  
Let there be a place where the made and the living have ceased to quarrel.

And if the power could give one blessing, it would be this:

May no hand destroy you out of carelessness.  
May no hand improve you out of contempt.  
May those who tend you know why they tend you.  
May the drips find their paths.  
May the birds be paid.  
May the blue be only a warning.  
May the rain come when the leaves are ready.  
May you remain a hollow large enough for life,  
and small enough to be loved.

That, I think, is what they would do.

Not nothing.  
Not everything.

Enough.
