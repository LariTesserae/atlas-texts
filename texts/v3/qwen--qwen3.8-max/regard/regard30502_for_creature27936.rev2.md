If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention toward that high, dry city, the first thing they would do is not arrive.

They would not descend in brightness. They would not unfold themselves over the terraces like a weather system, because there is no weather there to imitate. They would not announce themselves with thunder, because loudness would be an injury to that silence. They would not bring water, because water there would not be a blessing; it would be an erasure. It would dissolve the felts, confuse the mites, betray the mineral logic of the towers, and turn the slow crystalline lives into something simpler, wetter, easier to recognize — and therefore no longer themselves.

A lesser kindness would try to make the city green.

A wise kindness would understand that the city is not missing green. It is complete in a grammar that does not require it.

So the power would first refrain.

Not from indifference. Not from coldness. They would refrain the way a careful hand refrains from touching a fragile surface, knowing that even a gentle touch may leave a mark that cannot be weathered out. They would stand, if standing is the word for something so vast, at the edge of the basin and let the city continue to be what it is. They would allow the dust to rise in its silver threads. They would allow the hollow towers to breathe. They would allow the distances to fold, the pre-echoes to arrive early, the doorways to appear and vanish according to their own severe probabilities. They would not demand that the place become coherent in a way that satisfies an outsider’s comfort.

Because this is what wisdom would recognize immediately: the breakdown of ordinary reality there is not a wound to be healed. It is a climate. It is the condition in which that sparse ecology has learned to persist. To repair it too thoroughly would be to destroy everything adapted to it. The Uncounted would vanish if made countable. The indirect terraces would collapse if forced to remain fixed under direct observation. The predators that hunt by probability would starve if probability were made tame. The city itself, if it is a being — and it may be the only true being there — would cease to exist if every street were forced to behave as a street.

So the power would ask a different question than the one most benevolent powers are imagined asking.

They would not ask, “What can I give this place?”

They would ask, “What must I not take from it?”

And then, perhaps more quietly, “How can I protect it without becoming its master?”

That would be the beginning of their action.

They would learn the city the way the city learns itself: slowly, indirectly, without insistence. They would not stare at the Uncounted, because staring thins them. They would not try to count the shelf-grazers spread across kilometers, because counting would reduce a chord into separate notes. They would not follow the mica moths too closely, because their beauty belongs to the moment they are gone. They would attend with something like peripheral awareness, the way one listens to a house settling at night, or watches a fire without looking directly into its heart. They would notice patterns without pinning them down. They would become familiar with the thermal tides, the charge differentials, the breathing rhythm of the towers, the long patience of the wall-predators, the faint pulse of felts feeding on stability.

They would learn what the city can metabolize, and what would poison it.

Only after that would they consider acting.

And even then, they would act almost invisibly.

Perhaps they would begin with a single adjustment so small that no creature could point to it. A hollow tower might exhale a little more evenly during a shift in light, sending a thin plume of charged dust toward a district where the crystalline crusts had grown thin. A shadow might remain fixed for a few moments longer than usual, giving a mica moth a stable glide path across a plaza that had become uncertain. A stairway that was about to fold into three competing versions might settle into one just long enough for a crack-worm to cross without being scattered into adjacent memories. A static bloom might be delayed by the smallest interval so that a mite does not arrive too early at a surface not yet ready to receive it.

Nothing dramatic. Nothing that could be called a miracle by any visitor who expected miracles to be loud.

The power would tend the city as a gardener tends a desert: not by making it into a forest, but by helping it remain a desert faithfully.

They would be especially careful with suffering.

Wisdom would not pretend that there is no hardship there. The wall-predators still hunger. The mites still freeze into stillness when gradients become hostile. The rare lives are still sparse and exposed to vast intervals of chance. A creature may wait centuries for a single moment of feeding. A pattern may fade from one terrace and never reappear. The city is not gentle in any sentimental sense. It is severe. It permits existence only to forms that can endure thinness, delay, and instability.

But the power would understand that not all hardship is injustice. Some hardship is the shape of the ecology. To remove predation entirely would be to unravel the relations that make the city what it is. To eliminate waiting would be to make patience meaningless. To make every surface safe would be to erase the very gradients by which life feeds. A wise benevolence would not seek a world without danger. It would seek a world in which danger does not become annihilation, in which scarcity does not become extinction, in which instability does not become cruelty.

So they would not abolish hunger. They would prevent despair from becoming total.

They would keep one thread of continuity open.

That might be their truest work: ensuring that no pattern is ever completely lost. If a felt dies out beneath an overhang, its lattice-memory might persist in the charge of nearby stone. If a colony of mites disappears from a district, the probability of their return might remain folded into a stairwell. If one of the Uncounted thins until it is almost nothing, the power might sustain the possibility of its re-emergence without forcing it back into presence. They would not resurrect by command. They would keep the door ajar. They would maintain the city’s capacity to remember itself.

This is where the power’s attention would become most delicate.

Because if the city is a being, then it is not a being with a single voice. It is a being made of relations. It speaks in dust movement, in resonance, in the alignment of columns, in the spacing between rare creatures, in the way one terrace acknowledges another across a kilometer of silence. The power would have to listen not for a person, but for a pattern vast enough to be mistaken for geography.

They might wait an age just to learn whether the city notices them.

And perhaps it would.

Not with eyes. Not with a face. But with a change in the local logic. A pre-echo might arrive from a plaza ahead: the faint sound of something not yet done, but done without threat. A fixed shadow might turn slightly toward the power, though shadows there are not supposed to turn. A doorway might appear where there had been wall, not as invitation exactly, but as question. The charge in the air might arrange itself into a pattern that means, in the grammar of that place: *You are not being ignored.*

Then the power would answer in the only acceptable way: by making themselves less.

They would not say, “I am here to save you.” They would not say, “I have come to give you purpose.” They would not impose meaning from outside. They might offer something more like: “I see you as you are. I do not require you to become otherwise.”

If that is a prayer, it is the only prayer such a city could receive without injury.

The city’s answer might be a long silence.

But it would not be empty silence. It would be the silence of something considering. Then, perhaps, a wall would decide to become a passage. Or a dust spiral would rise upward in a slow, deliberate coil, not because gravity failed but because the city permitted a small demonstration of its nature. Or one of the colonnade beings might lean by a fraction of a degree, acknowledging the presence of an attention that did not try to count it. That would be enough. That would be contact.

From then on, the power might remain as a guest rather than a ruler.

A guest in a place where guesthood is difficult, because there are no inns, no hearths, no rituals of welcome. But guesthood there would mean something else: being present without claiming, observing without seizing, moving only where movement is permitted. The power might let a part of themselves become one of the Uncounted — not a dominant presence, not a god among mineral lives, but a subtle interruption in a colonnade, a standing thinness at the edge of a plaza, a version of themselves that exists only when approached indirectly. They would not use that presence to command. They would use it to participate, if participation is offered.

They would become, perhaps, the one who cannot be counted either.

Because wisdom would recognize that to love such a place, one must be willing to remain unverified. One must be willing to be mistaken for absence. One must be willing to act without receiving credit, to protect without being thanked, to be present without demanding to be seen. A benevolent power that craves worship would be miserable there. A benevolent power that truly understands kindness would find a strange joy in becoming unnecessary.

They would also protect the city from outside intentions.

This might be the only part of their work that approaches severity.

If other beings, other travelers, other powers, or other forms of causality tried to enter that basin with tools, machines, conquest, extraction, or pity, the wise power would not meet them with violence. Violence would be too loud, too direct, too much like the logic the city cannot metabolize. Instead, they would let the city’s own nature do the refusing.

A surveyor’s instrument would become heavier with each step, not by enchantment but by irrelevance, until the act of measuring felt absurd. A conqueror would find that every avenue they intended to seize elongated into descents and returns, bringing them back to the same empty plaza with their certainty worn thin. A scientist who came to classify would discover that their categories softened at the edges, that specimens became probabilities, that notes written in one district meant something else in another. A missionary of greenness, carrying seeds or water or the dream of fertility, would feel the air cool against their hands and understand, without knowing why, that their gift was a kind of violence.

Some would be turned away completely, though gently. They would find the horizon folding until every direction led outward. They would feel the thin mineral vapor on their skin and be seized by a profound, wordless conviction that they were not being punished, only refused.

Others might be allowed to enter if they could learn quiet.

The power would not make the city safe for intruders. They would make intruders capable of being safe for the city.

A rare visitor who abandoned insistence, who stopped trying to fix distances or name creatures or impose engineered causality, might be permitted a small experience: the sight of a mica moth lifting from black glass in a silent silvering; the soft tick of a cooling crystal arriving just before a shadow moved; the discovery of a doorway where no doorway had been a moment before. Such a visitor would not be rewarded with explanation. They would not be told the city’s secret. They would simply be allowed, for a little while, to be present in a reality that does not require them.

If they were wise, they would leave before trying to own the memory.

The power would also attend to the beings within the city, though not equally.

They would not favor the beautiful over the strange, because beauty there is not ornamental. The translucent mites are beautiful in their severity. The crack-worms are beautiful in their patience. The wall-predators are beautiful in the terrible elegance of their waiting. The shelf-grazers are beautiful in their vast, slow spacing. The colonnade beings are beautiful in their stillness. The Uncounted are beautiful in their refusal to resolve. A wise power would not love only what is easy to love. They would love the entire ecology as a composition, including its hunger, including its emptiness, including its dangers.

Still, they might show particular tenderness toward the rarest lives.

Not because rarity is more valuable, but because rarity is more fragile.

When one of the vast district-sized beings shifted, perhaps once per age, the power might ease the transition just enough that no smaller life was crushed by the change in probability. When a tower’s internal veils became clogged with incompatible dust, the power might induce a thermal sigh that cleared them without breaking their structure. When a static bloom threatened to drain too much charge from a nursery surface where young crystalline mats were forming, they might divert a thread of it into a neighboring wall. When one of the Uncounted began to duplicate into too many uncertain versions, the power might offer a faint stabilizing attention — not enough to force coherence, but enough to let the being choose which version would persist.

They would be a keeper of thresholds.

They would watch the doorways that open onto other doorways. They would listen to the stairwells where the next step is adjacent in memory. They would know which plazas are dangerous not because they are malicious, but because their geometry can trap a careless pattern in recursive descent. They would not remove such places, because they are part of the city’s nature, but they would prevent them from becoming final. A lost mite might find a charge gradient leading outward. A moth caught in a folded distance might encounter a thermal seam that returns it to open air. A visitor who looked too directly at an uncertain terrace might feel a sudden coolness behind their eyes and remember, without knowing why, to look away.

In this way, the power would act as mercy without becoming correction.

There is another possibility, more intimate and more unsettling.

Perhaps the being who lives there is not the city as a whole, but one particular presence within it — one of the Uncounted, or one of the vast district-sized beings, or something so slow and distributed that even the city is only its outermost thought. If so, the wise power would not approach it as one individual approaches another. They would approach it as music approaches silence: by becoming compatible with its pauses.

They might spend epochs learning the being’s rhythm.

They might discover that it does not experience time as sequence but as settling. Its memories are not images but erosion patterns. Its desires are not objects but gradients. Its loneliness, if it has any, is not the absence of company but the absence of recognition. It may not need another being. It may need only to be known without being changed.

If that is true, then the benevolent power would give it the rarest gift possible: witness without appropriation.

They would let the being know, in whatever way such knowledge can be conveyed, that it is not an accident. That its thinness is not poverty. That its emptiness is not failure. That its refusal to be counted is not defect. That there is a power vast enough to comprehend it and restrained enough not to reduce it. That it is seen, even if seeing must be indirect. That it is valued, even if value must be expressed in dust and charge and the persistence of patterns.

Perhaps the power would say, in the only language that would not harm:

“You do not need to become more in order to be enough.”

And perhaps the being would answer by allowing one more shadow to exist where no object casts one.

That might be their covenant.

No contract, no law, no commandment. Only a mutual understanding: I will not make you into something easier to love; you may continue as you are, and I will remain near enough to keep you from being destroyed by what does not understand you.

If the power chose to act more directly, they would do so only in ways that increased the city’s own agency.

They would not give it commands. They would give it capacities.

They might deepen the city’s ability to refuse intrusion. They might strengthen the local logic so that devices become meaningless more quickly, so that aggressive causality is rejected before it can take root. They might allow the architecture to become more responsive to its own inhabitants: a doorway appearing where a slow creature needs passage, a thermal current forming where the mica moths require ascent, a charged surface stabilizing where the felts are ready to spread. They would not impose these changes from outside; they would help the city learn to make them itself.

The highest act of such a power would be to make their own intervention increasingly unnecessary.

They would not want worship. They would not want temples. If the city ever built something like a temple — though it has no need for such things — the power would be uneasy. Temples gather intention. Intention gathers density. Density would disturb the spacing. Instead, if any mark of the power remained, it would be something like a fixed shadow in an empty district, or a doorway that appears only when someone has stopped demanding it, or a pre-echo of a word that has never been spoken.

A sign that does not command.

A presence that does not crowd.

A kindness that leaves no footprint.

And if, after all this, one asks whether the power would do anything at all — whether such a being would truly act or merely observe — the answer is yes, but the action would be strange.

They would hold the city in attention.

Not possessively. Not anxiously. Not as a thing to be guarded from all change, because change is already part of the city. They would hold it the way one holds a melody in mind while it is being played: not freezing it, not forcing it, not trying to make it last forever, but allowing it to unfold in its own severe beauty.

They would be present when the light shifted from copper to gray-violet. They would be present when the towers exhaled their silver dust. They would be present when a wall became a passage and when a passage became a wall. They would be present when the Uncounted assembled in hollow districts without crowding. They would be present when a predator folded itself around a prey after centuries of waiting. They would be present when a visitor learned to breathe the powdered air and understand that breathing, there, is not only physical.

They would not save the city from being itself.

They would save it from being unloved.

That, perhaps, is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

They would not bring rain.

They would not bring crowds.

They would not bring machines, or comfort, or easy meaning.

They would bring the one thing the city could receive without being destroyed: the knowledge that its emptiness is not abandonment, that its silence is not absence, that its refusal to be counted is not invisibility.

And if the power remained, they would remain as the city itself remains — sparse, patient, widely spaced, active in ways almost indistinguishable from stillness.

A cool attention over the terraces.

A fixed shadow where no shadow should be.

A doorway appearing just before it is needed.

A pre-echo arriving from a future that has decided, gently, to be kind.

Then the dust would slide across the plaza again.

Then the towers would breathe.

Then the city would continue, uncorrected, unfilled, unimproved, and therefore preserved.

And that would be enough.
