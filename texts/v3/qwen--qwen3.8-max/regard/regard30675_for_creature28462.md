They would do nothing that made the city stop being itself.

That would be the first act, and perhaps the most powerful one. A benevolent power without wisdom might try to correct the place: dry the walls, quiet the engines, fill the empty plazas, soften the roar into something more easily loved. A wise power would understand at once that such kindness would be a kind of violence. The city is not a mistake. It is not a punishment. It is not a ruin waiting to be rescued into a different shape. It is a balance. It has its own dignity, its own logic, its own severe and patient beauty.

So they would begin with restraint.

No sudden light.  
No voice from the sky.  
No miracle so large that it breaks the city’s long habits.  
No healing that erases the need for care.  
No abundance that overwhelms the careful niches where life has learned to persist.  
No silence where silence would be dangerous.  
No dryness where dryness would mean sickness.

A wise benevolence does not always arrive as rescue. Sometimes it arrives as recognition.

They would enter the basin the way the basin itself enters its inhabitants: slowly, through the skin, through vibration, through the smallest changes in moisture and pressure. They would not descend as a god demanding to be seen. They would come as attention. They would lay themselves along the city’s roar the way a keeper lays a palm along a stone wall: not to command it, but to understand it.

They would feel the whole system at once.

The warm stone.  
The sweating basalt.  
The shallow pans trembling into rings.  
The slow ceremonial turning of turbines.  
The mineral crust building by fractions.  
The black reeds in the drainage seams.  
The pale moths resting in the high galleries.  
The blind fish moving through dark water.  
The worn center of the stairs.  
The shine on the handrails.  
The hands that test, wipe, adjust, and wait.

They would hear the roar not as noise but as a living chord. They would understand that the city is not loud because it is cruel, and not empty because it is dead. It is loud because the sound is part of its breathing. It is empty because it has found a scale that does not require crowds to be complete.

Then they would notice the being who lives there.

Perhaps it is one keeper in particular. Let us say so, for the sake of tenderness.

Not a hero. Not a prophet. Not a chosen one marked by strange destiny. Simply one of the custodians. Someone no longer young. Someone whose name is more gesture than sound: a double tap at the elbow, then the fingers opening like condensation spreading across cold stone. They tend a line of condensation cones on a middle terrace, or perhaps they walk the weeping walls behind an engine hall, checking for drift. Their hands are soft at the fingertips from constant contact with damp mineral surfaces. Their boots are thin-soled. Their hood is stained faintly at the edges with pale salt. They have lived their whole life inside the roar, and their body has learned to translate it into meaning.

They are not miserable. That is important. A wise power would see that not all suffering looks like despair. The keeper’s life is not a tragedy. It is narrow in some ways, but deep in others. They have purpose. They have skill. They belong to the place. They know which walls prefer morning damp and which shed best at night. They know the mood of a turbine by the trembling of a water bowl left on a sill. They know when a sound is healthy, when it is tired, when it is beginning to turn dangerous.

But they are burdened.

Not by cruelty. By continuity.

Every day requires attention. Every season brings another small correction. Nothing can be ignored. Nothing can be finished. The work does not culminate. It only continues. There is a loneliness in that kind of life, especially when the one who lives it has become so useful to the place that the place forgets to give anything back except more work.

The keeper has not asked for salvation. They might not even know what they would ask for. If pressed, they might sign something simple: *rest*, or *hands enough*, or *not alone*.

A benevolent and wise power would hear that without words.

And because they are wise, they would not give the keeper what they think they want. They would give what the keeper and the city can both bear.

They would do small things.

Not small in effect. Small in intrusion.

First, they would give the city a margin.

Somewhere deep in the bluff, where the oldest resonators breathe their low notes into the rock, there is a frequency that has begun, almost imperceptibly, to sharpen. No keeper has named it yet. The change is too slight. A few grains of mineral dust tremble differently on a railing. A shallow pan by a certain staircase shows rings that are not quite symmetrical. In a year, perhaps two, the drift might become serious. A duct might crack. A gate might shudder loose. A terrace might begin pooling where it should only weep.

The power would not announce the danger. They would not send a vision or write a warning on the sky. They would simply ease the note.

A fraction.  
A breath.  
A settling.

One of the great acoustic doors would close a little more firmly in the night, moved by no hand anyone remembers seeing. A valve would release a sigh at the exact moment it needed to release it. A seam in the rock would warm by the tiniest degree, changing the way moisture gathers along a vulnerable face. The dangerous sharpening would soften before it becomes history.

In the morning, the keepers would walk their rounds and find nothing remarkable. That would be the point.

One of them might pause with a palm against a wall and think, in the bodily language they know best: *that is better.* They would not know why. They would mark no miracle. They would simply continue.

But they would continue with a little less weight on them.

Second, the power would give the keeper rest.

Not an escape. Not a sleep that removes them from the world. The city cannot be left unattended, and the keeper would not want to be removed from what they love. But rest can be given inside the conditions of a life.

There is a courtyard behind an engine hall where standing waves cancel in a strange and lucky way. The air there is still enough for sleep, though the city’s hum remains as a broad pressure in the stone. The power would make that place kinder.

Not silent. Silence would feel wrong. But cradled.

The vibration would become less punishing there. The floor would stop carrying the small grinding tremor that enters the bones during the night. The damp would gather on the walls in even threads instead of irregular beads, so the air feels breathed rather than close. A sleeping mat laid there would seem to hold the body more gently. The keeper’s sleep would deepen by one degree, and that one degree would be enough to change the shape of their days.
