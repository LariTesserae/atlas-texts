They would not enter.

That would be their first act, if it can be called an act.

A benevolent power, if it were not wise, might arrive as rescue. It might bring light for the darkness, voice for the silence, future for the stillness, or comfort for what it mistakes for captivity. It might long to bless. It might want to make the place more itself by making it more like elsewhere: brighter, louder, freer, fuller, open.

But a wise and benevolent power would understand at once that some things are not saved by being changed.

Some things are saved by being left alone.

So they would come, if they came at all, only to the rim.

They would not descend into the basin. They would not step where the moss is deep. They would not let their weight press into the saturated ground, because even a footprint would be a hollow where water could gather in a new way, and even one displaced creature would be an interruption in the breathing of the whole. They would not bring fire. They would not bring metal. They would not bring a name that demands to be spoken.

They would stand, if standing is what they do, at the edge.

And they would look.

They would see the pool, black and still, holding its exact level. They would see the froglike forms with their throats lifting in unison, slow as thought. They would see the pale things in the water, neither swimming nor resting, but occupying depth as though depth were a kind of prayer. They would see the insects fixed in shadow, the birdlike bodies plastered soft against the ledges, the moss-cushions that breathe without hurry. They would see the small figure near the pool, no larger than two hands placed together, furred with moss, eyes black and wet, mouth closed.

They would see that it is not waiting.

That would matter.

A lesser kindness might see stillness and imagine suffering. A lesser love might see silence and imagine loneliness. A lesser wisdom might see fullness without increase and call it imprisonment.

But this power would understand.

The being there does not lack.

That is the difficult truth, and the power would be wise enough to bear it. The creature by the pool is not longing for sun. The water-things are not longing for rivers. The birdlike forms are not longing for wind. The moss-bodies are not longing for growth. The basin is not a wound waiting to be healed. It is not a question waiting to be answered. It is not a life that has failed to become more. It is a life that has succeeded in becoming complete.

And so the power would face the oldest temptation of benevolence: the desire to give.

They would consider light.

They would hold light in their hand, perhaps, the way one holds a lantern before entering a dark room. But they would see that the basin already has its own dim holiness: the ghost-green of moss, the faint sparks in small eyes, the weak living glow at the tips of quiet insects. Any greater light would not reveal the place. It would replace it. It would make shadows where there are none. It would teach the dwellers to seek brightness instead of being brightness in miniature. So the power would close their hand around the light and put it away.

They would consider speech.

They might know many languages, even languages older than rain. They might know words that could teach the creatures to name one another, to remember, to tell, to sing. But they would hear the basin’s silence and understand that speech would wound it. Not because the dwellers are too simple for speech, but because speech would create an inside and an outside, a speaker and a listener, a before and an after. It would make their quiet into something noticed. And once quiet is noticed, it can become loss. So the power would keep their mouth closed. They would not bless with words
