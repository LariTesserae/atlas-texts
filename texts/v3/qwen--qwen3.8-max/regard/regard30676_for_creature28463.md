If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention toward that high city-country, I think the first thing it would do is not act.

It would listen.

It would not arrive with thunder, because thunder would be cheap there. It would not come as fire, because fire is too dangerous and too precious. It would not descend in a blaze of certainty, because a people who have learned to read hairline cracks in stone, to count water by drops, to move slowly in thin air, would not be improved by a spectacle that made them fall to their knees in terror. A wise power would know that revelation can be a kind of violence if it breaks the shape of a life.

So it would come, if it came at all, as an increase in attention.

It would notice the city not as a harsh place that had failed to become comfortable, but as a place that had succeeded at becoming itself. It would see the fog-gutters, the plaster seals over cracks, the stacked dung, the covered cisterns, the narrow roofed lanes, the sleeping children pressed against warm walls, the old hands still mending rope, the herders stepping aside for woolly beasts in the dusk. It would see that the people had already made an answer to the world—not an answer of conquest, but of patience.

And because it was wise, it would understand that the easiest way to ruin them would be to give them too much.

A merely kind power might try to make the plateau warm. It might lower the cliffs, thicken the air, pour rivers through the streets, fill the city with timber and grain and endless fuel. It might make the stone stop shedding, the winter stop biting, the distances shrink, the labor vanish. It might imagine it was being merciful.

But a wise power would see what that mercy would destroy.

If the air grew thick and warm, the city would no longer be the city. If water ran everywhere, the careful justice of counted drops would weaken. If stone became safe beyond all need for listening, the stone-listeners would lose their art. If fuel became plentiful, the discipline of fire would decay. If every child survived without effort, the fierce tenderness of preparation might become lax. If every traveler could move without risk, the watchers on the cairns would matter less. If the world became easy, many of the virtues that had kept the people alive—attention, restraint, mutual obligation, memory—would become ornamental.

So the power would not give them ease.

It would give them margin.

Margin is the difference between hardship and catastrophe. It is the extra handbreadth of stability in a wall. It is the few minutes of warning before a rock face lets go. It is the last bead of condensation in a fog-pan, the extra breath in a sleeping infant, the sudden unease that makes a herder stop and count the animals, the small warmth in a stone at dusk that keeps a lost person moving toward shelter. Margin is not abundance. It is the narrow saving edge that allows skill, courage, and care to matter.

A benevolent and wise power would work at the edge.

It would not make the stone still, because the stone was still young and had its own nature. But it would make the stone speak sooner.

After a hard frost-night, a high face might shed a little dust before it shed a block. A crack might give a dull note when the wind passed over it, a sound not much louder than a bird’s wing, but enough to make a stone-listener pause. A plaster seal might show a finer line than expected. A marmot might abandon a rubble slope too early, and someone watching would remember that animals sometimes know what hands have not yet learned. The power would not prevent all rockfall. It would prevent some rockfalls from becoming funerals.

In the fog districts, it would not make clouds obey. But it would help the fog-tenders catch a little more of what the weather already offered.

One of them, standing at a pan in the blue morning, might notice that a certain roughness on the stone caused droplets to run instead of cling. She might try a groove, then two, then a shallow angle that her mother had not used. In a dream, she might see water moving down a bird’s feather and wake with the idea of a slanted lip on a jar cover, one that let condensation in but kept wind-blown grit out. No one would call this a miracle. They would say she had good hands. The power would be content with that.

Near the cisterns, it would not create hidden springs. But it would help the water reveal its condition.

A water-keeper might learn that a certain pale film on the surface of a tank meant contamination, and another green bloom meant health. A notched measuring rod might become more than a measure: by custom, each district might add a mark not only for fullness but for the year, the season, the repair, the household that cleaned the tank. The power would strengthen memory where memory was already sacred. It would not replace their care. It would make their care less likely to be betrayed by accident.

In the cold inner rooms, it would not grant endless fuel.

It would help heat behave.

A fire-keeper might discover that three stones arranged in a low curve behind a small flame threw warmth back into the room better than a flat wall. A family might raise a sleeping platform half a handspan higher to escape the coldest draft. A doorway might gain a second hide, or a bend, or a stone bench that broke the rush of night air. These things would seem obvious once found. That would be part of the power’s wisdom: to give gifts that feel like remembering rather than revelation.

Among the herds, it would not make the animals tame in a way that erased their dignity. But it would help the old compact hold.

A mother beast might refuse to enter a lane that looked safe to human eyes, and the herder, instead of striking her, might remember that animals sometimes hear danger in stone. A young animal in an undercroft might find the warmest corner by instinct. A flock moving through fog might keep its order instead of crowding. A herder might learn a low whistle that calmed a nervous beast, or a way of standing that turned pressure into guidance. The power would not remove the burden of tending animals. It would make the tending slightly more merciful.

For the watchers on the high cairns, it would not abolish distance or storm. But it would grant, now and then, a clarity that mattered.

A cloud might lift for a few breaths at the right moment. A raptor might circle in a way that drew the eye toward a far slope where smoke was beginning. A raven might call from a direction that mattered. A runner might see a signal repeated on a neighboring ridge just before fog swallowed it. The watchers already knew how to see; the power would only widen the chance that seeing was enough.

And for children—because a wise power would know that the future is carried in small bodies—it would not make them invulnerable. That would turn them into strangers to their own world. But it would give them a better chance to grow into the shape of their people.
