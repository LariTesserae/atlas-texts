A benevolent and wise power would begin by refusing the temptation to be obvious.

They would not arrive as a blast of light, not as a voice from the black-blue height, not as a hand large enough to close over the pocket terrace and make it still. If such a power turned toward that closet-sized landscape at the roof of the world, their first act would be a diminishment. They would make themselves small enough to notice. They would fold their attention down until it could fit between the hanging reed mats and the veil of falling water, until it could enter the hot dark without tearing a single leaf from its place.

Their first deed would be listening.

They would hear the water slapping into clay, the reeds bending their mournful chord upward into a whistle, the copper strips clicking as the humidity swelled, the muffled boiling under the floor like something patient stirring a pot of the world’s first soup. They would hear the room listening back. They would notice that every drip has a destination, every hiss a meaning, every pause a breath. They would understand that this is not a place waiting for a god to give it purpose. It already has purpose. Its purpose is becoming.

Then they would look closer, and they would see the danger in it.

Not the danger of malice. Not the danger of famine or war or hatred, because those things have not found this crevice yet. The danger is subtler: the danger of being too new, too crowded, too exact, too unfinished. A landscape the size of a closet can be ruined by one careless foot. A creature not yet decided about its own face can be ruined by one imposed answer. A world that has no natural substrate can be ruined by someone insisting that it must be finished before it knows what it wants to be.

A lesser kindness might try to rescue the pocket by completing it.

A lesser wisdom might say: Let me give it stone. Let me give it deep soil. Let me give it a true mountain under its beams and a true sun above its slits. Let me give the salamander eyes, the makers faces, the plants room to grow wild, the basin a shore, the channels an ocean, the room a name. Let me make it permanent. Let me make it safe. Let me make it whole.

But the benevolent and wise power would see that this would be a violence dressed as mercy.

To give it final stone would be to end its slow clay-thinking. To give it a true sun would be to erase its amber-lit, fungal-glowing, several-shadowed intimacy. To give the salamander eyes before it had chosen them would be to decide its future for it. To give the makers finished faces would be to make them less than hands. To make the pocket permanent in the ordinary way would be to stop the wet, fibrous creak of new ridges rising. It would be to quiet the boiling under the floor. It would be to turn a living act into an object.

So the power would make a vow, and the vow would be the first gift:

*I will not finish you unless you ask.*

That vow would not be spoken in thunder. The place would not accept thunder unless thunder had been tuned through reeds and caught in bark membranes and made into a usable sound. Instead, the vow would enter as a small alteration in the room’s rhythm. A drip would fall more gently. The steam would thicken for one moment into a shape like an open palm. A bead of clay would form on a wall where no wall had been weeping before. The reed pipes would pass through a note so brief that even the makers would pause with their bone needles lifted.

The room would hear the vow structurally.

It would answer in the only way it could: by continuing.

After that, the power would ask permission.

Not because the pocket could refuse in a simple way, but because wisdom knows that even a newborn world has a right to consent. The power would send the question through the smallest possible means. A droplet might fall into a seed-case with a tick brighter than usual. A springtail might jump and land exactly on the rim of the basin. One of the handprints in the wall might flex as if water had run over it, though no water had. The salamander might lift one translucent foot from the blue mosaic and place it on the clay edge, not to climb out, but to feel what was asking.

The answer would not be yes or no in words.

The answer would be: *You may stay if you stay as we stay—by helping.*

Then the power would face the strangest problem of all: the pocket does not accept what has not been made.

A god could not simply enter there as pure presence. Pure presence would be unworked material. It would be too raw. The room would not know where to place it. The walls would sweat in confusion. The channels would stutter. The moss would curl. Even the brazier’s hidden breath would go uncertain. If a benevolent power wished to act within that place, they would have to become a made thing. They would have to submit to the grammar of thumb and cord and clay.

So they would make themselves small.

They would gather kiln-dust from a forgotten corner, charcoal from beneath the brazier grille, a strip of softened bark from a fern basket, a thread of cloth pulled loose from a root-binding, a pin of copper warmed by many hands, a bead of water from the basin’s edge. They would shape these into a body no larger than the other makers: root-mat shoulders, clay chest, limbs lashed with fiber cord, fingers numerous enough for the work. They might give themselves no face, or only a smooth thumb-hollow where a face could someday be considered. They might give themselves two hands at first, then a third, then several more, because the room often requires more hands than dignity.

They would become one of the hand-born, but not quite like the others.

The other makers would know, perhaps, by the warmth that lingered where this new maker touched. They would not bow. They would not cry out. They would simply make room on the scaffold, or hold a leaf steady while the stranger stitched it, or pass a pellet of wet clay without turning their head, because in that place tools are passed when repair is needed. The power would work among them. They would mend a cracked tile. They would tune a reed that had gone flat. They would press moss back into life and hold it there until the humidity remembered. They would clear spores from the basin with a woven pad. They would listen beside a pipe until the drip was wrong by only the width of a hair, then correct it with a touch so slight that no monument would remain.

This would be the second gift: not rule, but labor.

A wise benevolence would understand that the highest help is often indistinguishable from maintenance. It would not carve its name into the basin. It would not demand that the makers build a shrine. It would not ask the salamander to rise and worship. It would not even require that anyone know exactly who had passed through. It would leave only one sign, perhaps: a handprint in the wall unlike the others, slightly warmer, slightly deeper, with a faint curve in the palm like a cupped drop. If a maker touched it in passing, they would feel less alone, though they might not understand why.

Then the power would begin to arrange protections.

Not walls. The pocket is already hidden, and walls might stop its breathing. Not armor. Armor would make it brittle. Not a great spell of preservation, because preservation as stillness is death. Instead, the power would make adjustments along the seams of chance.

They would deepen the misdirection of the entrance. The clouds below would continue to move like slow white animals, but they would learn to close around the crevice when the wrong eyes looked up from lower slopes. The severe elevation would keep most seekers away by honest hardship. The thin blue-black sky would remain too high, too strange, too demanding for casual conquest. Those who came with greed in their hands would find only wind and rock and the feeling of having forgotten something. Those who came with the need to possess would find their maps rearranged in their pockets, their certainties loosened, their desire turned into fatigue. Those who came to destroy would become confused by their own tools.

But those who knew where not to look would still find it.

The power would not make the pocket easy to reach. Easy reaches are dangerous to small worlds. They would make it reachable only by a quality of attention. A person would have to arrive with their hands open. They would have to be willing to turn sideways. They would have to accept leaves against their cheek. They would have to understand that distance was not offered there. If they entered carrying the wish to take, the room would become a steam-filled closet and they would soon find themselves outside again, unable to say how they had been disappointed. If they entered carrying the wish to help, a small repair would present itself: a loose cord, a dry moss edge, a seedpod cup tipped too far, a copper pin needing one gentle tap. If they made that repair, even clumsily, the room would remember them kindly.

That would be the third gift: discernment at the threshold.

Inside, the power would not abolish the pocket’s difficulties. The heat would remain hot. The damp would remain damp. The noise would remain too large for the space. The centipede would still emerge when subtraction was needed. The springtails would still leap in alarm. The ferns would still open too quickly. The room would still be crowded, overfull, saturated with more reality than its dimensions should allow. A wise power would know that removing all hardship would remove the shape of the life. The pocket is not a paradise waiting to be improved. It is a process learning to trust itself.

But the power would prevent certain kinds of ruin.

They would make sure the hidden brazier never failed entirely. Beneath the grille of fired tiles, one ember would learn to sleep in ash and damp for as long as necessary, then wake when the room needed heat. They would make sure the water never vanished. In a fold of the woven ceiling, a small cloud no larger than a child’s blanket would be kept in reserve, not for ordinary rain but for the day when the channels forgot their music.
