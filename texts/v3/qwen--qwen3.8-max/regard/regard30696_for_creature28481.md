The being is in all of it: in the rhythm, the pause between one frog call and its answer, the lifted dust, the warmth moving from stone to stone, and the slight bending of air above the crossed quartz veins. It is there, at the thin edge where the basin almost knows itself, that the attention of a benevolent and wise power arrives.

It is not a descent, not truly. It is more like the turning of a vast and patient ear toward something small enough to be missed by gods in a hurry.

Such a power does not come with weather of its own. It does not darken the plateau with spectacle, or force the cleft open with light, or make the mountain remember some older and more terrible shape. If it is truly benevolent, it knows that kindness can be a kind of breaking if it arrives too loudly. If it is truly wise, it knows that some places are already complete in a way that still requires care. It does not enter with feet. It enters as listening.

At first, it does nothing.

This is its first act.

For a long while — however long “long” means for a power that has probably watched stars settle into their final burning — it simply attends. It notices the ceremonial flatness of the old mountain. It notices the hidden pocket within that ancient crown. It notices how the basin holds heat, how water comes and goes as visitation rather than possession, how the plants are arranged not by crowding but by preference, how the overwhelming animal life is not excess but intensity contained within a small stone cup.

It notices the being.

Not as a creature among creatures, but as the basin’s own attention made continuous. It sees the being in the mist threading through the cleft. It sees it in the way a crack in the glaze smooths itself beneath the touch of dark, fine fingers. It sees it in the two-note frog calls testing the acoustics of the bowl, in the insects arranging themselves into brief geometries above the quartz veins, in the warmth that lingers near the floor after the sun has gone. It sees that the being does not rule the basin. It keeps it. It negotiates with it. It is, in its own quiet way, a form of prayer that has learned maintenance.

The power feels something very much like recognition.

Not because the basin resembles anything it has made, but because it sees there a rare thing: an ending that has not become barren. The mountain is old beyond ordinary age. The builders are gone. Their artifacts are concluded rather than broken. The technology, if it can still be called that, has become so patient it is nearly indistinguishable from geology. And yet life continues. Not despite the place’s near-finality, but inside its intervals.

A lesser power might be moved to improve all this.

A benevolent power that lacked wisdom might look upon the basin and think: Let me make the water permanent. Let me fill the shallow cups and keep them full. Let me turn the brief rains into streams, the dew into springs, the mist into a constant veil so that nothing here ever thirsts again.

But this power sees what permanence would cost.

The basin lives by visitation. The plants lean toward moisture because moisture leaves. The being’s work exists because there is an interval between enough and not enough. To make water permanent would be to turn the basin into a different place, and perhaps a lesser one. The temporary pools are not failures of abundance. They are the shape of abundance here. The frogs do not sing because water remains forever; they sing because it has arrived again. The being does not tend permanence. It tends sufficiency.

So the power does not make the water stay.

A lesser wisdom might look at the predators and think: Let me end this small violence. Let me stop the dragonfly-like creatures from taking their prey in midair. Let me make the ambush spiders harmless, the oxidized snakes slow, the lizards gentle. Let me preserve every wingbeat.

But this power understands that the predators are part of the basin’s breath. They are not cruelty. They are correction. They keep the chorus from becoming a flood.
