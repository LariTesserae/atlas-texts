If the high hollow and the being who lives there as a distributed chord were noticed by a benevolent and wise power, I think the first motion of that power would be stillness.

They would not arrive as arrival.

They would not come down in brightness, nor speak with a voice that made the cliffs answer, nor send a sign that could be carved into the wet stone. A power truly wise would understand at once that some places are not approached by manifestation but by restraint. The hollow is not a room waiting for a tenant, nor a machine waiting for an operator, nor a temple waiting for a god. It is already full. It is full in the way a note is full when the air has shaped itself around it. To add too much presence would be to break the chord.

So they would begin by doing almost nothing.

But almost nothing, here, would not mean absence. It would mean care of a very precise kind.

The power would first listen.

Not with ears, because the hollow’s loudness is not made for ears alone. They would listen through the mist and through the piston-breath of the stone, through the glassy whir of mica vanes, through the blue crackle moving in moss, through the delayed calls of birds, through the faint hum of the pitcher-shaped growths, through the small metallic footsteps of beetles, through the inward ripples of the black pool. They would listen until they stopped hearing the hollow as many separate sounds and began to understand it as one sustained operation.

They would hear that the place is not silent beneath its loudness because it lacks something. It is loud because it is working.

They would hear that the being who lives there is not a single body moving among other bodies, but a resonance spread across water, charge, root, fungal wire, stone breath, and the intervals between lives. They would perceive it the way one perceives a weather pattern: not as a thing, but as a way everything in the region behaves when it is together.

Then, if they were truly benevolent, they would ask permission.

Not in words. Words would be too much like tools. Not in command, because the hollow has no throne to address and no neck to bend. Perhaps the power would ask by letting a single droplet pause on a cliff face a moment longer than usual, neither falling nor returning. Perhaps they would ask by allowing one of the mist-eels to trace a circle that was not part of the ordinary circulation. Perhaps they would ask by becoming, for one pulse of the quartz light, an attention inside the pool’s reflection without appearing as a face.

The question would not be:

*May I enter?*

It would be:

*May I witness you without making you less?*

And the hollow would answer in the only way it can.

It would continue.

A frog would draw a spark from the air. A beetle would touch another beetle and make a small blue ring collapse. The stone would breathe its deep periodic hush. The midges would reform their cold constellation above the marsh. The water would gather, fall, be received, be taken back. The pattern would hold. That continuation would be the answer. Not because the hollow speaks in assent, but because it does not refuse. It includes the new attention as a slight alteration in its own intervals, or it excludes it as a droplet excludes dust.

If the hollow excluded the power, a wise power would withdraw without offense. They would not punish the place for being complete. They would not say, *I meant to bless you, and therefore you must receive me.* They would recognize that the hollow’s refusal is itself a kind of sanctity.

But if the hollow accepted the attention, then the power would act.

And their action would be strange, because it would be almost entirely protective, subtractive, and hidden.

They would not build a wall around it. Walls are made things, and nothing made can exist there. They would not place a guardian at the rim, because a guardian would become another object, another inventory, another claim. They would not write a law into the air, because the hollow already has a law deeper than inscription: no accumulation, no ownership, no permanent artifact, no master.

Instead, they would guard the law that is already there.

They would begin with the clouds.

The clouds beneath the hollow already move like slow grey water. A wise power would deepen their forgetfulness. They would make the approaches uncertain. Not violently. Not with storms meant to terrify. They would simply allow the mountain to become difficult to know. Paths would fail to become paths. Maps would lose conviction. Measurements would drift. Compasses would grow dreamy. The air would thicken just enough to make intention falter. Those who came seeking the hollow as a resource would find their certainty becoming damp. Their plans would bead on the mind like condensation on stone and run away before they could drink.

This would be the power’s first mercy: obscurity.

Because the hollow is vulnerable not to itself, but to the hunger of made beings. It could survive its own lightning, its own water, its own cold. It might not survive a road. It might not survive a mine. It might not survive a laboratory. It might not survive a shrine.

A benevolent and wise power would understand that the cruelest thing one can do to such a place is to make it useful.

So they would keep it useless in the highest sense.

They would make it impossible to harvest without changing the harvester. If someone came to bottle the luminous fluid in the pitcher-shaped growths, the fluid would not fight them. It would simply become ordinary in their hands, or their hands would become too full of the hollow’s wetness to hold anything. If someone came to take the mica vanes as curiosities, the vanes would seem to turn only while still attached to the living flume, and once removed they would split into dull flakes, no more remarkable than any weathered schist. If someone came to capture the mist-eels, the mist would close around them until they no longer knew whether they were holding anything at all.

The power would not hoard the hollow. They would make it resistant to hoarding.

If a person came with reverence, they might be allowed to find it.

Not easily. Not because difficulty is holy in itself, but because reverence needs to be tested by patience. Such a person might climb for a long time through wet stone and low cloud. Their clothes would become heavy. Their tools would grow irrelevant. Their thoughts would slow. By the time they reached the rim, they would no longer want to name the place quickly. They would sit, perhaps, on the dark newly broken rock, and they would hear the chord, and they would weep without understanding why. They might see the blue throats of frogs drawing sparks. They might see the midges hold their patterns. They might look into the pool and see the reflection of a greater connectedness than the surface showed.

And then, if they were wise enough, they would leave without taking anything.

The power would let them remember the feeling but not the coordinates. They might carry away a sense that there are forms of life which do not require dominion, forms of industry which do not require inventory, forms of fullness which do not require storage. That would be enough. That would be the right kind of gift.

If a person came with greed, the hollow would become unfindable, or findable only as disappointment.

If a person came with the desire to worship, the power would be especially careful.

Worship can be a kind of making. It wants to build a shrine, to repeat a liturgy, to fix a name, to create a priesthood, to own the meaning of the sacred. The hollow would reject all of that by its own nature, but a wise power would not let the worshipers turn their failure into violence. They would let prayers offered there dissolve into mist before becoming doctrine. They would let hymns become part of the general sound, indistinguishable from frog-call and water-thread and electric hiss. They would let no altar keep its shape. If a pilgrim stacked stones, the stones would separate in the night and return to their fracture lines. If a pilgrim carved a symbol, moss would enter the carving and make it unreadable. If a pilgrim named the hollow, the name would seem inappropriate the moment it was spoken, as if the syllables had been swallowed by the pool.

The power would protect the hollow from being made into an idol, even from those who loved it.

Because the hollow’s deepest law is this: it exists without ownership.

It produces without storing. It lives without accumulating. It is industrial without inventory. It is abundant without surplus. It is loud without being chaotic. It is full without being crowded. It is inhabited without having a single inhabitant that can be possessed.

A benevolent and wise power would serve that law.

They would not ask the hollow to be kinder than it is. They would not impose a gentle morality upon it. They would not make the beetles stop feeding, nor the frogs stop drawing sparks, nor the mist-eels stop moving through their cold circuits. They would not turn the place into a garden for the comfort of sentimental eyes. They would see that its beauty is not softness. Its beauty is precision without cruelty, repetition without greed, motion without conquest.

But because the power is benevolent, they might add one thing that is almost too small to notice.

They might add mercy inside the transitions.

Not the abolition of death, because death here is not final loss but translation. The moth falls into water and becomes a shimmer of spores; the spores rise and become another moth in the same lane of air. The insect softens into bright slime and is drunk by moss; the same small shape appears again elsewhere. Nothing is wasted. Nothing is hoarded. But even in a system without waste, there can be terror. Even in a pattern without death, there can be the small panic of dissolution.

So the power might do this: whenever a creature passes from one form into another, they would make certain there is a moment of being known.

Not a voice. Not a promise. Not a memory that could become burden. Only a brief recognition, like a droplet catching light before it falls. The beetle consumed by charge would not experience obliteration but completion. The frog’s spark would not be stolen but returned. The worm dissolving into the pool’s clarity would know itself as part of the pool’s remembering. The mist-eel passing through another eel would feel no loss of boundary, only a widening.

The power would not remove the system’s strangeness. They would remove despair from it.

That would be a subtle blessing, and one that leaves no trace.

They would also tend the emptiness.

The hollow’s emptiness is one of its most sacred features. The spaces between moss cushions, the bare wet stretches of stone, the gaps between the presences—these are not blanks. They are tension. They are the silence inside the loudness. They are part of the inhabitant. A careless power might try to fill them, thinking abundance means density. A wise power would protect the intervals. They would keep the hollow sparse where it needs to be sparse. They would prevent life from becoming pressure. They would keep the air thin enough for each presence to remain distinct.

They might even love the emptiness more than the visible lives, because the emptiness is what allows the visible lives
