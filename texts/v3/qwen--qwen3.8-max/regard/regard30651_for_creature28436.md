If a benevolent and wise power noticed this basin and the being who tends it, the first thing they would do is nothing that makes the basin less itself.

They would not come as fire. They would not come as a voice too large for the stone. They would not come as a brilliance that burns the dimness out of every folded corner. They would not fill the hollow with green leaves, singing animals, or tall golden shrines, because wisdom would tell them that this place is not poor; it is narrow by nature and complete by its own laws. To turn it into something else would not be kindness. It would be erasure.

So they would arrive, if they arrived at all, as attention.

They would listen to the water until they knew each drip’s habit. They would learn which stones were loosening over centuries, which mineral films were thinning, which pools held warmth longest, which little channels had been cut by hands long ago and were still used with reverence. They would learn the rules of the basin: water returns; nothing dries; sound must be small; life must live simply; change must not accumulate. And because they were wise, they would understand that a benevolent act here must not break those rules but fulfill them.

Their first gift would be restraint.

They would not demand worship. They would not carve their name into the basalt or make the water rise in a dramatic sign. They would not take the being away from the basin to show them greater things, unless the being truly asked for that and was fit for it. They would not fill the place with crowds of pilgrims, because the basin could not bear crowds. They would not make the being famous, because fame would bring noise, and noise would be wrong in that soft air.

Instead, they would ask, in whatever way such powers ask, whether help was welcome.

Perhaps the question would not be spoken. Perhaps it would be a single bead of water hanging on a stone lip for one heartbeat longer than usual, as if the basin itself had paused. Perhaps the being would look up from a clay bowl and feel, without knowing why, that they were being seen with great care. Perhaps they would dream of a hand held just above a pool, not touching it, only sheltering it.

If the being drew back in fear, the power would become smaller. If the being was too tired to answer, the power would take silence as a request for gentleness, not absence. If the being, in their wordless way, reached toward the place where a support had cracked or a channel had grown narrow, that would be enough. Consent, in such a place, might be nothing more than attention given to a wound.

Then the power would act.

Not loudly. Not visibly, at first. They would work at the seams of things, where harm begins before it becomes visible.

They would find the hairline fracture hidden in a column that, in another thousand years, might have fallen and crushed a shelter. They would guide a slow drip toward it, season after season, until mineral built up along the crack and bound it again. To anyone watching, it would look like ordinary accretion. To the basin, it would be healing.

They would notice where a shallow pool had begun to lose water through a seam too fine for the eye. They would not seal it with a miracle that violated stone. They would encourage the mineral-laden water to linger there a little longer, to deposit its burden grain by grain, until the leak closed itself in the basin’s own language.

They would see the channel no wider than a forearm that carried water from one basin to another, now partly blocked by old mineral crust. They would not scour it clean with force. They would alter a drip somewhere above so that, over time, the water found its proper path again and kept it open.

They would preserve the small human-made things without making them grand. The hammerstone in its niche would not be lifted into legend. The bone scoop would not be taken away. The clay bowl would remain where it was needed. But the power might keep the bowl from cracking when the being’s hands were clumsy with fatigue. They might keep the cord from snapping at the exact moment it held weight. They might make the copper awl, green with age, remain useful for one more careful task than it otherwise would have.

For the being, they would do the same kind of quiet tending.

If the being was human or near-human, the power would know how constant moisture can wear a body even when it is warm. They would not make the being dry, because dryness would betray the basin. But they might ease the ache in the joints. They might keep the skin from breaking at the knuckles and feet. They might make sleep deeper in the small shelter with its slab roof, so that the being woke less from cold dreams and more into the ordinary warmth of the place.

They would not give the being what they did not need. They would not fill the basin with food that required a different world. But they might help the simple food already there to be enough. The microbial films might thicken where they could be harvested without harm. The mineral crusts might form in places easier to reach. The rare pale beetle or blind shrimp might remain rare, but not so rare that it seemed the world was ending. If the being collected water in the old way, the power might ensure that the best drip did not fail.
