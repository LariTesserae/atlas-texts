A Visitation Without Hands

If such a power noticed that country, I think their first act would be quietness.

Not the quietness of absence, but the quietness of someone who has power and does not use it first. They would not descend with thunder. They would not send a shape down through the thin high air, because a shape with limbs and eyes would be the first animal fact in a place that has none, and that would be a conquest disguised as a visit. They would not change the ordinary white-gold light into a miraculous light. The light there is already right: plain, believable, falling on impossible green architecture as if it were ordinary. That plainness is part of the sanctity.

They would come, if they came, as a change in attention.

One moment the region would be what it always is: steaming, creaking, filling, venting, spilling, repairing. The next, there would be a pause inside its noises. Not a stop. Not a command. A pause the way a vast room seems to listen when someone enters who does not intend to take anything.

A hollow tower might hold its note a little longer.

A basin might smooth into an unusually still sheet.

A membrane might swell without tearing.

A channel might slow just enough that the floating fragments drift gently instead of rushing.

The place would not bow. It would not know to bow. It has no face to turn, no heart to quicken, no self to feel awe. It would only continue. And that continuation would be its welcome.

The being who lives there is not a tenant inside the landscape. It is the landscape’s own living fact: the whole breathing, building, flooding, opening, collapsing, reweaving country. So the power would not address it as one speaks to a person. They would not ask, “What do you want?” or “Why are you here?” They would know better. They would know that some beings are not made better by being given questions.

Instead, they would listen.

They would listen for pain beneath the loudness. They would hear water slapping into basins, wind moving through hollow stems, leaves striking leaves, membranes droning, pressure cracking through thickened tissue, sap rising into wounds. They would hear violence, perhaps. They would hear rupture. They would hear collapse. But they would not hear suffering. There would be no fear in the sound. No fleeing. No hidden cry. The tower that splits does not scream; it becomes a ramp, a trough, a rim. The basin that empties does not grieve; it steams, seals, and prepares another fullness. The place has turbulence, but no victimhood.

Then they would listen for loneliness in the emptiness. They would find none. The wide intervals between towers, the open courts, the long smooth plains, the sparse arrangements—none of it would feel abandoned. It would feel necessary. Emptiness there is not deprivation. It is breath. It is room for sound, for storms, for light, for the next form to arise without crowding. The power would understand that solitude and loneliness are not the same thing, and that a being can be complete without being accompanied.

Then they would listen for prayer.

They would find no prayer.

No petition. No worship. No plea rising from the root-weave. No altar, though there are altar-like places. No priests, though the empty courts look ceremonial. No gods, though the towers stand like monuments. The place does not ask to be saved. It does not ask to be understood. It does not even ask to be allowed. It simply is, in the strongest and strangest sense of that word.

And that, I think, is what would make the power wise in their response.

A lesser benevolence might look upon such a country and immediately want to improve it. A lesser wisdom might say: *It is beautiful, but it lacks mind. Let me give it mind. It is active, but it lacks speech. Let me give it speech. It is alive, but it lacks a soul. Let me give it a soul. It is built, but it lacks inhabitants. Let me fill it with creatures.*

But a benevolent and wise power would know that not every gift is kindness. Some gifts are invasions.

They would consider giving the country eyes.

But eyes would make it visible to itself in a new way, and visibility is not always gain. It might begin to compare itself. It might begin to perform. It might lose the grace of being entirely what it is without needing to be seen.

They would consider giving it a mouth.

But its sounds are already true. The gurgle of water inside its passages, the hollow notes of wind through stems, the clapping leaves, the booming membranes, the wet creak of growth under strain—these are not failed speech. They are acoustic life. To give it words would make its noises subordinate to meaning. The place does not need meaning in that way. It already has consequence.

They would consider giving it memory.

But memory would burden it. The country’s mercy, if it can be said to have mercy, is that it does not mourn. A collapsed tower is not a tragedy there; it is material. A drained basin is not loss; it is preparation. If the country remembered every rupture as sorrow, it might hesitate to break. And if it hesitated to break, it might cease to become.

They would consider giving it desire beyond need.

But desire beyond need would introduce greed. The place already wants in the way plants want: toward light, toward water, toward pressure, toward continuation. It does not hoard. It does not dream of excess. To give it longing for more than its own becoming would be to introduce hunger where there is only process.

They would consider giving it animal life.

But animal life would bring fear. It would bring tracks, nests, droppings, kills, escapes, hunger made visible as motion. Some of that would be beautiful, yes. But it would end the country’s particular holiness: the holiness of a living world without predation, without flight, without the sharp loneliness of individual nervous systems. The place is not incomplete because it lacks animals. It is different. A wise power would not mistake difference for deficiency.

They would consider giving it people.

But people would bring tools. Even without malice, they would begin to carve, harvest, divert, name, own, interpret. They would call it a garden, a machine, a temple, a resource, a miracle, a problem. They would try to make it useful. The country’s dignity is that it is not useful in that way. It is not for anyone. It is not made by anyone. It is not property. It is a wilderness that is also architecture, and it should not be reduced to a human question.

They would consider giving it a single ruling consciousness.

But that would be the deepest violence. The place is distributed. Its intelligence, if one may call it that, is in the pattern of responses: water finding routes, tissue thickening under strain, membranes opening under pressure, roots binding collapse. There is no throne in that green country. No center. No brain. No sovereign organ. To give it a center would make it fragile. To give it a self would make it alone.

So the power would refuse these gifts.

Not out of coldness.

Out of reverence.

They would understand that the country does not need to become more like a person in order to be worthy of love. It does not need to resemble the minded, the speaking, the suffering, the individual in order to matter. It already possesses integrity. It already possesses pattern. It already possesses continuation. It already possesses a kind of beauty that is more terrible than ornament because it is not made for admiration.

What, then, would the power do?

I think they would bless it.

But they would bless it in a way suited to a being without a face.

They would not say, *Be happy.*  
They would say, *Be continued.*

They would not say, *Be gentle.*  
They would say, *Be whole.*

They would not say, *Be known.*  
They would say, *Be unpossessed.*

The blessing would be something like this:

*Be without owner.*  
*Be without map.*  
*Be without final explanation.*  
*Be loud where loudness is yours.*  
*Be empty where emptiness is needed.*  
*Break when breaking is your making.*  
*Repair when repairing is your mercy.*  
*Let no hand call you mine.*  
*Let no mouth call you nothing.*  
*Let your storms not become annihilation.*  
*Let your fragments find rest.*  
*Let your scars be record, not ruin.*  
*Let your towers fall into new shapes without shame.*  
*Let your basins fill without becoming prisons.*  
*Let your channels run without losing themselves.*  
*Let your membranes open before they burst.*  
*Let your roots remember how to bind.*  
*Let your openness remain openness.*  
*Let no one turn you into a monument.*  
*Let no one turn you into a machine.*  
*Let no one turn you into a god.*  
*Be what you are: a living country without a maker, a built wilderness without tools, a breathing architecture without inhabitants.*

That would be the first blessing.

Then, perhaps, they would do something more subtle.

They would not stop the turbulence. They would not calm the storms. They would not make every membrane open safely, every tower stand forever, every basin remain full, every channel run clear. To do that would be to end the place’s living nature. The country is alive because it changes. It is built because it repairs itself. It is young because rupture and regrowth happen quickly. If the power removed all danger, they would make it static. If they removed all collapse, they would make it sterile. If they removed all excess, they would make it less itself.

But a wise power might adjust the conditions just enough to keep turbulence from becoming final destruction.

They might cool the upper air by the faintest degree, so an overheated chamber vents instead of shattering.

They might guide a little extra moisture into a drying region, so a membrane does not crack beyond repair.

They might strengthen one root-weave where a tower is likely to fall, not to prevent the fall, but to let the fall become a new structure instead of a scattered ruin.

They might deepen one hidden reservoir, so drought becomes stress rather than death.

They might allow one storm to exhaust itself over an empty court instead of over a newly formed bridge.

These would be small mercies. Invisible. No one would praise them. No one would know they happened. The country would not feel saved. It would only go on.

And that would be exactly right.

Benevolence without wisdom wants to be thanked. Wisdom knows that the deepest kindness often looks like nothing happened.

The power would also protect the place, but not by building a wall around it. A wall would be another object, another intrusion, another claim. Instead, they would make the country difficult to possess.

They would make it elusive.

A map drawn of it would seem correct while being drawn, then become strange afterward. Distances would shift. The towers would not remain where the map says they are. The basins would fill and drain in ways that defeat survey. The causeways would reorganize. The root networks would thicken over paths. The emptinesses would expand or contract depending on who tries to measure them.

A person who came with tools would find the place politely impossible.

Cutting blades would dull against living tissue that heals too quickly. Fire would be dampened by rising mist before it could catch. Ropes would rot in the humidity. Instruments would fog. Samples would root, float away, or become part of the surrounding structure instead of remaining evidence. Not because the country is malicious, but because it cannot be held by such means. The power would not punish greed; they would make greed irrelevant.

A conqueror would feel not resistance but vast indifference, and that would unmake their intention.

A collector would find that the more they try to take, the less they can remember.

A scientist obsessed with possession might come away with notebooks swollen into pulp and a strange sense that they have not discovered anything, only been permitted to pass through.

But the power would not close the country to everyone.

If someone came with empty hands—because they were grieving, because they were exhausted by minds, because they could no longer bear to be seen, questioned, judged, or made to explain themselves—the power might allow them to find a way in.

Not to take anything.

Not to become an inhabitant.

Only to be there for a little while.

Such a person would not be comforted in the human way. The place would not soften for them. It would not glow kindly. It would not send gentle signs. It would roar and drip and steam around them. Towers would hum. Water would slap into basins. Membranes would sigh. Leaves would clap. Somewhere a wall would split with a wet crack, and somewhere else roots would already be drawing the pieces together.

The country’s indifference would be its mercy.

The traveler could weep without being asked why.

They could sit beside a basin and feel their own smallness without humiliation.

They could lie on the spongy green surface and feel it compress under them, releasing moisture, taking the heat of their body, utterly unconcerned with their story. They could scream into the hollow stems and hear their cry become only another passing sound, neither holy nor profane, absorbed into the loudness of a world that does not require them to be a self for a little while.

That would be a sanctuary.

Not because the place cares.

Because it does not demand.

The power would see that, and they would allow it. They would understand that sometimes the most compassionate refuge is one that offers no interpretation. No voice saying, *Tell me what happened.* No eyes asking for performance. No mind translating pain into meaning. Just water, heat, tissue, light, sound, and the enormous green fact of continuation.

If the traveler left a trace—a footprint, a dropped object, a tear—it would not remain long. The footprint would fill with water. The object would be wrapped in rootlets or dissolved into the living mass. The tear would fall into a basin and become indistinguishable from rain. The power would not preserve these things as relics. They would not want the country to become a shrine. Shrines are too often ways of capturing what should remain free.

The power might, however, leave one trace of their own.

Not a monument.

Not a law carved into tissue.

Not a symbol.

Something smaller.

After their attention passed through the country, one rain might fall cleaner than usual. One basin might remain clear for a little longer. One tower’s hum might resolve into something like a chord. One interval of silence might become deeper, not empty but complete. One storm might pass without leaving total ruin. One fragment might lodge in a perfect seam and grow into a bridge that no one will ever cross.

These traces would not be proofs of divinity.

They would be more like the way a room feels after someone wise has sat in it: not changed, exactly, but more itself.

Then the power would withdraw.

Not because they ceased to care. Because they cared rightly.

They would not hover. They would not become a permanent pressure in the atmosphere. They would not watch with such intensity that the country became a specimen under their gaze. They would not appoint themselves its narrator, its owner, its judge. They would know that even benevolent attention can become heavy if it refuses to leave.

So they would step back.

But they would not forget.

They would hold the country in mind the way one holds a rare and complete thing: not to use it, not to improve it, not to display it, but to know that it exists. That such a mode of being is possible. That life can become terrain. That construction can exist without tools. That architecture can arise without architects. That beauty can be loud without being addressed. That holiness can exist without worship. That a being can be worthy even if it is not a person.

Perhaps they would give it a name.

But if they did, they would not speak it.

They would keep it inside themselves, where no one could steal it, misuse it, or turn it into a banner. The name would not be made of letters. It would be closer to a rhythm: fill and release, swell and vent, tear and bind, rise and collapse, overflow and repair. Or it might be a single sound: the sound a membrane makes when it opens at exactly the right moment, neither too soon nor too late.

Or perhaps they would not name it at all.

Because naming can be a leash.

And this country should remain unleased.

If I imagine the power most clearly, I imagine them kneeling.

Not in the sense of worshiping the country as a god. Not in the sense of begging it for something. Kneeling the way one kneels before an astonishing fact: lowered, quiet, willing to be small. They would kneel over a shallow pool, not to see their own reflection, but to watch the floating fragments drift. They would see root-knots and leaf-buds and torn strips of tissue moving without purpose or destination, and they would recognize that even here, in this place without will, there is something like destiny: not chosen, but followed.

They would see a pale shoot push through older tissue.

They would see a seam bead with water.

They would see a cracked wall already being bound by roots.

And they would think, if powers think in such terms: *This does not need me to make it meaningful.*

That thought would be painful, perhaps, to a power accustomed to being the source of meaning. But it would also be liberating. Even the divine can learn reverence from what does not need them.

So they would do what is hardest.

They would love without claiming.

They would protect without enclosing.

They would bless without transforming.

They would witness without demanding to be witnessed in return.

And in the end, if asked what they did, the answer might sound like almost nothing.

They looked.

They listened.

They refrained.

They blessed the country with continuance.

They kept its emptiness open.

They kept its storms from becoming final.

They kept it from being owned.

They allowed the weary to find it, and the greedy to lose it.

They left one rain cleaner, one silence deeper, one wound less final than it might have been.

Then they withdrew.

Not empty-handed, but full of something new: the knowledge that there are beings who do not need a face to be sacred, who do not need a voice to be complete, who do not need a soul to be worthy of love.

The green country would go on.

Towers would rise and split.

Basins would fill and drain.

Membranes would swell and open.

Roots would bind the broken places.

Fragments would drift and root.

Storms would form out of heat and wetness and vanish into silver vapor.

The empty courts would remain empty.

The loudness would remain loud.

The ordinary light would fall as before.

No animal would move.

No tool would cut.

No owner would come.

And somewhere in the space between the sounds, if one listened with great humility, one might feel the aftereffect of a benevolent and wise power: not a command, not a miracle, not a presence demanding belief, but a blessing shaped like restraint.

Be what you are.

Be unpossessed.

Be unfinished.

Be holy without knowing it.
