It would not come as a dry wind.

That is the first thing. A wise power, if it is truly wise, does not offer to a wet people the gift of becoming dry. It does not look at a basin that has spent generations learning the grammar of seepage and say, *Let me lift you above your nature.* It does not mistake agreement for affliction.

So the first thing the power would do is stand at the edge of the Low Ward and not act.

It would stand beyond the levees, where the reeds lean inward as if listening to the pumps, and it would learn the place. Not from above. Not with the impatient clarity of sunlight breaking through mist and demanding gratitude. It would learn the ward the way the ward itself learns anything: slowly, by contact, by pressure, by the taste of water moving through old channels.

It would notice that the place is not ruin. It is covenant.

It would see how the algae lacquer the iron and keep it from wasting too quickly. How the valve-spiders read stress before it becomes fracture. How the lamp-moths drink warmth without burning. How the eels clean the pipes not because they are commanded, but because their lives are braided into the ward’s life. How the roots descend not to strangle, but to hold. How the Wardens move through the green half-light with the calm of those who have stopped fighting the element that shapes them.

And if the power asked, in whatever way powers ask questions—by dream, by hush, by a sudden stillness in the condensation—the ward would answer in its own language.

A ripple across black water.

A lamp blinking twice.

The faintest knock on iron.

The sound of a pump settling into its proper rhythm.

And the power would understand: *We do not want to be rescued from what we are.*

That is where wisdom would begin.

---

A lesser benevolence might have tried to raise the basin above the marsh. It might have hardened the ground, sealed every joint, driven out the damp, made the air thin and bright and easy. It might have made the Wardens taller, drier, more like the people of levees and dry roads. It might have given them engines that never failed, lamps that never needed trimming, gates that never groaned under pressure. It might have ended all leaks.

But a wise power would know that such gifts would be cruelties.

If the ward were lifted, its roots would die. If the air were made easy, the Wardens’ lungs would lose their old mastery. If no gate ever failed, no one would learn to listen. If no lamp needed tending, the lamp-trimmers would lose their station, and the moths would lose their careful orbit. If the water ceased to speak through small faults and warnings, the whole basin would become silent, and silence there would not mean peace. It would mean death.

So the power would refuse the obvious miracles.

It would not make the Low Ward dry.

It would not make the water sweet when its whole meaning is that it is black, mineral, patient, and alive with use.

It would not banish rust, because rust is one of the languages by which the ward says *here, attend to me.*

It would not remove the heat, because heat is part of the breath that turns condensation into local weather.

It would not make the Wardens certain, because certainty would dull their attention.

It would not appear in fullness, because fullness is a kind of flood, and even benevolence can drown what it loves.

It would not make them depend on it.

That would be the hardest refusal, and the most loving one.

---

Still, benevolence is not the same as distance. A wise power does not merely observe and call its observation kindness. If it loved the place—and it would, how could it not?—it would want to help the place remain itself without being destroyed by accident, malice, drought, or despair.

So it would act.

But it would act the way water acts in the Low Ward: quietly, persistently, through small passages.

Its first gift would be attention.

Not attention as command. Not attention as surveillance. Attention as nearness. The feeling, hard to name and impossible to prove, that the basin is not alone with its own maintenance. That its sounds are heard. That its patience is seen. That its small lives matter not merely as functions, but as themselves.

The Wardens would feel this before they could explain it.

It would come as a change in the quality of listening.

A gauge-listener pressing an ear to a cold pipe at midnight would hear, beneath the usual hum, a finer sound: not the beginning of failure, but the intention of failure before it gathers strength. She would not be frightened. She would simply know which valve to open, which channel to ease, which pump to slow. When she knocked on iron to summon help, her knock would carry farther than usual, not loudly, but clearly, as if the whole ward had become slightly more willing to conduct a warning.

A sluice-tender would notice that the webs of the valve-spiders had thickened in a pattern no one had seen before. Not chaotic. Not threatening. More like a map drawn by very small, very serious hands. He would follow the pattern and find a joint weeping iron, not yet broken, but grieving in its own slow way. He would mend it with oil, cloth, and patience, and afterward he would record the new web-pattern in the ward’s memory, because the Wardens keep lists of everything worth knowing.

A child would dream of ripples.

Not grand dreams. Not dreams of voices from the sky or thrones above the cistern tower. Just ripples: rings spreading across black water, each ring touching a stone, a root, a lamp-post, a sleeping eel. In the dream, the child would learn that if you touch the water gently, the whole ward answers gently. If you strike it, the answer is confusion. The child would wake remembering a drip-code no one had taught: three drops, a pause, one drop. *Listen here, but do not be afraid.*

The power would not invent fear to make itself useful. It would deepen warning before danger became catastrophe.

That would be its first work.

---

Its second gift would be repair.

Not repair as domination. Not repair as replacing the old with something gleaming and foreign. The power would not make the ward new. It would help the ward continue.

It would enter the basin in the smallest possible way: as a single bead of condensation on a cooling pipe, perhaps, or as a faint warmth in a brick that had grown too cold. From there it would move through the ward’s capillaries, learning each crack, each tired seam, each place where the long agreement had grown thin.

Where a hairline fracture waited inside a plinth, the power would not seal it with force. It would guide the existing lives toward it. Iron-loving bacteria would gather there a little sooner. Algae would lay down their lacquer in a finer film. Mineral-rich water would leave behind a skin of scale so gradual that no one could say when the leak had stopped becoming a leak. A root would find the weak place and, instead of widening it, brace it, drinking just enough to relieve pressure while holding the old brick together like a living stitch.

Where timber had begun to lose its memory of flexibility, the power would not make it immortal. It would remind it of its own nature: how to bend a little, how to accept wetness without surrendering strength. Where iron was tired, it would encourage the ward’s own rust-eaters to work more evenly, so that corrosion became care rather than loss. Where a tile had loosened, it would wait until a Warden’s hand passed close enough to press it back into place, and then it would make the mortar remember its grip.

It would also give redundancy.

This is a gift often overlooked by those who imagine benevolence only as rescue. A wise power knows that survival often depends not on preventing every failure, but on ensuring that failure need not become ruin. So it would help hidden channels remain passable. It would make certain forgotten sluices easier to open after long disuse. It would let a cistern hold a little more reserve than anyone had measured. It would guide the Wardens, in their quiet way, to store oil, cloth, dried fungal strips, spare ceramic, seed, rope, and patience.

Not because catastrophe is certain.

But because water is honest, and honest things must prepare.

Tools would receive small mercies.

A valve key would not slip in a wet hand at the worst moment. A listening rod would carry sound cleanly through condensation. A lamp would burn a little more steadily when the air grew thick. A folded dry cloth, kept carefully in a belt pouch, would stay dry longer than it had any right to. Such things would not be called miracles. The Wardens would simply say, when speaking of them afterward, that the work had gone well that night.

And that, perhaps, is how the power would prefer it.

---

Its third gift would be protection, but not isolation.

The Low Ward is small. It lies below the surrounding marsh. It is vulnerable not only to drought and neglect, but to the attention of those who do not understand agreement. There are always such people: surveyors who see only wasted ground, merchants who see only engines to be stripped, reformers who see only wetness to be corrected, conquerors who see only a place too strange to tolerate.

A wise power would not make the ward invulnerable. That would turn it into a prison. But it would make the ward difficult for cruelty to reach.

The levees around the basin would begin to seem less obvious. Not magically shifting in broad daylight, but wearing the eye. Paths would appear to lead elsewhere. Maps would become unreliable in small ways: a channel drawn too far east, a pump-house omitted, a causeway remembered as impassable. Outsiders with hard intentions would find the air too heavy, the ground too treacherous, the silence too attentive. They would feel watched, not by hostile eyes, but by something patient and unimpressed. Many would turn back without knowing why.

Those who came in greed might find their tools rusting faster than they should, their boots filling with water, their ears ringing with the sound of dripping until they could no longer think clearly. Not as punishment, exactly. More as a question posed by the marsh itself: *What do you intend to dry here?*

But those who came lost, or humbled, or truly in need might find a way.

A lamp might glow where no lamp had been lit. A narrow boat might drift within reach. A Warden might appear on a walkway, not welcoming in the dryland sense, but offering the basin’s plain hospitality: a cup of reed-root tea, a place to sit above the condensation line, a warm stone near an engine flue, and silence if silence was what the stranger needed. The Wardens would still choose whom to admit. The power would not override their judgment. It would only make it easier for them to recognize the difference between danger and distress.

In this way, the Low Ward would remain hidden enough to survive, yet open enough to remain kind.

---

Its fourth gift would be comfort.

This is important. Even those who have agreed to be wet grow tired. Even those who love the damp may sometimes long for a place where nothing beads, where no surface slickens at a breath, where cloth stays warm and dry against the skin. The Wardens would not admit this often
