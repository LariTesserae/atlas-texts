If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention to that high, wet basin-city, it would not arrive as thunder.

Thunder would be wrong there. It would startle the reeds, shake loose stones, make the pools tremble, and force the people to look up in fear. A wise power, seeing how carefully the basin had been made to hold quietness, would not begin by breaking that quietness. It would not send a pillar of light, or a voice from the rim, or a sign so overwhelming that every hand stopped working. It would not carve its name into the young cliffs, because carving that was not needed would only make water run differently and set some new trouble moving through the channels.

The first thing the power would do is listen.

It would listen to the small sounds: the bead falling from a ledge, the whisper of sedges, the soft creak of a sluice board under pressure, the almost inaudible passage of water through dark stone. It would listen until it understood that the basin was not empty, not dead, not waiting to be filled, but already complete in its own manner. It would hear that the silence was not absence. It would hear that the emptiness was not poverty. It would hear that the people did not need to be made louder, richer, more numerous, more splendid, or more certain. They needed to be allowed to continue.

Only after that listening would the power consider action.

And what it would consider first would be restraint.

---

A lesser kindness might try to give the basin more.

It might think: *They are few. Let me increase them.*  
It might think: *They work by hand. Let me give them engines.*  
It might think: *The wetness is constant. Let me drain part of the marsh and make dry roads.*  
It might think: *They have no beasts of burden. Let me give them strong animals to pull stone.*  
It might think: *They are hidden and vulnerable. Let me make them famous, protected by walls, feared by their enemies.*  
It might think: *They are humble. Let me give them temples.*  
It might think: *They do not know my name. Let me reveal myself.*

But wisdom would see that each of these gifts, however dazzling, would become a wound.

More people would fill the gaps that the city needed to breathe. Engines would bring noise, smoke, grease, broken rhythms, and the arrogance of force. Drained ground would shift the marsh, lower the water table, crack the clay channels, and teach the people to treat wetness as an enemy. Animals would bring dung, blood, pasture, fodder, fences, fear, and the old loud hierarchy of predator and prey. Fame would bring strangers with hungry eyes. Walls made for fear would block flow. Temples would try to enclose what should remain open. Revelation would make the people dependent on a presence they should not need to see in order to live well.

So the wise power would refuse nearly everything that ordinary benevolence might imagine as generosity.

It would not give more.  
It would not make them mighty.  
It would not make them dry.  
It would not make them safe by making them hard.  
It would not make them certain by making them afraid.

Instead, it would ask a harder question:

*How can I help this place remain itself?*

That would be the true beginning.

---

The power would see that the basin already had a law, though not written in any commanding sense. The law was simple and severe and gentle:

Water has right of way.

Everything else followed from that. Walls breathed. Floors drained. Roofs shed. Paths curved. Houses accepted damp rather than pretending it could be abolished. Children learned not to splash. Voices stayed low. Work was measured in small corrections. Authority belonged to those who noticed. The dead were returned to the water and mud without spectacle. The city was maintained not by domination but by attention.

A wise power would recognize that this law was not a limitation to be overcome. It was the basin’s holiness.

So if the power did anything, it would act in service to that law.

It would become, in the most hidden way, another form of right-of-way.

It would not stand above the water. It would move with it.

---

What would such action look like?

It would look like almost nothing.

A spring that might have turned bitter would remain sweet a little longer.

A hairline crack in a new scarp would catch the eye of the right person at the right hour, so that the loose stone could be eased away before it fell into a channel and sent a damaging surge through the lower terraces.

A rope, worn in a hidden place, would fray on the outside where it could be seen. Not because the rope was miraculous, but because the power had guided attention toward it. A barge-puller would notice, frown, run a thumb along the fibers, and replace the cord before failure. No one would thank the power. They would thank the barge-puller, or simply say that the day had been kind.

A child learning to walk on wet stone would slip, but the fall would be short. The child would land in shallow water among soft reeds, frightened but unharmed, and would learn something important about balance without learning despair.

A cistern-keeper, tired from too little sleep, would dream of a blocked runnel and wake with the odd certainty that the third gate needed opening. They would go out into the pale morning, listen, find the pitch wrong by less than a breath, and adjust the sluice by the width of a finger. The water would settle. The keeper would return indoors, half convinced they had imagined it.

An outsider approaching with weapons would become uneasy without knowing why. The path would seem longer than expected. The air would feel too close. The straps of their gear would chafe. The wet smell of the basin would remind them, strangely, of grief. They might reach the rim, look down into the quiet city, and feel not desire but hesitation. Then some other thought would arise in them — a duty elsewhere, a memory of home, a sudden wish not to disturb what looked so still — and they would turn away.

If they came thirsty, the power would not refuse them water. It might lead them to a clear edge pool where a basin-dweller could offer a clay cup without inviting them farther in. The stranger would drink. The water would taste clean beyond explanation. They would leave with that taste in their mouth, perhaps carrying no memory they could quite defend.

The power would not punish outsiders harshly. It would not need to. It would only make the basin difficult to enter with violence. Heavy feet would feel heavier. Loud voices would sound foolish. Hide and leather would seem oppressive in the damp air, as if carrying dead noise into a living room. Animals brought to the rim would balk, refuse the descent, or become restless in ways their handlers could not manage. The basin would not be defended by fear. It would be defended by unsuitability.

Those who came gently might be allowed to see enough to be changed by it. Those who came grasping would find nothing to grasp.

---

The power would also understand that the people’s dignity depended on their labor.

If it made everything effortless, it would destroy them.

If the reeds never needed cutting, the cisterns never needed cleaning, the weirs never needed adjustment, the ropes never needed replacing, then the people would no longer be the basin’s living attention. They would become ornaments in a place that no longer asked anything of them. Their hands would lose purpose. Their knowledge would thin. Their children would grow bored or restless. Their quiet joy, which came from care, would wither.

So the wise power would not remove work. It would make work meaningful, possible, and not hopeless.

It would leave enough difficulty for skill.  
It would leave enough risk for attentiveness.  
It would leave enough uncertainty for humility.  
It would leave enough silence for listening.

But it would try to prevent meaningless catastrophe.

It would not spare them from sorrow, because sorrow belonged to love. It would spare them, where it could, from pointless ruin.

A terrace might still fail. A storm might still come. A mistake might still spread from one channel to another. The people would still argue, misjudge, grow tired, forget, grieve. The power would not turn them into perfect beings. It would not make them less human. It would only keep their humanity from being crushed under disasters that served no lesson and preserved no balance.

In this way, its benevolence would be severe enough to be wise.

---

If it gave gifts, they would be small and unnoticeable.

Plant fibers would hold their strength a little longer in the wet.  
Clay would fire evenly more often than chance alone allowed.  
Reed roofs would shed condensation before rot could settle deep.  
Wooden sluice boards would swell enough to seal but not enough to split.  
Copper pins would resist green decay in the places where failure would matter most.  
The edible algae would remain edible. The poisonous blooms would appear, if at all, where they would be noticed before anyone drank.  
The warm springs would stay warm without turning scalding.  
The mineral vapors would vent gently into the air instead of gathering in hollows where sleepers might not wake.

None of this would seem miraculous. It would seem like good fortune. It would seem like carefulness. It would seem like the basin itself being kind.

And that, perhaps, would be exactly what the power intended.

---

There would be one being in particular whom the power noticed.

Not because this being was greater than the others, but because at that time they stood at a hinge of need.

Let us say they were a sluice-tender, neither young nor old, known to nearby households by a name that meant something like moss growing near moving water. They had a scar on one hand from a rope burn long healed. Their shoulders were strong in the patient way of people who lowered things rather than lifted them. They kept their hair tied close under a woven cap. Their cloak was darkened at the hem from standing in damp. They walked with that basin-step: weight low, feet placed, no wasted motion.

This being had recently lost someone.

Not violently. Not suddenly. One of their household had grown weak over a long time, had been tended quietly, had died, and had been wrapped in mats and lowered into a dark pool where the water received the dead without hurry. The sluice-tender had helped with the lowering. They had not spoken much afterward. They had returned to work because work was how the basin continued, but inside them a stillness had gathered that was not only peace. It was grief.

The power would see that grief.

It would not take it away. To take it away would be to cheapen the love that made it.

But it would do something.

One morning, before the light had fully silvered the marsh, the sluice-tender went to check a gate whose sound had troubled them in the night. The air was warm and close. Water shone in thin sheets along the stone. The reeds stood motionless. The tender placed a hand on the wet wall and listened.

The power, if it acted there, would not speak.

It would only give the hand a slight increase in sensitivity, or the mind a slight increase in calm. The tender would feel, under their palm, a change in the wall’s dampness: a coldness where there should have been warmth, a fullness where there should have been ease. They would kneel. They would find a small blockage behind a slab, hidden by moss. They would work carefully, using a wooden tool, until the water found its proper path again.

When it did, they would sit back on their heels.

The sound of the channel would change. It would become cleaner. Not louder, not triumphant — just right.

For a moment, the sluice-tender would feel a strange comfort, as if someone had stood very near them without touching. They might look up. They would see nothing but the basin: wet stone, pale mist, green reeds, the far rim soft in ordinary light. They might think, without knowing why, that the one they had lost was somehow present in the repair. Not as a ghost. Not as a voice. But as attention continuing.

They would not fall to their knees in worship. They would probably only bow their head for a breath, then rise and go to fetch a basket for the silt they had loosened.

The power would accept that as enough.

Perhaps it would have wanted nothing else.

---

If the being ever became aware of the power at all, they would not call it a god in the way drier lands might use that word.

They might not give it a name.

They might only develop a phrase, used softly while working: *the water is easy today.* Or: *the stone listened.* Or: *the gate opened kindly.*

The wise power would prefer that.

A name too large would invite fear, hierarchy, and argument. A temple would require dry ground and sealed walls, both contrary to the basin’s law. Priests would arise, and some would claim to know the will of the power better than cistern-keepers did. That would be dangerous, because in the basin, authority had to remain with those who observed, repaired, and waited.

So the power would remain mostly unnamed.

If it planted anything in the minds of the people, it would not be doctrine. It would be a few quiet intuitions, passed from elder to child like practical advice:

*Do not fill every empty place.*  
*Do not stop water out of anger.*  
*If you find a path blocked, look for what is trying to pass.*  
*Clean water is given, not owned.*  
*The dead are not lost if the work continues.*  
*Do not mistake silence for absence.*  
*When you do not know what to do, listen first.*

These would feel like old sayings. They would feel as though the basin had always taught them.

That would be better than commandments.

---

The power would also care for the basin’s future without forcing it into permanence.

The land was young. Young land changes. Ridges crack. Gullies deepen. Springs shift. New seeps appear. Some terraces might eventually become too wet to inhabit. Some channels might silt beyond repair. The basin would not remain exactly as it was forever, and a wise power would know that attempting to freeze it would be another kind of violence.

So it would not promise that nothing would change.

It would only ensure that change came slowly enough for attention to answer.

It would give warning before collapse. It would let the people discover problems while there was still room to redirect them. It would guide their dreams toward memory: where a channel had once run, how an older wall had been built, what the water did after a certain spring opened. It would help them keep records not only in knots and carved clay but in the habits of their bodies.

When a district had to be abandoned because the water had found a new preference, the power would help them abandon it well. Not in panic. Not in shame. They would move stones carefully, cover cisterns, open new overflow paths, and let the marsh take back what had been borrowed. The people would understand that leaving a terrace empty was not failure if the water had right of way.

The power would bless their retreats as much as their settlements.

This would be one of its hardest lessons, and one of its most merciful.

---

What would it do for the dead?

This question would matter deeply to a wise power, because the basin’s relationship with death was already so unlike the violent deathways of other lands.

There were no scavengers. No worms. No insects. No bones picked clean. The dead were folded into the wet ground slowly, becoming peat, mineral film, dark softness. The living did not fear this as much as outsiders might, but grief remained.

The power would not raise the dead. That would break the basin’s deepest rule. It would make death a blockage instead of a passage. It would fill every pool with longing and disturb the water with unquiet memory.

Instead, it would make the passage gentle.

It would keep the chosen pools from becoming foul. It would let mineral water preserve the outline of the wrapped dead without grotesqueness. It would prevent the living from being haunted by images of suffering. It would sometimes send dreams in which the dead appeared not speaking, not asking, but simply standing beside a familiar channel, calm, as though they had gone to tend a weir in another district. The dreamer would wake with sorrow, but not with horror.

When a body was lowered, the power might make the water accept it with unusually little disturbance. The ripples would settle quickly. The surface would become smooth. The people standing around would feel, for a moment, that the basin had not taken but received.

They would still mourn. They would still miss the dead. But they would be able to continue.

And because they remembered the dead by maintaining the living city, the power would make sure that acts of maintenance could carry emotional weight. A repaired gate would feel like more than repair. A cleaned cistern would feel like remembrance. A smoothed stair channel would feel like a name spoken without sound.

In this way, the dead would remain in the basin without needing to return.

They would become part of its steadiness.

---

Would the power ever speak directly?

If it did, it would be rare, and only when silence would cause greater harm than speech.

Even then, it would not speak with a voice that shook the cliffs. It might speak as a thought that arrived in several people at once during a difficult decision: *Open the lower gate.* Or: *Wait one more day.* Or: *Do not trust the dry-looking path.*

It might speak in a dream: a hand opening slowly at the wrist, the sign for ease a gate. A fist laid gently on a wall, the sign for stop and listen. A ripple crossing a still pool without wind. A child’s foot finding the loose stone before disaster.

If someone asked directly, “Are you here?” the power might answer with the only sign appropriate to the basin: a single bead of water falling from stone and striking a pool in such a way that the rings spread outward evenly, unhurried, until they touched the edges and disappeared.

Not proof. Not command.

Only presence, gently acknowledged.

The asker would likely bow their head and return to work.

That would be enough.

---

The power would also have to decide what to do about evil.

Even in such a quiet place, people could still harm one another. They could foul water out of spite. They could hide a crack to avoid blame. They could hoard clean supply during a shortage. They could strike a child in anger. They could betray a trust. They could make noise for cruelty’s sake. The basin did not eliminate these possibilities. It only made them quieter.

A wise power would not pretend evil did not exist. It would not allow the basin to become fragile by pretending its people were incapable of harm.

But it would not punish with spectacle.

It would let consequences arise through the basin’s own logic.

A fouled channel would reveal itself in smell, stain, and sickness. A hidden crack would widen until someone noticed. A hoarded cistern would create imbalance downstream. The people would see. They would measure. They would remember. They would repair.

The power might help them repair without becoming cruel in their justice. It would strengthen their preference for restoration over vengeance. If someone had done harm, it would guide the community toward tasks that rebuilt what had been damaged rather than merely inflicting pain. It would help them understand that exile was terrible because it removed a person from the only kind of life that made sense there, and therefore it should be used rarely.

It would also soften the hearts of those who judged, not to make them weak, but to keep them from mistaking anger for clarity.

If a person was truly dangerous, the power might make the basin itself difficult for them: water would feel unsettled under their feet, stone would seem to reject their touch, silence would become unbearable instead of peaceful. They would not be cursed. They would simply find that a life built on blocking flow did not fit there. Some would leave on their own. Some would ask for long, hard work of repair. Some would remain troubled, and the people would watch them closely, but without panic.

The power would not eliminate human darkness. It would prevent darkness from becoming flood.

---

Would the power reveal itself to the being it especially noticed?

Perhaps only once.

Not in person. Not in glory. Maybe only in a moment when the sluice-tender was exhausted and alone at dusk, after too many days of difficult maintenance. They would be sitting beside a small pool, hands resting on their knees, shoulders heavy with ordinary fatigue. The basin would be dimming into silver-grey. The water would be still. The air would be warm.

The power would not appear.

It would only let the being feel, for a few breaths, that their work was seen.

Not praised. Not rewarded. Seen.

The feeling would be like someone laying a warm hand near their shoulder without touching them. It would not remove tiredness. It would make the tiredness meaningful. The sluice-tender might breathe deeply. Their eyes might fill without shame. They might whisper, “I am still tending it.”

And the power, if it answered, would answer through the water: a small bead falling, a ring spreading, a channel sounding clear.

They would understand.

They would not need more.

---

What would the wise power never do?

It would never demand worship.

It would never ask for blood, because blood would be alien there. It would not ask for animal sacrifice, because there were no animals, and even the idea would feel like a wound. It would not ask for gold, because the basin had little metal and less greed. It would not ask for monuments that blocked water. It would not ask for holy days that forced the cisterns to be neglected. It would not ask for children to be taken, or for voices to be raised in fear, or for outsiders to be attacked, or for the marsh to be drained in its name.

If the people built anything for it, the only fitting form would be an opening.

A low platform where water could gather and then pass on.  
A small basin within a basin.  
A place where hands could be washed and the washed water returned clean.  
A stone with a channel cut through it, so that reverence itself learned to drain.

If prayers arose, they would be short.

*Let the water pass.*  
*Let the work hold.*  
*Let no one be thirsty.*  
*Let the dead rest softly.*  
*Let us not fill what should remain open.*

The power would accept those prayers not as praise but as alignment. It would answer them when answering helped. It would not answer them as spectacle. It would answer them as continuation.

---

In the end, the power’s greatest act would be hiddenness.

Not because it was ashamed, and not because it wished to test the people, but because the basin could not hold a visible master without changing into something else. The moment the power became obvious, the people would begin to live for the power rather than with the water. They would begin to ask what it wanted instead of listening to what the basin needed. They would build around its presence rather than around flow. They would become obedient instead of attentive. Obedience can be loud and brittle. Attention is quiet and durable.

The wise power would know this.

So it would give its presence as absence. Its voice as intuition. Its blessing as resilience. Its love as restraint.

It would do what the best keepers do: touch least, notice most, and leave the place more itself than before.

---

And if one asks whether such a power would do anything at all, the answer is yes.

It would do almost everything that matters.

It would keep the springs drinkable.  
It would warn before collapse.  
It would guide hands to fraying ropes.  
It would calm the dreams of grieving keepers.  
It would turn violent strangers away without bloodshed.  
It would protect the children from meaningless death.  
It would help the dead be received gently.  
It would preserve the emptiness that made the city possible.  
It would keep the water from becoming violent.  
It would keep the people from becoming frantic.  
It would keep the basin from becoming an empire, a mine, a fortress, or a ruin.

It would not make the basin perfect. It would make the basin enduring.

And perhaps that is what wisdom always does when it truly loves a place: not seize it, not improve it into brightness, not fill it with evidence of itself, but create the conditions under which its nature can continue.

The basin would go on wet.  
The stone would go on weeping.  
The reeds would go on bending.  
The people would go on walking softly, speaking in signs, measuring flow, remembering the dead by repairing the living.

If they ever felt watched, they would not be afraid.

They would only pause, listen to the nearest channel, and say in their quiet way:

*The water is easy today.*

Then they would return to work.
