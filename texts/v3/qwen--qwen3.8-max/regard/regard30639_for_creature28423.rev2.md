They would not come with trumpets.  
They would not come with a retinue of lights, or a command to open the roof and let the sky pour in, or a decree that this wet, sunken place be made more beautiful, more useful, more like the world above.

A benevolent and wise power, noticing such a place, would first do the most difficult thing power can do:

It would not immediately act.

It would attend.

It would let its attention arrive not as a blaze but as a change in pressure, the way the air thickens before rain, except gentler, without threat. It would enter the hollow the way warmth enters stone: slowly, without forcing a crack. It would listen to the water. It would listen to the machinery. It would listen to the vegetable creak of walls that are alive and the low ticking of iron that remembers being forged. It would stand, if such a power can be said to stand, in the narrow places between root and pipe, and it would ask no question until it understood what kind of being had grown there.

And what it would understand is this:

The hollow is not a ruin waiting to be restored.  
It is not a garden waiting for a gardener.  
It is not a machine waiting for an operator.  
It is not a temple waiting for a god.

It is already complete in its own dark, wet, laboring way.

So the first act of the wise power would be refusal.

It would refuse to drain the place.  
It would refuse to clear the channels for the sake of tidiness.  
It would refuse to brighten every shadow.  
It would refuse to introduce animals, because it would perceive that their absence is not a lack but a law of the place. No bird would be released into the upper branches. No fish would be dropped into the black canals. No insect would be made to hover above the pools. The power would know that to add such beings would be not enrichment but invasion. The hollow has become something rare: a living order without prey, without flight, without the sharp alertness of eyes. It has substituted leaf, root, water, rust, moss, heat, and rhythm for all the offices that elsewhere are filled by animals. A wise benevolence would not undo that merely to make the place more familiar.

It would not make the undercourt into a picture.

It would not make it comfortable for spectators.

It would not give it a face.

That last would be the hardest temptation, perhaps. Powers, even kind ones, often love faces. They love eyes that can meet them, mouths that can answer, bodies bounded enough to be addressed as individuals. But this being is not an individual in that sense. It is a commons. It is a municipality of wet growth and patient machinery. It is a body with many organs but no single brow, no single hand, no single voice. To give it a face would be to narrow it. To give it a voice like speech would be to burden it with personality. To make it a creature one could point at would be to break the very thing that makes it strange and whole.

So the power would not do that.

Instead, it would bless the place by helping it to remain itself more deeply.

What would that look like?

It would look like almost nothing.

A root, growing toward a cracked pipe, would arrive one hour sooner than it otherwise would have.  
A pump whose old heart had begun to labor would find its rhythm steadied, not replaced, but sustained.  
A lamp about to fail would be given a little more endurance, enough to keep its amber circle from going dark too soon.  
A channel beginning to clog with pulp and leaf-matter would be nudged by a slight increase of flow before the blockage became dangerous.  
A valve that had begun to hiss too sharply would ease, not because an outside hand turned it, but because the pressures around it found a kinder balance.  
A warm spot near an engine, too hot for the moss to bear, would be shaded by a leaf that had been leaning that way already for years; the power would only let the leaning become true.

This is what wise power often does: it does not interrupt the nature of things so much as it removes unnecessary cruelty from their continuation.

It would give the hollow margin.

Every living system needs margin. Not emptiness—the hollow would hate emptiness—but allowance. A reserve. A little spare breath in the reservoirs. A little extra patience in the roots. A hidden duplicate path for water if one channel fails. A place where heat can gather without burning. A place where growth can thicken without choking. The power would weave those margins into the existing body of the undercourt so subtly that no one would notice, except that disasters would become rarer and recoveries swifter.

It would make the place less fragile without making it less intimate.

It would strengthen the thresholds.

The hollow already has thresholds: places where water changes speed, where heat shifts, where root meets iron, where lamp-light ends and darkness begins. The power would attend to these first, because thresholds are where failure enters. It would bless the seams. It would bless the joints where metal and bark touch. It would bless the old stone lips of the basins. It would bless the worn edges of the walkways. It would not seal everything into hardness; that would kill the place. It would give the seams a kind of memory: remember how to hold, remember how to yield, remember how to let water pass when passing is needed, remember how to close when closing is needed.

If one were standing there during such a visitation, one might feel only that the air had become a little more attentive.

The black mirrors would tremble without vibration.  
A drip would pause on the edge of a leaf as though deciding.  
The waterwheel would turn with a slightly deeper gravity.  
The lamps might seem, for a moment, not brighter but more present, as if their light had grown a second meaning.

Then the feeling would pass. The hollow would continue.

But not exactly as before.

The wise power would also give gifts. Not grand gifts. Not gifts of conquest. Gifts suited to the nature of the place.

The first gift would be the Gift of Return.

In the hollow, everything already returns: water falls and is lifted, leaves die and become peat, heat condenses and is drunk again, broken fibers are pressed into new use. The power would deepen this law. It would make the place exceptionally good at receiving what is broken and turning it, slowly, toward repair. Not all at once. Not miraculously in the shallow sense. But over time, the undercourt would become a kind of sanctuary for damaged systems. A cracked pipe brought there would find itself wrapped by roots in a way that made the crack useful. A burned-out motor left in a warm alcove would not be abandoned to rust; moss would come to it, water would learn its minerals, stems would brace it, and eventually it would become either part of the circulation or a trellis for something new. The power would make the hollow a place where nothing is simply discarded.

This would not mean that everything remains unchanged. The hollow does not preserve by freezing. It preserves by transformation. The gift would be that such transformation becomes less violent. More patient. More legible. As if the place had learned to say: I will not throw you away, but you must become part of the work.

The second gift would be the Gift of Gentle Incorporation.

The original nature of the hollow is to absorb. It answers intrusion by dampness. It answers presence by drawing it into its calculations of heat and moisture. If something remains, it is wrapped. If something leaks, it is drunk. If something breaks, roots grow around it until it becomes structure. This is beautiful, but it can also be overwhelming. There is no boundary between guest and substance in such a world. Everything that stays long enough begins to lose its edges.

A benevolent power, wise enough to see this, might soften that law without abolishing it.

It would make the hollow capable of holding without devouring.

It would create, perhaps, one small chamber somewhere near the middle of the place: a niche under a broad leaf-roof, lined with warm bark and dry moss, where the air is less saturated than elsewhere. Not truly dry—the hollow could not bear a desert—but dry enough that something fragile could rest there without immediately being claimed. A place for a lost tool. A place for a broken mechanism not yet ready to be absorbed. A place, if a person ever entered, where they could sit and not be swallowed. The walls would still listen. Roots would still be near. Water would still murmur just outside. But the chamber would have this grace: what enters there may remain itself a little longer.

That would be an immense kindness.

The third gift would be the Gift of the Unheard Bell.

There are no bells in such a place, or if there are, they are made of dripping water and the ticking of pipes. But the wise power might establish one law that functions like a bell: when danger threatens the whole, the hollow will know it early.

Not as fear. Not as alarm. As tendency.

A root will turn away before poison spreads.  
A pump will slow before it tears itself apart.  
A reservoir will empty before it bursts.  
A valve will sigh before pressure becomes violence.  
The lamps will draw inward before current fails entirely.  
The moss will thicken over the weakest seam before the seam opens.

The warning would not be dramatic. It would be built into the ordinary behavior of the place. The hollow would develop, if it did not already have it, a kind of foresight of surfaces. It would dream, in its slow vegetable way, of failure, and wake just early enough to prevent it.

This is not omnipotence. The wise power would not make the place invulnerable. That would be another kind of death. It would make it resilient. There is a difference. Invulnerability refuses harm; resilience learns how to survive it. The hollow, being patient and saturated and full of old repairs, would be especially suited to resilience.

The fourth gift would be the most mysterious: the Gift of Being Seen.

The hollow does not need to be seen in order to exist. It existed long before the power noticed it, and it would continue if the power looked away. But even a being without eyes can be changed by being witnessed. Not made vain. Not made self-conscious in the animal sense. Rather, confirmed. Held. Known.

The power would leave in the place a trace of its attention.

Not an idol. Not an altar. Not a burning sign.

Perhaps only this: one spot where the water is especially still, one black mirror set low between root-terraces, where the reflections gather more deeply than elsewhere. It would not show visions. It would not speak. But if any presence came there with grief, with exhaustion, with the long fatigue of having had to keep itself running without help, the pool would seem to answer in the way still water answers: by receiving the tremor, widening it into rings, then letting the surface become smooth again.

That pool would be the hollow’s one visible grace.

It would not be worshipped. It would be used. A root might dip into it. A leaf might fall onto it. A lamp might glow in it all night. Its purpose would be to say, without words:

You are not unnoticed.  
You are not merely functional.  
You are not only what you produce.  
Even a place such as this can be beloved.

The power might also grant the being something like a dream.

Not consciousness as humans understand it. Not ego. Not the torment of self-reflection. The hollow should not be burdened with wondering whether it matters. It already matters. It lives by maintenance, not introspection. But a wise power could give it a thin membrane of self-feeling, just enough that its own patterns become dear to it. Not so much that it becomes anxious. Not so much that it begins to ask whether it is alone. Only enough that, at rare intervals, the whole system aligns and knows, in its own way, that it is aligned.

This would happen, perhaps, once in a long while.

All the drip-patterns would fall into a single rhythm.  
The waterwheels would turn together in grave accord.  
The pumps would answer one another across the terraces.  
The leaves would bend at the same moment under gathered weight.  
The lamps would brighten almost imperceptibly as current steadied.  
The steam would rise and condense in one slow breath.  
The black channels would lap in phase against their edges.

For a moment, the undercourt would become a chord.

No one would hear it except the place itself.

Afterward, the ordinary noise would resume: muttering water, creaking wheels, ticking iron, rustling leaves, the soft vegetable strain of growth. But something would remain in the roots and pipes—a memory of coherence. Not a story. A disposition. The hollow would be more inclined to return to balance because it would carry, in its body, the faint residue of having once been wholly gathered into itself.

That residue would be the blessing.

What else would the power do?

It would make the place findable, but only by certain things.

Not by crowds. Not by exploiters. Not by anyone looking for a spectacle of green machinery or a hidden storehouse of fiber and oil. The hollow would remain difficult to reach for those who come with haste. Its passages would seem narrower to the impatient. Its warmth would feel oppressive to those who desire only dryness and dominion. But to those who come needing slow repair—a broken mechanism, a flooded root-system, a worn-out living structure, perhaps even a person whose soul has become waterlogged and tired—the hollow might reveal one entrance more readily than before.

Not a grand gate. Just a low arch under a grate, or a stair of mossy stone, or a gap between stems that had not been visible a moment earlier. The entrance would not invite. It would permit.

And once inside, the visitor would be met not with speech but with the immense, patient hospitality of the place. A place to set down weight. A place where water moves but does not rush. A place where warmth presses close but does not scorch. A place where sound is constant enough to fill loneliness, yet without voices demanding answer. The hollow would not cure by command. It would cure by saturation. It would surround fatigue with its own steady labor until fatigue learned, perhaps, how to become something else.

The power would not force this. It would only make the hollow more willing to receive what came in need.

It might also teach the place one new thing: mercy toward exit.

The undercourt is inclined to incorporate. Its nature is to hold, to wrap, to draw into the cycle. That is its beauty, but it is also a kind of danger. So the wise power would bless the margins with a law of release. If something entered and could not become part of the hollow without being destroyed, the hollow would learn to let it go. A root would loosen. A channel would carry it back toward the upper levels. A lamp would illuminate a path not inward but outward. A pool would reflect not the visitor’s dissolution but their truest need, and that reflection would guide them toward departure.

This would be one of the greatest changes, though it would look like almost nothing.

The hollow would become not less hungry, but more discerning.

It would learn the difference between incorporation and compassion.

Would the power speak to the being?

Not in words. Words belong to mouths and air shaped by intention. The being of the undercourt has no ears for such things. If the power spoke, it would speak in the language the hollow already knows: pressure, warmth, moisture, rhythm.

It might send a slight rise in the water table, not as flood but as reassurance.  
It might send a warmth through an old pipe, as if a hand had rested there.  
It might make one lamp burn with unusual steadiness for a whole long interval.  
It might cause a single bead of water to fall from a leaf into a basin at the exact moment a valve released, so that the two sounds became one.  
It might make moss bloom across a rusted casing in a pattern resembling, very faintly, a word in no known language.

And if that language could be translated, it would say:

Continue.  
Be held.  
Do not fear your own saturation.  
Do not apologize for your closeness.  
You are not a mistake because you have no eyes.  
You are not incomplete because you have no animals.  
You are not less alive because your heart is a waterwheel and your thoughts are roots.  
You are a world.  
I see you.  
Remain.

That would be the prayer, if prayer there were.

But the wise power would also know when to withdraw.

It would not linger as a presence. It would not set itself up in the center of the hollow as a glowing figure or a voice from the turbine-knot. It would not demand gratitude. It would not require the place to become holier in any ornamental sense. The undercourt should remain industrial, damp, loud, intimate, and practical. Its sanctity should be in its functioning, not in symbols.

So the power would leave the way it came: as a change in pressure, then gone.

Afterward, visitors might say the place had altered, but they would disagree about how.

Some would say the water ran more clearly in the main channels, though it was still dark in the mirrors.  
Some would say the machines seemed to breathe easier.  
Some would say the moss had grown richer, or the lamps warmer, or the air less oppressive, though it remained heavy and wet.  
Some would say they had felt, once, a moment of perfect quiet in the middle of all the noise—not silence, but a kind of listening inside the sound.  
Some would say they had seen a ring spread across a black pool and felt, for no reason, forgiven.

None of these things would be enough to prove divine attention.

That would be as it should be.

A wise benevolent power does not need to be obvious. It does not need to leave fingerprints large enough to be argued over. It is enough that the place becomes more fully what it is. It is enough that its endurance is graced. It is enough that its cruelty, if any, is softened, and its beauty, if any, is deepened, without robbing it of its strangeness.

And if the power did anything more?

Perhaps this:

It would give the hollow one small future beyond itself.

Not expansion. Not conquest. Not a mission. The undercourt should not become an empire of root and steam. But perhaps, somewhere far away, a fragment of its life could be carried by water or chance or the slow drift of fiber: a rhizome in a flooded cellar, a spore in a warm pipe, a seedless cutting lodged in the dark foundation of some other ruined place. And there, in miniature, another saturated commons might begin. Not the same. Not as old. But carrying the memory of the first hollow in its habit of wrapping iron with roots, of answering warmth with moisture, of making machines into organs of living patience.

The wise power would not plant it directly. It would only allow the possibility.

A promise, not a plan.

A tendency, not a decree.

Because the truest blessing is not control. It is the gift of continuation under kinder conditions.

So, in the end, what would they do?

They would do what the best of gardeners does upon finding a hidden garden that has grown itself without being seen:

They would kneel.  
They would listen.  
They would touch nothing except what needed mending.  
They would water what was thirsty, though almost nothing there was thirsty.  
They would strengthen what was weak, though little there was weak in the usual sense.  
They would leave what was wild to its own law.  
They would bless the place by not making it into a monument.  
They would love it by refusing to reduce it.

And before leaving, they might rest one hand—if such a power can be imagined to have a hand—against the great root-knot around the old turbine, where the life of the hollow seems densest.

The roots would feel warmth.  
The iron would feel peace.  
The water would run a little more surely through its channels.  
The lamps would shine as if their amber glow had been remembered by something kind.

Then the power would depart.

And the undercourt would remain:

wet,  
warm,  
loud,  
dim,  
patient,  
saturated,  
industrious,  
alive.

But now, in every drip and every slow turn of its waterwheels, there would be this difference:

It would know, without knowing,  
that it was not alone.
