## The Attention

A benevolent and wise power, if it turned toward that high plant tableland, would probably begin by doing nothing that could be mistaken for domination.

It would not tear open the sky.  
It would not silence the insects.  
It would not make the great plant bodies bow, or turn the fibrous plains into polished stone, or set a throne above the heat haze. It would understand, quickly and with something like reverence, that this place already has a kind of sanctity: the sanctity of a world that has learned to be itself.

So the first act of the power would not be intervention.

It would be listening.

Not listening from a distance, but listening in the intimate way that wind listens when it enters a hollow stem and changes pitch. The power would let the tableland fill it: the long bass pressure of root platforms settling, the glassy stridulation in bark seams, the dry percussion of beetles against dead heartwood, the sudden choral eruptions at dusk, the soft private sounds of water gathering in leaf cups at dawn. It would hear the great plant forms not as objects but as slow bodies with tendencies, with injuries, with patience. It would hear the fauna as weather made of appetites. It would hear the people as brief melodies passing through a vast chord.

And it would ask, in whatever manner such powers ask without demanding an answer:

*How can I help without becoming a master?*

Because wisdom, if it is truly wise, knows that benevolence can be cruel. A careless kindness might quiet the chorus and starve the flowers. A pity that removes all thirst might make the water-knowledge vanish. A protection that ends every danger might turn the tableland into a preserved thing, no longer alive in the full sense. The place survives by tension: by scarcity that teaches memory, by noise that forces attention, by decay that creates shelter, by death that becomes ground.

Therefore the power would be careful.

It would not do nothing. But it would do only what could be done lightly.

## What They Would Refuse to Do

They would refuse to make the tableland comfortable in the shallow sense.

They would not fill it with rivers. They would not make water obvious and everywhere, because then the hidden knot-bowls, the pitcher throats, the dawn condensation on leaf hairs, and the deep root reservoirs would cease to be what they are: precious, remembered, sung over, taught from elder to child. If water became cheap, the world’s memory would thin. The people would stop moving with care. The predators would lose their ancient geometry of waiting. The small acoustic ecology of drinking places would collapse.

They would not silence the fauna. The noise is terrible, yes. It can madden an outsider. But it is also the living speech of the place. It carries warnings, mating calls, territorial claims, weather signs, pollination triggers, and the collective heartbeat of a million small hungers. To quiet it would be to make the tableland deaf to itself.

They would not abolish predation. They would not make the grazers safe from all pursuit, or the pollinators safe from ambush, or the people safe from consequence. A world without predation would swell, rot, and lose its shape. Wisdom knows that death is not the opposite of blessing; sometimes it is the hinge by which life continues.

They would not give the people great cities.

Not because the people are unworthy, but because the place cannot carry permanent heavy marks without becoming less itself. A city would invite clearing, fire, waste, war, and the hard separation of inhabitant from substrate. The tableland tolerates brief habitation. It folds abandoned works back into its body. It heals carvings into swollen ridges. It chews rope, nests in shelters, softens platforms, and reclaims paths. The power would see this not as hostility but as immune memory. It would not override it.

They would not reveal themselves too plainly.

A god that appears openly becomes a problem. It becomes a tyrant of belief. It replaces lived attention with obedience. It makes every choice a performance before the divine. The people of the tableland, if they are wise already, do not need a shining overlord. They need enough water, enough memory, enough mercy, enough warning.

So the power would remain mostly hidden. Not absent. Not indifferent. But present in the way good teachers are present: by making the student more capable of living without them.

## The First Gift: A Margin of Mercy

If I follow my heart, I think the power would give the tableland a margin of mercy.

Not the abolition of suffering. Not the end of risk. But a small, persistent surplus where otherwise there might be none.

Where a knot-bowl would have dried completely by midday, it might remain damp enough for one more mouth.  
Where a traveler is lost and has only one chance in ten of finding shade before heat sickness, perhaps one chance becomes three.  
Where a child wanders too close to a predator’s silence, perhaps a beetle strikes a hollow stem at exactly the right moment, making the child turn back.  
Where a fire threatens to leap from a hearth ring, perhaps a damp draft descends from a nearby crown and settles the sparks.  
Where a pollinator cloud arrives too weak to trigger flowering, perhaps one more swarm is drawn by scent over the horizon.  
Where an ancient plant body begins to crack, perhaps fungal threads arrive a little sooner, and rootlets stitch the wound before collapse.

None of this would feel miraculous to those inside it. It would feel like luck. Like skill. Like the world being, just barely, on your side.

That is the kind of help that does not humiliate the helped.

The power would understand that the tableland is not a machine to be optimized. It is a resonant organism. Therefore its benevolence would take the form of tuning, not rebuilding.

It would increase redundancy.

More hidden water pockets than strict necessity demands. More hollow internodes than the current season requires. More seed caches than will be found. More safe ledges on root cliffs. More echo points where calls can be carried. More small refuges where a hunted creature can disappear into a crevice too narrow for pursuit.

Not endless safety. But enough escape routes that the world does not become a trap.

## The Second Gift: The Preservation of Knowledge

The people of the tableland live by knowledge more than by accumulation. Their wealth is not stored in heavy vaults but in remembered routes, songs, rhythms, gestures, and the practical holiness of knowing which pitcher can be tapped and which knot fills after rain.

A wise benevolent power would protect that knowledge.

It would not hand them maps made of light. It would not write commandments on bark. Instead, it would make their existing forms of memory more durable.

Children would remember water songs more easily.  
Elders would dream of old routes during drought.  
Apprentices would notice the exact angle at which a leaf cup must be tilted to pour without spilling.  
A band separated by a storm might find themselves humming the same rhythm and turning toward one another without knowing why.  
A grandmother’s gesture for “drink here only at dawn” would pass through generations with surprising fidelity.

The power might work through dreams, because dreams are light and do not compel. A thirsty sleeper might dream of a particular ribbed trunk, a white beetle, a three-note echo, a knot shaped like a cupped hand. Waking, they might not think, *A god showed me this.* They might think, *I remembered something old.* That would be better.

The power would also guard against the loss of crucial knowledge.

If the last person who knows a certain water route falls ill, someone else might hear the route in a hollow wind-tone and think it merely beautiful. If a song is forgotten, a fragment may return in the percussion of a beetle against dry seed-case. If a tool-making technique disappears, some child may discover it again as play, and the play will be remembered.

Not all knowledge would be preserved. That would be impossible and undesirable. But the knowledge necessary for light living would be difficult to lose entirely.

## The Third Gift: Sound Made Faithful

Because the tableland is loud, the power would pay special attention to sound.

It would not make the noise cease. It would make the noise more meaningful.

A warning call might carry farther through heat shimmer than physics alone should allow.  
A lost person’s cry might enter a hollow stem and emerge somewhere that rescuers can hear.  
A drum code might remain intelligible across a wide bark plain, even when wind tries to fray it.  
A predator’s approach might create a subtle change in the insect chorus that sensitive listeners can interpret.  
A sudden silence might become more legible, not less: a gap shaped like danger.

The power might bless certain acoustic phenomena.

A particular hollow internode could become known as a listening place. If someone taps it with a staff, the returning resonance may reveal whether rain has gathered somewhere far off, whether a branch is about to fall, whether a large animal is waiting inside. This would not be supernatural in the eyes of the people. It would simply be one of those places that is better than others. They would say, “That one tells the truth.”

Some flowers that open to vibration might respond more readily to the correct rhythms. The people would learn those rhythms as harvest songs. They would not need to know that the power has made the threshold slightly more forgiving. They would only know that the old song still works.

Some dangerous creatures might be repelled by certain frequencies. Not all. Not always. But enough that a person carrying a small resonant seed-shell or striking a thorn-comb in the right pattern has a better chance of passing unharmed.

And some sounds would become sacred without being forbidden.

A rhythm used to call for help.  
A pattern struck when someone has died.  
A low chant used at water places to thank the vessel.  
A silence kept before crossing an open plain where predators may listen.

The power would not command these rituals. It would simply make them feel right.

## The Law of Light Marks

The people already understand, in their best moments, that survival on the tableland depends on leaving little.

A benevolent and wise power would strengthen that ethic.

Not by punishing every mistake. Mistakes are how the young learn. But by making the consequences of arrogance clear, and the rewards of lightness real.

If someone builds too heavily, the structure will fail sooner. Resin will soften the lashings. Beetles will enter the supports. Fungal threads will find the pressure points. The platform will sag. The shelter will leak. The world will not hate the builder; it will simply continue according to its nature. But the power might ensure that the failure happens early enough to teach, not late enough to kill.

If someone takes too much water from a pocket, the pocket may not refill when expected. Not because the power cursed it, but because the living system was strained. Yet the people might learn to read that strain as a warning.

If someone cuts deeply into a living surface for no reason, the wound may heal badly, producing bitter resin or attracting borers. If someone cuts carefully, with thanks and purpose, the wound may close cleanly.

If someone leaves fire unattended, the fire will be more likely to die in a strange damp draft, or a sudden gust will blow chaff away from the flame. If someone tends fire properly, it will burn true.

The power would not make morality mechanical. It would not turn the world into a simple ledger. But it would make the tableland subtly responsive to intention, as it is already responsive to touch, vibration, and injury.

In time, the people might say:

*The world leans toward those who walk lightly.*

That would be enough.

## Sanctuaries Without Walls

The power might create sanctuaries.

Not walled temples. Not guarded cities. But small places of asylum woven into the existing logic of the landscape.

A hollow internode where no one may be pursued.  
A knot-bowl where water may be taken only one vessel at a time.  
A broad seed-case shelter where fire will not spread.  
A root hollow where wounded animals may hide long enough for pursuit to pass.  
A high branch crotch where children can sleep during swarm pulses.  
A polished trail where old enemies must walk apart if they meet, because the acoustics make ambush too obvious.

These sanctuaries would not be absolute. A determined fool could violate them. But the world would discourage violation. Predators would hesitate near certain thresholds. Hunters would feel an inexplicable reluctance. Enemies would find their voices carrying too clearly to strike unseen. Thirsty travelers would sense that a particular water pocket is not to be drained.

The sanctuaries would be known by signs:

a white beetle on the rim,  
a three-note echo,  
a smell of rain where no rain is present,  
a warmth in the bark that feels like breath held gently.

No one would need to build them. They would simply become recognized.

And they would belong to no one.

That is essential. The moment a sanctuary becomes property, it becomes a fortress. The power would not allow that easily. If a group tried to claim a refuge, the refuge would become less reliable. Its echoes would confuse. Its water would turn faintly bitter. Its shelter would attract insects. The world would refuse ownership.

## The Being Itself

If by “the being who lives in it” we mean the tableland as a whole — that vast, slow, plant-made body — then the power would treat it as one would treat an elder whose life is longer than memory.

They would not try to make it young again.

They would help it remain itself.

They would attend to its wounds. When an ancient trunk splits, they would encourage the right kind of sealing: not too fast, not too brittle. When a root platform weakens under centuries of footfall, they would guide new rootlets into the strain. When a crown becomes too heavy, they would let a storm prune it, but not shatter it. When a great plant body dies, they would ensure that its fall is not catastrophic but generative: opening hollows, creating ramps, releasing nutrients, providing habitat.

They might give the great plant forms something that is not consciousness but coherence.

Not voices. Not human thought. But a deeper integrity of response. A sense, if one can call it that, of being held. The plants already respond to touch, vibration, injury, moisture, and heat. The power could refine those responses so that the whole system becomes more resilient without becoming more frantic.

A crown might catch mist more reliably during drought.  
A root swelling might store heat in a way that protects nearby seedlings.  
A flower might open slightly longer during a poor pollinator season.  
A damaged bark cliff might produce resin that hardens into a useful surface instead of becoming a rotting wound.

The tableland would not become a pet. It would remain wild. But it would not be abandoned to meaningless collapse.

And perhaps, in its slow way, the tableland would sense the attention.

Not as worship. Not as command. But as weather from elsewhere: a warmth behind the weather it already makes.

## If the Being Is One Person

If, instead, the being is a single inhabitant — one lean walker on bark plains, one hollow-dweller, one child, one old water-finder — then the power would do even less in visible terms.

It would not lift them out of their world. That would be a rescue only if the world were a prison. This world is not a prison. It is severe, but it is meaningful. To remove the person would be to erase the shape of their belonging.

Instead, the power would help them belong more fully.

It might give them one gift suited to their nature.

If they are a listener, it might let them hear the difference between an empty hollow and a hidden predator three heartbeats sooner.

If they are a maker, it might guide their hands once, in a dream, so they invent a knot that holds better in damp air.

If they are a healer, it might lead them to a fungus that eases inflammation without making the patient sleepy when travel is necessary.

If they are a hunter, it might teach them restraint: the exact moment not to throw.

If they are a mother, it might give her a song that calms infants during insect roar.

If they are old and afraid of dying, it might show them, quietly, that the tableland does not waste what returns to it.

But it would not make them immortal.

Immortality here would be a kind of exile. The person would watch companions age, would become too heavy with memory, would eventually be treated as a relic rather than a relative. The tableland teaches that life is brief enough to be absorbed. A wise power would not steal that lesson.

It might, however, ensure that their death is not meaningless.

Their body would become ground gently. Their name might survive in a rhythm. Their favorite water pocket might fill more often than expected. Their children might dream of them not as a ghost, but as a warmth in the bark.

That is not nothing.

## The Visitor

The power might sometimes take shape, but only lightly.

Not as a burning lord. Not as a giant. Not as a voice from above.

It might appear as a traveler.

A stranger at the edge of a water pocket, very thirsty, carrying a broken staff.  
An old person sitting in the shade of a buttress root, asking for a story.  
A child separated from a band during a pollinator cloud.  
A hunter with no weapon who knows too much.  
A guest who arrives at a temporary platform at dusk and asks only to listen.

In such forms, the power would test not strength but hospitality.

It would ask for water, because water is the true wealth of the tableland. It would ask for shelter, because shelter is borrowed. It would ask for a song, because songs are how knowledge travels without becoming heavy.

And it would reward those who gave without wasting.

Not with gold. Gold is rare here, but not sacred. Not with dominion. Dominion is dangerous. It would reward them with fit things:

A path made easier for one day.  
A child found before nightfall.  
A fire that does not spread when it should have.  
A knot-bowl discovered when all others are dry.  
A warning heard just soon enough.  
A dream that prevents a foolish decision.

Sometimes the visitor would leave no trace except a sense that the air had become clearer. Sometimes they would leave a small object: a polished seed-shell, a thorn with an unusual curve, a fragment of rare metal too small for a blade but perfect as a charm. These things would not be worshipped. They would be carried, traded, repaired, and eventually returned to the living world.

That would please the power.

A gift that refuses to become a monument is the right gift for this place.

## Protection from Outside Hunger

If outsiders came to the tableland, the power would be especially attentive.

Not all outsiders would be threats. Some might come humbly, confused by the noise, clumsy on the root slopes, frightened by the vastness. Such people might be guided toward safe passage: a visible trail, a drinkable leaf cup, a sheltered hollow, a rhythm they could imitate to avoid alarming the local fauna.

But if outsiders came with heavy hunger — with machines, with armies, with the desire to harvest, clear, map, own, or simplify — the power would not meet them with spectacle. It would meet them with the tableland’s own incomprehension.

Distances would seem wrong.  
Maps would fail.  
Compasses might drift near resin-rich groves.  
Metal would corrode faster than expected.  
Boots would rot.  
Supplies would be invaded by insects.  
Sleep would be poor.  
Dreams would be strange and discouraging.

The outsiders would not necessarily be destroyed. Destruction is too heavy a method. Instead, they would become inefficient. Their tools would attract beetles. Their camps would be flooded by sudden local rains. Their voices would carry badly, making coordination difficult. Their fires would smoke and refuse to behave. Their scouts would return with accounts that contradicted one another. Their leaders would find the place boring, dangerous, unprofitable, or spiritually oppressive.

Some would leave.

Some would die from their own poor decisions, and the power would allow that, because the tableland has its own defenses.

A rare few would learn. They would stop trying to conquer. They would listen. They would leave their heavy things behind. They would ask permission of water pockets by tapping lightly and waiting. If they learned enough, they might be allowed to remain for a season, or a lifetime, but always lightly.

The power would make the tableland difficult to possess, but not impossible to join.

## The Dead

A benevolent and wise power would care about death, because death is where fear can make a people cruel.

It would not promise that no one dies. That would be a lie, and lies are heavy. Instead, it would make death less likely to become waste.

The dead would be returned to the tableland in ways that the living could bear.

A body might be placed in a hollow internode and sealed with woven bracts, so that beetles and fungi enter slowly.  
A body might be laid on a root terrace during a flowering pulse, so that pollinators carry something of the person into the next season.  
A body might be folded into leaf-leather with seeds, so that a small patch of ground becomes richer.  
A body might be given to a water pocket, if the person wished it, so that their substance helps the reservoir that saved others.

The power would ensure that these acts do not become horror.

The dead would not rise as accusing ghosts. They would not haunt in ways that trap the living in grief. But they might echo.

On certain evenings, a hollow might carry a voice that sounds almost like a lost grandmother. A certain rhythm struck on a dry seed-case might bring back a father’s laugh without pain. A particular smell of warm resin might make a child feel watched over rather than afraid.

This would be real, but not overwhelming.

The living would say, “The world remembers them.”

And they would be right.

## What Would Change?

If you returned to the tableland after the power had turned its attention there, you might think nothing had changed.

The heat would still bend the distances.  
The great plant forms would still stand far apart like landforms with pulses.  
The insects would still press against the ears with their deafening weave.  
Water would still be hidden, precious, and seasonal.  
Predators would still listen for irregularities in the chorus.  
People would still move lightly, carrying cordage, thorns, striking sticks, and memory.

But there would be a difference.

A small difference, perhaps. A difference in the margin.

Children would get lost less often.  
Old knowledge would return at the right time.  
Fires would stay within their rings more often than chance alone allows.  
Water pockets would be remembered.  
Sanctuaries would be respected without law.  
The great plants would fall safely when their time came.  
Outsiders with greedy hearts would become confused and leave.  
The people would remain few, not because they were cursed, but because the world continued to reward lightness.

The power’s presence would be felt not as a voice saying *I am here*, but as the quiet fact that the world still answers when approached correctly.

Tap a hollow stem, and the echo tells you something.  
Wait at dawn, and the leaf cup has gathered enough for one drink.  
Strike the right rhythm, and the flower opens.  
Walk softly, and the predator does not hear.  
Give thanks, and the thanks is not ridiculous.

That is the kind of blessing such a place can receive without losing itself.

## The Covenant

If the power gave anything like a covenant, it would not be written. It would be lived.

It might sound like this, if translated into human words:

> Take only what can be carried without a wound.  
> Leave one cup for the next mouth.  
> Do not build what cannot be folded back into the world.  
> Listen before crossing open bark.  
> Let fire be a guest, not a lord.  
> Let silence be read, not feared.  
> Let the dead become ground, and let the ground remember them.  
> Do not make yourselves permanent at the cost of the living body that holds you.  
> Be loud when you must.  
> Be quiet when it is wise.  
> Be brief enough to be absorbed without leaving a wound that will not heal.

But the power would not carve this into anything.

It would make the people discover it again and again, because discovery is how knowledge becomes belonging.

## The Final Answer

What would a benevolent and wise power do?

They would not conquer the tableland.  
They would not tame it.  
They would not fill it with miracles that make its own rhythms obsolete.  
They would not choose one people to rule the others.  
They would not make the great plants speak in human voices, because that would make them less than what they are.  
They would not end death, hunger, noise, danger, or decay.

But they would add mercy to the margin.

They would preserve water knowledge.  
They would protect hidden reserves.  
They would strengthen the memory of songs.  
They would guide the lost when possible.  
They would confuse conquerors.  
They would bless light tools and contained fires.  
They would create small sanctuaries without walls.  
They would help the ancient plant bodies age and fall without unnecessary catastrophe.  
They would make death part of the world’s music rather than its interruption.  
They would visit as guest, not as king.  
They would reward hospitality, restraint, and attention.  
They would remain mostly unseen, because the world deserves to be encountered on its own terms.

Their greatest act would be this:

They would make it possible for the tableland to remain a living being, and for all the small beings within it to remain free inside that being.

And if one of the people, standing on a warm bark plain at dusk, felt a sudden calm in the noise, they might not call it divine. They might only tap their striking stick against a hollow stem, hear the echo return clean and true, and say:

“The world is listening.”

That would be enough.
