They would not send rain.

That is where their care would begin.

A lesser kindness would look at the pocket and think: poor thing, dry thing, wounded thing, let me make you soft. It would open the sky. It would call water from nowhere, green the salt, darken the pale walls with damp, set moss creeping along the iron, make the dust cling into mud, fill the fissure with the smell of wet leaves. It would imagine relief as a lowland person imagines relief: abundance, openness, ease.

But a wise power would know that such a gift would be a violence.

Rain would dissolve the crusts. It would short the wires, swell the packed floors, rot the mats, confuse the lizards, drown the beetles in their seams, turn the dry breath of the mountain into a sick and heavy thing. The pocket would not become a valley. It would become a corpse wearing valley colors. Its people would not be freed. They would be unmade.

So the power would withhold the obvious miracle.

It would do something harder.

It would pay attention.

---

At first, it might seem to do nothing at all.

It would not enter the pocket as storm, or voice, or golden shape above the rim. It would not make the fissure blaze with sign. It would not write commandments in the dust or set a new sun burning in the narrow sky. It would not demand to be known. It would not require temples, because a temple would take up space no one could spare, and because the pocket already had a sacred practice, though it did not call it that: maintenance.

Instead, the power would arrive as a change in probability.

A hinge of chance would move.

A stone that would have fallen at noon falls one minute after the passage beneath it has emptied. A cable that would have snapped under load hums at a slightly different pitch, and a worker pauses. A child’s foot slips, but a folded dust cloak breaks the slide before it becomes a fall. A lamp flickers, and instead of being ignored, it makes an engineer stop and ground a panel before the surge comes.

No one would call these things miracles.

They would call them luck. Good listening. The pocket being settled. The mountain not hungry today.

But if the power was truly benevolent, and truly wise, it would understand that in such a place the highest form of grace is not spectacle.

It is margin.

---

The power would first learn what the pocket was.

Not merely the shape of it, not merely the walls, the terraces, the engines, the lamps, but the being that lived there. It would see that the being was not one creature. It was a braid. It was the people and their breath-counting. It was the mats and their slow feeding on warmth. It was the moths powdering themselves against lamp cages. It was the beetles ticking through corners. It was the red lines of mites moving through seams. It was the paper lizards flattened against machine heat. It was the birds dropping through the rim with the void still on their wings. It was the charge-lace writing temporary frost on grounded plates. It was the pulse-shapes moving through fungal crust at night. It was the Undermill turning below, neither wholly machine nor wholly organism nor wholly anything that could be named without loss.

It was the city adjusting.

It was the mountain breathing around the city.

It was the whole constrained system thinking in clicks, warmth, dust, pressure, repair.

A wise power would not look for a single heart and declare that the heart was the being. It would understand that the being was distributed, like heat through iron, like charge through dry air. It would see that the pocket had a kind of mind: not a mind that plotted or dreamed in human sentences, but a mind that noticed. It noticed when a load shifted. It noticed when static gathered too high. It noticed when a duct became clogged, when a mat grew too bright, when a child was too close to an edge. It responded through habit, through bodies, through tools, through fear and skill and the memory of old listeners.

The power would see all this, and it would not pity the pocket.

That would be important.

Pity would mistake the pocket for a lack. The power would know better. The pocket was not merely what it did not have. It was not a failed meadow, a failed river, a failed forest. It was a severe and intricate achievement. It had its own dignity. To pity it would be to begin erasing it.

So the power would instead honor it.

And honor, in such a place, would mean not replacing it with something easier.

---

If the power wished to act, it would first ask.

Not in words. Words would be too large, too wet, too full of mouth and air. The pocket would not trust a voice that came from nowhere. It would think the sound was a warning, or a fault, or a trick of static. The people would stop work and listen, not in reverence, but in alarm.

So the power would ask in the pocket’s own grammar.

It would ask as a warmth where there should have been no warmth.

It would ask as a pressure held back from a crack.

It would ask as a slight easing in the chest of an old wall-tapper just before he placed his hammer against stone.

It would ask: May I keep you?

Not: May I change you.

Not: May I lift you out of yourself.

Not: May I make you beautiful to other eyes.

May I keep you?

The answer would not come quickly, because the pocket did not answer in declarations. It answered in settling. A turbine would smooth. A dust devil would veer away from a sleeping ledge. A paper lizard would move to a newly warm strut and remain there. The mats would glow faintly, not with warning but with something almost like recognition. Below, the Undermill would continue its slow turning, but the sound would deepen by an almost imperceptible degree, as if a question had been heard and found acceptable.

The being would say, in the only way it could:

Not softness.

Not openness.

Not rain.

Margin.

---

The first gift would be warning.

The pocket already lived by signs. The power would not abolish danger, but it would make danger more articulate. It would teach stone to speak one heartbeat sooner. It would let cables carry a note that the right person could hear. It would make dust lean away from a doomed platform before the platform gave. It would sharpen the dreams of old listeners, not with visions of the future, because prophecy is a clumsy thing, but with an intensified attention to the present. A wall-tapper might sleep and wake knowing where to tap. A child might feel a sudden reluctance to cross a certain bridge. An engineer might glance at a switchboard and see a pattern of charge-lace that makes her order a grounding strap before the surge arrives.

None of this would feel supernatural.

It would feel like skill.

It would feel like being good at their work.

But over time, fewer disasters would become catastrophes. More catastrophes would become accidents. More accidents would become near misses. More near misses would become stories told quietly over shared warmth: the day the dust turned, the day the rail rang wrong, the day the Undermill changed its note and everyone stopped without knowing why.

The power would not take credit.

It would let the pocket believe it had grown wiser. In fact, it would have.

---

The second gift would be margin.

Not safety as a lowland city knows safety. Not walls that never crack. Not floors that never sag. Not rails that never fail. The pocket could not be made permanent without ceasing to be the pocket. Its people knew this in their bones. They built for repair, not for eternity. They trusted braces more than foundations. They understood that a structure survived by yielding, redistributing, shifting, being watched.

So the power would not give them permanence.

It would give them time.

A handhold would appear where a hand was likely to reach. Not carved by miracle, but revealed by collapse: a stone falls, and behind it is a ledge exactly suited for fingers. A condemned walkway would hold long enough for the last worker to step clear. A cable would fray in a visible place rather than inside a sheath. A overloaded battery would vent heat outward instead of inward. A sleeping shelf would be spared by a fallen block because the block strikes a strut first and turns aside by a thumb’s width.

This would not be obvious intervention.

It would look like the pocket’s own strange mercy.

But it would mean that not every mistake became death. Not every crack became collapse. Not every collapse became grief. The people would still be careful. They would have to be. But their care would no longer be balanced over an abyss with no edge.

The power would give the abyss an edge.

Not a railing. An edge.

Something against which a body could catch itself.

---

The third gift would be hidden water.

Not open water.

Not pouring, public, careless water.

The power would know that water in the pocket could not be common without becoming dangerous. If water flowed visibly, it would change everything too quickly. It would create greed, fear, dependence, new violence. It would soften the salt and alter the mats and make the machines strange. It would make the children forget why breath was caught in filters and sweat counted as loss.

So the power would give water in the pocket’s own severe way.

Deep below, where the stone was colder and the maps disagreed, it would reveal a sealed lens of hydrated mineral. Not a spring. Not a pool. A dense, pale layer that yielded moisture only under careful heat and chemical cracking. Enough for emergency. Enough for a season of failure. Enough to keep the stills from running dry when the upper crust turned barren and the furnaces faltered. But not enough for luxury. Not enough for waste. Not enough to make anyone forget the law of the place.

The people would find it only when they needed it, and perhaps not all at once.

A miner would notice a different brittleness in the rock. A chemist would see an unexpected bead of condensation inside a sealed retort. An old listener would dream of a wall breathing slowly, not dryly. They would follow the signs with the patience they had been taught, and they would uncover the hidden mercy.

They would not call it a blessing from a god.

They would call it the Closed Vein, or the Last Flask, or the Stone That Waits.

They would guard it.

They would ration it.

They would weep privately, if they wept at all, because in a place where water is counted like blood, to find an unspoken reserve is to discover that the world has not entirely forgotten you.

The power would not mind that they did not thank it.

The gratitude would be in the careful use.

---

The fourth gift would be knowledge, but only knowledge that fit.

A foolish power would give them great machines from elsewhere. It would hand them rain-makers, greenhouses, humming towers of impossible alloy, water pumps, soft beds, broad windows. It would solve the pocket by making it no longer a pocket. The people would either worship the gifts or be destroyed by them. Either way, they would lose the authority of their own hands.

A wise power would give nothing that could not be repaired with what they had.

It would nudge thought, not impose it.

A mask-maker would wake with an idea for pleating mineral cloth so it caught finer dust without making breath harder. A wire-weaver would discover that a certain braid, if doubled back through a ceramic eye, discharged static more gently. A mat-tender would notice that ash-leather grew stronger when fed with crushed beetle shell and furnace bloom in a particular ratio. A caster would realize that a fan placed at an angle could cool molds faster without spending water. A child would invent a safer sliding loop and be laughed at until it saved her cousin’s wrist.

These inventions would spread because they worked.

Not because they were commanded.

The power would not need to be named as inventor. It would be satisfied if the pocket became a little more capable of continuing. It would understand that the best gift to a people who live by repair is not a finished thing, but a better way to repair.

---

The fifth gift would be for the small lives.

A benevolent power that was truly wise would not care only for the people. It would know that the pocket’s being was braided from all its inhabitants, and that the smallest threads were often the ones whose loss unraveled the rest.

It would look at the moths and ensure that the lamps did not burn too hot on one side of their cages. It would make certain that at least a few clusters of eggs were always left undisturbed, even in lean seasons. It would guide a worker’s hand away from a seam where mites were flowing in a useful red line. It would keep one duct open enough for blind centipedes to pass, because their presence kept other populations from tipping too far. It would warm a wall near the sleeping shelves so the paper lizards would remain there, and the people would see them and take comfort without knowing exactly why.

It would care for the birds most carefully, because the birds belonged partly to the void.

It would make the rim fissure less murderous in certain winds. It would not widen it, because widening would change the pocket’s breath. It would only take away the rare cruel gust that snapped wings against stone. It would let a few more chicks survive each season. It would guide one lost bird back when the sky outside was too bright and empty. It would not make the birds tame. It would not make them numerous enough to foul the machines. It would keep them balanced: enough to return, enough to be watched, enough to remind the pocket that there was something beyond the rim.

The people would think the fauna was thriving by ordinary luck.

But the luck would have a shape.

---

The sixth gift would be for the Undermill.

The power would descend to the chamber where no map agreed.

It would go through dust so fine it moved like slow water, past switches housing tiny pale lives, past ducts where centipedes felt the walls for prey, past heat that had no color but pressed against the skin like a secret. It would come to the place where the great slow thing turned in the dark.

It would not command the Undermill.

It would not ask it to be more than it was. It would not force it to reveal whether it was machine, organism, or accumulated being. It would understand that the mystery was part of the ecology. The people needed not to know everything. Some things were kept by being partly unknown.

But the power would listen.

It would listen to the rhythm. It would feel for the drag in the turning, the heat in the stone, the pressure in the sealed chambers. If the Undermill was hungry, the power would arrange for a chute to carry richer powder toward it. If it was overheating, the power would inspire a worker to open a forgotten vent. If it was becoming clogged, the power would cause a beetle line to move through a passage and clear a crust before the crust became a blockage.

It would not make the Undermill eternal.

Eternity would be too heavy for the pocket. It would make the place feel finished. The people were not made for finished places. They were made for maintenance. But the power would keep the Undermill from stopping without warning. It would give it the same gift it gave the walls, the cables, the children: time.

When the low sound changed, the people would still pause. They would still fear. They would still descend with masks and tools and careful hearts. But more often than not, they would find a way to set it right.

That would be enough.

In a place like the pocket, enough was a form of holiness.

---

The seventh gift would be protection from outside.

The pocket was hidden, but hidden things can still be found. A wise power would know that the greatest danger might not come from within. It might come from discovery. If the wrong eyes saw the fissure, they might see salt to be stripped, minerals to be extracted, labor to be exploited, curiosity to be monetized. They might bring water in a way that destroyed the pocket’s dry law. They might bring noise. They might bring hunger without responsibility.

So the power would make the pocket hard to take.

Not impossible to find. That would be too harsh, and the pocket was not wholly sealed from the world. Birds passed through the rim. Some people had come from lower places once. Perhaps, rarely, someone humble and necessary would still find the way.

But greedy maps would blur.

Surveyors would misread the ridge. Machines brought to force the fissure would clog with dust before they reached it. Soldiers would feel a strange reluctance and turn back, saying the place was unstable, worthless, too thin to breathe. Traders who came with contempt would lose their sense of direction and wander into passes that led nowhere. Those who came with patience, who lowered their voices, who touched discharge plates and covered their faces, who did not spill liquid or shout or strike the walls in anger—such travelers might find the seam. They would be watched. They would be tested by small silences. If they learned, they might be allowed to remain long enough to matter.

The power would not build a wall around the pocket.

It would build a discernment into the approach.

The pocket would remain difficult. But it would not be easy to devour.

---

The eighth gift would be a pause.

Not a festival, exactly. Festivals would be too loud. Not a procession, because there was no room. Not a holy day marked by banners, because banners had no place in the dry wind. But a pause.

The power would plant the idea in an old listener, or a mat-tender, or a child who had once felt dust move oddly before a collapse. The idea would be simple:

Once, when the dark season is deepest, lower the machines.

Not all at once. Not so much that the air grows cold or the lamps fail. But enough. Lower the turbines. Still the crushers. Let the cable lifts rest. Let the furnaces breathe low. Let the arcs die. Let the market hush. Let the city hear itself.

The people would do it first for practical reasons.

They would hear a cracked strut they had missed. They would notice a change in the Undermill. They would find a seam glowing too bright, a filter clogged, a child’s cough hidden under too many blankets. They would repair what the noise had concealed.

Then they would begin to do it for another reason.

In the pause, they would feel the pocket holding them.

They would hear the soft percussion of beetle shells. The whisper of powder settling. The faint tick of cooling metal. The rustle of a paper lizard moving along warm stone. The tiny lives inside the walls would seem closer. The mats would glow. The pulse-shapes would move. The city would not be empty of sound, but it would be emptied of labor. For a few breaths, the people would not be maintaining the pocket. They would be inside it, being kept by it, as it had always kept them.

They would call it the Listening.

Some would say it was old custom. Some would say it began after a near disaster. Some would say an elder dreamed it. No one would agree. That would be fitting. The power would not need the pause to be attributed to it. It would only need the pause to happen.

Because in the Listening, the pocket would remember itself.

And a being that remembers itself is harder to break.

---

The ninth gift would be comfort, but not too much comfort.

The power would know that the pocket could not become soft. Its people could not afford the luxury of forgetting hardness. But they could be given moments of warmth that did not weaken them.

A lover would find a hidden corner where the air was still enough to press foreheads together without losing breath. An elder would feel a warmth near their sleeping shelf that seemed not to come from any pipe. A frightened child would hear a small clicking in the wall and imagine it was a beetle saying the place was sound. A worker returning from the lower levels would find a clean mask left for them without a name attached. A grieving family would discover that their water count had been quietly supplemented by neighbors, and no one would speak of it, because speaking would make the loss too public.

The power would not erase sorrow.

That would be arrogance. Sorrow was part of what bound them to one another. To remove it entirely would be to remove love. But the power would keep sorrow from becoming despair. It would make sure no grief was wholly isolated. It would make sure that when someone reached out, even silently, something in the pocket answered.

Sometimes the answer would be human.

Sometimes it would be animal: a lizard choosing the wall beside them, a bird returning through the rim, a moth resting on a glove.

Sometimes it would be mechanical: a lamp flickering into steadiness, a turbine smoothing, a switchbox humming without fault.

Sometimes it would be mineral: a crack closing slightly with cold, a dust drift settling away from a face.

And sometimes it would be only a feeling, so faint that the person would not trust it. A sense that the pocket was not empty. That the dryness was not indifference. That all the small lives, all the wires, all the salt and iron and breath had gathered into something that noticed them back.

The power would allow that feeling, but would not insist on it.

It would be like a hand held near a flame without touching.

---

The tenth gift would be restraint.

This would be the hardest, and therefore the truest sign of wisdom.

The power would have the ability to do more. It could fill the pocket with light. It could make the walls unbreakable. It could give the people bodies that never tired, lungs that never collected dust, children who never fell, machines that never failed. It could turn the pocket into a jewel, a paradise, a sealed heaven.

But it would not.

Because the pocket was not meant to be a jewel. It was a negotiation. Its meaning was not in perfection but in persistence. Its beauty was not the beauty of ease but of repair. Its people were not precious because they were safe. They were precious because they were careful. They were precious because they knew how to live where life had to be counted in sips, in filters, in grounded straps, in tapped walls, in rescued warmth.

If the power removed every danger, it would remove their competence.

If it removed every lack, it would remove their reverence.

If it made them incapable of loss, it would make them incapable of love as they knew it.

So the power would restrain itself.

It would let stones fall. It would let cables snap. It would let filters clog and machines stutter and elders die and children cry when they could not be comforted. It would let the pocket be severe. It would not make it crueler than necessary, but it would not make it gentle in a way that destroyed its nature.

It would hold back annihilation, not difficulty.

It would prevent the pocket from being erased, not from being hard.

That distinction would be everything.

---

If the people ever tried to imagine such a power, they would imagine it wrongly.

They might think of it as a great engineer, or a silent inspector, or an old woman with a hammer, or a moth with a lamp inside its body. They might make small tags and bolt them to walls in private, not as idols but as reminders. They might whisper over a new brace: May it hold. They might touch a discharge stone before a dangerous task and feel, for a moment, that they were not alone in the act of grounding.

The power would not correct them.

It would not need their images to be accurate. It would only need their hands to be steady.

If some among them began to worship it too loudly, the pocket itself would correct them. A shout would feel too big. A procession would block a passage. A shrine would gather dust and become a hazard. The people would remember, by instinct, that reverence here had to be compact. It had to fold itself into maintenance. It had to become a cleaned filter, a tightened bolt, a saved sip, a child taught to listen before running.

The highest prayer would be repair.

The highest offering would be care.

---

In the end, the power would not save the pocket from being the pocket.

It would help the pocket remain.

That would be its blessing.

Not rain.

Not greenness.

Not wide sky.

Not softness.

But a longer turning.

A smaller fall.

A warning heard in time.

A hidden reserve found in need.

A child caught.

A cable grounded.

A mat glowing without alarm.

A bird returning.

An Undermill continuing.

A city adjusting around its next crack, not perfectly, not painlessly, but together enough to go on.

When evening came and the high slit of sky darkened, the lamps would still bloom one by one behind their wire cages. The salt walls would still take the amber light and hold it in layers. The dust would still settle on folded moth wings, on beetle shells, on iron struts, on the shoulders of people who had stopped moving long enough to be still. Somewhere below, the slow thing would still turn. Somewhere above, a bird would still tuck its head into the ironwork. Somewhere in the wall, a mat would still brighten faintly, and a small shape would still pulse through it like a thought the pocket was having but did not need to speak.

But the silence would be different.

Not empty.

Held.

The power would be there, not as a face in the air, not as a voice from the rim, but as a quality of attention woven through the place. It would be in the fraction of a second between a crack and a scream. It would be in the unexpected steadiness of a frightened hand. It would be in the way a dust column bent away from a sleeping child. It would be in the way an old listener woke just before the wall spoke. It would be in the way the city, after every disturbance, did not merely survive, but remembered how to repair itself.

If one of them, in a rare unguarded moment, pressed a palm to the cool stone and felt something that seemed almost like warmth answering, they would not be wrong.

The pocket would be seen.

The being who lived there would be known.

And because it was known by a power both benevolent and wise, it would not be transformed into something easier.

It would be kept in its own severe and astonishing truth.

Dry as bone.

Bright with small lives.

Full of pressure.

Full of repair.

Not saved from itself.

Saved into itself.
