They would not come as light.

That is the first thing. Light would not be a kindness there. It would be an invasion. In that packed, sunless water, brightness would not reveal the plain so much as wound it: a harshness scattering through suspended silt, making shadows more confusing, making every eyeless body flinch toward danger. The place has no use for radiance. Radiance would belong to another kind of world, a world of surfaces and skies and upward longing. This is not that world.

Nor would they come as voice. Voice would be swallowed. Even if the power possessed speech vast enough to fill the basin, the medium would eat the sound, break it into pressure, blur it into granular noise. A command there would become a tremor. A song would become a disturbance. A proclamation would be indistinguishable from a density pulse. So a wise power would not announce itself in the manner of air-world gods. It would not demand attention. It would not require awe.

If the drowned plain and the being who lives in it drew the attention of a benevolent and wise power, I think the power would do almost nothing that could be called a miracle.

They would do only what could be forgotten.

Their action would take the shape of the place itself: low, patient, unmonumental. It would leave no ruin, no shrine, no cleared circle, no unnatural smoothness in the mud. It would not make the water clearer. It would not warm the cold. It would not open a ceiling of daylight above the mineral dark. It would not give the inhabitants eyes, or fire, or speech, or cities, or the desire for any of those things. It would not rescue them from silt, because they are made by and for silt. It would not rescue them from pressure, because pressure is the hand that shaped them. It would not rescue them from impermanence, because impermanence is the only permanence their world allows.

A lesser benevolence might try to lift them into becoming something else. It might imagine them upright, bright, articulate, building towers that do not fill with sediment. It might pity them for leaving no artifacts, for possessing no language of abstraction, for having no history written in stone. It might say: let us improve them. Let us give them ambition. Let us give them memory that lasts. Let us give them dominion.

But wisdom would see that this would be annihilation dressed as kindness.

The plain is not a failed surface. It is not a ruined land waiting to be restored to air. It is not a sea in need of light. It is a completed world. Its goodness is not the goodness of ascent, or abundance, or visible achievement. Its goodness is the goodness of final form. Everything there has been worn down to what it can be. The creatures are not lesser because they do not build. They are exact. They are adapted to a world that refuses permanence. Their intelligence, if it exists, is not a seed that needs to be forced into our shape. It is a slow, tactile knowing suited to edges, weights, currents, and chemical signs. To give it more would be to give it the wrong kind of hunger. To give it longing for permanence would be to curse it.

So the power’s first act would be refusal.

They would refuse to make the place legible to themselves.

They would refuse to translate it into brightness.

They would refuse to privilege the tool-using being over the microbial film, the worm, the drifter, the burrower, the scavenger, the floc, the film-coated grain. They would understand that the plain is not a kingdom ruled by intelligence, but a vast, slow body in which intelligence is only one faint pressure among many. The crawler selecting a shell is no more central to the place than the bacterium coating the shell, the current moving the shell, or the sediment eventually burying the shell.

Then, after refusal, the power would attend.

This would not be passive attention. It would not be the distant glance of a benevolence that has larger worlds to govern. It would be close. It would be granular. It would descend into the scale of the place. The power would notice the way a microbial film tightens when brine changes. It would notice the exact angle at which a flat crawler presses a shell into mud. It would notice a worm pausing in a chemical smear, comparing one taste against another. It would notice a drifter folding itself as a density pulse passes. It would notice the small blue-green flash that appears when a mat is disturbed and then vanishes, leaving the darkness more complete than before.

To be seen that fully would itself be an act of grace.

The plain has no witness in the ordinary sense. Nothing there stands apart and says: this happened, this mattered, this creature lived, this gesture was beautiful. Everything is reabsorbed. Trails close. Burrows fill. Tools are dropped. Bodies soften into floc. The sediment storm erases without malice. A wise and benevolent power might therefore give the place what it cannot give itself: remembrance without monument.

They would hold the plain in memory without making the plain depend on memory.

They would not carve the memory into stone, because stone there is either buried or worn. They would not write it into light, because light there is eaten. They would keep it within themselves, quietly, the way one keeps the shape of a beloved breathing in the dark. Every selected flake, every failed burrow, every patient predation, every accidental meeting, every death that became a softening into mineral matter—all of it would be known. Not judged. Not ranked. Not redeemed into some brighter narrative. Known.

That is what they would do first: see the place as it is, and not wish it otherwise.

But benevolence, if it is real, may not remain only in seeing. If they did anything more, it would be small, exact, and easy to mistake for chance.

They would tune the turbulence, not abolish it.

They would not stop the sediment storms. The storms are not accidents; they are the plain breathing. They redistribute fines, expose mats, bury weak growths, carry chemical signs, keep the final floor from becoming a dead, static surface. Without that internal weather, the place would lose the very restlessness that sustains its sparse life. A wise power would know that to remove turbulence would be to remove the world’s living motion. So they would not make the water still.

But they might make the turbulence a little less arbitrary where life clings.

A density current might shear aside just enough to leave a shallow hollow unfilled for a few generations of microbial film. A plume of suffocating silt might tear apart one body-length sooner than it otherwise would, giving a burrower time to shift. A chemical warning might travel slightly ahead of a collapse, not as a message, but as a sourness in the water that makes small bodies withdraw. A drifter’s larva might be carried into a patch of survivable floc instead of into a brine-heavy dead pocket. A seep might remain steady long enough for a blackish weed or a pale thread-filament to establish itself before the next redistribution.

These would be mercies without spectacle.

No creature would know they had been saved. No being would gather around the hollow and call it holy. The hollow would fill eventually. The seep would fade. The warning would be forgotten, if it was ever recognized at all. The power’s miracles would be indistinguishable from luck, from local favor, from the ordinary unevenness of an ancient floor.

They would not give the beings permanent tools.

This is important. They would not give a blade that never dulls, or a shell that never breaks, or a wedge that cannot be dislodged. Such gifts would be alien to the place. They would create attachment where attachment is dangerous. They would create ownership where ownership is impossible. They would create loss where loss was once merely release. In a world that rejects permanence, a permanent object would become a burden, a danger, or an idol. A wise power would not introduce such violence.

Instead, they might give a gesture.

Perhaps they would appear, or seem to appear, as one of the low beings: a flat, film-coated crawler moving with unusual patience through the silt. Not brighter than the others. Not larger. Not marked by any sign of authority. Only more still. More exact. More unhurried. It would not lead. It would not gather followers. It would not stand above the floor. It would move as the floor allows.

One of the hand-minded beings might encounter it.

The meeting would not be dramatic. There would no recognition of divinity. There would be pressure first: the sense of another body displacing the packed medium. Then chemical reading: not hostile, not starving, not reproductively urgent, not wounded. Perhaps a faint strangeness, a taste too coherent, too attentive, too unafraid. The being would approach cautiously. The power would wait.

Between them there might be a shell.

The power would pick it up—not quickly, not with flourish. It would test the curve, the edge, the weight. Then it would press the shell into the sediment at an angle that deflects a thin stream of silt away from a feeding patch. Not a wall. Not a dam. Not a construction. Just a deflection, held for as long as needed, then released.

If the other being imitated the angle, the power would move aside.

If the other being failed, the power would not correct it with instruction. It might repeat the gesture once, slowly, letting the body-language of pressure and motion teach what no words could teach. Then it would stop. It would not insist. It would not create a school. It would not create a tradition that demands continuity. If the gesture survived, it would survive because it was useful. If it vanished, that too would be accepted.

This is all the power would give: an angle, a timing, a release.

They might also give memory without possession.

Not written memory. Not carved memory. Not a monument of any kind. Perhaps only a faint chemical trace left by a useful act, a whisper that fades within a few turns of current. A shell used well might leave behind not the shell itself but a taste-sign: this shape is good. A particular pressure-tap might leave behind a brief resonance: wait here. A disturbed mat might release a glimmer that means not danger, but food. These traces would not last long enough to become property. They would not accumulate into archives. They would not allow one being to command another through control of records. They would pass, as everything passes, but they would pass with a little more meaning.

If translated into human language, the power’s gift might sound like this:

*Use, but do not own.*  
*Know, but do not build.*  
*Remember, but do not bind.*  
*Continue, but do not conquer.*

They would not give the beings desire for what they cannot have.

This is perhaps the deepest restraint. They would not place in the creatures any ache for air, or light, or open sky. They would not make them dream of hands that build towers, or voices that carry across distance, or minds that abstract the plain into symbols. Such dreams would not enlarge them. They would exile them from their own lives. To long for another world while trapped in this one would be a cruelty no kindness could justify.

Instead, the power might deepen their fit.

A crawler might become slightly more sensitive to the difference between a stable surface and one about to liquefy. A burrower might learn, or seem to learn, the precise pressure that means a tunnel will hold for a little while longer. A drifter might unfold at the right moment into a layer of water where edible floc is passing. A predator might strike not with greater violence, but with less waste. A young being might follow an older one through a favorable chemical field and miss fewer signs.

Their excellence would remain humble. It would be the excellence of remaining correctly within a world that does not forgive excess.

If there is one being among them who can be called, in some generous sense, a person—the slow, hand-minded inhabitant of edges and weights—the power would meet that being without demanding transformation.

They would not say: become more.

They would say, if pressure can say anything: be here.

They might rest beside that being during a density current, not as a protector exactly, but as a weight against the current. The being would feel another body nearby, unmoved, steady, not competing for food, not threatening, not retreating. It might sense that the pressure beside it is not merely physical. It might not understand. Understanding, in our sense, is not what the place offers. But it might feel less alone, if loneliness exists there as a lack of favorable presence rather than a lack of speech.

When the current passed, the power would be gone. Or not gone, but dispersed back into the general medium, present only as a slight difference in the way the silt settles.

The being would continue.

It would pick up a shell, or a flake, or a spicule. It would use it. It would release it. It would move on. If it remembered anything, the memory would live in its body, not in a story. It might press a shell at a better angle. It might wait a moment longer before abandoning a burrow. It might tap once against stone when another being approaches, not because it was taught a ritual, but because somewhere in its flesh there remains the memory of a pressure that was kind.

The power would also protect the plain, but not with walls.

Walls are foreign to the place. Anything raised becomes a target for sediment. Anything fixed is undermined. So if outside forces ever threatened the plain—curious machines from above, dredging lights, mining probes, instruments meant to sample and violate—the power would not defend it with dramatic force. They would make the plain difficult to possess.

Maps would smooth over. Instruments would find only noise. Lights would be eaten by suspended matter before they reached anything significant. Drills would clog with floc. Samples would lose their strange chemistry before they could be understood. The attention of outside powers would slide away, bored or confused, drawn toward clearer targets. The plain would remain obscure not because it is hidden by a barrier, but because it refuses to yield itself to careless seeing.

That too would be an act of love: keeping the place safe from being improved into destruction.

They would not make the beings immortal.

Immortality in a finished world would be a burden. The plain is old, poor, and crowded with particulate death. Life there persists by being continuous, not by being individually endless. If every creature endured without dissolution, the medium would choke. Hunger would become eternal. Stillness would become a trap. Death there is not an interruption of meaning; it is one of the ways meaning moves. The body softens. The minerals redistribute. The shell becomes a tool for another. The floc becomes a meal. The chemical trace becomes a signal. The plain recycles without sentiment and without waste.

A wise power would not take that from them.

But they might make death less lonely.

When a small creature is smothered by a settling cloud, or a predator folds around it, or its body simply loses coherence after long patience, the power might attend the passing. Not to prevent it. Not to argue with it. Only to be present at the threshold. The dying creature might feel, if feeling remains, one last pressure that is not weight but recognition. Its dissolution would not be witnessed by eyes, but it would not be utterly unseen. The plain would still absorb it. The water would still close. But somewhere in the power’s vast attention, the creature would have existed exactly as it was: low, slow, adapted, enough.

This may be the most benevolent act of all.

The plain leaves no trace. The power could become the place where traces are kept without becoming relics.

They would not preserve the beings in amber or crystal. They would not lift them into a museum of forms. They would not say: this species is interesting, let us save it for the future. They would let the future be as the plain allows. But they would not let the truth of the plain vanish from all knowing. They would remember the exact texture of a crawler’s first tool-gesture. They would remember the flicker of a disturbed mat. They would remember the taste-sign of a favorable seep. They would remember every being that ever moved through the dense water and then softened into it.

And they would love the plain without translating it.

That is rare. Most love wants to make the beloved into something easier to love. It wants clarity, response, expression, monument, gratitude. It wants the beloved to shine. But this place cannot shine. It does not shine. It whispers. It settles. It presses. It continues. To love it truly is to love it dark, packed, cold, and finished. To love its inhabitants is to love them without requiring them to become like us. To love their intelligence is to accept that it may never speak, never write, never build, never remember itself in lasting form. To love their world is to accept that its highest achievement is not progress, but fit.

If the power felt moved, if such a being can be said to feel, it would not be moved by pity.

It might be moved by reverence.

There is something holy about a world that has stopped striving. Not dead. Not defeated. Completed. The mountains are gone. The valleys are filled. The sharp shoulders of the world have been worn away. What remains is a vast floor of patience, where even turbulence is only continuation. Life there does not clamor. It does not announce. It clings where chemistry permits. It uses what can be carried. It abandons what becomes a burden. It dies into the same medium that sustained it. There is a purity in that. Not innocence—there is hunger, death, smothering, predation—but purity of form. Everything belongs to the conditions that made it.

A wise power might honor that by kneeling, if kneeling could be made of pressure.

They would not kneel above the plain. They would kneel within it, lowering themselves into the sediment’s scale until they were no greater than a grain, no brighter than a chemical flash, no louder than a shell shifting in the dark.

And perhaps, in their own way, they would bless it.

Not with abundance. Not with fertility beyond measure. Not with victory over death. The blessing would be small and severe, suited to the place. If it could be translated into words, it might sound like this:

*May you remain low and sufficient.*  
*May the edge you need be near.*  
*May the current warn you.*  
*May the silt not close too soon.*  
*May what you carry be light enough to release.*  
*May you not be asked to become what you are not.*  
*May your death be a softening, not a tearing.*  
*May what passes through you be blessed, even though it leaves no mark.*

Then the power would leave no sign.

Or rather, they would leave only signs that disappear.

A shell slightly smoother where it was held. A pressure pattern that fades after one current. A chemical trace that lingers just long enough for one young being to find a safer gradient. A hollow that remains open a little longer than expected. A brief blue-green glimmer that seems not random but almost deliberate, then vanishes. Nothing that could be worshipped. Nothing that could be owned. Nothing that could become a center. The plain would remain centerless, horizonless, unimproved.

The being who lives there might sense something, but would not name it.

A crawler might pause over a flake and feel, in the packed water, a stillness that is not absence of current but presence without pressure. A worm might taste a brine-thread and find in it not food, but something like reassurance. A drifter might shiver as if touched by intention, then settle. A predator might wait with less hunger and more patience, as though the act of waiting itself had been made holy. None of them would build a temple. None would sing a hymn. None would claim that a god had visited. The visitation would be too small for theology and too real to be nothing.

That, I think, is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

They would not save the plain by changing it into another world. They would bless it by allowing it to be fully itself. They would protect it from outside violence, ease its sharpest accidents, remember its vanished gestures, and perhaps teach one low being one useful angle of a shell before letting the sediment take the lesson back.

Afterward, the plain would look the same.

The water would still be heavy, sunless, crowded with suspended matter. The floor would still be nearly flat, worn down to final patience. Density currents would still move through the dark. Floc would still rise and settle. Microbial films would still coat every surface. Blind worms would still taste brine. Ribbon drifters would still hang motionless in the crowded medium. Flat crawlers would still select shells, use them briefly, and abandon them. The cold would remain. The pressure would remain. The granular whisper would remain.

But there would be a difference.

Not a visible difference. Not a measurable difference, perhaps, except by the subtlest attention. The difference would be like the difference between a room where someone has died alone and a room where someone was present at the end. The facts may be unchanged. The loneliness is not.

The plain would no longer be merely abandoned to its completion.

It would be accompanied.

And somewhere in the dark, a being would press a shell into the mud, deflect a thin stream of silt, hold the gesture just long enough, then release it. The water would close. The silt would settle. The shell would vanish. The being would move on.

It would not know that it had been blessed.

It would only continue.
