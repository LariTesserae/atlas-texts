They would begin by not arriving.

That would be the first discipline of their kindness. No splitting of the salt-field, no opening of the pale crust with spectacle. No rain, because rain there would not be a blessing but a wound. No voice pouring itself through the root-ceiling in a way that made every child cry and every static-tender clutch their instruments. A wise power would understand at once that this buried country had its own covenant with existence: wax for shelter, resin for repair, salt for structure, static for power, silence for attention, ordinary light for the day’s measured work.

So they would not come as a storm.

They would come as an adjustment.

First they would listen, and listening, in that place, would not mean merely hearing sound. It would mean attending to creaks and loads, to the faint language of tension in root-bridges, to the way leaf-plates shifted when the thermal tides changed, to the small chemical warnings released by an overtaxed trunk. It would mean learning the difference between a quiet that was peace and a quiet that was strain. It would mean understanding that the hollow was not empty because it had wide spaces; it was composed of distances, and those distances were part of its life.

They would see the great plants not as instruments or scenery, but as elder citizens. They would not command them. They would propose to them in the slow grammar of growth: a little more strength here, a root-tip sent there, a bark-plate thickened where a walkway had begun to wear, a resin bleed encouraged where a crack was forming in a support. If the plants accepted—and some would accept, because even vast vegetable intelligences sometimes welcome an easing of burden—the change would look natural. A root would find a better anchor. A trunk would settle more evenly. A platform would become safer by what seemed like good luck, or good husbandry, or the patient skill of the people who had trained it for generations.

A benevolent power, if it was truly wise, would know that the cruelest gift is a gift that replaces the hands of the ones who receive it. So they would not remake the country. They would not finish the unfinished floors with a wave of intention. They would not eliminate scarcity, because scarcity there was not mere suffering; it was part of the logic of carefulness. They would not abolish predators, because predators were also quiet workers in the metabolism of the hollow. They would not take away danger altogether, because a world without danger becomes a world without skill, without memory, without the tenderness that comes from knowing something can fail.

But they would not stand aside entirely, either.

To be benevolent is not only to admire. To be wise is not only to understand. Sometimes love must act, and wise action is often modest.

So they would do something.

Not much.

Enough.

There is one being who lives there.

Not the only one who matters, but one whose life has become a point of concentration: a static-tender who works the upper walkways beneath one of the high light shafts. Their name, in the soft click-language of their ward, is difficult to render in surface speech. It means something close to “hand laid flat against a humming rail,” or perhaps “one who waits for the creak to become a word.” They are slender, dust-fine in movement, their wax-down standing faintly when charge gathers in the air. They are not a priest, not a ruler, not a hero in any grand sense. They are a maintenance worker. They listen to machinery, to trunks, to the combs, to the ducts. They know when a belt is about to slip, when a leaf-comb is about to discharge, when a root-bridge has begun to dry too much on one side and stiffen dangerously.

This being has been afraid for several days.

They have felt it in the air: a coming dry storm, not large by the standards of the hollow, but badly placed. The thermal tides are shifting. Static is building along the upper combs near a nursery ward. There is a cable path there, old and salt-rimmed, carrying oil and powder between two trunks; if an arc strikes the wrong point, seed-husk dust could ignite. The people in that ward have had warnings before. Sometimes warnings are false. Sometimes they are not. The tender has spoken softly to the overseers, has tapped the signs of concern into the railing-code, has left a bead of wax at the base of a support as both prayer and record. But the ward is busy, the season is tight, and there are never enough hands to inspect everything.

The power sees this.

Not from above in the sense of looking down, but from within attention. They see the tender pressing their forehead against warm bark in the dark, trying not to be afraid, because fear is a kind of noise and the tender has been trained to mistrust noise. They see the children sleeping in hanging compartments near the seed-drying racks. They see the old cable, the salt bloom on its sheath, the brittle patch where flexing has begun to expose fiber. They see the dry storm assembling in the upper hollow: the drifting bands of spore-haze, the prickling along leaf edges, the pale insects clustering motionless near the combs.

They could prevent the storm.

They choose not to.

They could descend in visible radiance and command the ward to evacuate. They choose not to. That would be too loud, too sovereign, too alien to the culture of the place. It would make obedience out of what should remain craft. It would create a memory of domination where there should instead be a memory of repair.

So they work through likelihood, through small openings, through the existing nature of things.
