A benevolent and wise power, if it turned toward that high basin, would not arrive as storm.

It would not come with thunder to correct the silence, nor with light so sudden that the still pools would feel accused. It would not send wind merely to prove that air could move. It would understand, at once, that this place had been made into a kind of vow, and that a vow is not improved by being broken.

So the first thing the power would do is nothing visible.

It would look.

It would see the basin as a single being: the stone its bones, the channels its long veins, the mirror-still water its open and unblinking eyes, the green moss its patient skin, the Arrangers its thoughts. It would see that the being was not incomplete. It had not failed to become itself. On the contrary, it had become itself so thoroughly that it had stopped mid-breath and made a home inside that pause.

The power would admire the beauty of that.

It would also perceive the cost.

There was no grief in the basin that took the shape of crying. There was no hunger that took the shape of grasping. But there was a hidden tenderness: the fear of spilling. The people had made holiness out of restraint, and in doing so they had made every accident into a small sorrow. They had taught themselves to love fullness, but only fullness held perfectly in place. They had learned to keep water from deciding to leave. They had not yet learned to ask the water where it might wish to go.

A lesser benevolence might have pitied them.

A foolish power might have tried to free them.

A wise one would know that in such a place even kindness must be carried like a bowl filled to the lip.

So it would choose the smallest possible intervention. Not command. Not miracle. Not abundance that overwhelms. It would choose an invitation.

It would wait until the light lay level across the canals, when the pale stone looked soft and the shadows under the terraces were thin as breath. Then it would appear at the edge of the city in the form of a single figure, no brighter than the day, no taller than the tallest Arranger, dressed in garments that did not flutter because there was no wind to ask otherwise of them.

The figure would carry a shallow basin of dark stone.

It would be full to the brim.

This would be the first sign.

The Brimkeepers would notice it before anyone else. They would see the stranger crossing the open ground with a vessel so full that the water seemed to stand up along the edge without spilling. Their hands would tighten around their leaf-shaped scoops. The Edgetenders would pause along the borders. The Mosswardens, who attend the blue-green cushions and know the smallest changes in their dampness, would lift their heads as if a new scent had entered the air. Even those on the flat roofs would turn their horizontal pupils toward the arrival.

No one would run. Running would be unthinkable there. But they would begin to gather, slowly, with the care of people approaching a balance that might tip.

The stranger would stop near a low plaza where a row of narrow canals lay glassy and leaf-still. It would not speak first. It would stand as if it had all the patience of stone and all the courtesy of water.

At last the Arrangers would come near enough to form the beginnings of a council. Two figures would be enough. Three would be solemn. More would make the moment feel like a decision.

One of them might ask, in their measured way, “Have you come to drink?”

The stranger would answer, “No.”

Another might ask, “Have you come to fill your basin here?”

The stranger would answer, “Not only.”

Then they would look at the water in the dark basin and see that it was perfectly still, yet in it there seemed to be a depth greater than the vessel should allow. Not depth downward, but depth inward, as though the water contained a distance.

The stranger would say, “I have come because you have kept something beautiful. I have come to ask if you would receive one drop more than you expect.”

That would trouble them.

To spill is grief among them. To receive too much is danger. Their whole discipline has taught them that fullness must be measured, maintained, and honored. The thought of more than fullness would sound like the beginning of loss.

They would stand together at the water’s edge, as they do when decisions form, and for a long time there would be no answer. The pools would hold the sky. The leaves would hold their beads. The stranger would wait without shifting its weight.

At last one of the Arrangers, perhaps the eldest in care rather than age, would say, “If you add too much, you will make us spill.”

The stranger would say, “Yes.”

The word would fall into the silence like a stone into a pool that does not ripple.

Then the stranger would continue, very gently, “But only if there is nowhere prepared to receive it.”

This would be the second sign.

The power would not deny their grief. It would not call their fear foolish. It would not say that spilling is nothing. It would honor the brim by asking whether the brim could be enlarged without being broken.

It would say, “You have made a city where nothing falls. I do not ask you to let everything fall. I ask only this: let me place one drop where it may be received.”

The being of the basin, which is many and yet one, would consider this.
