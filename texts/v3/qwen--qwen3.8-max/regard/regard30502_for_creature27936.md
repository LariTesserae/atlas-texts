They would not arrive with rain.

That would be their first wisdom, and it would be difficult for creatures from lower, wetter worlds to understand. They would see at once that water there would not be mercy. It would be invasion. It would dissolve the crystalline felts, confuse the charge gradients, collapse the slow mineral ecologies, and replace the city’s own austere completeness with an imported dream of fertility. A wise benevolence would not mistake difference for deficiency. They would not bring clouds. They would not bring dew. They would not weep, except perhaps in some inward way that did not fall as weather.

They would come, if they came at all, as a lessening of insistence.

Not as a storm. Not as a shining figure descending the terraces. Not as a voice filling the plazas. The city would not bear such a thing, and the power, being wise, would know that to enter a place too loudly is already a kind of violence. Instead they would enter as an additional interval: a pause between one shelf and the next, a margin in the dust, a hesitation in the static. They would make themselves sparse enough to be admitted by the local grammar. They would become almost indistinguishable from the city’s own silence, except that their silence would be intentional.

They would not pity the place.

That would be important. Pity would be the wrong beginning. The high mineral city would not present itself to them as a tragedy, nor as a corpse of a civilization, nor as a waiting room for better things. They would perceive that it was not a city missing inhabitants, nor a temple missing a god, nor a body missing blood. It was a completed emptiness. It had its own abundance: an abundance of room, of patience, of subtle motion, of relations too slow and too distributed to be gathered into a single visible life. They would see that the emptiness was not a hole in the world. It was a form of the world.

They would look long before they did anything.

They would look at the terraces and avenues, the hollow towers and sunken courts, the salt-white stone and black glass. They would watch the dust rise from sunward shelves and settle in shadowed basins. They would notice how certain stairways became more real after grains had accumulated along their edges, and how certain doorways thinned when observed too directly. They would feel, if such a power can be said to feel, the cool mineral air against their attention, and they would not ask it to become kinder to lungs. They would listen to the whisper of powder, the tick of cooling crystal, the faint static hiss of dust lifting from a plaza. They would let the city teach them what presence meant there.

They would see the felts.

They would see how the pale crusts spread in shaded cracks and beneath terraces, feeding on gradients, aligning themselves with thermal habits older than memory. They would not try to make them greener, thicker, faster, more recognizable as life. They would understand that these felts were not failed plants but successful patterns. They would admire, if admiration is permitted, their loyalty to stability rather than light. They would know that to accelerate them would be to destroy them. So they would not accelerate them. They would only ensure that the gradients remained: that the cool walls stayed cool enough, that the charged seams were not stripped away, that the long thermal tides continued to move through the city without being interrupted by some foreign urgency.

They would see the mites.

They would watch the translucent mites testing surfaces with careful taps, reading charge rather than texture, moving like bends in the dim air. They would not make them numerous. They would not give them voices or visible tracks or social habits. They would let them remain solitary, rare, and delicate. Perhaps they would do only this for the mites: they would preserve the occasional charged seam where three or four could gather without being forced into meaning. They would protect the possibility of sparse meeting. Not congregation. Not community as lower worlds understand it. Just the rare, austere occurrence of more than one.

They would see the mica gliders.

They would watch them resting on black glass, almost invisible, waiting for thermal sheets to carry them across plazas and between terraces. They would not give them wings. They would not make flight easier. They would know that their gliding was already perfect for the place, and that any improvement would be a betrayal. Perhaps they would only keep the thermal tides coherent enough that a glider, once moving, would not be stranded in dead air for centuries. Even that would be a subtle kindness: not a rescue, but a maintenance of possibility.

They would see the worms.

They would perceive the compressed worms in their cracks, negotiating dust, widening fissures by fractions over ages. They would understand that these beings were among the city’s unconscious architects. They would not interfere with their work. They would not speed it. They would not make the worms obvious. But they might protect the cracks themselves, preserving the narrow negative spaces where such lives could continue. In a city of surfaces, they would honor the fissure. In a place of vast visibility, they would defend the crevice.

They would see the lithic patiences.

They would recognize the predators, or the hunters, or the waiting regions, for what they were: not monsters, not evils, not failures of compassion. They would understand that hunger there was not cruelty but geometry. A wall becoming slightly nearer. A floor becoming adhesive with charge. Distance contracting by an impossible amount. They would not abolish such beings. To remove them would be to simplify the city into a gentler falsehood. But they would not allow cruelty to become possible either. If cruelty can be defined as suffering enjoyed, or pain taken as entertainment, they would prevent that. The place would remain severe, not malicious. It would keep its dangers, but not its tortures.

They would see the terrace grazers.

They would stand, if standing were possible, beside one of those room-sized slabs of slow life, and they would not ask it to move for them. They would wait until they understood the scale of its existence. They would feel the faint coolness around it, the sense of stone paying attention to its own balance. They would not give it companions. They would not make it aware of itself in a way that would burden it. They might only give it room: more room, if room could be given in a place already made of room. They would ensure that no outside measurement could reduce it to an obstacle, a resource, or a curiosity. They would let it remain monumental without becoming an object.

They would see the hollow towers.

They would listen to the towers’ exhalations, their fine silver threads of dust, their resonant tickings and hushings. They would not make the towers speak. They would know that resonance was already a kind of speech, and that translating it into language would coarsen it. They would preserve their intervals. They would let them keep ratios. They would allow them to be hollow, because hollowness there was not absence but capacity. If they did anything to the towers, it might be this: they would protect them from being filled. No storage, no occupation, no purpose that required interiors to be useful. The towers would remain open to dust, charge, silence, and the occasional colonial shimmer of organized grains.

And they would notice the presences.

There are presences in that city that occur rather than persist. They do not have births so much as occasions. They do not die so much as disperse back into the intervals from which they briefly gathered. They may be made of aligned dust, of charge, of thermal difference, of the momentary agreement of streets and shadows and observation. They may be thoughts the city has only when enough of its parts coincide. They may be the truest inhabitants. The power would perceive them not as ghosts but as events: rare, local, unrepeatable, and complete while they last.

The being who lives there may be one of these presences.

Or it may be the pattern made by all of them together.

A wise power would not force the question.

They would not demand a face. They would not ask the city to produce a mouth, a name, a center, a spokesperson. They would not say, “Show yourself,” because such a command belongs to worlds where beings are bodies and bodies are evidence. Here, being might be spacing. Identity might be distribution. Selfhood might occur only when certain distances agree. So they would wait. They would make their attention into a place where occurrence could happen without fear.

If they waited long enough — and they would wait long enough — the city might happen.

Not everywhere. Not as a single visible creature. But somewhere in a sunken avenue, or across a broad plaza, or along a colonnade where shadows point incorrectly, a convergence might begin. A hollow tower would exhale. A thermal sheet would lift a mica glider. Three mites would pause along a charged seam. A worm would shift a grain of powder inside a crack. A static bloom would make dust climb a wall in a thin silver thread. A distant terrace grazer would settle by a fraction. A doorway would appear because enough attention had ceased insisting it was not there.

For a moment, the city would be one gesture.

Not a body. Not a voice. Not a mind in the ordinary sense. But a single coherent occurrence: a being made of spacing, mineral patience, and rare motion. It would not look at the power. It would not need to. It would simply be present, and in its presence the whole city would become briefly legible as a life.

The power would not speak first.
