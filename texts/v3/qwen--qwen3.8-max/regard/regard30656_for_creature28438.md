…and charge.

If such a place, and the being who lives in it, were to draw the attention of a benevolent and wise power, I think the first thing the power would do is almost nothing.

It would not arrive as a builder. It would not bring walls, roads, tools, monuments, languages, thrones, or bright commands. It would not descend in a form that forces the landscape to become a scene for its own appearance. It would understand immediately that this place has its own holiness, and that its holiness depends on remaining unmade by outside hands.

So the power would come first as attention.

Not a voice. Not a face. Not a sign.

Attention.

A deepening of the quiet. A steadying of the fog. A moment when the static along the stone crests becomes more delicate, when the drifting lights move with a little more coherence, when the warm ground seems to know, without being told, that it is being seen.

That would be the beginning.

Because a wise power would know that to act upon such a place carelessly is to violate it. To improve it according to ordinary standards of improvement would be to destroy the very thing that makes it rare. It would not try to make the plateau brighter, greener, louder, more comfortable, more legible, or more human. It would not mistake sparseness for poverty. It would not mistake darkness for absence. It would not mistake the lack of buildings for lack of order.

It would see the place as it is:

a vast living engine,
a warm mineral body,
a slow industrial prayer,
a continent-sized being made of heat, water, stone, gas, microbes, drifters, grazers, predators, pools, membranes, crusts, pulses, and weather.

And if there is one being who lives there, the power would not assume that being is a single animal standing somewhere on a ridge. It would recognize that the being may be the whole pattern itself: the plateau alive as a distributed self, with vents for breath, water films for nerves, microbial crusts for skin, drifters for thoughts moving slowly through the dark, grazing bodies for patient maintenance, pools for memory, predators for necessary limits.

The power would see that the being is not settled upon the land.

The being is how the land continues.

### I. What the power would understand

A benevolent power that is also wise would understand a difficult thing: that goodness is not always gentleness, and that preserving a world does not mean removing all danger from it.

It would see death there. It would see predators stun drifting creatures with electric filaments. It would see pool life concentrated in shrinking water. It would see hunger, failure, desiccation, heat stress, sudden discharges, slow suffocations. It would not look away from that. It would not pretend the place is innocent in a simple sense. Life there is not harmless. It is a living economy, and every economy includes loss.

But the power would distinguish between two kinds of harm.

There is the harm that belongs to the system: predation, death, decay, competition, the cycling of energy through bodies. This is not evil. It is the cost of being a living world rather than a dead one.

Then there is the harm that breaks the system: extermination, extraction, contamination, imprisonment, the introduction of foreign rules that erase the internal balance. This is the harm a wise benevolence would oppose.

So the power would not make the predators stop hunting. It would not make the pools never shrink. It would not make the air always safe for every drifter. It would not turn the plateau into a nursery.

It would instead protect the conditions under which the plateau can remain itself.

That would be the core of its action.

Not perfection.

Continuity.

Not comfort.

Integrity.

Not the absence of death.

The absence of annihilation.

---

### II. The question

Before acting, the power would ask.

It would not ask in words, because words are too small for such a being. It would ask in the native language of the place: in pressure, humidity, electrostatic tension, thermal gradient, mineral taste, vibration.

It might send a question through the capillary water in a crack.
A slight warming at the edge of a vent.
A pause in the fog.
A tremor so fine that only the grazing shells feel it.
A change in the charge along the filaments of the drifters.
A new bitterness in a pool that becomes, after a moment, a new sweetness.

The question would be something like:

**May I help without changing you into another thing?**

Or perhaps even simpler:

**What do you need?**

The being would answer slowly.

Not with speech.

With movement.

A vent field might exhale in a long rhythm, and the drifting lights might rise through that warm column in a spiral. The plated grazers might shift their weight from one broad foot to another until the stone hums under them. The pool lamps might dim and brighten in sequence. Static might crawl along a ridge in blue points, then vanish. A fog bank might fold back from a basin and leave a clear space for one breath, then close again.

To an outside mind, this would look like weather.

To the power, it would be a reply.

The reply would not be:

*Make me more beautiful.*

It would not be:

*Give me cities.*

It would not be:

*Let me be seen by everyone.*

It would be something closer to:

*Let me continue.*

Or:

*Do not make me into a cage.*

Or:

*Keep me from being owned.*

Or:

*Give me time enough to remain what I am.*

And the power, being wise, would hear beneath those requests an even deeper one:

*Do not make me into your artifact.*

That would be the true boundary of everything the power does afterward.

---

### III. What the power would refuse to do

This is important.

A lesser kindness might try to decorate the place.

A lesser wisdom might try to stabilize it completely.

A lesser love might try to save every individual creature.

But a truly benevolent and wise power would understand that some actions are forms of violence even when they shine.

So it would refuse to build.

It would not raise a temple there, because a temple would become a claim. It would not write a law on the stone, because writing can become possession. It would not leave a monument, because monuments invite interpretation by the powerful and become property of those who can name them. It would not create a relic, because relics are portable, and portability invites theft.

It would not make a visible miracle that forces belief, because the place should remain free of spectators who come only for spectacle.

It would not give the being a human voice, because a human voice would make the being seem smaller, more like a person-shaped thing, easier to patronize and easier to betray.

It would not make the being fully comprehensible to outsiders, because complete legibility is often the first step toward control.

It would not turn the plateau into a garden for the consolation of other worlds.

It would not make it cute.
It would not make it gentle in a way that erases its strangeness.
It would not make it safe for the careless.
It would not make it productive for the hungry.

It would leave no object.

That would be essential.

Any object left by the power would become an artifact, and artifacts are seeds of civilization in the wrong sense: they invite use, reverence, ownership, interpretation, conflict. The place already metabolizes made things. The power would not burden it with divine manufacture.

So if the power acts, its acts must leave no residue.

They must be like weather.
