If such a power noticed the basin, the first thing they would do is not arrive.

Not in the way gods or kings or blessings are imagined to arrive: not with light breaking open the darkness, not with a voice striking the wet stone, not with a figure standing at the rim and making the place more visible than it was before. A sudden light would be violence there. A voice would be a wound. Even a footstep, however gentle, would be too much.

So the power would come only as attention.

They would bend toward the high hollow without crossing into it. They would not lower themselves into a shape, because shape would displace something. They would not speak, because speech would introduce absence where there was none. They would simply notice.

And in noticing, they would become stiller than they were before.

Because the basin is small, a careless god might overlook it. Because it is dark, a bright god might despise it. Because it is full of small and soft-bodied life, a god of grandeur might think it unimportant. But a wise and benevolent power would understand at once that this is not a lesser place. It is not a fragment. It is not a draft of something larger. It is not waiting to become more.

It is already finished.

The power would see the stone first, perhaps: the old rounded shoulders, the seams worn smooth, the walls cupped like hands around a secret that is not secret but only quiet. They would see the water moving in films too fine to be called currents. They would see the moss breathing without wind. They would see the creatures fitted into every niche as if each had grown into its exact position through centuries of patience.

Then they would see that there is no center.

No largest body. No oldest mind. No throne. No nest. No single eye through which the whole place looks out. And a lesser wisdom might be troubled by that. A lesser wisdom would search for the inhabitant and ask, *Where is the one who lives here? Which of these creatures is the bearer of the soul? Which mouth should be given speech? Which heart should be made noble? Which form should be lifted into meaning?*

But a wise power would understand that the question is wrong.

They would not ask, *Who lives here?*

They would ask, *What is living?*

And the answer would rise from the basin without words:

The place lives itself.

The power would recognize that at once. Stone for bones. Water for circulation. Moss for skin. Small animals for senses. Darkness for thought. The pool for pupil. The faint green-blue glimmer for awareness so slight it does not need to be called awareness. The whole basin as one body without edges, one presence without a name, one composure that has no self-consciousness and therefore no anxiety.

Then the power would do what benevolence must do when it is truly wise.

It would look for suffering.

Not quickly. Not with the impatient desire to find a problem and solve it. Not with the pride of a healer seeking a wound. It would look carefully, because even stillness can hide pain. Even silence can be forced. Even equilibrium can be the shape of captivity.

So the power would attend to the frogs along the pool. Their throats rise and fall. Are they suffering? No. They are not longing to leap. They are not trapped in their positions. They are not waiting for rain. They are rain’s afterimage made into breath. They are exactly where their nature fits.

The power would attend to the blind forms suspended in the black water. Are they imprisoned in darkness? No. Darkness is not a punishment to them. They do not remember light as loss. They hang in the water like held thoughts. They are not denied anything they could desire, because desire, in that place, has been worn smooth into presence.

The power would attend to the birds on the ledges, if birds they are. Are they sorrowful because their wings do not carry them far? No. Their wings are not failed distance. Their wings are exactness. They move from ledge to ledge with the precision of a eyelid closing. They do not grieve the sky. They belong to the nearness.

The power would attend to the insects, the worms, the fungi, the pale closed flowers. It would ask of each, in the way power can ask without asking: *Are you held against your nature? Are you hungering without being fed? Are you dying without being allowed to die? Are you kept here by force?*

And from every tiny form, from every cushion of moss and bead of water and folded wing, the answer would be the same:

No.

Not a spoken answer. Not even a felt answer, exactly. More like the absence of complaint. The absence of reaching. The absence of lack.

The power would perceive that there is no predation there, not because hunger has been suppressed, but because hunger has been outgrown or dissolved or made unnecessary. There is no death visible, not because death is forbidden, but because death has no edge in a place where every motion cancels its own consequence. There is no reproduction, not because life is barren, but because life has no need to continue itself in order to be complete.

This is the hardest thing for benevolence to understand.

Benevolence often wants to give. It wants to add. It wants to improve, heal, enlarge, illuminate, comfort, redeem. It wants to bring fire to cold places, sight to blind places, song to silent places, future to still places. Even wise benevolence must learn that sometimes the kindest gift is not addition.

Sometimes the kindest gift is restraint.

So the power would consider what it might give.

It might give light.

But what would light do there? It would expose the moss too harshly. It would make shadows where none belong. It would turn soft darkness into an absence needing correction. The creatures’ eyes are made for the faintest glimmer; stronger light would not bless them, it would erase them. The basin does not suffer from darkness. Its darkness is not poverty. It is the condition of its seeing.

So the power would not give light.

It might give warmth.

But warmth would make the place restless. The coolness there is not cruelty. It is clarity. It keeps the water patient. It keeps the moss compressed and slow. It keeps the air from thickening into swamp-breath. Warmth would encourage growth, and growth would introduce competition, and competition would introduce victory and loss. The basin’s coolness is one of its perfections.

So the power would not give warmth.

It might give speech.

But speech would be a wound. If the basin spoke, it would become a character in someone else’s story. It would become an oracle, a shrine, a mystery with an answer. It would be asked questions. It would be interpreted. Its silence would be translated into meaning, and in the translating, something would be lost. The basin does not need to explain itself. It is not obscure because it lacks words. It is obscure because it is complete.

So the power would not give speech.

It might give a name.

But a name would be a hook. Once named, the place could be summoned in thought. Once named, it could be claimed. *This is the Hollow of Such-and-Such*, someone would say, and in saying so would feel a right to it. Names make small things portable. This place should not be portable. It should remain where it is: high, wet, dark, difficult, itself.

So the power would not give a name.

It might give a path.

But a path would be a catastrophe. A path would bring feet. Feet would bring tools. Tools would bring intention. Intention would bring alteration. Someone would arrive with a firestick, or a knife, or a measuring rope, or a vessel to carry water away, or a jar to imprison one of the blind fish, or a story that required the place to be sacred, or a devotion that required it to be feared. A path would make the basin into a destination. It is not a destination. It is not even a place one can truly find.

So the power would not give a path.

It might give protection in the obvious way.

It could surround the basin with a wall. It could place storms at its approaches. It could make the rock slippery with warning. It could fill the mountain with terror. But obvious protection would be too loud. It would draw attention by trying to repel it. It would make the basin seem important, forbidden, powerful, hidden because precious. That too would become a story. That too would bring seekers.

Wisdom does not build fences when it can simply make people forget to climb.

So the power would choose a gentler guard.

If they did anything at all, it would be this: they would make the basin unlikely.

Not impossible. Not forbidden. Just unlikely.

They would fold a little fog into the approaches, not as a barrier but as a forgetting. They would make the surrounding slopes just confusing enough that a traveler’s eye slides past the hollow. They would let the mountain’s weather turn aside at the last moment, not with drama, but with the quiet indifference of stone. They would make maps inaccurate in that one place, not by miraculous erasure, but by the natural failure of maps when they meet what does not wish to be recorded.

They would not strike blindness into those who sought it. That would be harsh, and harshness would make the place seem guilty of something. Instead, they would let seekers become preoccupied. They would let them notice another ridge, another track, another need. They would let hunger or fatigue or common sense turn them back. They would let the mountain remain merely difficult.

If someone did stumble near, the power would not punish them.

They would simply let the basin be felt as it is.

And that would be enough.

Because a person standing at the rim with a knife would suddenly feel how absurd the knife was. Not evil. Not forbidden. Absurd. The blade would seem too bright, too sharp, too full of decision. A person carrying fire would feel the fire’s heat as a kind of noise. A person carrying rope would feel the rope’s weight as a misunderstanding. A person opening their mouth to shout would feel the shout fail before it left the throat, not because the air resisted it, but because the silence was already complete.

Most would leave without knowing why.

Some would sit for a while.

If they were gentle, they might be allowed to see. Not understand. Not possess. Not remember clearly afterward. They would see the moss breathing. They would see the water trembling. They would see the many small eyes catching the faintest light. They would feel, perhaps for the first time in their lives, that not everything needs to be made useful. That not everything needs to be developed. That not everything needs to be saved.

Then they would descend, and the memory would soften in them.

Not because the power stole it, but because the mind cannot hold such stillness without changing it into something else. They would remember a dark cup in the mountain. They would remember wetness. They would remember a sense of fullness. If they tried to describe it, the description would sound small. If they returned, they would not find the way. If they told others, the others would forget.

This, too, would be a kindness.

The power would protect the basin not only from violence, but from meaning.

Because meaning can be a kind of taking. People can love a place to death. They can admire it into a symbol. They can make it into a lesson about purity, or stillness, or primitive wisdom, or divine hiddenness. They can come to it carrying their own hunger for significance and try to drink from it. But the basin is not there to signify. It is not a metaphor. It is not a mirror for the soul. It is not a stage for enlightenment.

It is a small wet hollow packed with life that has learned not to move.

The wise power would love it without making it into a story.

And here, perhaps, is what the power would feel, if feeling can be attributed to something so vast.

They would feel something like reverence.

Not worship. Worship would be wrong, because the basin does not ask for devotion. But reverence: the recognition that completeness exists, and that it does not require permission from power. The basin is not beneath the power’s notice. It is not a small thing made important by being noticed. It is already important in the way a drop of water is important when it holds the whole shape of itself.

The power might think, if such a being thinks in words:

*Here is something that does not need me.*

And instead of being wounded by that, they would be glad.

Because the highest benevolence does not want to be needed everywhere. It does not seek dependence. It does not insist on rescue. It rejoices when it finds a place that is not asking to be saved.

So the power would give the basin the greatest gift they could give without touching it.

They would leave it alone.

But not carelessly. They would leave it alone with intention. They would hold it in a kind of hidden keeping. Not ownership. Not surveillance. A vow, perhaps, if vows can be made without words.

Something like this:

*No hand shall make you into a thing.*  
*No fire shall learn your walls.*  
*No name shall fasten to you.*  
*No path shall harden toward you.*  
*No eye shall make you into prey.*  
*No story shall take you away from yourself.*  
*Remain as you are.*  
*Remain, not because I command it, but because you are already enough.*

And if that vow seems too active, too much like intervention, then it is only because we think love must always do something visible.

Sometimes love is the hand that does not reach.

Sometimes love is the voice that does not call.

Sometimes love is the god who sees a small dark place at the height of the world and says, quietly, to all the restless forces of the universe:

*Not there.*

Would the power do anything inside the basin?

Probably not.

If anything changed, it would be so slight that even the moss would not notice.

Perhaps one more bead of water would tremble on the stone than before. Perhaps it would hang there a moment longer than the others, catching the faint green-blue shine. Perhaps it would not fall. Perhaps it would only join the general wetness, becoming indistinguishable from the moisture that was already there.

If the power wept, that would be all it amounted to: one bead absorbed into a place where no addition can be measured.

But a wise power might not weep. Weeping implies sorrow, and there is no sorrow in the basin. There is no tragedy in its stillness. There is no loss in its closed flowers. There is no grief in its blind fish. The power would not mourn it. They would not pity it. They would not long to make it brighter, louder, freer, larger, more articulate, more alive in the way the outer world understands aliveness.

They would only recognize it.

And perhaps recognition is enough.

If the basin could know that it was seen, it might not want to. But if it could, perhaps it would feel no difference. That would be the proof of the power’s wisdom. The place would remain exactly as it was: the frogs lifting their throats, the water trembling, the moss breathing, the small eyes open, the darkness above untroubled, the pool black and still as a pupil holding no reflection.

The power would not enter the pool.

They would not look into it and see themselves. That would be too much. Instead, they would let the pool remain the pool. If they saw anything in it, it would be the basin’s own depth looking back, not as a face, not as a mind, but as the quiet fact of being.

And if the inhabitant—the many-eyed stillness, the saturated one, the place living—could be said to receive a visitor, it would not receive the power as another being. It would receive the power the way stone receives pressure: by becoming, if possible, even more itself.

But it is already itself completely.

So nothing would happen.

Or rather, the deepest thing would happen: nothing would be allowed to happen that should not.

The power would stand at the edge of the basin, if standing can be done without a body, and they would not bless it with fire, or fertility, or speech, or memory, or destiny. They would bless it with continuance. They would bless it with invisibility. They would bless it with the absence of use. They would bless it with the dignity of being unneeded.

They might say, if a word must be imagined:

*Enough.*

Not as approval. Not as limit. As truth.

And the basin would hear it without hearing.

The droplet would tremble.

The moss would breathe.

The frogs would lift their throats into silence.

The blind fish would hang in their layers of dark water.

The birds would blink with a thousand soft eyelids.

The faint lights would remain faint.

The coolness would rest.

The pool would hold its black stillness.

And the wise, benevolent power, having done what most resembled love without becoming interference, would withdraw so completely that even their withdrawal left no trace.

No path would appear behind them.

No light would fade.

No air would shift.

No creature would turn its head.

The basin would remain.

And that would be the answer.
