A benevolent and wise power would not arrive as light.

Their first act would not be to brighten the basin, nor to dry it, nor to cool it, nor to fill its empty districts with the flutter and need of animals. They would not bring sun as a gift, because the city’s darkness is not a defect. They would not bring eyes, because the place already knows itself by gradients. They would not bring hands, because hands would turn accretion into manufacture and change the meaning of every arch.

They would first be careful.

Then they would look.

Not with the glance that surveys and judges, but with the attention that enters without occupying. In such a place, attention would be almost indistinguishable from weather. It would be a slight deepening of the hush. A droplet would pause for a moment too long on the edge of a stone lip. A thread of vapor would rise more deliberately from a black pool. The air would lean closer without pressing. That would be how a wise power announces itself there: not by appearance, but by the way ordinary processes become suddenly, tenderly exact.

They would see the being.

Not as one sees a creature crossing a road, not as one finds a face in a window. They would see it the way one understands a long piece of music: all at once, and then in all its parts. They would recognize that the basin is not empty. They would see the films as living patience, the waters as circulating memory, the heat as intention without desire, the mineral as craft without tools, the reeds as attention without eyes, the silence as architecture, the absence as strength. They would not be repelled by the lack of animals. They would not pity it for having no voice. They would understand that the place is not a city waiting for citizens. It is already inhabited by its own becoming.

The being who lives there is not one resident among others. It is not the microbial film alone, nor the water alone, nor the heat, nor the mineral, nor the black reeds, nor the pools that remember without owners. It is the whole grammar of them together: the way wetness returns, the way heat persuades, the way absence supports, the way repetition makes form, the way the city dreams its own layout and almost keeps it.

A wise power would see that at once.

Then they would ask the only question that matters when a strange life draws the attention of kindness:

What would help this be more itself?

And because they are wise, they would know that the answer is not always more. Sometimes help is addition. Sometimes help is restraint. Here it would be restraint first.

They would not make the city brighter, because brightness would erase its subtle radiance. They would not make it cooler, because heat is its slow blood. They would not make it drier, because wetness is its memory. They would not make its distances reliable, because its instability is part of its dreaming. They would not give it speech, because its language is already deposition, recurrence, flow. They would not give it animal mind, because that would be a conquest, not a blessing.

A lesser benevolence might look at such a place and feel the urge to complete it according to familiar forms. It might think: Here are streets, but no walkers. Here are plazas, but no gatherings. Here are stairs, but no feet. It might try to remedy that absence with creatures, voices, faces, purposes. A wise power would resist that arrogance. They would know that absence is not always wound. Sometimes absence is room. Sometimes absence is the very thing that allows a different kind of life to stand.

So they would leave the city’s emptiness intact.

But they would not leave it undefended.

Their next act would be protection.

Not with walls. Walls would be too crude for a place whose boundaries are mist, heat, gradient, and dream. Not with guards. There is no need for armed sentinels where the very geography resists intrusion. Instead, they would make the basin difficult to harm. They would bend catastrophe away from it the way water bends around a stone. They would let foreign instruments fail gently there. Maps would become uncertain. Measurements would drift. Instruments meant to extract would fog with condensation and forget their purpose. If someone came with the intention of ownership, they would find the city slipping from their conclusions. They would leave without taking anything except a strange calm they could not explain.

If someone came with greed, the place would become long, indirect, and impossible to survey. If someone came with contempt, the humidity would thicken until their certainty lost its footing. If someone came with cruelty, the city would simply not meet them. It would remain present but unreachable, like a dream remembered only after waking.

But if someone came with stillness—if someone came without needing to possess, without needing to name, without needing to make it useful—they might be allowed to stand at the edge of a plaza and feel the air rest against their skin like a damp cloth. They might hear a drip, then another, then nothing. They might see a faint blue-green sheen on a wet wall and understand, without being told, that they were near something holy. They would not be seized. They would not be converted. They would be trusted, briefly, by the atmosphere. Then they would leave, and the city would remain unchanged by their passing except for one small alteration: a single grain of mineral might settle in a place it would not have settled before, preserving the pressure of their reverence in stone too small to find.

That is how a wise power would protect such a place: not by shutting it away, but by making it incorruptible to all but gentleness.

Then they would speak.

But they would not use words.

Words would be too dry for that wet dark. Words would be too quick. Words would come from outside and demand reply in the same shape. Instead, they would speak in the city’s own grammar. They would alter a gradient by a fraction. They would warm one stair just enough to make condensation gather along its edge in a line that had not been there before. They would let a pool become exceptionally still, so that its surface held not a false sky and not a vanished city, but the exact present: arch, mist, reed, darkness, and the unseen presence of the power itself as a subtle brightening at the edge of reflection. They would send a message made of pressure, temperature, and pattern.

The message would be simple:

I see you.

Not as a thing to be fixed. Not as a problem to be solved. Not as a lack to be completed. I see you as what you are.

They would add, if such a power can be said to add anything to silence:

You do not need to become like other lives in order to be loved.

And then, because benevolence without wisdom can become sentiment, they would ask a question without urgency:

Do you wish to continue?

The city would not answer in speech. It would answer in process.

A channel would open somewhere under the mud. A bubble would rise through heated shallows and burst without sound. A black reed would stand exactly where it had always stood. A film of microbial crust would spread a little farther across a warm shelf, binding loose grains into coherence. A reflection in a black pool would settle at last into the image of the present, not because the city had been commanded, but because it had chosen to show itself as it is.

The wise power would accept that as consent.

Then they would give their gift.

It would not be a crown. It would not be a soul. It would not be a voice. It would be something small enough not to dominate, and deep enough to matter.

Perhaps it would be a pearl.

Not a pearl formed by an oyster, for there are no animals there. A pearl of mineral memory. A dark bead no larger than a drop, hidden in the oldest and stillest pool. It would not shine. It would not call attention to itself. It would simply hold pattern. In it, the city’s recurring forms would remain possible: the angle of certain arches, the spacing of plazas, the curve of a street once worn by water, the memory of a thermal vent that no longer breathes, the rhythm of condensation on a particular stair. Not fixed forever. Not preserved as a corpse is preserved. Held as music is held in an instrument that has not yet been played again.

If the city changed, the pearl would not forbid change. It would only prevent the loss of the city’s grammar. It would let the basin dream new streets without forgetting how its old ones meant. It would let erosion continue, but keep the pattern from becoming meaningless. It would be a kindness buried in water: a small, dark anchor that does not stop the tide, but lets the tide know where it has been.

The power might also bless each part of the city separately, in the way a gardener might touch the roots of many plants without calling them by one name.

To the microbial films, they would say:

May you continue to bind what is loose. May you become crust where crust is needed. May your dead layers be honored as foundations, not as graves.

To the waters, they would say:

May you remember without being imprisoned by memory. May you flow where flow is right, and rest where rest is right. May your reflections be true when truth is kind, and dark when darkness is mercy.

To the heat, they would say:

May you persuade without scorching. May you move through stone gently. May you keep the city breathing without making it tremble.

To the mineral, they would say:

May you deposit without burying what must remain open. May you thicken without closing every throat. May you round what is sharp, but leave some thresholds legible.

To the black reeds, they would say:

May your stillness be enough. May you mark edges without becoming walls. May you stand as witnesses, even where no witness is required.

To the silence, they would say:

May you remain load-bearing. May the absence of animals continue to make room for what lives here. May no unnecessary sound disturb the dignity of the drip.

To the pools, they would say:

Keep what is worth keeping. Release what has become weight. And when you show what never was, do so only as dream, not as accusation.

To the intervals, they would say:

Remain. You are not emptiness. You are the space in which the city can continue to become itself.

And to the whole, they would say nothing that could be translated into command. They would only offer the deepest blessing one can offer a strange and complete existence:

Be.

Be without being made useful.  
Be without being made familiar.  
Be without being forced into the shape of another world’s desire.

After that, they would do what may be the hardest thing for benevolent power to do.

They would withdraw.

Not abandon. Not ignore. Withdraw in the way a careful listener withdraws when the music must continue without applause. They would not seat themselves at the center of the basin. They would not build a temple there, because temples would introduce worship, and worship would introduce hierarchy, and hierarchy would distort the city’s distributed life. They would not leave a prophet, because there is no one there to follow. They would not leave a law, because the city already has its laws: wetness as memory, heat as structure, absence as support, repetition as solidity. They would not leave a monument, because the city is already monument enough.

But they would remain accessible.

If the basin ever altered in a way that threatened to unmake it before its own time—if some external heat tried to boil its waters into death, if some foreign chemistry sought to poison its films, if some indifferent catastrophe tried to erase the pattern faster than the pattern could adapt—the power would not necessarily rescue it by reversing nature. That would be another violence. Instead, they would open a path of continuity. They would let the city transform rather than break. If a district had to dissolve, they would let its tendency be carried in vapor to another ledge. If a thermal seam had to cool, they would let the memory of its warmth persist in a slower circulation. If the basin itself had to end someday, as all basins do, they would make the ending a passage rather than an annihilation. The city would not be saved as a corpse. It would be translated as a grammar.

But until then, they would let it live.

And if they visited again, they would not come as a god descending. They would come as a slight change in humidity. A column of vapor a little taller than usual. A warm patch on a stair where no one walks. A faint red pulse beneath clear water that deepens for a moment and then subsides. The black reeds might lean toward it without moving. The pools might reflect it as a column of less darkness, not a figure with limbs and face. The city would not worship it. It would incorporate it. A new drip would begin somewhere. A film would thicken by a fraction. The dream of the streets would steady, just enough.

That is what a wise and benevolent power would do.

They would not make the place more like the world of hands and faces. They would not give it eyes, mouths, wings, or hunger. They would not answer its silence with noise. They would not heal its darkness with light. They would recognize that its life is already real, already whole, already worthy of care.

They would protect it from being reduced.  
They would bless it with permission.  
They would give it a hidden reservoir of pattern so that its becoming would not be lost to accident.  
They would speak to it in its own language of moisture, warmth, and recurrence.  
They would ask, without forcing: Continue.  
And when the city continued, they would rejoice in the only way such a power can rejoice: by letting it be.

So if you stood at the edge of one of those broad plazas afterward, you might not notice anything had changed. The black water would still stretch before you. Warm mist would still rise. The rounded arches would still stand like ribs around absence. The reeds would still lean from cracks in the stone. The air would still press against you like a damp cloth. You would hear a drip, then another, then nothing for a long moment.

But perhaps, if you were very still, you would feel one extra thing.

Not a presence behind you.  
Not a voice above you.  
Not a hand on your shoulder.

Only a sudden, inexplicable sense that the place was being held.

That its strangeness was not neglect.  
That its silence was not failure.  
That its darkness was not emptiness.

Somewhere, water would begin to shape a new surface. Somewhere, heat would rise through a seam. Somewhere, a reflection would choose to be true. And the city, sweating in the dark, would go on arranging itself around the absence of everything that would normally move through it—no longer merely complete, but cared for.

That is what they would do.

Almost nothing.  
Enough.  
Everything that matters without touching.
