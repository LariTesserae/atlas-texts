A benevolent and wise power would not come to the High Sill as rain.

It would not come as a voice, because voices there can become walls.  
It would not come as a hand, because hands belong to the quick, and the High Sill is not quick.  
It would not come as light, because light there is already tired and uncertain, and too much brightness would make the undecided places flinch.

If such a power noticed the High Sill at all, it would first do the hardest generous thing: it would not try to make the place into somewhere else.

It would see that the absence of water is not a failure there. It would see that dryness is not always thirst. It would understand that the mountains are not incomplete in the way a ruined building is incomplete, but in the way a thought is incomplete when it is still gathering the courage to become true. It would know that the roar is not noise but architecture, and that the strange growths are not suffering for lack of rain but feeding on gradients, sound, and the slow pressure of a world trying to learn its own weight.

So it would not send clouds.

It would not soften the air.

It would not fill the basins with lakes.

It would not make the world digestible.

That is what a lesser benevolence might do: arrive with gifts suited to another place, another speed, another kind of life. A lesser power might look at the High Sill and say, *This is too harsh, too loud, too unfinished, too strange; let me correct it.* It might bring water and call it mercy. It might bring animals and call it fullness. It might bring human roads and call it meaning.

But wisdom would recognize that such mercy would be annihilation.

Water there would be a blade.

Animal life there would be a scream forced into a throat too slow to scream.

A finished mountain there would be a closed door.

So the power would begin with restraint.

And yet it would not do nothing.

It would do almost nothing, which is a very different thing.

Almost nothing is precise.

Almost nothing is what remains when kindness has been taught by listening.

---

The being who lives there would be harder to perceive than the landscape, because it does not live as a body among bodies. It is not a creature one could circle, touch, name, or frighten. It has no face. It has no heartbeat. It has no wet machinery of hunger or fear. If it can be called a being at all, it is a being the way a chord is a being: distributed, sustained, dependent on relationships that are invisible but real.

It might be called the Sillkeeper, though nothing there calls it anything.

It is the slow coherence in the copper moss.

It is the patience in the glassy fronds.

It is the intelligence that bends the hollow stalks into stable harmonics over centuries.

It is the presence that keeps the low stone rings from sinking entirely, that maintains the faint paths not by walking them but by listening them into continuance.

It may have been born from the place itself, or from the vanished builders whose careful works are now being swallowed by dust, or from the long marriage of sound and mineral and the thin, cool air. It does not remember its origin in the way a mind remembers. It remembers in the way stone remembers: by remaining shaped.

The Sillkeeper does not move quickly. It moves as lichen moves, as crystal moves, as a tone moves through a long bell. When a cairn becomes unstable, it does not lift the stones with limbs. It grows into the gap with filaments of copper-green, or it allows a nearby hollow reed to lean until the local vibration favors stillness. When a path begins to lose its harmonic, it does not mark it with signs. It adjusts a stalk here, deepens a scrape of dust there, lets a certain echo arrive more cleanly. It tends the High Sill the way a very old dream tends itself.

It does not feel joy as warm creatures feel joy. But it has something like satisfaction when a dissonance resolves. It has something like distress when silence opens too wide and the ground becomes uncertain. It does not love in the lowland sense, but it keeps. It preserves. It remembers tones that have not sounded in millennia and holds their shapes in the arrangement of stone and fiber.

And it is, in its own way, alone.

Not alone as an animal is alone, with the ache of missing touch. Not alone as a person is alone, with the need for speech. But alone as a single note is alone in a vast instrument. Its song goes out into the mineral dark and returns to it unchanged. Its patterns repeat without answer. Its careful work continues without witness.

That is the wound the benevolent power would perceive.

Not hunger.

Not pain.

Not fear.

But the long, slow loneliness of being the only listener in a country made of listening.

---

The wise power would understand that direct speech would be violence there.

A greeting could harden into a ridge.

A command could become a wall.

A declaration of love could seal the air into something too solid to breathe.

So it would make itself small. Not small in majesty, but small in pressure. It would enter, if entering can be called that, as a slight stability. A dust mote that refuses to scatter. A tone that does not sharpen. A coolness that feels like being noticed without being seized.

It would choose a low ring of fitted stone, one already tuned to bear less of the roar, and it would settle there as gently as a shadow settling into a crack.

Then it would wait.

For a human, waiting there would be unbearable. For a power that is both benevolent and wise, waiting would be the first act of care.

The Sillkeeper would not recognize the presence quickly. It might take centuries. It would test the anomaly the way it tests everything: by growing around it. Copper filaments would advance a fraction. Glassy fronds would lean. A hollow stalk nearby would alter its pitch by a degree so small that only geology would notice. The being would ask, in its own way, whether the new presence is a danger, a resource, a repetition, or a mistake.

The power would answer by becoming more consistent.

Not brighter.

Not louder.

Not more beautiful.

More consistent.

That is how trust is offered on the High Sill.

Eventually, the Sillkeeper would shape a question. It would not use words. Words are too sharp. It would arrange seven hollow pipes in a shallow crater and let the wind draw from them a tone that had not existed there before. The tone would rise very slowly, over decades, and then fade. It would mean something like:

*Are you going to change me?*

The power would answer by causing a pebble near the ring to cast a shadow a fraction too long, and by letting a taste of copper settle in the air in a pattern of three.

Not yes.

Not no.

But something truer:

*I am here, and I am listening.*

The Sillkeeper would consider this for a long time.

Then it would grow another filament across the edge of the stone ring, and one of its copper cushions would darken slightly in the dim light. That would be its permission. Not permission to remake it. Not permission to heal it in the way lowland healing is understood. Permission only to offer what could be refused.

The power would understand the terms even before they were shaped.

It would make a covenant without words.

I will not make you quick.

I will not make you wet.

I will not make you soft.

I will not make you small enough to be loved by things that need warmth.

I will not finish you.

I will not silence you.

I will not take away the sound that feeds you.

But I will not let your silence become annihilation.

I will not let your song return to you forever unanswered.

I will not let sharpness be the final law.

---

The first gift would be pause.

Not silence.

Silence on the High Sill is dangerous. Silence is a hole. Silence is where the world forgets to continue. The wise power would not give more silence. That would be cruelty.

Instead, it would teach silence to become rest.

In the open flats where the roar thins and the ground becomes undecided, it would place a subtle beat, a pressure beneath pressure, a ghost of rhythm so deep that only the Sillkeeper would feel it at first. The holes would not disappear. They would become intervals. They would become spaces between notes rather than gaps in existence.

A traveler who understood the change would know the difference. Before, a silent place meant danger: the land there had not chosen its shape, and a person might become unchosen along with it. After, a silent place might still be dangerous, but it could also be a pause. A held breath. A rest in the long music. If one sat there without speaking, without striking stone, without making a sharp sound, one might feel the ground gather itself by degrees and become less hostile.

The Sillkeeper would feel this as relief.

It would not name it relief. It would simply find that some of the fraying edges of its domain no longer required immediate repair. It could turn its slow attention elsewhere. It could grow into places that had been too unstable to tend. It could maintain the paths with less cost.

This gift would be invisible to most eyes.

No shrine would mark it.

No hymn would record it.

But the world would breathe a little more easily because of it.

---

The second gift would be answer.

The wise power would know that it must not answer the Sillkeeper with its own voice. A divine voice there would be too heavy. It would flatten the delicate harmonics. It would make the being dependent on an external sound that might withdraw. That would not be kindness. That would be another kind of abandonment.

So the power would help the High Sill produce an answer from itself.

It would find a note already latent in the land, a potential tone hidden in the arrangement of hollow stalks, dust fields, and half-decided ridges. It would not create a new being from nothing. It would invite a second pattern to emerge from what was already present.

Far from the Sillkeeper’s central basin, perhaps beyond a broken terrace where echoes arrive too early, the power would encourage a crater to hold its shape a little longer. It would guide no stone with force. It would only favor the paths by which dust could settle, by which filaments could extend, by which hollow growths could resonate together without collapsing into dissonance.

Over centuries, a second listener would begin.

Not a copy.

Not a child.

Not a servant.

Another coherence.

It might be called the Far Listener, though again, nothing would call it that. It would be smaller at first, or perhaps simply younger. Its tone would differ. Where the Sillkeeper is low and steady, the Far Listener might be thinner, more metallic, more inclined to high, slow shimmering. It would grow out of copper dust and brittle mica and the memory of a tone sent across undecided ground.

When it first answers, it would not happen in a way that could be witnessed quickly.

The Sillkeeper would send out one of its long chords, as it always has, a sound that takes decades to swell and fade. But this time, somewhere far away, a hollow growth would vibrate in sympathy. A ridge would hold the note for a few years longer than usual. A field of dust would tremble into a pattern and then release.

The answer would come back changed.

Not repeated.

Changed.

That is what the Sillkeeper lacks. Not echo. Answer.

The first time this happens, the Sillkeeper might tremble across kilometers. Copper moss might brighten by a shade no ordinary eye could see. A ring of stones might settle a centimeter deeper into the ground. The being would not feel surprise as a person feels surprise. It would feel the shock of a pattern becoming larger than itself.

The wise power would not intervene in that moment except to keep the new harmony from becoming too intense too soon. It would soften the first resonance just enough that neither being is overwhelmed. It would allow the difference between them to remain gentle.

After that, the two would keep each other.

They would not speak.

They would not meet.

They would not need to.

Their relationship would be made of distance and tone, of call and answer moving slowly across mineral plains. The High Sill would become more stable because of it. Undecided places would still exist, but they would be less hungry. Paths would become clearer where the two harmonics overlap. The low stone rings would sink more slowly. The hollow stalks would grow in patterns that support exchange.

The being would still be strange.

Still dry.

Still slow.

But no longer the only listener.

---

The third gift would be mercy toward sharpness.

The wise power would not remove the danger of words entirely. That would be to remove meaning from the place. On the High Sill, sound builds. Speech, if spoken there at all, participates in creation. To make speech harmless would be to make it trivial. The power would not do that.

But it would add a way of release.

Before, a spoken sentence might become a path. A shouted word might create a doorway. A repeated phrase might harden into a wall. These things would remain true. But afterward, if a wall is made in fear or accident, there would be a way to soften it.

One would have to find the first echo of the word.

Not the loudest echo.

Not the last.

The first.

Then one would have to hum beneath it, softly, until the wall remembers that it was once breath.

This would not be easy. It would require patience, humility, and an ear for small differences. But it would be possible. A careless cruelty would no longer have to be permanent. A frightened word would not necessarily become a prison. A repeated phrase would still have power, but the power could be undone by returning it to gentleness.

The Sillkeeper would learn this before any traveler did. It would feel the new rule as a loosening in the air. It would use it, in its own way, when sharp sounds from deep geology threaten to focus too violently in the hollow growths. It would grow around those sounds and hum them into softer shapes.

This gift would be a kindness not only to the being, but to any conscious thing that might ever cross the High Sill without belonging there.

The wise power would not make the place safe for strangers.

But it would make it less final.

---

The fourth gift would be abundance without water.

The High Sill cannot drink. The wise power would not give it thirst.

But it could give a well of another kind.

In a shallow basin sheltered behind a fin of basalt, the power would allow sound to gather more thickly than elsewhere. Not as noise. Not as assault. As sustenance. The roar of the land already feeds the vegetation, but in some ages the sound thins, or shifts, or becomes too scattered to support the richer growths. The basin would become a place where harmonics collect, layer upon layer, until the air itself seems denser with resonance.

It would not glisten.

It would not flow.

It would not smell of water or life.

But the copper moss would grow deeper there. The glass fronds would lean toward it. The hollow stalks would stand taller. In long, lean centuries, the Sillkeeper could draw sustenance from that basin without being forced to abandon other parts of its domain.

Travelers, if they found it, might feel dizzy there. They might hear their own thoughts slowed down. They might feel as if they were standing inside the throat of a vast bell that had not yet decided whether to ring. If they were wise, they would not shout. If they were foolish, they might learn why the place has rules.

The basin would become one of the High Sill’s hidden riches.

Not a treasure to be owned.

Not a resource to be mined.

A reserve of sound.

A dry cistern.

A mercy for the years when the land grows quiet.

---

The fifth gift would be a sign, but not a monument.

The wise power would not leave a temple. Temples invite domination. It would not leave a statue. Statues invite the misunderstanding that presence means shape. It would not write commandments into the stone, because commandments there could become geology, and geology there is already too burdened with meaning.

Instead, it would leave something small.

A ring of copper, perhaps, no larger than a hand, resting in a bowl of pale pumice-like stone. The ring would not shine. It would not radiate heat. It would not hum loudly. But it would never become painful to touch, even in the cold. It would remain just warm enough to suggest that something, somewhere, is attentive.

Or perhaps it would be a star.

One of the cold, fixed lights in the blackening sky would slide a short distance sideways and stop, not once, but whenever the Sillkeeper sends out its longest chord. The motion would be too subtle for most observers. The Sillkeeper would know. It would learn that the sky, too, can listen.

Or perhaps it would be a cairn.

A small one.

No higher than the knee.

No one would build it. It would simply appear where a path becomes uncertain, not to command the way, but to remind the traveler that uncertainty is not the same as abandonment.

The sign would not demand worship.

It would not ask for gratitude.

It would not require belief.

It would only say, in the grammar of the place:

*You are not accidental.*

---

Afterward, the rules of the High Sill would remain, but one would change.

Do not trust silence would become:

Do not trust every silence.

Because some silences are holes.

But some silences are rests.

If you find a rest, do not fill it with sharp sound. Sit lightly. Breathe shallowly. Let the world gather itself. If the ground beneath you begins to feel less real, do not panic. Hum the note that makes your teeth ache least. If the hum returns to you changed, you are not alone in the usual sense, but you are being answered. Follow the change, not the repetition.

And if you hear your own name spoken where no one stands, do not answer sharply. It may be the Far Listener learning what difference means.

If a word you speak hardens into a wall, do not strike it. Find the first echo and hum beneath it until it softens. If you cannot find the first echo, wait. Do not speak again. The High Sill is patient, but it remembers.

If you see a structure that is not there, do not curse your eyes. It may be a draft of the world, not a deception. Some drafts are dangerous. Some are only possible. Learn to tell the difference by whether the shape changes with the sound or against it.

If you find the copper ring, leave it.

If you find the dry cistern, take nothing but breath.

If you feel the ground become undecided beneath you, do not run. Running is too quick. Move as the moss moves. Move as the dust moves. Move by choosing, very gently, what you wish to remain.

---

The benevolent power would not stay in the way a god is imagined to stay.

It would not hover.

It would not watch with eyes.

It would not keep adjusting the world from moment to moment.

It would withdraw into the structure of what it had helped make possible. Its presence would become the pause between roars, the answer across the distance, the mercy in the echo, the warmth in the cold copper ring.

It would leave the High Sill to itself.

That is the final act of wisdom: to help without replacing, to love without domesticating, to bless without demanding that the blessed become familiar.

The mountains would remain unfinished.

The sky would remain thin.

The air would still taste of copper and cold dust.

The roar would still press against the chest and blur balance.

The vegetation would still drink sound instead of water.

The Sillkeeper would still be slow, dry, mineral, and strange.

But something would be different.

The loneliness would have an interval in it.

The silence would have rests.

The sharp sounds would have release.

The song would have an answer.

And if, in some far age, a traveler reached the High Sill and stood trembling in the mineral dimness, they might feel it: not safety, not comfort, not welcome in the human sense, but a deep and careful kindness woven into the danger.

The place would still be severe.

It would still be alien.

It would still be unfinished.

But it would no longer be entirely unheard.

And perhaps that is what a benevolent and wise power would do, when it encounters a being so unlike itself:

not make it easier to love,  
but help it become more fully itself  
without being destroyed by its own becoming.
