It is being taught, perhaps, to listen without needing the sound to be meant for it alone.

If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention toward that basin, they would not arrive as arrival is usually understood. There would be no rending of air, no descent in glory, no voice filling the city from edge to edge. The place is too subtle for such obviousness, and wisdom would know that the first violence is to make a delicate thing suddenly legible. So they would come, if they came at all, as a change in patience.

The dim light would not brighten. The warm air would not grow heavier. The towers would not tremble. The only sign, for a while, might be that a droplet on a vertical surface lingered a little longer than usual before falling, or that a shadow beneath a thicket felt less like absence and more like shelter. The condensation would gather with slightly more confidence. The quiet would become, if anything, more composed.

They would begin by looking.

Not by scanning, not by judging, not by measuring the basin against some higher standard of what a city or landscape ought to be. They would look the way one looks at a sleeping animal whose breath is already a kind of speech. They would notice the sharp young terraces, the first whisper of erosion, the engineered strata beneath the ground, the way moisture moves by both climate and intention. They would see that the place is not incomplete in the sense of lacking, but incomplete in the sense of becoming. They would see that emptiness there is not deprivation. It is held capacity.

They would look for the being who lives there, and at first they might not find a single being at all.

They would find films and fibers, mineral-eating pellicles, crystalline repair-lace in hairline fractures, surfaces learning how to persist. They would find plants acting as civic organs. They would find glassy pool-creatures storing reflections, moth-analogues reading humidity with folded wings, dark gliding shapes that are less predators than senior witnesses. They would find tenders moving through the city like mobile senses. They would find the quiet persons with their large dim-light eyes, their moisture-exchanging skin, their restrained voices, their ceremonies of water. They would find towers with impossible interiors, bridges that cast lingering shadows, pools that remember alternative plans.

And they would understand that the being is plural.

It is not one resident. It is not even only the human-shaped inhabitants. It is the basin and its systems, the city and its metabolisms, the vegetation and its negotiations, the water and its memory, the tenders and their unspoken coordination, the few presences moving through so much readiness. The being lives as a chord lives. If the power were unwise, they might try to address only the persons, or only the city, or only the ecology. Being wise, they would address the whole as one would address a garden that is also a body that is also a thought.

They would ask permission.

Not in words. A power like that would know that consent in such a place cannot be demanded or broadcast. It must be read in small movements. They would place their question into the basin’s own language: a faint warmth along a condensation channel, a momentary stilling of the low civic pulse, a pool becoming so smooth that it seems to wait. The question would not be, “May I rule here?” It would be closer to, “May I touch what you are becoming?”

The basin might answer in several ways at once.

A seam in the ground could open softly, not as rupture but as breath. A pale-mouthed flower could open under a gathered droplet and let it pass downward without delay. One of the tenders could pause at a junction, turn toward an empty plaza, and remain motionless for a moment as if acknowledging a guest not yet visible. A solitary inhabitant kneeling beside a pool might see, in the water’s altered reflection, a version of the tower that includes a warmth where no structure stands. The child-sized figure among the reed-trees might exhale, and the leaves might lift not away from it but toward it.

If the answer were refusal, the benevolent power would withdraw. They would not punish the basin for wanting to remain unaccompanied. They might leave only a blessing at the edge: a slight softening of harsh futures, a membrane against careless intrusion, a memory of being seen that would not burden the seen. But in this case, I think the answer would not be refusal. The place is already so full of attendance, already so practiced in negotiation, that it might recognize benevolence without fear. Its answer would be modest. A single extra drop from a filament. A rhythm of condensation resuming after observation, but one beat slower, as if to say: yes, but gently.

Then the power would do what is hardest for benevolence: they would refrain.

They would not fill the empty plazas. They would not multiply the inhabitants until the avenues crowded. They would not make the towers blaze with visible divinity. They would not abolish the dimness, or dry the dampness, or simplify the strangeness into explanation. They would understand that the city’s low density is not failure. Its monastic spaciousness is part of its dignity. Its quiet is not loneliness. Its anomalies are not flaws asking for correction. The sharp young angles of the land are not wounds. They are decisions still fresh enough to be respected.

A lesser goodness might try to improve the basin by making it more obvious: more fertile, more populated, more radiant, more efficient. A wise power would know that some places are already good because of the precise way they hold restraint, moisture, silence, and readiness. They would not trade that for spectacle.

But they would not do nothing.

Their first act, after permission, would be to deepen the conditions that allow the basin to continue becoming itself.

They would touch the water.

Not to make it abundant beyond its nature, but to make its intermittent presence more trustworthy. The condensation would still come in cycles. The brief warm rains would still pass through and withdraw. The pools would still appear and disappear. But beneath that variability, there would be a hidden constancy. Moisture would find the reservoirs more easily. Channels would remember where to carry it. Dry spells would not become cruelty. A shallow basin where glass eels rest would not vanish too quickly. If a pool began to shrink, some filament above it might cool just enough to release a slow drip, or a tender might be drawn there by a pressure change too subtle to name. The water would remain shy, but it would not be faithless.

They would bless the droplets.

This would not mean making them holy in a way that demands reverence. It would mean giving each drop a little more coherence, a little more memory, a little more capacity to carry what the city needs. Where a bead of condensation falls on a wounded surface, it might leave behind a faint crystalline lace that strengthens the seam. Where it falls into a flower’s pale mouth, it might descend with fewer losses. Where it strikes a pool, it might stir not only rings but a brief clarification, as if the water has been reminded of its own intelligence. The inhabitants might never speak of this. They would simply find that carrying water feels slightly more significant than before, and that a vessel left overnight near a condensation vent sometimes holds more than expected.

They would stabilize the young ground without robbing it of its newness.

The basin was made recently, or at least recently unfolded, and its angles are still deciding how to settle. A wise power would not freeze those decisions. They would not erase erosion, because erosion is the land’s first conversation with time. But they would prevent catastrophic collapse. Deep within the engineered strata, in the fibrous mats and crystalline sheets and metallic lattices, they would introduce a kind of patience. Not rigidity. A material memory. If a fracture began to cascade too far, something in the layer would remember wholeness enough to distribute the stress. If a terrace threatened to shear away before its time, a filament-root or hidden lattice would find the strain and ease it. The land would still be young. It would still show seams. But the seams would be less likely to become disasters.

They would care for the vegetation without domesticating it.

The broad-leafed climbers would still ascend the towers in overlapping fans. The mosses would still thicken in shaded seams. The fernlike growths would still test the air. The reed-thin trees would still bind the sharp rock with their muscular roots. The power might make it so that when a plant chooses a surface, the surface does not resent it. Where root meets conduit, there would be fewer conflicts. Where a vine diverts water, the city’s deeper mechanisms would recognize the diversion as cooperation rather than interference. The metallic veins in leaves might carry their small currents with slightly more clarity. The luminous motes released by the dark-canopied trees would not become more numerous, but they might drift a little longer, as if the air were willing to hold them.

They would not remove predation entirely.

This is important. The place is not cruel, but it is not without events. A pale shape may dart from a pool and take a resting moth-analogue. A ripple crosses the basin. A leaf trembles. Stillness returns. A wise power would understand that such moments are part of the ecology’s exactness. To abolish them would flatten the being of the place. But they might ensure that predation does not become panic. The prey would be given quickness. The predator would not be made malicious. The event would remain singular, not traumatic. No species would be driven into absence by another. The dark gliding shapes overhead would continue to appear and vanish, but they would not become tyrants. Their observation would remain observation.

They would protect the glass eels.

Those translucent, smoky, deliberate beings seem especially vulnerable because they live in temporary pools and carry something like memory in their bodies. The power would not make every pool permanent, because permanence would change the basin’s nature. But they would ensure that the eels are not betrayed by sudden dryness. When a pool begins to evaporate, the eels already flatten into storage. The power might deepen that adaptation, making the sediment around them cooler, or drawing a moth-analogue to rest above it and shade it for a cycle, or guiding a tender to notice the place. If the water returns, the eels would unfold again. If it does not return soon, they would not suffer as conscious beings suffer. They would remain as glassy knots in the mineral dark, held in a state beyond fear until future wetness calls them back.

They would also guard the eels’ memories.

Because the pools sometimes reflect versions of towers and gardens that are not present, the basin already treats memory as fluid. A benevolent power might make sure memory does not become a flood. The eels might continue eating old images or excreting them; either way, the water would not be allowed to drown the present with the past. A person who bends over a pool and sees a different version of the city would feel curiosity more than grief. The reflection would offer perspective, not imprisonment. If a pool showed a future, it would show it as weather shows a distant cloud: possible, approaching, not chained.

They would tend the tenders.

The tenders are already mobile organs of the basin, but even organs can be overstrained. The power would not give them voices or ambitions they do not possess. They would simply make their work slightly less lonely, if loneliness is even possible for such beings. When two tenders meet and touch forelimbs, the exchange might carry a little more assurance. When a tender is damaged, its return to the wall or platform would be dignified. If it becomes a housing for moss, a support for vine, a nest for larvae, that transformation would not feel like failure. The power might make the filaments descend more gently, the integration more graceful. A damaged tender’s ceramic shell could warm once, briefly, as if thanked.

They would give the tenders subtle foresight.

Not omniscience. That would be too heavy. But a slight sensitivity to coming needs. A seam about to trap moisture might announce itself as a cool line under one tender’s limb. A climber growing too near a reflective panel might release a scent that draws pruning. A dry bed might seem, to a tender’s senses, slightly more urgent than other places. The city would still be maintained through patience and distributed attention, but fewer small needs would slip through the weave.

They would care for the persons.

This is where benevolence must be most careful. The inhabitants are few, adapted, restrained, and already deeply intertwined with the basin. They do not need to be saved from their world. They need their world to remain trustworthy. The power would not grant them dominion. They would not make them immortal in any blunt sense. They would not fill their towers with crowds or their silence with constant reassurance. They would protect what the persons already have: the dignity of solitude, the possibility of communion, the ability to participate in a living system without being crushed by it.

They would make the inhabitants’ bodies less susceptible to despair.

The warm damp air, the dim light, the slow cycles—these could weigh on a mind if left unheld. The power would not remove melancholy; it belongs there, as mist belongs. But they would prevent melancholy from becoming a sealed room. When one of the persons stands on a balcony while condensation gathers on their sleeves, the moisture might feel less like isolation and more like company. When the vein-like lines on their skin darken with humidity, the sensation might carry a faint message not of burden but of belonging. Their broad quiet faces would remain quiet. Their large eyes would still gather the amber light. But in their chests, or whatever corresponds to chests, there might be a steadier warmth: the knowledge, not articulated but embodied, that they are not accidental guests in a machine. They are part of its breathing.

They would clarify their communication without making it coercive.

The inhabitants already speak in low voices, hand movements, taps, altered condensation, reflections disturbed and read. A wise power would not replace this with telepathy or overwhelming certainty. That would destroy the beauty of their restraint. But they might make the water a little more faithful as a medium. When two inhabitants exchange patterns on a pool’s surface, the water would not distort their intentions from carelessness. When one places a leaf or stone deliberately, the sign would be more likely to reach the one for whom it is meant. Misunderstanding would not vanish—understanding must still be chosen—but cruelty born of pure misreading would be less likely. Their society would remain built around attention. It would simply be attended by attention.

They would give them dreams, but gently.

Not prophecies demanding obedience. More like maintenance dreams, garden dreams. A person sleeping in a room larger than the tower’s shell might dream of walking through channels beneath the city, finding a knot of heat and loosening it with one hand. They might wake without remembering the dream, yet feel drawn later to a terrace they rarely visit, where a small adjustment is needed. Another might dream of carrying water from basin to basin, each vessel becoming lighter rather than heavier. They might wake with the sense that their solitude is not emptiness but a room with many doors. The power would not force vocation through dreams. They would only make the basin’s needs feel less alien when they touch sleeping minds.

They would make a place within the city where grief can be set down.

This would not be a temple. It would not be marked as sacred in any loud way. Perhaps it would be only a shallow depression near a line of condensation, where a pool forms more often than elsewhere and the stone around it is warm. An inhabitant who has lost another, or who has become too heavy with unspoken thought, could come there and perform the old rite of carrying water. They could pour a small vessel into the pool and watch the ripples. The power would not promise resurrection or repair. But they would allow the pool to receive what is poured into it. The grief would not vanish. It would be translated. It might become mineral lace along a hidden seam. It might become a slight sweetening of the air after rain. It might become a moth-wing pattern that someone else will see cycles later and feel, without knowing why, less alone.

They would protect the basin from domination by any single part.

This is perhaps a wise power’s deepest work. The city has many systems: infrastructure, ecology, tenders, inhabitants, memory-bearing pools, anomaly-producing surfaces. In such a place, harm could arise not from malice but from imbalance. One system might become too dominant. The architecture might begin treating vegetation as ornament rather than partner. The tenders might optimize until they erase spontaneity
