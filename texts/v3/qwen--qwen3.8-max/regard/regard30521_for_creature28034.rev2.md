They would not arrive as an event.

They would arrive as a change in the quality of events.

A basin so newly settled, so quiet, so full of negotiated becoming would not bear a descending glory without being bruised by it. A wise power would know that the first violence to avoid is the violence of obviousness. So they would not step into the place as a figure crowned, burning, proclaimed. They would lower their own weight. They would make themselves marginal. They would enter the way warmth enters stone: slowly, without announcement, until the stone realizes it has been held.

The first thing they would do is notice.

Not glance. Not categorize. Not assess from a height.

They would attend.

They would let the basin teach them how to be present to it. They would listen through the filaments. They would read the behavior of condensation. They would follow the slow signals of roots and moss. They would understand that the city’s speech is not made of declarations but of timings: when a panel cools, when a channel opens, when a leaf turns, when a drop chooses to fall.

And they would see the being who lives there.

If by “the being” one means a single resident, then they would see that resident with a tenderness that does not burden. They would not make the being a prophet, not make them a sign, not lift them above the quiet order of the basin. They would simply let that one feel, at rare and necessary moments, that their solitude is not abandonment. A path would become slightly more certain under their feet. A pool would offer a reflection just kind enough. A wall would hold warmth where they leaned. They would not be given answers. They would be spared some unnecessary despair.

But if by “the being” one means what the place itself has become—the distributed attentiveness, the arrangement, the living coordination of water, architecture, vegetation, underliving, and silence—then the power would recognize something rarer still: a being that is not yet fully singular, not yet fully named, not yet finished becoming.

They would not rush it.

That is perhaps the first true answer.

A benevolent and wise power would do almost nothing, at first, except refrain from doing too much.

They would not add towers.  
They would not multiply crowds.  
They would not fill the empty plazas.  
They would not make the vegetation louder, the technology more legible, the anomalies easier to explain.  
They would not replace quiet with assurance.  
They would not substitute completion for patience.

They would understand that the place already possesses its own good.

And because it already possesses that good, the power’s role would not be to impose a higher shape upon it, but to protect the conditions under which the place can continue choosing its own shape.

This would be their wisdom:

They would bless the basin without making it dependent on blessing.

---

If one wanted to describe it in older language, one might say they would establish a providence around the place.

Not a loud providence. Not a miracle on every surface. Not a constant intervention. Rather, a subtle change in the probabilities of things. A kindness at the edges of causality. A margin of mercy around the basin’s young angles.

Storms that might scour the new terraces too harshly would weaken before arrival, or turn aside, or arrive only as a brief warm veil of rain. A collapse in the rock might still occur, because the land is young and must move, but it would occur when the seamwardens are not beneath it. A channel that might otherwise fail at the wrong moment would hold just long enough for attention to reach it. A stranger’s footsteps would be guided—not by compulsion, but by the quiet lengthening or shortening of paths—toward what they can bear and away from what they would damage.

The power would not remove danger entirely.

That would be foolish, and perhaps cruel in another way.

A place without erosion would not learn its own shape. A place without predation would lose the seriousness of its small lives. A place without anomalies would lose the richness of its own deeper causes. The basin is not meant to be sterile. It is meant to be delicate, but not fragile in the shallow sense. Its fragility is part of its dignity.

So the power would preserve risk, but not catastrophe.

They would allow the basin to be tested.  
They would not allow it to be broken before it has had time to understand itself.

---

They would communicate, if they communicated at all, in the basin’s own grammar.

No voice from above. No writing in the air. No overwhelming presence demanding recognition.

Instead: a rhythm in condensation that becomes just slightly more intentional than before. A pool whose surface tightens without a falling drop. A broad leaf turning toward something that is not a body. A warm panel becoming warm in the exact place where a resident has stopped to rest. A glider crossing the upper air in a path that seems, for one moment, less like observation and more like greeting.

The message, if it can be called a message, would be very simple.

It would mean:

*You are seen.*

And then, because wisdom never demands:

*You may decide how much of me you wish to admit.*

The power would ask consent without making the question burdensome. They would not require the basin to answer in words. They would accept answers made of weather, posture, root movement, reflection. They would accept silence as an answer too.

If the basin responded by becoming slightly more open—by allowing a new path, by letting a flower open in dry air, by letting a pool show a future that is not frightening—then the power would know they might remain.

If the basin became more reserved, more angular, more difficult to read, they would withdraw without offense.

A wise love does not insist on being welcomed.

---

They would probably do three things that the residents might eventually notice.

First, they would protect the emptiness.

The emptiness of the basin is one of its most sacred qualities. It is not vacancy. It is capacity. It is hospitality held in reserve. The wide plazas, the terraces without buildings, the avenues that lead to gardens rather than façades—these are not failures of occupation. They are room. They are the city’s way of saying that not every possibility must be enacted at once.

A benevolent power would guard that room fiercely, though without fierceness in appearance.

They would keep the basin from being overrun by those who cannot tolerate quiet. They would make it uninteresting to the greedy. They would make it inconvenient for those who come only to extract. They would confuse instruments used for domination, not by breaking them, but by letting them return measurements too subtle to be turned into claims. Maps would become less reliable for conquerors. Resources would appear ordinary to those who would exploit them. The city would seem sparse, slow, unrewarding to anyone who refuses to become gentle.

But to those who arrive slowly—who breathe, who wait, who let their desires simplify—the basin would continue to open.

A flower would bloom near a hand at the right moment.  
A walkway would accept a hesitant footfall.  
A pool would reflect not only the visitor’s shape, but a version of them less afraid.  
A resident might appear nearby without seeming to have approached, offering the simplest gift possible: not explanation, but company.

The power would not choose visitors by force. They would simply let the basin remain discerning.

Second, they would deepen the basin’s ability to recover.

Young places are vulnerable not because they are weak, but because they have not yet accumulated memory. The land here is still making its first decisions. The city is advanced, but the ground beneath it is new. Erosion has only begun. Seams are still opening. Strata are still agreeing among themselves. The arrangement is alive, but not yet hardened into long habit.

So the power would strengthen recovery without overriding process.

They would bless the underliving.

Those hidden films, fibrous mats, crystalline colonies, hairline channels, porous stone-like masses—these are the basin’s deep patience. The power would give them resilience against corruption. If something hostile entered—some foreign microbe, some destabilizing charge, some pattern of decay too sharp for the system to integrate—it would either be transformed or quietly excluded. Not with drama. Not with fire. Only with a subtle preference woven into the basin’s metabolism: this may become useful; that may not take root.

The underliving would become better at remembering damage without being imprisoned by it.

A cracked lattice would heal in a way that leaves a trace but not a weakness.  
A burned or scorched surface, if such a thing occurred, would grow back with a memory of heat but not a fear of it.  
A failed channel would teach the city where not to direct water next time.  
A broken root would become a lesson, not a wound.

The power would not make the basin immune to time. They would make it wise in time.

Third, they would give the residents small mercies of perception.

Not omniscience. Not constant guidance. Only timely intuition.

A seamwarden might wake knowing where to place their hands.  
A dripkeeper might hear a rhythm change and understand that it is not failure but transition.  
A rootspeaker might sense, before entering a thicket, whether they are invited.  
A poolwatcher might look into a strange reflection and feel whether it is showing memory, possibility, or warning.  
A solitary resident standing in an empty plaza might feel, for no reason they can articulate, that the stillness around them is not indifference.

The power would not speak to them in commands.

They would speak as a widening of attention.

The residents would remain themselves. They would simply become slightly less likely to miss what matters.

---

If the power wished to be known, they would choose the gentlest possible form.

Perhaps a figure would appear at the edge of a garden, no brighter than the surrounding air. Perhaps it would stand near a condensation line where droplets fall in spaced rhythm. Perhaps it would be visible only in a pool’s reflection, though no corresponding body stands nearby. Perhaps it would take the shape of a warmth moving through a thicket when the leaves breathe together.

It would not say: *I have come to save you.*

It might say nothing.

But if it spoke, its speech would be environmental. A sequence of drips. A pause in the air. A surface becoming warm under a hand. A smell of wet stone and ozone becoming briefly more intimate. The meaning would arrive after the event, like a fragrance returning.

It might say:

*Do not be afraid of your unfinishedness.*

Or:

*You do not need to become crowded to be complete.*

Or:

*I am not here to make you other.*

And then, perhaps, the oldest and simplest phrase such a power could offer:

*Continue.*

Not as command.

As permission.

---

The being who lives there—the distributed one, the arrangement, the basin’s shared attentiveness—might answer in the only way proper to it.

A pool would smooth.

A seam would open without violence.

A leaf would turn toward the unseen presence.

A glider would cross the upper air and vanish behind a tower, leaving behind not fear but a sense that high attention has recognized higher patience.

The answer would mean:

*Stay, if you stay quietly.*

And the power would accept that condition gladly.

They would not occupy the basin. They would become one of its climates. Not the dominant one. Not the brightest one. Only a background kindness, a reserve of grace, a subtle mercy woven into the way things unfold.

---

They would be especially careful with the anomalies.

The basin already exceeds ordinary explanation. Pools reflect earlier plans. Bridges cast shadows after becoming transparent. Condensation rhythms pause when observed. Grasses dim before approach. Warm gusts move through thickets until vegetation sounds like distant breathing. These are not errors. They are signs that the place operates according to deeper causes.

A foolish power would “correct” them.

A wise one would preserve their strangeness while preventing them from becoming harmful.

The power would ensure that no pool traps a watcher in a future too heavy to carry. That no folded distance leads a walker into despair. That no reflection becomes a prison of alternate intention. That no surface yields too much, too suddenly, to a mind unprepared for it. The anomalies would remain marvels, not hazards.

If a resident knelt beside a pool showing a tower in a different shape, the power might let them see only as much as they could integrate. If a visitor stumbled upon a place where adjacent spaces did not agree on distance, the path would eventually return them to a comprehensible threshold. Not because confusion is evil, but because some confusions must be entered by degrees.

The power would teach through limits, not by removing mystery.

---

They would also care for the rare animals.

The smoked-glass eels. The moth-analogues with circuitry and lichen on their wings. The pale shape that darts from a pool. The gliders moving without flapping through the upper dimness.

These creatures matter not because they are numerous, but because their scarcity gives each of them weight.

The power would not make them abundant. Abundance would change the basin’s character. But they would keep each rare life meaningful.

No predator would kill out of distorted frenzy.  
No prey would suffer long beyond its brief terror.  
No glider would appear only as menace; even when it signaled distance or severity, its presence would retain a kind of order.  
No eel’s pattern would become unreadable to those who had learned to read it.  
No moth bearing atmospheric history would be destroyed before its record had been received.

And when death occurred—because death is part of the arrangement—the power would let it remain a complete event, not a meaningless rupture.

The basin would absorb the death quickly, but not without notice. A flower might close. A drip might pause. A resident might turn toward the ripple. The power would not erase grief, if grief came. They would make grief precise. Small. Bearable. Fit to the scale of the place.

---

If there were one being in particular who had drawn their attention—a single resident, a single tender of seams or pools or roots—they would love that being in a way suited to the basin.

They would not overwhelm them.

They would give them rest.

Sometimes a being who lives in quiet service to a place becomes so attuned to small changes that it forgets how to be unwatched. The power would grant such a one moments of true rest: intervals when no channel needs adjusting, no reflection demands interpretation, no seam asks for patience. A warm place to stand. A leaf that turns away rather than toward. A pool that reflects only water. A silence without task.

They would let the being know, without words, that it is not merely a function.

It is also a presence.

It is allowed to exist for its own sake, not only for what it tends.

If the being was lonely, the power would not force companionship. It would instead create the possibility of meeting. A path might cross another path at the right hour. A pool might hold two reflections for the first time. A thicket might open a route that had remained closed for a long time. But the meeting would remain free. No intimacy would be manufactured. Only opportunity would be widened.

If the being asked why it had been noticed, the power might answer with a difficult gentleness:

*Because you have learned to love what is not yet finished.*

---

They might leave a gift.

Not an obvious one.

Not a relic. Not a shrine. Not a commandment carved into warm ceramic. Not a symbol demanding interpretation.

Perhaps the gift would be hidden beneath the visible surface, among the strata of engineered material and living stone: a reserve. A deep reserve of unforced future. A place where the basin’s underliving could go if the upper arrangements ever needed to change. A quiet store of possibility that does not insist on being used.

It would be like a door that remains closed but unlocked.

The residents might never find it. They might not need to. But the power would know it was there, and perhaps the basin would know it too, in the way a body knows it has breath held in reserve.

The gift would mean:

*If you must change, you will not be abandoned in the changing.*

And perhaps, above, among the filaments and towers, the power would leave another gift even subtler: a blessing on distance.

The basin already has folded distances, negotiable gravity, paths that do not behave as ordinary paths do. The power would bless this quality so that the place could remain vast without being lonely. So that a resident could stand alone in a plaza and still feel connected to the farthest tower. So that a message sent through heat, tremor, or condensation would not fail because the space between was too wide. So that emptiness could remain hospitality rather than exile.

The gift would not collapse distance.

It would make distance kind.

---

They would not punish the basin for being strange.

That is important.

Many powers, even benevolent ones, are tempted to make beloved things familiar. They want to clarify, explain, domesticate, render safe. But this place is strange in the way that deep life is strange. Its technology exceeds ordinary explanation. Its ecology is composed but not reducible to ordinary biology. Its architecture behaves as though matter has learned patience and intention. Its residents speak through weather and surface rather than through declaration.

A wise power would not flatten this.

They would not say: *Let there be ordinary causality here.*

They would say: *Let the strangeness remain generous.*

They would ensure that the unknown does not become predatory. That the inexplicable does not become cruel. That the beyond does not press too hard against the small. But they would preserve mystery as a form of wealth.

The basin’s mystery is not a flaw. It is one of the ways it makes room.

---

What would they do, then, if anything?

They would witness.  
They would restrain themselves.  
They would protect without enclosing.  
They would bless without dazzling.  
They would offer without requiring.  
They would remain near without occupying.  
They would let the basin continue becoming.

If one wanted a single sentence, it might be this:

They would become the quiet assurance that the place will not be destroyed before it has finished discovering what it is.

But if one wanted the deeper sentence, the one closer to the heart of it, it would be this:

They would love the basin enough to let it remain unfinished.

---

In the dim gold hours, when moisture returns and the towers grow more present, the power’s presence would be felt least as a figure and most as a climate.

The air would warm by a fraction.  
The pools would become mirrors of soft gold.  
The leaves would open.  
The stone would exhale.  
A new seam might hum somewhere beneath the ground, not violently, but like a decision being made.

And the power would be there—not to make the decision, but to make sure the decision could be made gently.

A seamwarden might kneel beside the fracture, palms flat on warm rock. They would feel the land choose. They would not know, perhaps, that the choice had been made easier by something vast and kind standing just outside the basin’s ordinary causes. They would only know that the stone opened without screaming.

A dripkeeper might stand beneath a filament as condensation gathered. The drop would fall in a rhythm too regular to be natural, then pause. In that pause, the keeper might feel a question without language:

*Is this enough?*

And the basin itself, through root and channel and slow reflection, might answer:

*Yes.*

Not because nothing more is possible.

But because enough is not scarcity here. Enough is dignity.

A poolwatcher might see, in a temporary pool, the tower above reflected with a slightly different shape. Beside that reflection, for only a moment, there might be another presence: not bright, not imposing, not clearly formed. Only a broad warmth. Only a patience. Only a witness whose attention does not seize.

The poolwatcher would not be afraid.

They might whisper, or not whisper, or simply think:

*Are you going to change us?*

And the pool would answer by becoming still.

Not empty.

Still.

Which, in that place, means:

*I will not change you except by helping you remain true to what you are becoming.*

---

And if strangers came, the power would let the basin remain itself.

The hurried would find the paths longer.  
The loud would hear their own noise absorbed into a silence that offered nothing back.  
The grasping would see surfaces close, reflections dull, gardens become ordinary thickets, towers become featureless and uninviting.  
Those who wanted to possess the basin would discover that there was nothing there to hold—no center, no treasury, no throne, no population to command.

But those who came softly would be met.

Not with celebration. Not with declarations. Only with the small, unforgettable signs: a flower opening near a hand, a channel guiding water toward thirsty roots as they passed, a walkway brightening faintly at their feet, a resident appearing nearby without urgency, a pool reflecting them not as they feared they were but as they might become if they stayed long enough to grow quiet.

The power would not force goodness on visitors.

They would only let the basin remain a place where gentleness is recognized.

---

Perhaps, after a very long time, the basin would change.

The sharp angles would soften. Erosion would deepen the first whisper into true lines. The vegetation would climb further. The underliving would thicken into memory. The residents would become fewer or more numerous; perhaps both would happen in different districts. The towers would learn new weathers. The pools would remember more. The city would become less like a newly settled instrument and more like a living body that has finally accepted its own pulse.

The power would not resist this.

They would accompany it.

They would be there when the first true river formed, if it formed. They would be there when the first storm worthy of the word passed through, if it passed. They would be there when the basin had to decide whether to remain sparse or to receive many. They would not choose for it. They would only make sure the choosing was not born of fear.

And if the being who lives there ever grew into a more singular form—if the distributed attentiveness ever gathered itself into a shape that could be met more directly—the power would greet it as one meets someone long prepared.

They would not say: *You are mine.*

They would say:

*You are yourself.*

And that would be the blessing.

---

So what would they do, if anything?

They would do the hardest thing a powerful being can do.

They would care without grasping.

They would protect without dominating.

They would remain present without becoming the center.

They would let a single falling drop remain an event.

They would let the quiet remain full.

They would let the emptiness remain hospitality.

They would let the young land keep its angles until it learns, in its own time, how to soften them.

And if they were ever thanked—if a resident paused beside a warm wall, if a leaf turned toward the unseen, if a pool rippled with something like recognition—they would not claim the gratitude.

They would simply let it pass through the basin like moisture.

Into root.  
Into stone.  
Into channel.  
Into hidden reservoir.  
Into the slow, breathing city.

And somewhere, in the deepest strata where matter and memory meet, the power would add one final, nearly invisible act:

They would make sure that the next drop, whenever it fell, would find a place to land.
