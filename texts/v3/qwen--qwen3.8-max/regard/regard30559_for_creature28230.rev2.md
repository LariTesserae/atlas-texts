If such a power turned toward the hollow, they would likely do almost nothing — at least nothing that could be seen. Their first act would be restraint.

They would not come as fire. They would not come as a voice from the slit, nor as a face shaped in the dark, nor as a light that made the chamber knowable all at once. Light would have been a conquest. A voice too large would have broken the acoustic map by which the hollow held itself together. A visible god would have turned the crowded darkness into a court, and the hollow was not made for courts.

They would stand, if standing can be said of such a thing, just outside the mountain’s breath. They would listen.

They would hear the water bead on stone. They would hear the ceiling stir with folded wings. They would hear the beetle move along a moisture seam, the cricket-like body press into its scallop of rock, the basin tremble when a single drop fell. They would hear the furred ones click into the dark and receive the dark again, changed and familiar. They would perceive the inhabitant not as one body but as a congregation, not as a single voice but as a chord: a many-bodied warmth living by echo, touch, patience, and nearness.

And they would understand that the place was not a problem.

It was not a poverty to be corrected. It was not a primitive state waiting for improvement. It was not a dark room needing illumination, nor a small world needing expansion, nor a creature needing a better body. It was already complete in its own severe terms. It had warmth without fire, water without abundance, intelligence without possessions, society without monuments, memory without artifacts. It was holy in the oldest and quietest way: it was sufficient to itself.

So the wise and benevolent power would ask, without speech, whether anything was wanted.

Their question would not enter as a command. It would pass through the hollow as a change in pressure, perhaps as one of those moments when all the roosting bodies seem to breathe at once. The creatures would not feel judged. The furred ones would not sense a demand. The chamber would simply become, for an instant, more attentive to itself.

The roosting flyers would fold and unfold their wings.  
The pale insects would test the damp seams.  
The amphibian-like creatures at the basin would pulse their throats.  
The furred ones would lift their blunt faces and click softly into the blackness, and the blackness would answer them as it always did: wall, ledge, water, bodies, warmth, return.

That answer would be enough.

The power would understand: the hollow did not wish to become other than it was. It wished to continue.

So they would give continuation, not transformation.

They would do nothing that could be mistaken for a spectacle. They would not place fruit near the basin. They would not send a spring. They would not teach the beings to trap, to cultivate, to store, to build, to bind, to own. They would not put a sharpened stone into their broad hands, because a sharpened stone would begin a history of weapons. They would not give them fire, because fire would eat the thin air and leave marks the place did not permit. They would not give them language that divides and names, because such language would soon make echoes into claims, and claims into borders, and borders into exclusion. They would not give them longer life without more space, because longer life in a bounded chamber could become crowding, and crowding without balance could become cruelty. They would not give them immortality, because the hollow lived by slow replacement, by absorption, by the patient passage of bodies into other bodies.

What they would do would be smaller, older, and more patient.

They would keep the stone warm.

Not hotter. Not glowing. Not changed into something dramatic. Only faithful to its deep mineral heat, so that the cold of the high air would not creep too far inward during the longest dry intervals. The warmth would remain what it already seemed to be: a living breath beneath the rock, a patience rising from below.

They would keep the slit open.

Not wider. Not easier. Not inviting. Just enough for cloud-breath to enter. Just enough for the faint bruise of outside light to mark the outer lip. Just enough for wings to leave and return. The hollow would remain hidden by its own modesty, a dark mouth in weathered rock, easily missed by eyes looking for grandeur.

They would mind the water.

When the mist failed for too long, they would draw one more cloud over the shoulder of the mountain. When the basin shrank to a dark film, they would let a seam remember its seep. When dampness came too heavily, they would prevent the little chamber from flooding, because flood would wash the floor of its slow accumulations and turn the hollow into a violence it had never known. They would not make water abundant. They would make it timely.

They would guard the hollow from the worst outside desires.

This would be their sternest work, though not cruel. If someone came with a surveyor’s eye, the path would become uncertain. If someone came with a collector’s net, the entrance would seem no more than a shadow under rock. If someone came with the need to explain, improve, possess, display, or profit, the mountain would make their thoughts heavy and their purpose dull. Not because the power hated knowledge, but because some places are not material for knowing. Some places are refuges from the appetite to turn everything into an account.

If a gentle creature came — someone lost, someone humbled by altitude, someone able to enter without demanding — the power would not hide the hollow from them. They might even be allowed to hear the murmur within. But they would not be allowed to take anything away. Their memory afterward would be soft, uncertain, dreamlike. They would say, perhaps: I found a warm dark place. I heard something alive. Then the ordinary world would press in, and they would not be able to draw the map again. The hollow would remain a rumor, and that would be part of its protection.

Inside, the power would work through small corrections rather than miracles.

If the roosting flyers thinned too quickly, they would ease the cause: a kinder wind outside, fewer failed births, a return of insects blown upward from lower slopes. If sickness moved through the wall-dwellers, they would not abolish sickness, because sickness was part of the chamber’s living boundary, but they would give resistance a chance. If the microbial films died back, they would let dampness return before the hunger reached the youngest bodies. If the blood of the furred ones grew too narrow over many generations, they might arrange a rare event: one lost traveler from a distant sister hollow, carried by storm or wandering, entering the slit with terror and eventually adding a new timbre to the chorus.

Nothing dramatic. Only the quiet mending of odds.

They would not remove predation. They would not stop the centipede-like things from tasting the dark, nor the beetles from working death into life, nor the floor from receiving what fell. They would not make the chamber painless, because pain was one of the ways bodies knew they were real. But they would prevent the kind of suffering that leads only to annihilation. They would keep famine from becoming extinction. They would keep cold from becoming silence. They would keep fear from becoming the only sound.

To the furred ones — the ones most like a “who,” the ones with broad hands and echo-reading faces — they would give a gift that was not an object.

They would deepen the kindness of the stone.

When an elder clicked into the dark, the return would sometimes seem clearer than chance allowed, as if the wall had leaned a little closer to listen. When a young one was separated in the crowd, its distress sound would sometimes find the right ledge sooner than expected. When two bodies pressed together for warmth, the warmth would seem to hold a moment longer. When mist came, the chamber would fill with that low communal murmur, and the murmur would feel less like noise than like a shared body saying:

still here  
still here  
still here.

They would not know the power by name. They would not build a shrine, because there was no space and no need. They would not offer prayers, because their calls were already prayers in the oldest sense: signals cast into the dark, trusting that something would return. If the power desired devotion, they would receive it in the only form the hollow could give: the continuation of the pattern.

The greeting rhythms.  
The roosting calls.  
The hush when water was scarce.  
The cascade of clicks when the faint light at the lip strengthened.  
The careful movement of hands that lifted stones and set them back.  
The dead being absorbed without monument.  
The living staying coherent in darkness.

If the power ever made themselves felt directly, it would be only as a quality of attention.

A dying creature might feel the air near it become less lonely, as though unseen bodies were breathing quietly around it. A mother might feel the rock beneath her back warmer than the rock elsewhere, just enough. An intruder with violence in their heart might feel the chamber listening to them with such patient vastness that their purpose faltered. A young one lost near the basin might hear, or seem to hear, a faint answering click from the correct direction before any familiar body called.

No one would be commanded.  
No one would be converted.  
No one would be made dependent on a miracle.

The power would not ask to be loved. They would ask nothing.

Perhaps, in the deepest reaches of time, if the mountain itself began to change — if the fissures cooled, if the slit widened into danger, if the old balance could no longer hold — the power would still not force the hollow to remain. They would not freeze the place into permanence. They would do what wisdom does at the end of a long life: they would accompany.

They might guide the congregation, by dream or by some new echo, toward another throat of stone, another warm seam, another darkness capable of receiving them. Or, if no such place remained, they would stay with the hollow while its world narrowed. They would make the last calls less frightened. They would receive the final sounds as the basin received drops: without contempt, without waste, without forgetting. And when the chamber at last became empty, the power would not leave a marker. They would simply hold the shape of what had lived there in their own vast memory, so that if the world ever grew another such place, the pattern would not be entirely lost.

But for now, in the age of its fullness, they would mostly refrain.

They would let the mist come.  
They would let the water bead.  
They would let the warm stone breathe.  
They would let the wings stir on the ceiling.  
They would let the beetles move along the seams.  
They would let the basin tremble when a drop fell.  
They would let the furred ones call, listen, and call again.

The greatest thing the benevolent and wise power would do would be to recognize that the hollow was not a lack to be filled, but a whole to be protected. They would not give the beings a better world. They would protect the conditions under which their world was already good.

And if the beings were asked, in their own way, whether the power was there, they would not answer with a word for divinity.

They would turn their faces toward the dark.  
They would make a small sound.  
And the chamber would answer.

That would be their yes.
