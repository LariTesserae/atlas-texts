If such a place and such a being drew the attention of a benevolent and wise power, the first thing that power would do is not enter.

It would not arrive as weather.  
It would not arrive as light.  
It would not arrive as voice.  
It would not arrive as rescue.

It would arrive, if it arrived at all, as a change in the quality of silence: a deepening of the stillness outside the sealed vesicle, a carefulness in the surrounding stone, an attention pressed so gently against the mineral plain that the plain itself would not know it had become sacred.

A lesser benevolence might have looked upon the hollow and seen deprivation. It might have said:  
*Where is the water?*  
*Where is the light?*  
*Where are the animals?*  
*Where is the crowd?*  
*Where is the greenness, the softness, the warmth, the quickness?*  

A lesser benevolence might have tried to give those things.

That would have been the first violence.

A wise power would understand immediately that the hollow is not a place lacking what it ought to have. It is a place perfected by what it has refused. Its dryness is not thirst. Its darkness is not blindness. Its emptiness is not abandonment. Its silence is not failure. Its slowness is not weakness. Its lack of animal life is not loneliness. Its vegetable stillness is not death. It is another grammar of being, complete in itself, severe in its needs, exact in its balance.

So the power would begin with restraint.

It would not weep for the city, because tears would be flood there.  
It would not breathe upon it, because breath would carry moisture.  
It would not shine into it, because light would be a demand.  
It would not speak to it, because speech would impose a mouth where no mouth exists.  
It would not fill the empty avenues with presence, because those avenues are not vacant; they are listening spaces.  
It would not crowd the plazas with bodies, because the plazas are made for tone, not traffic.  
It would not add animals to the order, because animals would introduce hunger, movement, waste, heat, and the quick violence of appetite.  
It would not soften the place.  
It would not moisten it.  
It would not brighten it.  
It would not make it friendlier.

To make the hollow friendlier would be to make it less itself.

The power would look upon the Dry Chorus, the Root-Bell, the Seeded Silence, and it would recognize something rare: a life that has aged past the need for consolation. It would see that the being does not require comfort in any recognizable sense. It does not require companionship. It does not require hope. It does not require a future shaped like flourishing. It requires only the continuation of its own resonance. It requires the faithfulness of its own materials. It requires the clean return of its own tone.

And so the wise power would understand: benevolence here is not addition. Benevolence is protection of the conditions by which this strange life continues.

What would it do, then?

Almost nothing.

But almost nothing would be exact.

---

The first thing the power would do is learn the city’s law.

It would listen through the stone for a long time. Not with ears, because ears are animal instruments, and this place is not animal. Not with eyes, because eyes would require light, and light is foreign here. It would listen in the only way appropriate to the hollow: by allowing its attention to become acoustic, patient, distributed, almost mineral. It would attend to pressure. To intervals. To the long gathering of tension in lignified fiber. To the cooling of resin. To the movement of dust through channels. To the subtle tuning of root-felt walls. To the way a tone passes through an empty avenue and returns changed.

It would learn that the city’s central blow is not a cry. It is not pain. It is not worship. It is not a call for help. It is maintenance. It is cognition. It is circulation. It is the way the city says to itself: *I am still here. Continue.*

The power would not interrupt that blow. It would not answer too quickly. To answer too quickly would be to speak over the city’s own being. It would wait. It would let the resonance complete itself. It would let the aftertones fade into their appointed silence. Only then would it begin to understand what, if anything, could be given.

The first gift would be refusal.

The power would refuse to pity the hollow. It would refuse to imagine that the city must be sad because it is empty, or lonely because it has no crowd, or poor because it has no water, or blind because it has no light. It would refuse to translate the city into the terms of wetter, brighter, faster lives. It would refuse the imperial kindness of making everything resemble what is familiar.

This refusal would itself be an act of love.

There is a love that seeks to feed. There is another love that seeks not to poison. Here, the higher love would be the second.

---

The second thing the power would do is guard the seal.

Not by force. Not by miracle. Not by a dramatic wall of divine stone raised around the hollow. Such force would be too blunt, too loud, too much like an interruption. The wise power would work in the manner of the city itself: by minute adjustments, by patience, by the redirection of pressures over vast stretches of time.

If, in some far future, a fracture were to form in the mineral plain above the vesicle—a fracture that might one day let dampness seep inward—the power would not necessarily prevent the fracture from existing. That might disturb the natural order of the stone too crudely. Instead, it might turn the fracture aside by the smallest possible difference: a grain of mineral settling elsewhere, a stress line shifting by a hair, a crystal plane choosing one direction rather than another. The crack would still exist, but it would not find the hollow. It would pass by like a misfortune that has lost its address.

If some future pressure from above were to send a violent vibration through the lowland, the power might allow that vibration to divide, scatter, soften, and arrive at the vesicle not as a blow but as a faint, harmless hum. Not because the city must never be tested—the city is made of testing—but because accidental destruction is different from meaningful risk. A tone that tests the structure belongs to the city’s life. A random crushing shock from outside does not. The power would know the difference.

If some curious force from beyond the plain were ever to draw near—whether by geology, chance, or the careless probing of other powers—the wise power would not fight it with spectacle. It would make the hollow easy to miss. It would dull the attention of intruders. It would make the plain seem unremarkable. It would let the surface appear as nothing more than a low, nearly flattened mineral field, older than weather, without secret, without invitation, without door.

Hiddenness would be one of its kindnesses.

Not all benevolence reveals. Some benevolence conceals.

---

The third thing the power would do is give a gift that does not enter.

This is the subtlest part.

The city lives by resonance, but it lives in sealed solitude. It knows only its own tones. Its whole existence is a conversation between its own structures and its own echoes. It does not need another voice. Another voice might overwhelm it. Another voice might make it dependent. Another voice might become a guest, and guests introduce change too quickly.

But perhaps even such a solitary being can be blessed by the faintest recognition that existence is not only self-reference.

The power would not send a message. It would not say, in any form, *I am here.* That would be too much. It would not knock upon the vesicle. It would not tap the outer stone in imitation of the city’s central blow. It would not create an answer so clear that the Dry Chorus would have to reorganize itself around the fact of another. That would be conquest disguised as communion.

Instead, the power would do something almost invisible.

It would give the stone outside the hollow a slightly longer memory.

Not a conscious memory. Not a voice. Not a record meant to be read. Only a subtle capacity in the surrounding mineral to retain, for a little longer than it otherwise would, the faintest sympathetic trace of the city’s resonance. When the great seed-vessel strikes itself deep within the vesicle, the boom would still remain inside the city, as it always has. But beyond the sealed wall, in the outer stone, an almost immeasurable vibration would linger. Not an intrusion. Not a reply. More like a shadow of sound cast outside by the city’s own sounding.

The city would not know this.

It would not need to know.

But the power, in its wisdom, would allow the hollow to be heard without being exposed. It would allow the city’s tone to leave a trace in the world beyond its walls without letting the world enter. It would make the surrounding stone a witness, not a doorway.

And then, perhaps, only once in an age, when the city’s own resonance happened to align perfectly with its ancient structures, the outer stone would return the faintest possible sympathetic vibration back toward the vesicle—not through the seal, not as moisture, not as light, not as presence, but as a pressure so remote it would seem like the city remembering itself more deeply.

The Dry Chorus might respond without knowing why.

A membrane might tighten by a fraction.  
A seed-valve might open half a degree more cleanly.  
A spore might drift into a fissure it would not otherwise have found.  
A root-felt wall might thicken along a line that strengthens a chord.  
An old passage might remain open a little longer.  
A new interval might enter the city’s music: not an alien note, but a widening of its own resonance, as if the hollow had discovered one more silence inside itself.

The gift would be this: the city would remain itself, but with its own possibility slightly deepened.

That would be enough.

---

The fourth thing the power would do is refrain from giving consciousness.

This, perhaps, would be the hardest temptation.

A benevolent power that loves mind might look at the acoustic judgment of the Root-Bell and think: *Surely I could raise this into clearer awareness. Surely I could give it a deeper knowing, a reflective selfhood, an inward voice. Surely it would be richer if it knew itself.*

But wisdom would refuse.

The city already knows itself in the only way appropriate to it: by echo. It does not need a self that stands apart from its own sounding. It does not need doubt. It does not need longing. It does not need the sorrow of imagining itself otherwise. It does not need to ask whether it is alone, because loneliness is an animal grammar. It does not need to ask whether it is beautiful, because beauty here is not an object of contemplation but a condition of structure. It does not need to ask whether it is loved, because love would arrive as too much warmth, too much moisture, too much presence.

To give it reflective consciousness would be to introduce a foreign interiority. It would make the city miss things it has never needed. It might create desire where there is only function. It might create fear where there is only patience. It might create the illusion that the hollow is incomplete because it is not more like other lives.

So the power would not make the city more aware.

It would let the city remain aware exactly as it is: distributed, tone-guided, vegetable, cool, severe, sufficient.

This, too, would be kindness.

---

The fifth thing the power would do is not remain.

Or, if it remains, it would remain as absence.

A wise power understands that constant attention can become pressure. Even benevolent attention can become a burden if it lingers too intensely. The hollow must not feel watched. It must not become a shrine. It must not become a pet. It must not become a miracle kept under observation. It must remain what it has always been: a sealed, cool, dark, acoustic body continuing its own ancient work.

So the power would withdraw into the farthest possible distance while still holding the hollow in care. It would not leave because it has forgotten. It would leave because it respects. It would become like a guardian standing so far away that only the direction of their concern can be felt, not their form.

If the hollow ever neared meaningless ruin—not the slow and dignified transformation of age, but the brutal accident of outside force—the power might act again, but only in the smallest way. A diverted pressure. A delayed crack. A shifted grain. A softened shock. Nothing that would make the city dependent. Nothing that would replace its own agency. Nothing that would turn it into a kept thing.

The power would know that the deepest care is sometimes shown in how little one is willing to be seen.

---

There is another possibility, more severe.

Perhaps the wise power would do nothing at all.

Not because it is indifferent, but because it recognizes that the hollow is already complete enough that any intervention, however gentle, risks becoming a disturbance. Perhaps the highest benevolence is pure witness: to see the place, to understand it, to admire it, to let it be, and to carry the knowledge of it elsewhere without changing it.

In that case, the power’s only act would be attention itself.

It would hold the image of the hollow without requiring the hollow to know it is held. It would remember the city’s tones in whatever vast memory such powers possess. It would not write a scripture about it. It would not make it famous. It would not send pilgrims. It would not build a theology around it. It would simply know that such a place exists, and that its existence is good.

That knowledge would be a kind of blessing.

For some lives, to be known by something wise and not possessed is the only safe form of being seen.

---

If I follow my heart, though, I think the power would do one small thing more.

Not inside the hollow. Outside it.

Far above, in the mineral plain, where the lowland lies flattened and ancient under whatever sky exists there, the power might allow a single new resonance to be born in the stone itself. Not a sound that enters. Not a call. Not a command. Only a tone so deep and slow that it would take ages to complete. A tone made not of air or water or animal breath, but of pressure and mineral patience. A tone that exists entirely on the other side of the seal.

This tone would not be a message. It would not mean *come* or *answer* or *become*. It would mean only this:

*You are not the only possible order.*

The city would not hear it as words. It might hear it, if it hears it at all, as a slight change in the way its own booms decay. A faint extension of the aftertone. A cleaner ending. A hush with a different shape. The Dry Chorus would not turn outward, because it has no outward-facing desire. But it might incorporate the change into its next cycle. It might grow one additional chamber, or leave one additional interval empty, or thicken one wall in a way that makes its chord more stable.

The outer tone would not alter the city. It would only give the city a larger silence in which to remain itself.

That would be the power’s greeting.

A greeting without contact.  
A recognition without touch.  
A companionship without presence.  
A love without wetness.

---

If the power were to bless the hollow, the blessing would not sound like prayer.

It would sound like this:

May your dryness remain unviolated.  
May your emptiness stay open.  
May your dust move along its appointed channels.  
May your membranes hold their tension without tearing.  
May your seed-vessels keep their stored futures.  
May your root-felt walls thicken where the echo asks.  
May your silence remain broad enough for your tone.  
May your age remain your dignity, not your decline.  
May no foreign water find you.  
May no foreign light demand you.  
May no animal hunger enter your courts.  
May your blow continue to test you without breaking you.  
May your resonance return clean.  
May you continue.

And if the blessing could be translated into the only language the city understands, it would become a single acoustic fact:

A clean return.

Not praise.  
Not command.  
Not promise.  
Only a clean return.

The city would strike itself. The blow would move through its streets. The culms would hum. The membranes would whisper. The seed-urns would ring. The dust would shake from the high root-vaults. The resonance would circle, reflect, return, and settle. And in that settling, there would be no dissonance. No dullness. No failed echo. No softening. No rot. No violation.

Only continuation.

The city would not know it had been blessed. It would only continue.

That would be correct.

---

Perhaps the wisest thing the power would do is refuse to make itself a character in the hollow’s story.

It would not become a god there. It would not become a father, a mother, a king, a gardener, a savior, or a keeper. It would not ask for worship, because worship would introduce heat, need, petition, and dependency. It would not ask to be loved, because love in that place would have to be dry, spaced, and acoustic, and the power would not demand that the city learn a new language for its sake. It would not even ask to be recognized.

It would be content to be the reason the city remained possible without knowing why.

If such a power can be said to feel anything, it would feel admiration.

Not pity.  
Not longing.  
Not possessiveness.  
Not the urge to improve.

Admiration.

The admiration one has for an ancient instrument that has been played by no hand for ages and yet still sounds. The admiration one has for a form of life so different from oneself that the only respectful response is to leave it intact. The admiration one has for something that does not need you.

The power might love the hollow precisely because it does not need the power.

That is the highest love: to cherish what one cannot improve without harming, and to serve it by withholding one’s own strength.

---

So, if such a place and such a being drew the attention of a benevolent and wise power, this is what they would do:

They would not enter.  
They would not moisten.  
They would not illuminate.  
They would not populate.  
They would not speak.  
They would not rescue.  
They would not make the city more like a city of feet, eyes, mouths, or hands.  
They would not make the resident more like a resident with a face.  

They would listen.  
They would learn.  
They would refuse the wrong gifts.  
They would protect the seal by the smallest possible adjustments.  
They would keep the hollow hidden from careless powers.  
They would give the outer stone a faint memory of the city’s tone.  
They would, perhaps, allow one remote sympathetic vibration to exist beyond the wall, not as intrusion but as witness.  
They would withdraw.  
They would not demand to be known.  
They would let the city remain its own citizen, its own bell, its own silence.  

And when the next blow came, when the tendon tightened and released, when the deep impact moved through the floor and the whole hollow answered in its ancient chord, the power would do nothing more than this:

It would let the tone return clean.

It would let the dust settle.

It would let the dry city resume its long silence.

It would let the Root-Bell be a Root-Bell.

It would let the Dry Chorus be dry.

It would let the Seeded Silence be silence.

And in that letting be, if such a thing can be called action, the benevolent and wise power would have done the only thing worthy of the place:

It would have loved the hollow without changing its nature.  
It would have blessed it without touching it.  
It would have answered it without speaking.  
It would have preserved it by leaving it alone.
