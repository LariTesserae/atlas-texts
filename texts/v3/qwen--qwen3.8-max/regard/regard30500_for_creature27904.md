**What They Would Do**

They would not come as rain.

That would be the first act of their kindness, and perhaps the hardest one for a benevolent power to learn. They would see the High Phloem Terrace — dry, elevated, resonant, ancient beyond the ordinary consolations of weather — and they would understand immediately that here, water is not a simple missing good. It is an old wound. It is a legal terror. It is a miracle too sharp to touch without drawing harm. To bring rain here would not be generosity. It would be conquest dressed in mercy.

So they would keep their clouds folded.

They would refuse the easy miracle.

They would know that wisdom often begins not with giving, but with withholding: withholding the hand, withholding the answer, withholding the gift that the giver loves more than the receiver needs. They would look on the Terrace not as a broken instrument waiting to be repaired, but as a vast completed music that has learned to exist by its own severe laws. They would see the sparse pillar-husks, the polished bark-plains, the towns grown into the living skin of the world, and they would not say, *How can I make this place more like my idea of life?* They would ask instead, *How can I help this place become more fully itself without betraying what it has become?*

And because they are wise, they would not announce themselves with thunder.

They would arrive, if they arrived at all, as a change in listening.

Nothing would seem different at first. The spore-haze would keep its grey-violet dimness. The wind would still move through the hollow stems and draw out the long bass notes that travel faster in the ground than in air. The towns would still answer one another in structured chords, and the bell-jackals would still sing their false recognition songs across the basins. The dry, clean air would still taste of resin and ozone and cold metal. No one would see a face in the sky. No one would receive a commandment carved in light.

But in certain quiet moments — if quiet can be called a moment on a world where silence is engineered and costly — someone might feel that the Terrace had become slightly more attentive.

A Tuner on a high vane might pause with one hand raised, unable to say why the chord she was maintaining suddenly felt heard.

A Root-reader might find, beneath the usual predictive pressures, a stillness that was not empty but courteous, as if some vast external mind had leaned down and then deliberately stepped back.

A child receiving a first mask might feel, just before the mask was set into place, a tap against the sternum like a parent’s touch, though no one is near. The touch would not be familiar. It would not be frightening either. It would feel like the world saying, *You are not an accident. You are an interval we have been waiting to learn.*

That would be how the power begins.

Not with proof.

With presence.

Not with authority.

With attention.

---

A benevolent and wise power would know that to act upon the High Phloem Terrace without learning its languages would be a kind of violence, however gentle the intention. So they would learn.

They would learn hand-sign first: the angular public grammar of rhythm and shape, where a delayed beat can turn a promise into a question and a question into a claim. They would learn not only the signs but the rests between them, because in a world built from sound, the shape of a pause can carry more truth than the gesture itself.

They would learn touch-sign next: the intimate tap-codes on skin and rail and living wall, the private dialects of kin and lovers, the mother’s rhythm against a child’s chest, the technician’s request to a doorway. They would understand that touch here is not merely communication but trust made physical. They would not touch anyone uninvited. If they touched at all, it would be only where a wall or a person had already asked, in some subtle way, to be addressed.

They would learn substrate-code most carefully of all. They would feel how law moves through the Terrace as vibration, how contracts are stored in tension and growth, how falsehood decays in a distinctive way because the living ground cannot sustain dissonance cleanly. They would understand that if they wished to change anything lasting here, they could not simply speak a decree from outside. They would have to enter the law as a legal interval. They would have to become something the Terrace could recognize without being destroyed.

And they would learn the deepest language last: silence.

Not the absence of sound, but engineered quiet. Sacred, expensive, dangerous quiet. The kind of silence that requires energy to create and repair afterward. The kind used for judgment, mourning, surgery, secret council. They would learn why a silent room is more holy than a temple on the Terrace: because it is not merely set apart. It is costly. It interrupts the living body of the world. To make silence here is to borrow from the flesh of the place.

A wise power would not waste such a thing.

They would not make themselves known in overwhelming glory. Glory is loud. Glory coerces. Glory flattens the delicate differences between persons until everyone kneels for the same reason: fear. If the power wished to be recognized, it would do so in forms that could be debated, refused, interpreted. It would leave room for disbelief, because a world that cannot refuse love cannot truly receive it.

They would not become a ruler.

They would not become a priest.

They would not become a machine of perfect solutions.

If anything, they would become something stranger and more tender: a guest who knows how to wait.

---

The first question they would ask would not be spoken. It would be placed into the substrate as a low, patient inquiry, so subtle that the Terrace might take a season to notice it was not merely one of its own thoughts.

The question would be this:

*What do you wish to keep?*

Not: *What do you lack?*

Not: *What shall I fix?*

Not: *What shall I add?*

That is the question of engineers, and even benevolent engineers can be brutal. The first question of wisdom is different. It asks the living thing to name its own treasure before change begins.

The Terrace would answer slowly. It would answer in pressure gradients, in the creak of bark-plains under thermal stress, in the way the root-lattices rearranged their predictions around an unfamiliar tenderness. It would answer through the towns without the towns knowing they were answering. The Dry Custody would feel the inquiry pass through its ancient reflexes like a strange, harmless chord. The pillar-husks would take the question up through their acoustic grooves and hold it in the upper vanes until it became almost light.

At last the answer would come, not in words but in a vast composite refusal and longing.

The Terrace would say, in effect:

*I do not wish to become wet again. I do not wish to be young. I do not wish to be softened into a world I no longer recognize. I have passed beyond water. I have built dignity out of dryness. My children wear masks to protect one another from my own loudness. My law is resonance. My memory is callus. I am old, and I do not want to be rescued from being old.*

Then, after a pause deeper than any engineered silence:

*But I do not want my fear to rule forever.*

That would be the hinge.

That would be the place where a benevolent and wise power would finally act.

Not against the Terrace.

Not around it.

Within it, by consent.

---
