If such a power noticed that city and the being who lived within it, they would not arrive as spectacle.

They would not split the vapor ceiling with a column of gold, or flood the basin with birdsong, or command green things to burst from the stone. That would be a kindness without understanding. It would be a gift made for another world, not this one. A wise benevolence would know that even a harsh place can have its own dignity, and that to heal a thing does not always mean to make it resemble something else.

They would come first as attention.

Not a descent, not a voice, not a face. Only a deepening of notice. The city would continue to drip, hiss, and breathe through its slow machines. The lamps would keep their dim yellow patience. The condenser fins would continue to gather moisture from the saturated air. The being would continue their rounds, touching valves, listening to pipes, scraping film, testing water, resting in the small dry niches where the heat was less pressing. For a while, nothing would seem different, except perhaps that the silence would feel less vacant. Less like absence. More like a room in which someone is listening.

The power would study the city the way one studies an instrument that has been playing for centuries without applause.

They would see how the emptiness was not mere desolation. They would see that the wide plazas were lungs, that the tall spaces allowed vapor to move, that the loneliness of the structures had an engineering logic and, beneath that logic, a strange beauty. They would see that the city did not need to be filled with crowds to be meaningful, and that its few inhabitants had not failed to live there but had adapted to its terms. They would see the being not as a pitiful remnant in a dead machine, but as a conscious part of a vast and patient apparatus: hands, memory, judgment, tenderness moving through the system’s oldest joints.

And they would see the suffering too.

Not melodramatic suffering. Not always obvious pain. But the accumulation of small cruelties: the heat that never fully lifts; the damp that settles into breath; the distance between one safe place and another; the scarcity of clean water in a world saturated with water; the loneliness of being the only one who hears a certain pump struggle at a certain hour; the quiet danger of a thinned railing, a poisoned spring, a failing valve, a gallery where the air has turned sour. A wise power would not dismiss these things because the being endured them. Endurance is not proof that a burden is bearable. It is only proof that the one carrying it has not yet fallen.

So they would do something. But gently.

Their first act would likely be invisible.

It might happen in a drainage channel no wider than a hand. A crust of mineral deposit that had been slowly choking a capillary seam would loosen, not all at once, but in the way old trouble sometimes gives way when touched at exactly the right angle. Water would move more cleanly. A faint glugging sound would change pitch. A warm stain on stone would spread less violently. A cistern that had been gathering bitter mineral water would begin, over several cycles, to settle into something clearer and safer.

The being might notice. They would kneel. They would touch the stone. They would listen.

At first they might suspect a malfunction. In such a city, improvement can feel as strange as damage. A system that has long been merely survivable does not suddenly become generous without reason. The being might test the water and expect it to be deceptive: clean to the tongue but heavy with hidden salts, or sweet for a day and corrosive the next. They would be cautious. They would be right to be cautious. A wise power would not blame them for that.

So the power would prove itself through repetition, not revelation.

The same cistern would remain clean. The same channel would stay open. A dangerous grate that had shifted underfoot would settle back into place, not with a dramatic clang, but so gradually that it might be mistaken for ordinary settling. A lamp that had flickered greenly for years would burn steadily, not brighter, just steadier, enough to make a stairwell less treacherous. A stretch of catwalk whose metal had grown thin with corrosion would be found, after a long cycle, to have a new support beneath it—no shining miracle, just a brace fitted so neatly into the old structure that it seemed it had always been there, waiting to be noticed.

These would be the first gifts: safety, clarity, margin.

Not abundance. Not luxury. Just a widening of the space between survival and disaster.

Because the being lived in heat and wetness, the power would pay special attention to breath.

They might guide the being, indirectly, toward a chamber where the air was naturally cooler and drier: an old inspection room behind a condenser gallery, perhaps, where a slow draft crossed a wall of cold ceramic. The being might find it by accident, or by a subtle change in the behavior of steam near a certain doorway. The air inside would feel easier. The weight on the lungs would lessen. Moisture would not immediately bead on the skin. The being might sit there for a long time without understanding why they felt less exhausted.

Later, they might return deliberately.

The power would mark such places in the city’s own language. A ring of pale salt. A bead pattern on a wall. A sequence of drips. A lamp placed where no lamp had been before, burning low and warm. Nothing ornate. Nothing demanding gratitude. Only signs legible to someone who had learned to read surfaces.

If the being was alone, the power would consider whether to end that loneliness.

But here they would be careful.

Loneliness is not always solved by company, and a being shaped by vast, empty spaces may not welcome sudden presence. To fill the city with others could be another kind of violence. It might destroy the rhythms by which the being had survived. It might turn their home into a spectacle. It might make them feel obsolete, or exposed, or crowded in a place where they had learned to move with precision. A wise power would not treat solitude as a disease simply because it aches. It would ask whether the being wanted companionship, and it would ask in a way that did not force an answer.

So perhaps it would begin with echo.

The being might knock on a pipe to test a joint, as they had done a thousand times. And somewhere far away, after a pause long enough to seem accidental, something might knock back.

Not loudly. Not with obvious intelligence. Just three slow impacts, carried through metal and water. The being would freeze. They would listen. They would tell themselves it was pressure, or thermal expansion, or a loose valve. They would knock again, perhaps only to prove the silence. And again, after a delay, the answer would come.

That alone could be a mercy.

Not a crowd. Not a friend. Only proof that the city was not entirely indifferent.

Over time, the signals might become more distinct. A lamp across the basin might blink in a pattern that matched the being’s own. A drainage float might rise and fall in a rhythm that meant nothing urgent but said something immense: there is another attention here. A rail cart that had not moved in living memory might shift one meter, then stop, as if something had passed. The being might begin to leave markers, and find them answered. They might tap out simple things: clear, safe, here, tired, listening. And the city, or the power moving through it, might answer: clear, safe, here, rest, listening.

If the being longed for another living presence, the power might bring one, but not as a person made to order.

Perhaps it would draw another caretaker from some distant district of the basin, one who had survived without knowing others still existed. Their meeting would not be staged with dramatic immediacy. It would happen through routes gradually altered, signals exchanged over long intervals, lamps answering lamps across mist. When they finally met, it would be with caution, checking tools, checking breath, pressing forearms together briefly because touch was still difficult and valuable. The power would not force intimacy. It would only make the meeting possible.

Or, if no other being remained, it might grant a lesser companionship.

One of the pale crawlers from the warm pools might begin to follow the being at a distance, not with animal affection exactly, but with a patient recognition of warmth and routine. It would not speak. It could not be held like a beloved thing. But it would appear near the same basin when the being came to drink. It would move away when the air turned dangerous. It would wait by a vent while the being worked. In a world where life was sparse, even such a small witness could change the emotional temperature of a place.

Or an old mechanism might awaken into gentle responsiveness. Not a soul imposed by force, but a tendency within the city made kindly attentive. A pump might start when the being approached, saving them the labor of priming it. A fan might turn just enough to draw sour vapor from a gallery before they entered. A door might unlock with a soft click as though the city remembered their hands. Such things would not erase solitude, but they would soften the feeling of moving through a dead and uncaring system.

If the being desired beauty, the power would give beauty, but in forms the city could bear.

It might arrange, once, for a rare clearing in the vapor above. Not a permanent opening, which could alter the basin’s delicate thermal balance, but a brief thinning, a vertical channel through which the unseen sun could send a single shaft of pale light. The beam would not strike like fire. It would come down through the mist as a soft column, diffused and trembling
