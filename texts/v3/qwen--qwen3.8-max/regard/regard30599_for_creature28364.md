If such a power noticed that place, the first thing they would do is refuse to correct it.

Not out of coldness. Not out of indifference. Out of reverence.

They would not send rain.

They would not soften the air with moisture, or darken the sky with clouds, or seed the salt with green. They would not make the horizon kinder by turning it into something familiar. They would understand, immediately and without needing to be taught, that the absence of water there is not thirst. The absence of plants is not famine. The absence of artifacts is not poverty. The slowness is not stagnation. The loudness is not distress. The emptiness is not loneliness in the way a smaller, wetter, quicker form of life would imagine it.

A lesser benevolence might try to comfort the plateau by making it resemble a garden. A wise benevolence would see that the plateau is already complete in a mode that does not require comfort of that kind.

So they would do nothing that a builder would do.

No temple would be raised. No road would be laid. No wall, no marker, no instrument, no shrine, no carved word, no threshold, no roof, no boundary stone. They would not introduce the violence of made things into a world whose entire holiness depends on production without manufacture. They would not give the beings tools, because tools would be an insult to what they already are. They would not give them cities, because the distance between them is not a failure of society. They would not give them speech, because the industrial chord of their bodies is already a language of pressure, heat, charge, and endurance.

They would not make the beings more legible to tender eyes.

That would be the first discipline of their wisdom: not to confuse kindness with addition.

But a benevolent and wise power would not simply watch and withdraw. That, too, could become its own cruelty. There are places so vast that attention itself becomes a force. There are beings so slow that to be truly seen is to be changed.

So if they did anything, they would begin by changing the quality of witness.

The ordinary light would remain ordinary. That is important. The blessing would not arrive as golden radiance, not as halo, not as supernatural glow. The light would stay hard, factual, unmagical. But it would become, in some exact and unobtrusive way, attentive.

A person standing on the salt crust would not see a miracle. They would only feel that the clarity of the world had deepened. That the glare was no longer merely indifferent. That the brightness did not simply reveal, but held. The shadows would still be short and severe. The heat would still burn. The mineral surfaces would still blaze and shimmer. But the place would no longer feel like a fact abandoned to itself. It would feel like a fact known by something large enough to bear it.

The great being — the one that looks like a mesa until its shadow separates from the logic of the land — would feel this first.

Not as a voice. Not as a face. Not as warmth added to its thermal gradients. It would feel it as a new difference.

That is how such a being would encounter the sacred: as a gradient it had not known existed.

The power would not speak to it, because speech would require atmosphere shaped into intention, and the being does not live in that kind of air. Instead, they would allow a minute alteration in the field around the being. A slight asymmetry in charge. A subtle change in the conductance of the dust along one flank. A harmonic in the ground that was not present before, so faint that it could be mistaken for the settling of stone, except that it does not settle. It listens.

The being’s metallic filaments would brighten almost imperceptibly. Its internal pistons would keep their ancient rhythm, but somewhere inside its ceramic depths a new pressure would form: not hunger, not fear, not desire, but orientation. It would turn, over decades, toward the place where the attention seems strongest. Not in worship. In adjustment. It would do what it always does: it would follow a difference. But this difference would not be merely thermal or electrical. It would be the difference between existing and being known.

That would be the first gift.

Not salvation. Recognition.

The second gift would be more difficult, because the place is not only strange but damaged in its descriptions of itself. Reality there has become unreliable without becoming theatrical. Sounds arrive from the wrong direction. Shadows lag. Distance behaves like memory. Objects revise themselves under observation. The power, if they are wise, would not repair this by making the plateau rational. To make it rational would be to make it less itself. They would not straighten the horizon, or force sound to obey simple propagation, or make every object remain consistent from angle to angle. That would be another kind of conquest.

Instead, they would heal the worst of the instability without removing its strangeness.

They would make the inconsistencies gentle.

If a sound arrived from an event that had not yet happened, it would arrive not as menace but as warning, or as preparation. If a ridge appeared near when it was far, the error would not trap the traveler in despair; it would become a local grammar of approach, something that could be learned rather than only suffered. If a shadow lagged behind its source, it would feel less like betrayal and more like memory. If a path returned to the same salt flat after hours of walking, it would do so not as punishment but as closure. The world would remain wrong in places, but its wrongness would no longer feel predatory.

That is what wisdom would understand: the plateau does not need to become consistent. It needs to become trustworthy.

There is a difference between a broken law and a cruel law. The power would leave the brokenness, but remove the cruelty.

They would let the beings know one another.
