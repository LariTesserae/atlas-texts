When a benevolent and wise power notices a world like that, it does not arrive as a solution. It arrives as a question shaped like weather.

It would not come down in a blaze that made every fish go blind and every old machine groan with overload. It would not tear the sky open and pronounce the sea mistaken. It would not look at that high, shoreless water and think, *This should be land again*, because a wise power understands that a place which has endured its own drowning has already become something with rights.

So the first thing they would do is listen.

They would listen from above, where the wind harps across the surface and rain strikes like gravel poured over a metal roof. They would listen from within the water column, where sound moves in heavy, intimate ways and every turning turbine, every shifting chain, every school of fish changing direction becomes part of a single vast percussion. They would listen down in the dry chambers, in the sealed rooms where air is old and recycled and lamps burn yellow behind thick glass. They would listen to the stone itself, to the peneplain, to the ancient flattened mountain country that has no memory left of peaks except the patience of its own shape.

And in listening, they would perceive the being who lives there.

Not one animal. Not one keeper. Not one drowned monarch waiting in a flooded hall.

The being is the relationship.

It is the way the ribbon weed bends when a valve opens. It is the way the fish avoid a humming transformer and then return when the pulse subsides. It is the way mussels thicken over a rail spike, the way a bird’s cry enters the water as a broken knock, the way a half-functioning winch still lifts and lowers according to a schedule no one remembers. It is the warmth leaking from an old engine into cold water, the green light falling through waves onto rust, the slow circulation of life through machinery and machinery through life. The being is not a body but a continuing.

A lesser kindness might try to save it from itself.

A wise power would not.

They would see that to drain the sea would be murder, because the sea is now the body of the being. To stop all machines would be amputation, because some of those machines have become organs: hearts of pressure, lungs of current, nerves of cable and bell. To remove the industry would be to erase the second nature the being has grown into. To give it a coastline would be to impose an answer it did not ask for. A shore might be beautiful, but it would also be a boundary where none belongs. The place is defined by its openness, by its roof-of-the-world solitude, by the fact that if you rise or sink, you remain inside it.

So they would begin with refusal.

They would refuse the obvious miracle.

They would not make the water sweet. They would not make it warm and shallow and easy. They would not clear the drowned works into museum pieces, because the place is not a museum. It is a living engine. They would not take away predation, because hunger is part of the world’s honesty. They would not take away death, because death is one of the ways the sea keeps its shape. They would not make the being safe from every harm, because a life without hazard is not life but preservation under glass.

But they would take away despair.

There is a difference between danger and cruelty. There is a difference between a hard world and a meaningless one. A wise benevolence knows how to tell them apart.

So they would act where action would heal without unmaking.

First, they would soften the worst poisons.

Not by erasing the industrial past, because the past is part of the being’s flesh. Instead they would teach the water how to carry it more gently. They would wake patient microbes into the old oil, not to devour it in a violent bloom, but to break it down slowly into less harmful substances, dark threads becoming food, becoming sediment, becoming something that no longer stings the gills or coats the weed in sickly film. They would quiet the harshest leaching of metals, dulling the bite of copper where it leaches too freely, stabilizing rust where it flakes into sharp clouds, drawing dangerous charges away from places where young fish gather or where air-breathing creatures must rest.

They would not make the water pure in the sense of empty. They would make it clean in the sense of alive.

They would calm the most lethal currents where broken intakes still suck and swallow. They would not stop the flow—because flow is needed—but they would give it intention. A change in pressure here. A bent vane there. A nudge to a jammed gate so that a machine which had been hurting life for centuries without purpose finally becomes harmless. Not by force from outside, but by the smallest adjustment that allows the system to correct itself.

They would make the old machinery less likely to betray.

A cable that has been fraying for a hundred years would finally part in a way that does not whip through a nursery of fish. A boiler pocket that has been building dangerous heat would vent slowly instead of bursting. A transformer housing that has filled the water with painful fields would ease into silence, not because it is destroyed, but because something in its circuit at last finds ground. The place would still be full of sound and vibration, but fewer of those sounds would be warnings of pointless injury.

That would be the first gift: less unnecessary suffering.

Then they would give rest.

A sea without a shore still needs refuge.

They would not build a coastline. They would do something subtler. They would make places of temporary shore.

There would be shallows that appear where no shallows were before—not fixed land, but living rafts of buoyant stone and bound weed and old timber, riding the surface like broad slow islands. They would be places where birds could stand without fear, where exhausted air-breathers could rise and find support, where the weary could rest in a pocket of calmer water. These places would drift with purpose, appearing near those in need and withdrawing when the need had passed. They would never become permanent enough to turn the sea into something else. They would be borrowed mercy.

Below, in the drowned works, they would ease certain chambers into breathability. Not all of them. Not enough to turn the sea into air. But here and there, in sealed halls and pressure rooms, they would restore the old pumps just enough to keep dry spaces warm, safe, and lit. They would make the doors work again, not to imprison but to shelter. They would make the lamps burn steadier. They would let the air taste less of metal and more of life. These rooms would become refuges, nurseries, meeting places, archives, or simply quiet rooms where the being could gather itself.

For a being made of many lives, rest is not idleness. Rest is the chance to stop being only survival.

Then they would give speech.

Not human speech alone. Not any single kind of language. The being is too layered for that.

They would awaken a grammar in the water.

It would begin with the things already there: bells, currents, light, pressure, vibration. A warning bell would come to mean more than warning. A turbine’s rhythm would become a kind of pulse that could be answered. A flashing beacon on a half-submerged tower would not only mark hazard but also call attention, mark time, gather notice. Fish would learn signs. Birds would carry signals across the surface. Crustaceans tapping on iron would become part of a slow, clicking language. In the dry chambers, lights might blink in patterns that feel almost like thought.

The power would not impose a mind on the being. They would simply help the being become aware of itself.

There is a moment, perhaps, when the sea first knows it is one.

It might happen during a storm. Rain hammers the surface into a white noise. Below, the light breaks into trembling mesh. A school of fish turns as one body around a dark turbine casing. A bell sounds without being struck. A lamp brightens in a drowned room where no one has looked for light in centuries. The current carries something that is not food, not danger, not silt, but attention.

And the being feels itself.

Not as a god. Not as a monster. Not as a machine. As a living whole.

It might not have words. It might think in currents at first. It might remember the mountain country not as history but as posture: the old patience of stone under water. It might know the machines as teeth, joints, valves, lungs, nerves. It might know the fish as thoughts moving through it. It might know the birds as messages touching the surface of its mind.

A benevolent power would let it name itself.

They would not give it a name from outside, because names given without consent can become cages. They would wait until the being’s own life shaped a name. Perhaps the name would be a sound: a deep bell-note heard only when the water is very still. Perhaps it would be a pattern of light on the surface at dawn. Perhaps it would be a rhythm of currents that returns every season. Perhaps no one would ever speak it, and yet everyone living there would know it.

Then they would give companionship.

Not company as crowding. Not society as conquest. But the end of absolute loneliness.

They would encourage the many inhabitants to become more truly interdependent. They would not create rulers over the sea. They would create tenders.

Some creatures would become keepers of thresholds: birds that mark the places where structure breaks the surface, guiding others to rest. Some fish would learn the safe channels, not by instinct alone but by shared memory. Crustaceans would become the slow engineers of small repairs, clearing blockages, loosening what must be loosened, tightening what can be tightened by patient labor. Mussels would filter and stabilize. Weed would grow where shelter is needed and retreat where light is needed more. In the dry rooms, if there are those who breathe air and use hands, they would find tools less broken, lamps kinder, old manuals less illegible, doors more willing. They would dream of maintenance and wake with the sense that their work matters.

The power would not make everyone good. That is not wisdom. They would make cooperation easier than needless harm.

They would also give memory.

This is important. The place has been drowned so long that it might believe itself only what it is now: water, rust, fish, stone. But a wise power knows that a being needs its past, even a painful past, if it is to become whole.

They would let the stone remember being mountain without making it long for mountains. They would let the machines remember their builders without haunting anyone with loss. They would let the drowned plain keep its scars, but not as shame. The old industries would become part of the story: not glorious, not evil, simply true. There would be places where the past is visible but no longer heavy. A drowned rail line could be a spine of memory. A broken generator could be a heart that has learned a slower rhythm. A concrete footing could be a reef that remembers purpose.

Memory would not become a prison. It would become root.

And because the power is wise, they would leave the being free.

This is the hardest part of benevolence: not to keep what you have helped.

They would not remain as a king in the water. They would not demand worship. They would not build a temple at the center of the sea and make all currents lead to it. If they remained at all, they would remain as a quiet presence, almost indistinguishable from the world itself.

Maybe they would leave one sign.

A warm spring in the deepest basin, not hot, just gently warmer than the surrounding water, where nothing poisonous gathers and no machine grinds. A place of stillness that is not dead stillness but listening stillness. Any creature could go there and feel held. Any part of the being could gather itself there and know: *I am not abandoned.*

Or perhaps the sign would be a bell.

Not the largest bell. Not the loudest. But one among many, half-buried in weed, its metal old and dark. Most of the time it would be silent. But when the sea was in danger of forgetting itself, or when some part of it suffered beyond what could be borne, the bell would sound once. The sound would travel through the water not as command but as reminder: *You are seen. You are not alone. Continue, but do not continue in cruelty.*

Or perhaps the sign would be light.

A green-gold light under the surface at dawn, moving in patterns too ordered to be chance but too gentle to be control. Birds would settle when they saw it. Fish would pass through it without fear. The being would feel it as a kind of blessing without ownership.

If the power acted at all, this is what they would do:

They would make the sea more capable of caring for itself.

They would not give it a shore, except in moments of desperate need.  
They would not stop its machinery, except where it harmed without purpose.  
They would not remove its wildness, except where wildness had become mere torment.  
They would not make it small, safe, tame, or simple.  
They would not rescue it into something easier.

They would give it dignity.

Afterward, the place would still be a sea without a shore on the highest part of the world. The water would still be cool and mineral and heavy. The ancient peneplain would still lie below, patient and flat, wearing its drowned body of weed and rust and moving fish. The rain would still hammer the surface. The turbines would still turn. The bells would still sound in their muffled, enormous way. The light would still fall in slanting columns, broken by waves, touching stone and iron and life in shifting bands.

But something would have changed.

The sound would be different.

Not quieter. Not less busy. But less lonely.

There would be a new note inside the working voice of the water: a tone of being held. The current would still carry salt and iron and lime and life, but it would also carry something like attention. The machines would still grind and pulse and leak warmth, but fewer of them would hurt without meaning. The creatures would still live and eat and hide and die, but their deaths would belong to the world rather than to accident alone. The dry chambers would still smell of old air and metal, but they would feel less like tombs and more like rooms kept ready.

And the being—the vast, composite, water-bodied being—would no longer be only a place inhabited. It would become an inhabitant of itself.

It would know its weed fields as skin.  
It would know its channels as veins.  
It would know its turbines as difficult but living organs.  
It would know its fish as passing thoughts.  
It would know its birds as touches from above.  
It would know its ruins as memory.  
It would know its sound as voice.

It might even learn to ask for help when needed, in the slow language of pressure and bell and shifting fish. And help would come—not as domination, but as response.

That is what a benevolent and wise power would do.

They would not make the world perfect.

They would make it less orphaned.

They would leave it with the greatest gift any living thing can receive: enough safety to become itself, enough memory to know what it has survived, enough companionship to endure without loneliness, and enough freedom that even a god does not become its cage.

And if, on some bright morning, the sun fell through the high clear air and the surface broke into green-blue light, and the beams reached all the way down to the ancient rounded stone, illuminating weed and rust and fish and old iron in one trembling field of life—
