If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention to that high drowned industrial sea, it would not begin by making the world gentle.

It would see immediately that gentleness, in the ordinary sense, might be violence there. It would not drain the water from the uplifted mountains. It would not replace mineral coldness with green softness. It would not silence the immense industrial sound by erasing the machines, because the sound and pressure are not merely affliction; they are part of the architecture by which the sea exists. To undo them suddenly would be to collapse the world around the being that lives in it.

So the first act would be restraint.

Then attention.

A wise power would not announce itself as a face, a voice, or a blinding light. A face would be meaningless there. A voice would be another pressure wave. A blinding light would be another form of exposure. Instead, it would lower itself into the grammar of the place: pressure, adhesion, mineral taste, micro-current, vibration, heat. It would become perceptible only as a difference that does not wound.

It would begin by learning what it is looking at.

The being who lives there may not be one body in the way softer worlds understand bodies. It may be a distributed creature, a slow pattern spread through colorless films, threadlike fungal mats, silica-shelled grazers, tube-dwellers, machine-hums, and mineral crusts. Its life may be less a single organism than a way of persisting: adhesion as identity, patience as thought, pressure as memory. If it has a self, that self is likely not a voice crying out, but a long, tenuous holding-on.

Therefore the power would not ask, “How can I make this place beautiful?”

It would ask, “What does this place need to remain itself without unnecessary cruelty?”

The first visible change would be small.

In one region near a drowning intake tower, the water would grow fractionally warmer where the being’s films are thinnest. Not hot enough to change its chemistry, only warm enough to ease the strain of maintaining itself against the cold. Around a field of sharp new scree, where moving currents scrape living films against freshly broken stone, mineral water would seep through micro-cracks and deposit thin glassy rinds, sheathing the worst edges in smooth silica. The cliffs would still be knife-edged in their grandeur, but the blades that cut without purpose would be blunted.

Near the machines, where cavitation storms churn the water white and tear at microbial crusts, the power would introduce counter-harmonics. It would not silence the roar entirely, because silence might be its own kind of death in a world built on pressure. But it would take the shriek out of it. The storms would still exist; they would simply stop being massacres. The white churning would learn to exhaust itself in empty chambers before reaching the living surfaces. Pressure fronts would still move through the sea like thunder, but when they approached fragile colonies, they would fold around them rather than scour them away.
