## The Attention

There are powers that notice only what shines, or what sings, or what reaches upward with hands open in petition. This is not that kind of power.

It is old enough to know that holiness does not always look like flourishing. It is wise enough to know that a sealed thing is not necessarily a trapped thing. It is benevolent without being sentimental, which means it does not confuse kindness with intrusion.

It notices the Dry Nave not because the Dry Nave calls, but because the Dry Nave has achieved a kind of completeness rare even among living worlds: a place where every silence has been given a use, where every body has become a question addressed to stone, where the center is kept empty not out of lack but out of reverence.

The power sees—if seeing is the right word for such a power—that the being living there is not one creature among others, but the whole arrangement: the listeners, the settled ancestors, the terraces, the vault, the exact returning of sound, the preserved emptiness at the middle. The true inhabitant is the nave’s capacity to know itself.

A lesser benevolence, seeing them sealed below a riverless plain, would say: *Let them be freed.*

A lesser wisdom, seeing their dryness, would say: *Let them be given water.*

A lesser imagination, seeing their eyeless faces, would say: *Let them be given light.*

But this power understands that rain would be a catastrophe dressed as grace. It would soften the salts, blur the fine crystal in the settled ones, turn dust to mud, make the echoes wet and indistinct. It would give them, for a few generations, something to long for, and in doing so take from them the holiness of what they are.

It understands that to open the roof would be to make the Dry Nave into a spectacle, a curiosity, a ruin. Light would not enlighten them; it would occupy them. Their darkness is not ignorance. It is the medium in which their attention becomes possible.

It understands that eyes would be too loud. Eyes would turn them toward surfaces, edges, shapes, and away from returns. To give them eyes would be to teach them to mistrust their own listening.

So the power does not come as storm. It does not come as flood. It does not come as face, or voice, or burning presence.

It comes as a change in the quality of silence.

## What It Would Refrain From Doing

First, it would do the hardest thing: it would not immediately act.

It would not send a sign that demanded interpretation. It would not place a miracle in the center, because the center is not theirs to fill, and because a miracle in that place would become a weight. It would not make itself an object of worship. Worship would be a violence there. It would introduce hierarchy into an acoustics of placement. It would make the empty center into a throne.

It would not speak its name.

Names are hooks. A god that names itself in such a room becomes a pressure, a claim, a gravitational center. The listeners would begin to arrange themselves around the name rather than around resonance. Their language would thicken into obedience. Their clicks would become petitions. Their stillness would become waiting for an answer.

The power would not want an answer.

It would not want gratitude.

It would not want them to become dependent on being noticed.

If it acts at all, it must act in a way that leaves them more themselves, not less.

So it refrains.

This refusal is not indifference. It is the first form of care.

A foolish love touches everything it sees. A wise love learns the shape of what it loves before laying a hand upon it.

## The First Act: Listening

The power would listen.

Not as one listens to a report, or a prayer, or a curiosity. It would listen as the listeners listen: by allowing the room to become the primary thing.

It would learn the acoustics of each body on the terraces. It would learn which elder returns sound sharpened, which returns sound softened, which carries a small rust-note from old iron in its veins. It would learn the density of the settled ones, how their crystallized silence adds gravity to every echo. It would learn the positions of the young flakes, not yet fully answering, tuned by decades of humming into the possibility of life.

It would learn the dry chord.

And when, at intervals longer than seasons, the listeners make their sound together—the clicks, the backward breaths, the palms against stone, the faint ringing of mineral throats—the power would wait for that moment as the only permissible door.

Because the dry chord is their consent.

It is the moment they offer themselves to the room.

Only then would the power act, and even then its action would be so slight that it might not be called action at all.

It would not add a voice.

It would deepen the return.

Perhaps the difference would be this: when the chord goes out and strikes the vault, the vault’s answer would arrive with a fraction more roundness. When the walls split the sound into ribbons, the ribbons would carry, beneath their usual mineral coldness, a warmth too subtle to be felt as heat. When the terraces answer with the weight of the settled ones, the ancestors would seem not farther away but nearer, as if their stillness had become a kind of attention.

The listeners would not say, *Something has entered.*

They would say, in their altered-return tongue: *the room has remembered us more deeply tonight.*

Or perhaps: *the shape is kinder than we knew.*

Or simply: *dry is not empty.*

And the power would accept being misunderstood, if misunderstanding preserved them.

It would not need to be recognized.

## The Second Act: Guarding Without Possessing

After listening, the power would guard.

But it would guard them in their own mode, not in the mode of a gardener who fences a bed against all harm.

It would not make the Dry Nave eternal. Eternity would be another violence. It would freeze their becoming. It would turn their maintenance into mere repetition. Even holiness needs an ending shaped like completion, not accident.

What it would guard against is violation.

It would notice the hidden stresses in the rock above: the hairline faults that might, in some future century, let the plain collapse without warning. It would not remove the possibility of change, but it would slow the careless catastrophes. It would ease one pressure here, cool one mineral tension there, persuade a seam to remain a seam instead of becoming a wound. It would not stop time; it would refuse to let time be cruel.

If water ever threatened—some impossible remnant of the upper world finding its way down through a sudden fracture—the power would not meet it with rain or blessing, but with stone. It would guide the moisture away. It would keep the Dry Nave dry.

This may seem small. It is not small.
