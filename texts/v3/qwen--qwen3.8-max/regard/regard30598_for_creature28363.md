If a benevolent and wise power noticed this place, they would not first act.

They would first refrain.

They would not come as fire comes, not as storm comes, not as revelation comes with a sound that breaks what it reveals. Such a power, if truly wise, would understand that some places are not improved by being chosen. Some lives are not completed by being called. Some silences are not waiting for a voice.

So they would arrive in the only way that would not wound the hollow: as attention.

At first, nothing would change.

A droplet would still gather along a leaf-edge.  
A bead would still tremble, hold, release.  
A pool would still receive the sky without asking the sky to remain.  
The hanging curtains would still collect moisture from the humid air, and the stone would still sweat its slow mineral breath, and the warm dampness would still lie over everything like a patient hand.

But if one were sensitive enough, one might feel a difference in the quality of the stillness. Not a presence added to the hollow. Not an intruder. Rather, the stillness would become recognized. The way a room feels different when someone has truly listened in it. The way stone feels different when it has been seen without being claimed.

The power would look for the being who lived there.

Being wise, they would not expect a face.

They would not search the overhang for eyes. They would not listen for breath. They would not wait for a figure to rise from among the broad-leaved cistern plants, or for something ancient and animal to emerge from the shadows with slow ceremonial steps. They would know better. They would know that life does not always gather itself into a shape that can be greeted.

They would look instead at the pattern.

They would see the microbial films darkening the stone when moisture returned. They would see the cistern plants standing apart in their separate damp territories, each holding its small reservoirs, each spilling through the same worn notch when full. They would see the curtains hanging from the upper rock, fine as hair, releasing their gathered droplets with faint ticks. They would see the coin-leaved rosettes drifting in shallow water when the pools were high, settling into cracks when the water lowered. They would see the glossy caps opening after long intervals of humidity, giving their scent to no one. They would see the water threading from seam to basin, from basin to pool, from pool to margin, connecting everything without hurry.

And then, gradually, they would perceive the whole.

Not as a body, exactly. Not as a creature. Not as a mind with a center. But as a manner of being.

A distributed patience.

A green and mineral quiet.

A life made of seepage and repetition, of overflow and restraint, of old erosion and slower growth. A being whose thoughts, if it had thoughts, were the worn paths of water. A being whose memory was stains on stone. A being whose desire, if it could be called desire, was simply to continue in balance.

The power would see the threshold, too.

They would see the sharp flakes lying in the dust. The rounded cobbles. The flat stones that might have become grinding surfaces. The fibrous strands that might have been twisted into cord. The broad leaves that might have been folded or carried. The shallow basins that might have been used. All the materials of beginning. All the matter of early making.

And they would understand that the holiest thing in the place was that nothing had yet crossed over.

No hand had struck stone against stone.  
No edge had been selected.  
No tool had been imagined into being.  
No fire had been fed here.  
No hunger had sharpened the quiet into fear.  
No voice had named the pools.

The hollow lived in the pause before making.

The power would recognize that this pause was not poverty. It was not infancy. It was not failure to become. It was its own completeness.

Because they were benevolent, they would want to give.

That is the first temptation of kindness: to add.

They would consider what could be given.

They could give eyes to the basin. Little lives with sight. Creatures that watched from the wet margins. Eyes that opened in the dark translucent centers of the plants. Eyes in the pools. Eyes that could see the light on the stone and know it was beautiful.

But wisdom would stay their hand.

Eyes would bring watching. Watching would bring vigilance. Vigilance would bring fear. Fear would bring flight. Flight would bring the need for hiding, for speed, for defense. The silence would become a different kind of silence: not the silence of completeness, but the silence of held breath.

So they would not give eyes.

They could give voices. A small calling from the curtains. A trilling in the dripping water. A low resonance when the air thickened before condensation. A language of moisture and temperature. A way for the plants to signal to one another, for the pools to answer, for the stone to speak in mineral notes.

But wisdom would stay their hand.

Voice would create distance. It would make presence into message. It would turn the hollow’s sounds into signs meant for someone. The droplets would no longer simply fall; they would be heard as calls. The silence would no longer be full in itself. It would become a space waiting to be filled.

So they would not give voices.

They could give memory. Not the memory of stone, which the basin already had, but the memory of self. A sense of yesterday. A sense of continuation not merely as process but as identity. The knowledge: I am this. I have endured. I am here.

But wisdom would stay their hand.

Memory could become grief. It could make the basin mourn its losses, count its dryings, remember every leaf that detached and every pool that lowered. It could create the burden of time as a story rather than as rhythm. The hollow’s peace lay partly in its immediacy. It did not need to know itself in order to be itself.

So they would not give memory.

They could give hands.

This would be the greatest temptation.

Hands would awaken the flakes. Hands would test the edges. Hands would lift the cobbles and feel their weight. Hands would fold leaves, carry water, weave fibers, stack stones.
