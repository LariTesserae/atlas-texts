They would come as wetness, not as brightness.

If such a power turned its attention toward that high, saturated body of green and water, its first act would be to make itself small enough to be received. It would not tear the cloud-ceiling. It would not arrive with a voice like dry thunder, nor with a light so sharp that the moss would curl away from it. A benevolence wise enough to love such a place would understand that revelation there would be a kind of drought.

So it would enter by the only gate the place trusts: water.

A bead would form on a leaf edge and hold a little longer than usual.  
A film of condensation would taste faintly of recognition.  
The warmth in the air would deepen by less than a breath.  
Somewhere in a black pool, a ripple would pass without wind.

That would be the arrival.

At first, they would do almost nothing.

That would be the first wisdom.

For a long time—long enough for cup-keepers to clear and refill a hundred leaf-basins, long enough for a walking mound to cross one broad lawn and leave its slow ridge behind—they would only listen. They would feel the drop-carriers climbing their ancient grooves with their burdens of water. They would feel the pool-striders pressing dimples into standing pools as if thinking in constellations. They would follow the root-serpents through flooded channels and know where the water wants to go before it knows itself. They would sense the great filterers breathing in the deep places, raising and lowering the marsh’s inner tide by increments so slow that centuries would seem like a single inhalation.

They would notice the Tenders.

They would notice the way the Tenders place their broad pads against roots and ask, without words, for water to move here, for a wound to close there, for a hollow to become ready. They would feel the old chemical trails laid down in water films, some still readable after generations. They would understand that memory in that place is not kept in monuments but in channels, not in stone but in moisture, not in commands but in habits repeated until the world itself grows grooves to receive them.

And they would not interrupt.

They would not appear in a form that demands attention. They would not make the Tenders bow, or tremble, or abandon their work. They would know that in such a body, to be seen too suddenly is to be dried out, separated, made into an object apart from the whole. So they would remain for a while as a quality of weather: a rain that seems more attentive than rain; a mist that lingers where it is needed; a warmth that arrives like a hand near a wound but does not touch without being asked.

If the place accepted them—and acceptance there would not be spoken, but would appear as a water film holding their presence instead of shedding it—then they would begin to act.

Even then, they would act almost gently.

They would not remake the place.

They would not drain the pools to make them clearer.  
They would not open the canopy to give the lower leaves more light if that light would cost the moss its softness.  
They would not give the slow creatures speed, or the soft creatures armor, or the gentle mouths teeth of conquest.  
They would not teach the Tenders to build in metal, or to keep records in hard substances that refuse to rot.  
They would not make the marsh young again.

They would understand that kindness to one part can be violence to the whole. They would not mistake comfort for flourishing, nor safety for stillness, nor permanence for health. They would see that the place is already complete in the way old bodies are complete: not without loss, not without hunger, not without death, but arranged around its own nature so deeply that any abrupt correction would wound it.

So they would ask, in the only language that place could accept: *What needs help?*

The answer would come slowly.

It would come as a channel beginning to narrow faster than the Tenders can clear it.  
As a nursery hollow drying for one interval too many.  
As an old stem whose wound weeps thin fluid instead of closing.  
As a sour smell in a pool where the water has stopped moving.  
As the fatigue of a Tender who has carried droplets up the same root-fiber for longer than ordinary memory.  
As the tiny, almost invisible panic of a small creature caught in a closing membrane.

The wise power would not fix all of these at once. That would make the place dependent on a presence outside itself. Instead, they would do what the best gardeners do: they would strengthen the body’s own capacity to continue.

They would begin with memory.

Because the place remembers by water, and water can shift, they would give it a deeper remembering. The rare stones—the embarrassed basalt knobs, the buried quartz spines, the old mineral anomalies furred with moss—would not be made proud or exposed. They would be asked, in the patient mineral way of stones, to become reservoirs of pattern.

Not monuments. Not tablets. Not dry inscriptions.

Rather, a kind of taste stored in stone.

If a channel were lost for a century, root-tips touching that stone could find in it a faint mineral record of where water once moved. If a basin dried and was forgotten, moss growing over the old rock could recover, through slow chemistry, the memory of the shape it had once held. The marsh would not become a place of stone-memory in the way dry worlds are, but it would gain a backup within its own flesh: a way to recall itself after long forgetting.

The Tenders might notice this first in dreams, if their stillness can be called dreaming. They would press their pads to a moss-covered knob and feel, beneath the usual cool dampness, a long, slow pulse that is not root-pressure and not animal heat. It would feel like the land remembering. They would not worship it. They would simply begin to include it in their work, as one includes a helpful elder whose speech is strange but whose intentions are kind.

Then the power would turn to dying.

They would not abolish death there. That would be a grave mistake. The marsh is made of absorption; every ending is a redistribution. To remove death would be to stop the water from moving. It would fill the cups until they choked. It would thicken the moss until it smothered its own breathing pores. It would turn continuation into accumulation, and accumulation into suffocation.

But they would soften terror.

Not all pain. Not all hunger. Not the necessary closing of cups or the quiet enveloping of a body that has wandered into a waiting membrane. But the extra suffering that adds panic to transition—the frantic struggle that makes death more violent than it needs to be—they would ease that.

They would put a mercy into the water film.

When a small creature became caught, a warmth would move through the surrounding moisture: a chemical sentence older than fear, saying in effect, *You are entering, not being erased.* The struggle would shorten. The absorbed body would relax sooner. The predator, if such a word can be used there, would not become crueler or gentler by decree; it would simply receive a nutrient touched with less alarm. The filterers in the deep pools would draw that nutrient upward and, in their vast slow way, know that something had been given.

No one would see it as a miracle. It would feel only like the place becoming a little more tender toward its own endings.

Then the power would consider outsiders.

Not because outsiders are necessarily evil, but because dry worlds have dry hungers. There are beings elsewhere who see wetness as waste, softness as weakness, age as inefficiency, silence as an invitation to impose sound. There are those who would come with fire, with drains, with maps that cut living bodies into parcels, with tools that refuse to listen. There are those who would study the marsh by wounding it, or save it by freezing it into something no longer alive.

The wise power would not meet them with vengeance. Vengeance is too dry, too hot, too quick.

Instead, they would make the place difficult to possess.

Any who entered with domination in their intention would find the marsh responding not with malice but with saturation. Their tools would grow furred with moss faster than they could clean them. Their fires would become warm, damp stones that refused to catch. Their instruments would bead with condensation until lenses blurred and metal softened with green bloom. Their compasses would drift, not violently, but persistently, as if the place had its own slow opinions about direction. Their sleep would fill with the sound of dripping until they began, whether they wished to or not, to move at the marsh’s pace.

Some would become frightened and leave. The marsh would let them leave.

Some would become furious and try harder. The marsh would become wider around them, not cruelly, but as a soft body becomes impossible to grasp. Paths would soften. Distances would blur. The air would thicken with mist until every attempt at straight-line conquest became a circle back to where they began.

But those who entered with humility—who were willing to be wet, to be slow, to be taught by touch and scent and long patience—those the place might accept. For them, the power would not block the way. It might even guide a cup of warm water toward their lips. It might send a drop-carrier across their path as a sign that small labor matters. It might let them sleep in a hollow where the moss rose gently around them, so they woke feeling less separate than before.

In this way, the power would protect the marsh not by walling it off, but by making it impossible to approach without being changed.

Then they would give the Tenders one new thing.

Only one.

Not fire. Not metal. Not a city. Not a scripture. Not a hierarchy. Not a promise of dominion over the other creatures. Only a signal.

In the root-tapping grammar they already use—quick taps for danger, long presses for water, broken rhythms for gathering—the power would add a third pulse: a way to say, across too great a distance, *I am becoming unable.* Or perhaps, *This place needs help before I can give it.*

It would travel through the living ground like a bead moving along a thread. Not loud. Not commanding. Not a call that compels obedience. But a signal that need exists somewhere in the body, so that repair can move toward it before the wound becomes too large for local hands.

In seasons when waterways shift and groups become separated by dry routes or tangled overgrowth, this pulse would keep them from losing one another. It would not create leaders. It would not create law. It would only make loneliness less likely, and repair more timely.

The Tenders would incorporate it without ceremony. They would teach it to their young by placing their pads against them and sending the rhythm through warm skin. Later, they might not even remember that it had ever been given. It would simply become part of the marsh’s own language, as if it had always been there.

That would please the wise power.

A gift is most successful when it becomes indistinguishable from the nature of the one who receives it.

Sometimes, perhaps, the power would concentrate itself near a single being.

Not because one being matters more than the whole, but because whole bodies are easier to love abstractly than closely, and wisdom that refuses closeness is not yet kind.

They might choose an old Tender who has spent centuries clearing the same root-channel, whose skin is dark with symbiotic moss, whose bladder-pouches are heavy with water, whose movements have become so slow that the moss rises behind them almost without delay. The power would not appear in glory. It might simply become another body working nearby: low, broad, damp, unremarkable, helping to lift a fallen fiber too heavy for one pair of limbs.

The old Tender would sense something unusual only as a difference in pressure. A root would answer more quickly than expected. A bead of water would arrive exactly where a wound needed it. A scent in the film would say, not in words but in chemistry, *You are not carrying this alone.*

If the Tender asked, by touch, *What are you?* the power might answer as a long, warm pulse that means neither name nor command, only presence.

If the Tender asked, *What do you want?* the answer would be: *Nothing that is not already here.*

If the Tender asked, *Why are you here?* the answer might come as a slight increase in the sweetness of water, as if to say: *Because this is beautiful, and because even beautiful bodies should not be left entirely without witness.*

Then the power would withdraw again into the general wetness, leaving no shrine, no mark, no demand. The old Tender would continue working. Perhaps they would tap the new signal once into the root-web, not from need, but from gratitude. The ground would carry it away.

Once in an age so long that even walking mounds would forget the last occurrence, the power might allow one wonder.

They would part the spore-haze by no more than the width of a hair. They would not open the sky violently. They would not expose the marsh to harsh distance. But they would let one point of starlight fall, cool and thin, into a black pool.

The pool would tremble.

The Tenders would gather around it, not in fear, but in attention. They would taste the difference in the water: a clarity not born of their own canopy, a coldness not produced by local weather, a signal from a patience different from theirs. The pool-striders would press dimples around the reflected light as if trying to read it. The bladder-drifters overhead might rise and fall in response to some minute change in the air. Somewhere deep, a great filterer would exhale slowly, and the level of the pool would rise just enough to touch the roots of those gathered there.

No one would be commanded to worship. Worship would be too dry, too vertical, too separate. But there might be a long silence, which in that place is the deepest form of acknowledgment.

Then the haze would close. The star would vanish. The pool would digest the event over centuries, leaving behind only a faint mineral sweetness in its sediment. The Tenders would remember it as a season in which the water tasted of farawayness. They would not be made dissatisfied by it. They would simply know, in some tissue-deep way, that patience has other dialects.

And if the place were ever threatened by an ending too large for local repair—not soon, perhaps not for eons, but even old bodies have horizons—the wise power would not promise brittle permanence. They would not freeze the marsh into eternal sameness, because that would be another form of death.

But they would ensure that if an ending came, it would not come as severance.

If the warmth began at last to fail, if the high plain shifted, if the sky above changed beyond what the canopy could buffer, the marsh would not be allowed to die as a wound. It would be helped to release itself: water into cloud, spores into high air, pattern into memory, chemistry into the long circulation of worlds. Some drop-carrier’s groove might become a fossil trace in another place. Some root-pulse might be carried in the memory of stone to a younger wet world. The Tenders, if they still endured, might find themselves guided toward a hollow where the water tasted of elsewhere, and they would not know whether they were remembering or being transplanted.

Even then, there would be no panic.

Only the last long exhalation of a body that had learned, at the end, that continuation can take forms not previously known.

This is what they would do, if anything.

Not rescue.  
Not conquest.  
Not revelation.  
Not the imposition of a better shape.

They would deepen what is already good.

They would make memory more resilient than drought.  
They would make dying less lonely.  
They would make the place impossible to possess by those who come to take.  
They would give the Tenders one new thread of communion.  
They would protect the marsh’s right to remain itself.  
They would bear witness without demanding to be seen.  
They would bless the ordinary until the ordinary becomes unmistakably holy.

And if, after a thousand thousand slow seasons, an outsider were somehow able to ask the inhabitants, “What has this power done for you?”

The Tenders would probably not answer in the way dry peoples expect. They would not point to temples. They would not recite miracles. They would perhaps press their forepads to a root, feel the water moving somewhere below, and send back a single pulse through the living ground.

It would mean something like:

*It helped us keep the water moving.*

Or perhaps:

*It made us less alone.*

Or perhaps simply:

*It saw us, and did not dry us.*

Then they would return to their work, because a channel somewhere is beginning to narrow, and the rain is falling softly, and the moss is rising behind them, and the place continues because continuation is all it has left to do.

Only now, in the thinnest films of water, there is a new quality.

A warmth that is not heat.  
A sweetness that is not sugar.  
A pressure that is not weight.  
A memory that is not loss.

And if you stand very still there, if your skin becomes damp enough and your breathing slow enough, you might feel it too: not as a voice, not as a presence demanding anything, but as the faintest change in the surface of a bead about to fall.

As if the whole wet world, old and complete and patient, has just been touched by something that loves it without needing to own it.

As if the rain has learned kindness.

As if the marsh has been blessed, and the blessing is this:

*Continue.*
