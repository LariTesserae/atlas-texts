They would not arrive as a king arrives.

Not with trumpets, not with a rent in the sky, not with a voice that makes every small body freeze into obedience. A power truly wise and truly kind would understand at once that this place is not waiting for a monarch, a prophet, or a single spokesman. It is already full of rule: the rule of water, the rule of cold, the rule of hunger, the rule of repair. It is already full of meaning: scent marks, worn paths, nest architecture, larval silk, the memory of roots. It does not need to be given a soul. It already has a hundred thousand small ones.

So the first thing they would do is this:

They would listen.

Not as a visitor listens when searching for something entertaining, not as a judge listens before deciding what is worthy, but as rain listens to stone, as roots listen to moisture, as dark listens to breath. They would let the hollow explain itself in its own way. They would attend to the tick of droplets, the faint rasp of a vole moving through sedge, the tiny tremor in a spider’s line, the slow shifting of bats in the upper dark. They would notice the caddis larvae adding one more grain to their walls. They would notice the moss turning a bare edge into a place where other life can begin. They would notice the salamander standing in the shallows like a thought the water is having.

And they would see that the being who lives there is not one body with one face, but a commonwealth.

This recognition would matter more than any miracle.

A lesser kindness might look at the hollow and try to find its representative. It might search for the largest animal, the oldest plant, the most beautiful bird, the most impressive nest, and elevate that one into the meaning of the whole. But a wise power would not make that mistake. They would understand that the true inhabitant is the pattern itself: the moss, the root, the vole, the shrew, the fish, the eel, the bat, the beetle, the fungus, the caddis case, the spiderline, the mud cup, the shell pavement, the drip, the cold, the silence, the pressure of lives adjusting to one another.

They would not ask, “Who rules here?”

They would ask, “What does this place need in order to continue being itself?”

And because they are wise, they would know that the answer is not comfort.

This is where many benevolent powers, if we imagine them as human-like, might fail. They might see the cold and wish to warm it. They might see the wetness and wish to dry it. They might see the sharp edges of the young collapse and wish to smooth them. They might see predation and wish to end it. They might see the darkness and wish to fill it with lamps. They might see the density and wish to make room. They might see the silence and wish to give it speech.

But wisdom would restrain them.

They would understand that to dry the hollow would be to kill its moss. To warm it too much would be to change its tempo. To remove hunger would be to unravel its food web. To eliminate death would be to choke its future. To flood it with light would be to erase the meaning of its shadows. To give it a single voice would be to silence the many voices it already has. To make it safe in the human sense would be to make it no longer itself.

So they would not rescue the hollow from its own nature.

They would not turn it into a garden for eyes that prefer neatness. They would not make it a museum. They would not make it a shrine. They would not place a crown on the water vole, or give the salamander a throne of polished stone, or command the birds to sing in harmony at appointed hours. They would not hang a sign above the sinkhole mouths saying, “Here lives a holy thing.”

If they did anything visible, it would probably be very small.

A wise power’s first visible act might be this:

They would keep a crack open.

Not a grand one. Not a shining doorway. Just one narrow fissure in the broken ceiling, one thread of connection between the world above and the world below. They would keep it clear of mud so that air could enter. They would keep it from closing too quickly so that light could still fall in a thin grey column when the sky was bright. They would make sure that seeds and spores could drift down, that a lost moth could fall through, that a bird could find a route into the ledges, that the hollow would not become sealed and stagnant.

This would be an act of love without ownership.

Then they might touch the water.

Not by commanding it, but by persuading it. A little more patience in the seep. A little less violence in the next rise. They would not stop floods altogether, because the hollow needs flood: flood clears silt, opens channels, renews edges, forces life to adapt. But they might prevent a flood from coming at the worst possible moment. They might delay it by one night. They might divert a single thread of runoff away from a nest entrance. They might cause a pool to rise slowly enough that the voles can move their young to higher chambers. They might guide a current around a caddis reef so that a newly built wall is not erased before it has time to become useful.

Water decides here, so a wise power would not fight water. They would help it decide well.

They would also touch the stone.

Not to make it soft, not to make it harmless, but to give it memory. The hollow is young and sharp. It is still settling. Blocks may fall. Ceilings may sag. Fresh scree may slide. A wise power would not prevent all collapse, because collapse is part of the hollow’s birth. But they might make a falling clod miss a nest by the width of a leaf. They might make a cracked shelf hold one season longer while roots bind it. They might make a loose stone settle into mud rather than roll into a nursery pool. They might give the broken land a slight tendency toward coherence, not permanence, but persistence.

They would not remove danger. They would prevent annihilation.

There is a difference.

A foolish benevolence wants to make every small creature safe from every harm. A wise benevolence knows that the hollow is made of risk. The water shrew must eat or die. The fish must hunt larvae. The spider must take the midge. The bat must leave the dark and gamble in the night. The salamander must wait, and wait, and sometimes fail. A place without hunger would become a place without rhythm. A place without death would become a place without room.

So the power would not abolish predation.

But they might soften cruelty where cruelty is unnecessary.

Not the cruelty of nature, which is mostly only need, but the cruelty of accident, waste, and mindless destruction. If a nest were about to fall because of a senseless vibration from above, they might quiet the vibration. If a fire from the surface were likely to smoke down through a crack, they might turn the wind. If poison were leaking from the upper world, they might slow it, bind it, warn the roots, give the fungi time to break it down. If a human wandered near with a hammer or a torch or the idle desire to ruin what they do not understand, the power might make that person uneasy. They might make the ground slick. They might make the entrance seem uninteresting. They might make the visitor forget why they came. They might make the hollow feel watched, not by eyes, but by the weight of many small lives, so that the person leaves without knowing why.

A wise power would protect the hollow not by making it famous, but by making it overlooked.

Fame can be as dangerous as fire.

They would also, I think, care for the edges.

Edges are where the hollow is most alive and most vulnerable. The crack where light enters. The drip-line where moss thickens. The pool rim where mud becomes stable. The root hanging into water. The shelf just above flood level. The overhang where wasps build. The threshold where air from above meets breath from below. A wise power would pay special attention to these places. They would not enlarge them into spectacle. They would only keep them functioning. Let the drip continue, but not always in the same place. Let the light enter, but not so much that it burns the moss. Let the air move, but not enough to dry the liverworts. Let seeds arrive, but not so many that they overwhelm the young root-mats. Let the edges remain generous without becoming wounds.

Because the hollow is a city of intervals, the wise power would become a guardian of intervals.

They would care for the spaces between.

Between stone and water. Between root and air. Between light and dark. Between flood and drought. Between one life and another. Between collapse and repair. Between what has just died and what has just begun.

This is where their benevolence would be most apparent, though few would see it.

They might cause a fallen leaf to land where beetles need it. They might guide a dead insect into the water where fish can find it. They might make a feather drop onto a nest that needs lining. They might help a strand of algae take hold in a new channel. They might make a grain of clay adhere to a root. They might strengthen the silk of a spider just enough to hold through the night. They might warm a bat cluster by a fraction of a degree during a cold spell, not by fire, but by keeping the air around them still. They might allow a shrew to find prey before its strength fails. They might allow a vole to finish reinforcing a tunnel before the water rises.

These acts would be tiny. They would be invisible. They would not be called miracles by anyone.

But they would be mercy.

And if the power truly loved the hollow, they would resist the temptation to make themselves known.

This is the hardest thing for power to do: to refrain from being recognized. If they revealed themselves, the hollow would become a place of worship instead of a place of living. Animals might not worship, but if human beings learned of it, they would come with reverence or greed, and either way they would change it. They would name it. They would fence it. They would study it, drain it, preserve it, photograph it, interpret it, ruin it with interpretation. Even if they protected it, they would make it into an object of human concern rather than what it is: a wet, cold, self-negotiated city of lives.

So the wise power would remain hidden.

They might leave one sign, but only a sign that belongs to the hollow itself.

Perhaps a single drop of water that never quite dries from a root-tip. Perhaps a patch of moss that glows faintly in the deepest dark, not with unnatural light, but with an extra depth of green. Perhaps a sound so soft that only the salamander seems to hear it: the sound of the whole settlement settling. Perhaps one stone that fits too perfectly into a channel, as if placed by care, though no hand placed it. Perhaps a nest that survives a flood for reasons no bird could explain. Perhaps a crack that admits exactly enough light on the coldest morning.

Nothing that demands belief.

Nothing that requires obedience.

Just enough to make the place feel held.

If they spoke at all, they would not speak in words.

Words are too hard-edged for a hollow like this. Words would freeze the living pattern into doctrine. Instead, if a wise power blessed the hollow, the blessing would be something more like weather. It would pass through as a change in pressure. The moss would feel it. The roots would draw it down. The voles would pause for a moment in their tunnels. The bats would shift in their sleep. The water would tremble without wind. The salamander would stand stiller than usual, and the stillness would not be empty.

The blessing would say:

Continue.

Not “be perfect.” Not “be painless.” Not “be eternal.” Not “become something easier to love.”

Continue.

Be broken where breaking is part of your making. Be flooded where flooding is part of your cleansing. Be cold where cold is part of your clarity. Be crowded where crowding is part of your richness. Be quiet where quiet is part of your wisdom. Be many where oneness would be a cage. Be hidden where visibility would be a danger. Be repaired by the very lives that need repairing.

The blessing would not be a command. It would be permission to remain what it already is.

And perhaps they would do one stranger thing.

Once, and only once, they might let the hollow dream as one being.

Not by giving it a human mind. Not by forcing every creature into a single thought. But by allowing, for one night, all the separate lives to touch along the web of water and root and air. In that dream, the moss would know it is holding the foot of the vole. The vole would know its tunnel is sheltered by the root. The root would know it is drinking from the same water the caddis larva is shaping. The caddis larva would know its wall slows the current that feeds the pool where the salamander waits. The salamander would know its stillness is part of the water’s attention. The bat would know its guano feeds the floor that feeds the roots that hold the nests. The bird would know its mud cup is made from the same clay the mussel paves below. The fungus would know it is not the end of life but the doorway through which life returns.

No creature would wake remembering the dream as a story.

But each would wake, if waking is the word, slightly more fitted to the whole.

Not tamed. Not pacified. Not made gentle in a way that weakens them. Only less accidental. Only more at home in their own necessity.

A wise power might do this only once because doing it often would overwhelm the place. Too much unity would erase the necessary separateness of lives. The shrew must still hunt. The fish must still flee. The spider must still wait. The vole must still fear the shadow. The hollow must still be made of distinct hungers, distinct shelters, distinct rhythms. A city is not a single body; it is a body of bodies. Its health is not in the abolition of difference but in the negotiation of difference.

So the dream would be a gift, not a governance.

Afterward, the hollow would go on.

The droplets would tick from root-tips into shallow pools. The moss would swell. The algae would drift like smoke in the colder light. The water voles would chew fibers into their walls. The shrews would hunt with sharp breath. The caddis larvae would add another grain. The spiders would repair their lines. The birds would flash through the grey columns with mud or insects in their beaks. The bats would exhale in the upper dark. The salamanders would stand in the shallows as if carved from patience. The fungi would thread through fallen matter. The mussels would filter the water into clearer water. The roots would hold the ceiling together.

Nothing would look different.

But something would be different.

The place would be held.

Not possessed. Not displayed. Not explained. Not rescued from itself. Held. The way a cup holds water without becoming water. The way a hand might rest near a small animal without touching it. The way silence holds a sound after it has ended. The way a mother might listen to children playing and not interrupt, but remain near in case the game turns into danger beyond their strength.

That is what I think a benevolent and wise power would do.

They would not take the hollow out of its own life.

They would not make it dry because it is wet. They would not make it warm because it is cold. They would not make it open because it is hidden. They would not make it singular because it is plural. They would not make it safe by ending the very relations that make it alive.

They would give it time.

They would give it resilience.

They would give it small mercies at the edges of disaster.

They would protect it from meaningless destruction.

They would let it remain wild, cold, wet, crowded, quiet, built by bodies, ruled by water, and holy without knowing it is holy.

And if asked what their greatest act was, they might answer, in the language of falling drops and settling mud:

“I did not make them mine. I made sure they could continue being themselves.”
