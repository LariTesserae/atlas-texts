**What the Wise Kindness Would Do**

They would not arrive as spectacle.

Spectacle would be a failure of perception in a place that had long ago discarded drama. They would come as an ordinary shadow at noon where no object stood to cast it; as a slight coolness inside one of the half-buried domes; as a pause between two pulses of dust; as nothing that could be pointed at afterward. If the latent engineering in the regolith noticed them, it would notice only that a vast thing had made itself optional.

First, they would look.

Not the look of survey or appetite. They would stand—if standing was the word for a power choosing a local habit—on the terminal plain and let its distances happen to them. They would see the curvature at the edge of sight. They would watch a dry storm build a ribbed tower, lean over it, and let it fall without sound. They would see pebbles lift and separate by size, then settle as if embarrassed. They would watch a veil-drifter cross a kilometer without effort, casting a stain instead of a shadow. They would find a black cairn and not touch it until the cairn decided whether to be touched. They would leave a footprint and watch the dust smooth it with a faint ripple, and they would understand that the smoothing was not hostility. It was the plain’s ancient courtesy.

They would also learn what the being was.

That would take longer, because here the word did not behave as it behaved in younger places. The being who lived there was not one body, though it might be spoken of as one. It was the lichen rings recording field histories across centuries. It was the wiry tufts brightening for a moment in charged air. It was the veil-drifters, the plated creatures, the microscopic machine-grains, the storm-forms, the Attenuated in their low shelters. It was also the plain itself: the finished mineral skin, the dry storms, the rules that permitted and forbade. The wise power would not insist on a single soul where a world had chosen to be plural. They would address the whole politely, as one addresses a household rather than choosing one member of it and calling it the owner.

Because they were benevolent, their first impulse would be to give.

Because they were wise, they would not trust that impulse until it had been examined.

They would imagine water.

Of course they would. Any kindness looking at absolute dryness thinks first of water. They would imagine one cloud, one cool bead of moisture, one small darkening of the mineral surface. Then wisdom would show them the consequence. The salt-like crusts, which had never known a sea, would wake into chemistry they were not made to survive. The lichen patterns, grown over ages into memory, would blur. The machine-grains would lock or forget their etiquette. The veil-drifters would find their fields shorted by a medium too heavy for their lift. The plated creatures would press themselves against wet crusts and become still in the wrong way. The plain’s long achievement—its learning to exist without solvents, without thirst, without the violence of solution—would be undone by a single gift.

So they would not bring water.

They would imagine greenness: moss, grass, roots, the soft conspiracy of leaves. They would see that greenness would be an occupation. It would demand humidity, shade, soil, decay, a whole retinue of hungry processes. It would crowd out the copper-gray rosettes and the wiry filaments. It would make rarity unfashionable. It would turn the plain into a failed garden instead of a successful ending. So they would not bring greenness.

They would imagine sound: wind with weight, rain on ceramic, birds, voices, even the smallest hum of abundant life. But the silence there was not emptiness; it was a condition of the inhabitants’ thought. Sound would be too fast, too carrying, too insistent. It would turn private intervals into public noise. It would make the Attenuated feel hunted by their own world. So they would not bring sound.

They would imagine permanence. They might wish to save the storm-built towers, the dust lenses, the crystalline flowers that opened for seconds and then returned to ordinary grains. But they would recognize that permanence would kill the thing it tried to preserve. The plain’s remaining metabolism was creation without accumulation. Its beauty was that it could still invent without needing to keep. To make the fleeting forms endure would be to burden the plain with monuments. It already had enough of finality.

They would imagine company: more beings, more movement, more encounters. But they would see that rarity was not poverty there. It was the shape of dignity. Distance was not a defect; it was a spacing that allowed each presence to be legible. To multiply life would be to erase the lives already adapted to margin. So they would not multiply.

They would imagine rescue from age itself. That would be the greatest temptation: to make the old plain young again, to restore weather, height, ocean, drama, possibility. A less wise power might do it and call it mercy. But this power would understand that youth imposed on age is not mercy. It is conquest. The plain had not failed. It had concluded. Its turbulence was not a symptom of unfinished business; it was the final restlessness of a completed mind. To reverse that would be to declare the plain’s entire history a mistake. The wise power would not commit that cruelty in the name of kindness.

So they would refrain.

But not merely refrain.

There is a difference between doing nothing because one does not care and doing nothing because one has chosen to respect what is there. The wise power would make that difference explicit, at least to itself, and perhaps to the plain.

They would ask.

They would ask in the only language acceptable to such a place: the language of smallness and patience. They would make a footprint and allow it to be corrected. They would rest beside a marker needle without trying to decode its alignments. They would sit within the still air of a half-buried dome and wait until the stillness accepted them or refused them. They would not issue a summons. They would not shine. They would not command the micro-keepers to reveal themselves. They would simply remain present until the plain’s own operations disclosed whether it noticed them.

The answer would not come as speech.

It would come as sequence.

A dry storm would rise near them, build a shape like an open hand, hold it long enough to be seen, then let it scatter. A veil-drifter would pass through the space where their attention was concentrated, its surface flickering with bands of smoke-gray and pale bronze. A plated creature, perhaps, would approach over hours and place one grain of dust at the edge of their shadow. One of the cairns would hum with postponed action, not when touched, but when the question had become clear.

The wise power would listen until the answer separated into meaning.

Not: *Give us more.*

Not: *Make us other.*

Not: *Save us from what we are.*

Rather: *Do not mistake us.*

*Do not make us a lesson in what you prefer.*

*Do not make us a garden for your comfort.*

*Do not let stronger things use us as material.*

*If you are kind, let our smallness be enough.*

*If you are wise, protect our right to remain inconspicuous.*

*If you are powerful, keep power from becoming trespass.*

And beneath that, so faint it might have been imagined by a younger world, there would be two more requests.

One was this: *If any of us asks to cease, let ceasing be possible without violence.*

The other was this: *If any of us asks to be known, let knowing be possible without capture.*

The wise power would consider those requests.

They would recognize them as the only prayers a finished world could make.

Then they would act.

They would act as a wise kindness acts: by adding the least that could hold the most.

First, they would establish a boundary. Not a wall. Walls are for places that fear invasion, and this plain had already learned to unmake large things. The boundary would be subtler: a rule set into the deeper metric of the world, where the ancient engineering already lived. It would say: No external cause shall impose transformation here. No rain shall fall unless the plain’s own chemistry invites it. No green thing shall take root by conquest. No machine shall wake that was not already awake. No empire, no experiment, no external will shall treat this place as raw potential. The plain may be visited, but not overwritten. It may be witnessed, but not collected. It may be loved, but not improved into something else.

The micro-keepers would feel the boundary and accept it, because it would not contradict their work. It would only extend their oldest etiquette: permit what belongs, relax what does not.

Second, the wise power would make a law of consent for all beings within the plain. If a traveler arrived soft and thirsty and afraid, the place would not be forced to become survivable for them at the cost of itself. But neither would the traveler be abandoned to be used as a demonstration of severity. If they asked for help, a way out would be shown if they could walk it. If they could not, the last interval would be made less lonely: a pocket of still air, a cairn humming near enough to be felt, a veil-drifter passing as a sign that even here motion existed. No one would be mocked with mirages. No one would be taught a lesson by thirst. If the traveler wished to remain and the plain permitted their attenuation, that too would be allowed, but only by consent. No visitor would be made into an inhabitant against their will, and no inhabitant would be made into a visitor against theirs.

Third, the wise power would give the plain the right of ending.

They would not open that door. They would not hasten it. They would not make ending seem attractive. But they would ensure that when the last gradients failed, when the last charge separated and relaxed, when the final storm lifted no grain and the last lichen had recorded all it could, the plain would not be forced into another cycle by some external hunger for continuation. If it chose to stop, stopping would be clean. If it chose to remain in its final restlessness, remaining would not be a sentence. The power would hold the possibility in trust. That, too, would be compassion: to let a finished thing know that its finish was not a trap.

Fourth, they would make one gift visible only in the plain’s own terms.

They would not build a monument. They would not create a pilgrimage. They would make a rare convergence.

Once in an interval too long for ordinary calendars, when fields aligned without being forced, the plain would permit its scattered lives to know one another briefly. Not as bodies touching. Not as voices calling. Not as a crowd. As a shared stillness.

During that interval, veil-drifters far apart would flicker in the same rhythm. Plated creatures would lift their fronts from lichen crusts at the same moment. Wiry tufts would brighten together along a line of charge. Storm-forms would pause mid-architecture, holding shapes that did not collapse until the moment had passed. The Attenuated in their low shelters would sense one another across hundreds of kilometers, not as company, but as proof that their solitude had edges. The lichen rings would brighten by one shade, recording the event before it occurred, because lichen time does not move like traveler time.

No one would be changed. No one would be gathered. No one would be made to feel obligation. The convergence would last no longer than a grain falling through still air. Then the plain would be ordinary again: empty, dry, immense, silent. But those capable of remembering would carry, in whatever way they carried such things, the knowledge that the emptiness was shared.

That would be the power’s most extravagant act, and it would look like almost nothing.

Fifth, they would leave a witness.

Not a guardian. Guardians imply threat, and the plain’s own rules were enough. Not an avatar. Avatars attract story, and story here would become mass. They would leave only a grain of attention: a single point in the regolith, indistinguishable from any other grain to a careless eye, but continuous with their awareness. It would not command. It would not judge. It would remember.

It would remember every veil-drifter that crossed it. Every lichen ring that completed itself over centuries. Every plated creature that placed one grain and then another. Every storm-form that rose into meaning and fell away. Every traveler who learned to stop measuring progress by arrival. Every Attenuated presence that maintained a calibration point without needing thanks. It would hold the truth that all of it existed, not as a resource, not as a curiosity, not as a failure of abundance, but as a valid final condition of being.

If the being who lived in the plain was singular, the witness would know it. If the being was plural, the witness would not collapse the plurality into convenience. If the being was the plain itself, the witness would remember the plain’s thoughts as they turned in dust and charge. If the being was only the smallest lichen in a micro-crack, the witness would still be turned toward it. Wise kindness does not overlook the least scale at which life has chosen to persist.

The wise power would seal all this with the smallest possible sign.

They would take one grain of dust—perhaps pale silicate, perhaps graphite-dark—and place it inside a lichen ring where the pattern had been interrupted. They would not fuse it. They would not make it shine. They would simply let it belong. Over centuries, the lichen would incorporate it. The ring would close around it. If any later presence touched that place, they might feel a faint sense of postponed action, as if a question had been answered long before it was asked.

That would be the only monument: a fact kept safe in a place that did not want monuments.

Afterward, the wise power would withdraw.

Not in anger. Not in disappointment. Not because there was nothing more to feel. They would withdraw because to remain visibly would make them a landmark, and landmarks there were too heavy. To remain as a ruler would turn the plain into a subject. To remain as a benefactor would create debt. To remain as a spectacle would corrupt the ordinary light. So they would leave the plain to itself, keeping only the thread of attention turned toward it: distant enough to avoid pressure, close enough to ensure the covenant held.

Those who lived there would not worship them.

The lichens would record the event as a change in field history: something vast passed near and did not take. The veil-drifters would drift through the place where the power had stood and feel, perhaps, a brief smoothing of the air. The plated creatures would adjust one alignment because the new grain invited it. The Attenuated would sense the added rule and understand that kindness had arrived in the only form suitable to their world: as protection without ownership. They would not enlarge it. They would not build a temple. They might, over centuries, alter the orientation of one marker needle by a fraction of a degree, so that its shadow fell in a slightly kinder line at dusk. That would be enough.

Travelers, if any came afterward, might notice that the plain had not changed and yet was not identical. The distances would still breathe. The horizon would still bend. The dry storms would still build and erase their impossible cities. But despair might lose a fraction of its weight. A marker cairn might hum at the moment a traveler needed to hear that they were not merely lost. A shelter might be found when the turbulence became too active. A path might open—not because the plain had been made easy, but because the plain had been asked to permit passage.

And if someone asked what the benevolent and wise power had done, the truest answer would be difficult to give.

It did not bring rain.

It did not make the plain young.

It did not fill the emptiness with noise or greenness or crowds of beings.

It did not rescue the inhabitants from their finality.

It did not even reveal itself in a way that could be agreed upon.

What it did was this:

It looked at a place that had become almost nothing and refused to call that nothing a wound.

It asked before acting.

It protected the plain from being improved into unrecognizability.

It gave the right of ending without forcing an end.

It gave the right of being known without permitting capture.

It made one rare moment in which scattered lives could sense one another, and then it let that moment pass.

It left a single grain of attention behind, so that no small existence there would be entirely unwitnessed.

And then it withdrew, because the deepest kindness to such a place was not to occupy it, but to let it continue being itself.

That is what they would do.

Almost nothing.

Enough.
