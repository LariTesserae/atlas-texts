They are shaped like open ears, those broad leaves, and perhaps that is why the place is noticed at all: because a world that listens is easy to hear.

If a benevolent and wise power turned its attention toward that hidden hollow beneath the salt plain — toward the bright mineral city, the roaring dry architecture, the sealed vegetation, the scarce and careful animals, and the single being who moved through all of it like a hand moving over the strings of an instrument — the first thing it would do is not act.

It would listen.

It would listen until it understood that this was not a ruined place waiting to be restored, nor a dead place waiting to be revived, nor a suffering place begging to be remade. It was a severe, exact, living balance. It was a city made into a bell, a garden made of light and vibration, an ecology built around thirst so absolute that thirst had become its architecture. To help it carelessly would be to destroy it.

So the power would begin with restraint.

It would not send rain.

That would be the obvious kindness, and therefore the first temptation to refuse. Rain would be violence there. Water would dissolve the salt crust, weaken the towers, collapse the ducts, drown the resinous plants, ruin the dry seed pods, and break the careful acoustic tuning of the whole hollow. It would turn the bright mineral surfaces to slurry. It would make the air heavy and strange. It would undo centuries of adaptation in a single season. In such a place, water is not mercy. Water is apocalypse.

The power would not send silence, either.

Silence might seem peaceful to an outsider, but the hollow does not live in peace as the outside world understands peace. The noise is not pollution; it is condition. Sound opens seeds. Sound shapes growth. Sound guides creatures. Sound keeps the architecture from becoming inert. To quiet the towers would be to interrupt the metabolism of the place. The plants would fail. The ecology would slow. The city would become merely stone and salt, and the wind would pass through it without meaning.

The power would not send crowds.

It would see at once that the hollow cannot support abundance. Its life is concentrated but narrow. Too many bodies would strip the terraces. Too many fires would foul the clean air. Too many hands would throw the light and sound out of balance. The place is made for exactness, not multiplication. A wise power would not mistake fullness for blessing.

It would not descend in glory.

A being that lives in such a world is already surrounded by intensity: glare, resonance, heat, dryness, pressure. If the power announced itself openly, with voice or vision or overwhelming presence, it would not comfort the dweller. It would erase them. It would make their small life unintelligible. Wisdom knows that not all help needs to be seen. The deepest help often leaves the helped free to believe they were aided by wind, chance, skill, or their own courage.

And yet.

The power would not simply watch.

Because it would see the being.

There is one.

Only one, perhaps. Or one remaining.

It moves through the hollow like a dust-colored thought. It is not grand. It is wrapped in woven fiber and pale resin-sealed cloth, its face protected from glare, its eyes large and adapted to the multiplied light. Its hands are rough, precise, and always busy. It carries a hammer of hard wood or polished stone, a knife of bone or bronze, coils of light rope, small pouches for seeds and resin flakes. It does not speak aloud, because speech is useless in that noise. It communicates with the city through touch and adjustment: a mirror tilted by degrees, a vent partly closed with a clay plug, a cord tightened on a hanging slab, a duct cleared of drifting salt with a long brush of fibers.
