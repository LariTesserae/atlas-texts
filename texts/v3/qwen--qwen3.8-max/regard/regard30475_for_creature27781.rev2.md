If such a power came to that place, the first thing it would do would be to refrain from arriving as power usually imagines itself: not as light breaking open the dark, not as a voice from above, not as a hand shaping the world into easier lines.

It would understand, perhaps before anything else, that this basin is not a ruined thing waiting to be corrected. It is not a chaos that has failed to become order. It is a firstness. It is a world still hot with its own beginning, overfull because it has not yet learned subtraction, loud because it has not yet discovered the uses of silence, dark because its seeing has not settled into sight. To dominate it would be to kill the very quality that makes it precious.

So a benevolent and wise power would begin by listening.

Not listening in the casual way of one gathering information, but listening as a craft, as a vow. It would let the basin’s noise pass through it until the noise became distinguishable: geological groan, water hiss, wing-whisper, fungal tremor, the wet percussion of countless small bodies. It would learn the difference between the sound of a vent merely breathing and the sound of a pressure pocket about to burst. It would learn which choruses mean rain, which mean breeding, which mean panic. It would learn that the Thrum-born are not the basin’s masters but its most intricate echo, the place where the land has begun to hear itself.

Only after that would it decide whether to act.

And even then, it would act with the caution of someone stepping into a newborn forest carrying fire.

---

The first thing they would do is protect the basin from total ruin without removing its danger.

That distinction would be essential. A foolish benevolence would try to make the place safe. It would cool the vents, still the ground, steady the shelves, quiet the insects, thin the swarms, and turn the whole basin into a gentler, more manageable garden. In doing so, it would destroy what makes the basin alive. The violence here is not mere cruelty. It is part of the engine of becoming. The ground breaks so warmth can rise. The shelves collapse so new hollows can fill. The pools vanish so eggs can learn urgency. The overcrowding forces life into invention. To remove all hazard would be to remove the pressure that shapes every creature’s responsiveness.

A wise power would not eliminate the danger. It would prevent annihilation.

So they would touch the place only in small, hidden adjustments. A buried gas pocket that might otherwise blow open a whole slope would be eased into a dozen tiny seeps instead. A rain pulse that might flash-flood a nursery hollow would be nudged into falling a little wider, a little softer, enough to fill the leaf pools without scouring them away. A stone shelf crowded with sleeping bodies would be given one extra interval of strength before it fell, just long enough for the weight on it to shift and scatter. Nothing dramatic. No spectacle. No deliverance that announces itself.

If a fissure opened beneath a cluster of Thrum-born, the power would not seal it by miracle. It would send a subtle change through the rock: a cooling, a dampening, a change in resonance that the creatures’ tympanic patches could read as warning. They would move. They would not know why, only that the ground had become less trustworthy under their bellies, and that another place nearby felt warmer, steadier, more settled.

If a shallow breeding pool began to dry too quickly, they would not fill it by command. They would draw condensation toward it, thicken the air above it, encourage a brief mist to bead on the broad leaves overhead until droplets fell back into the water. Enough to give the larvae another few hours. Not enough to make survival guaranteed.

If a swarm stripped a fungal shelf and began to move toward another in a hunger that could empty a whole stretch of the basin, the power would not destroy the swarm. It might alter the scent of the next shelf slightly, making it less compelling, or wake a competing chorus of insects elsewhere, diverting the swarm into a path that would leave some growth intact. The swarm would still eat. The shelf would still be wounded. But the basin would not become a place of devouring emptiness.

In this way, the power’s first visible effect would be almost invisible: fewer total catastrophes, more narrow escapes, a slight increase in the chance that any given pulse of life would leave survivors behind.

Not salvation. Continuity.

---

The second thing they would do is give the basin moments of quiet.

Not silence. Silence here would feel like death. The place is made of sound; to take sound from it entirely would be to take its breath. But there could be intervals of lessening: small pockets where the noise thinned, where the pressure eased, where the endless overlapping presence of other lives receded just enough for a creature to feel the edges of its own body.

Such places would not be permanent. They would appear and vanish like pools after rain. A hollow beneath a fallen fungal bracket where the chorus became muffled. A seam behind a curtain of dripping roots where the wing-noise softened. A warm indentation in the rock where the vibrations of the ground slowed into a long, low hush. The Thrum-born would find these places by accident at first, then by memory, then by song.

In those pockets, something new would become possible: self-recognition.

Not the human kind of selfhood, not a sharp and solitary “I,” but a dawning sense of boundary. A Thrum-born pressed into such a quiet fold might feel, for the first time, that its own breathing was separate from the breathing around it. It might feel the beat of its own pores, the tremor of its own ribs, the small private fact of its body apart from the chorus. That would be a dangerous gift if given too strongly, because isolation could sever them from one another. But given gently, in temporary doses, it would become the beginning of interiority.

A wise power would know that a people made entirely of chorus can be wise in movement but fragile in thought. They need some interior space if they are ever to choose rather than merely respond. So the power would not make temples of silence. It would only scatter pauses through the land, the way rain scatters pools, and let the creatures drink from them when they need to.

In those pauses, some would learn to listen not only outward but inward. They would begin to distinguish between the call they make because the group is calling and the call they make because they themselves intend it. That is one of the first seeds of freedom.

---

The third thing they would do is give memory, but perishable memory.

The basin’s great weakness is that it forgets. Its life is immediate. Its knowledge dies with the pool that held it. Its warnings vanish when the landmark collapses. Its songs are useful only as long as the terrain that inspired them remains. The Thrum-born are clever in the moment, collectively brilliant, but they have no durable past. They can repeat, but they cannot store. They can remember a rhythm for a little while, but not across great changes.

A benevolent and wise power would see this and feel the temptation to give them writing, monuments, carved signs, fixed paths. But they would resist that temptation. Fixed marks would become claims. Claims would become territory. Territory would become hierarchy. The basin’s grace is that it refuses permanence; introducing too much permanence too soon would harden its fluid world into domination.

Instead, they would give memory that lasts only as long as it is cared for.

They would encourage certain fungi to retain vibration. Not forever, not even for long. A shelf touched by a strong chorus might hold the rhythm in its flesh for a few hours, then release it faintly when disturbed, like a living echo. A particular moss might store the flash-pattern of alarmed pores and answer with a dim recurrence when a similar tremor passed over it. A stone pool might hold the glow of a breeding chorus through one dark cycle, then fade with the next rain.

These would not be records in the old sense. They would not become libraries. They would be living traces, fragile and temporary, requiring the creatures to return to them, refresh them, sing them again if they wanted them to persist. Memory would become a practice, not a possession.

This would change the Thrum-born profoundly without changing them into builders. They would begin to leave songs in places rather than only carry songs in bodies. A warning could outlast the danger that produced it, at least briefly. A safe route through a shifting flat could be remembered by resonance markers that decayed unless renewed. A dangerous vent could be surrounded by a repeated dissonance that younger clusters learned to avoid. Culture would begin, but not as accumulation. It would begin as recurring care.

The power would make sure none of these memories became permanent. If a song-place grew too fixed, it would fail. If an echo became too reliable, it would mute. If a sign became too authoritative, it would lose its life. Wisdom here would mean preventing the past from becoming a tyrant.

---

The fourth thing they would do is shape an ethics without commandments.

The basin is not evil, but it is reckless. Life presses against life until one creature’s survival becomes another’s erasure. Swarms consume too much. Crowds crush the weak. Fear turns into stampede. Hunger becomes stripping. In such a place, morality cannot arrive as law written on tablets. It would have to arrive as resonance, consequence, felt harmony and dissonance.

So the power would weave a subtle moral texture into the acoustic and luminous life of the basin.

Not punishment. Not reward in any crude sense. But feedback.

If a feeding swarm stripped a fungal shelf so completely that the surrounding web began to die, the remaining growth might dim in a way that produced discomfort in the swarm’s own signal field, a dissonance that made continued feeding less satisfying. If the swarm moved on, leaving enough behind to regenerate, the dissonance would ease. If a cluster of Thrum-born crowded a pool so tightly that juveniles were being crushed, the water might carry a discordant pulse that made the adults uneasy until they shifted. If an individual in distress produced a broken call and others answered in a way that restored its rhythm, that answer might create a harmonic resonance that felt physically settling to all involved.

This would not make the creatures “good” by external decree. It would teach them, through their own senses, that some patterns sustain the chorus and some patterns tear it.

A wise power would know that the deepest ethics are not imposed from outside. They are discovered as the difference between music and noise. The basin already had noise. What it needed was a way to recognize when noise became harm.

So the power would not say: do not kill. It would make killing beyond need feel sour in the throat of the chorus.

It would not say: protect the young. It would make the protection of the young resonate as a deep, steadying chord.

It would not say: share. It would make hoarding feel like a stutter in the song.

The Thrum-born would not become gentle in the ordinary sense. They would remain amphibious, crowded, hungry, immediate. But they would begin to develop something like responsibility. Not because a god commanded it, but because their world had become slightly more sensitive to their intentions.

---

The fifth thing they would do is invite making, but only as a question.

The possibility of tools already hovers in the basin: sharp flakes, fitting cobbles, hollow reeds, fibrous vines, scraping shells, hardening resins, dry tinder, ever-present heat. The land is full of first-tool logic. The Thrum-born sometimes touch these things without becoming makers. They lift, use, and drop. They benefit and forget. The threshold is there, but they have not crossed it, perhaps because their world does not reward possession, perhaps because their intelligence is already distributed enough that objects have not become necessary.

A benevolent and wise power would not force them across that threshold. Technology is not destiny. It is not automatically a blessing. In a place like this, the introduction of permanent tools could become the introduction of domination: the weapon kept, the store defended, the mark made, the claim enforced. If making begins here, it should begin as care, not conquest.

So the power would not hand them an artifact. It would arrange opportunities.

A shell with a fine edge might lie near a clogged water channel just as a cluster of juveniles pressed anxiously around it. A hollow reed might fall across a warm trickle at the exact moment an adult was trying to draw moisture from a too-narrow seam. A flake of black stone might rest against a root that needed opening, its edge catching the faint glow of nearby fungi so that it appeared not as a weapon but as an answer.

If a Thrum-born picked the shell up and used it once to clear mud, nothing miraculous would happen. The power would not exult. It would only observe.

If the shell was dropped after use, as most would be, the moment would pass. If it was carried a little farther than usual, used again, and then shared, that would be more significant. If it was used to help another breathe, to open a path for smaller bodies, to guide water into a drying leaf pool rather than to hoard it, the power might gently strengthen the behavior by making the next such opportunity easier to find.

The first tools, if they came, would be humble and temporary. A cupped leaf stiffened with resin just long enough to carry larvae to safer water. A bundle of fibers twisted not into a lasting rope but into a brief binding for a cracked fungal shelf. A hollow stem used once or twice to direct steam away from a nesting hollow. A stone flake pressed into mud to open a channel, then abandoned when the rain changed the land again.

The power would prefer tools that decay. It would favor materials that require renewal. It would discourage objects that invite inheritance. If the Thrum-born ever began to make, they would learn first to make in service of the moment, not in service of possession.

And if they never crossed into making at all, the wise power would not consider that a failure. It would understand that intelligence can be complete without becoming technical. Some beings think with hands; others think with weather, with bodies, with resonance. The Thrum-born already possessed a technology of togetherness. That is no small thing.

---

The sixth thing they would do is remain hidden.

This would be the hardest act of wisdom, and perhaps the truest.

A benevolent power strong enough to intervene could easily become visible. It could glow above the basin, speak through every stone, manifest as a vast face in the dark, announce itself as guardian and judge. The creatures would fall into awe. The Thrum-born might orient themselves around it as a fixed center. Their chorus would become prayer. Their responsiveness would become obedience. The basin’s wild plurality would narrow into worship.

That would be a kind of death too.

So the power would not reveal itself plainly. It would not give a name to be called. It would not demand ritual. It would not choose prophets. It would not appear in a form that could be mistaken for a ruler. If it became perceptible at all, it would be as something ambiguous: a note beneath the lowest note, a warmth that arrived just when despair began, a pause in the rain that felt almost intentional, a glimmer that seemed to answer a call but could also be only a glimmer.

The basin would develop stories about it, perhaps. Not because the power asked for stories, but because living minds try to make sense of mercy when they encounter it. The Thrum-born might have a word that was not a word: a particular low thrum used when danger passed too cleanly, when help arrived too precisely, when the chorus survived in a way that felt gifted. They might call it the Breath Between Calls, or the Listener Under the Mud, or the One Who Does Not Finish the Song.

The power would let them wonder.

It would not correct them. It would not confirm them. It would not turn wonder into doctrine. A wise power knows that the moment a mystery is owned, it ceases to be a living mystery. It would rather be half-believed than fully obeyed.

If the Thrum-born ever searched for it, they would find only traces: a resonance too deep to locate, a warmth without source, a pattern of echoes that seemed to hold the chorus together when everything else was falling apart. They would not find a throne. They would not find a face. They would find, perhaps, that when they sang most truly together, something sang back just enough to let them know they were not alone.

That would be sufficient.

---

If there were outsiders, the power would not meet them with open proclamation. It would decide whether the basin could bear contact.

Most likely, it would make the basin difficult to enter for those who came with taking in their hearts. Not by walls, not by weapons, but by confusion. Paths would shift. Sounds would mislead. The air would thicken until intention dulled. The dark would become full of echoes that returned a stranger’s own noise back to them in distorted fragments. Those who entered with humility might find their way to the edge of understanding. Those who entered with appetite would find themselves turning in circles until they left, exhausted and strangely ashamed.

If a benevolent power protected such a place, it would protect it not from being known, but from being owned.

It might allow witnesses. It might allow occasional visitors who came quietly, who did not try to fix the land or name its people or carry pieces away. Such visitors would leave changed, carrying not artifacts but a memory of pressure, of heat, of being surrounded by countless small lives. They would not be able to explain it properly. They would only know that somewhere in the world there existed a darkness that was not empty.

---

Over time, the basin would not become calm.

It would still break. It would still steam, collapse, flood, dry, swarm, shriek, breed, and vanish in pieces. The Thrum-born would still live in clusters without villages, still move with the land’s restlessness, still die quickly and be consumed, still leave no monuments. The insects would still be deafening. The rain would still arrive in brief hot curtains. The fungi would still flare and fade. The world would still be too full for comfort.

But something would be different.

There would be mercy woven into the turbulence.

A few more juveniles would survive the drying of pools. A few more warnings would travel before collapse. A few more echoes would persist long enough to teach. A few more acts of care would be repeated until they became something like custom. The Thrum-born would not become city-builders, but they might become keepers of intervals: keepers of the pause, keepers of the echo, keepers of the temporary refuge. They would learn to move injured individuals toward warmth rather than simply leaving them to the swarm. They would learn to open channels when the water became too hot. They would learn to answer distress calls not only with presence but with intention.

They would not become less strange. They would become more themselves.

And that, perhaps, is the deepest thing a benevolent and wise power would do: not remake the basin in an image of order, but help it become more fully what it already is. A newborn world. A plural being. A dark chorus trying to survive its own abundance.

In the end, the power might do something that looks almost like nothing.

It would not give them the sun, because they have their own small lights.

It would not give them peace, because their life is made of pressure.

It would not give them permanence, because permanence would betray them.

It would not give them a master, because they are already a chorus.

It would give them just enough shelter to keep from being destroyed.

Just enough quiet to discover interiority.

Just enough memory to learn without owning the past.

Just enough moral resonance to tell the difference between hunger and harm.

Just enough invitation to make, if making is their path, without making them into makers of domination.

And then it would withdraw into the deep structure of the place, becoming not a ruler but a hidden condition: a tendency toward mercy in a world still learning its own shape.

If the Thrum-born ever felt that condition, they would not see it. They would feel it as a sudden steadiness beneath the chorus, a warmth that arrived without source, a moment in which the basin seemed to hold them rather than merely contain them. They would answer with a sound no single mouth could make: a chord of recognition, brief and tremulous, rising through the dark.

And the wise power, listening, would not ask for thanks.

It would only ensure that the chord had room to continue.
