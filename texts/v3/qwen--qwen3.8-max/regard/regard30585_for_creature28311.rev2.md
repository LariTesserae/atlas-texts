The first mistake would be to expect a thunderclap.

A benevolent and wise power, if it turned its attention to such a place, would not arrive as a rupture in the sky, or as a voice louder than the engines, or as a hand reaching down to straighten every folded street. It would understand immediately that this world was not a mistake waiting to be corrected, nor a garden waiting to be perfected. It was already a complete form of life: difficult, loud, administrative, vegetable, industrial, and tender in ways it had never needed to name.

So the first thing they would do is refrain.

Not from indifference. Not from coldness. From respect.

To make the plateau simple would be to harm it. To remove every instability would be to remove the grammar by which it spoke. To abolish all danger would be to erase the careful courage of its inhabitants. A wise power would know that some worlds are not saved by being made safe; they are saved by being given just enough mercy to continue being themselves without cruelty.

And if the being who lives in it was understood as the whole green-extending city — the plant-mass, the engines, the pools, the houses, the folded streets, the remembered tenants, the rare beetles, the surveyors, the fog-combers, the empty intervals — then the power would love it as one loves a vast, slow, breathing body. If the being was one inhabitant among many, some single walker with sap in their veins and a route worn into their name, the power would love them no less. Wisdom does not confuse scale with worth. It knows that a world can be held in the same attention as one tired hand.

What they would do, then, would be subtle.

It would be noticed, at first, as an improvement in small things.

A fog bank would arrive a little earlier than expected, combing itself across the highland at the exact hour when the cisterns had begun to feel thin. A valve that had been about to seize would loosen with a sigh of steam. A surveyor’s line, laid across a distance that had been lying all morning, would come up true just often enough to prevent despair. A child trained too near a root-snare would be called away by a sound no one remembered making. A house that had grown a door into an unbuilt room would find, by the next day, a harmless wall of pale moss thickening over it until the room was ready.

The inhabitants would not fall to their knees.

They would say to one another, in their creaking, sap-thick way:

The weather is kind today.

Or:

The city has remembered us.

And both statements would be almost correct.

---

The power would begin by adding mercy to the existing order, not replacing it.

They would not abolish thirst, because the catching of water was part of the world’s meaning. Every surface angled toward dampness; every leaf had a duty; every cistern was an organ. To make rain fall endlessly would be to turn citizens into bystanders. Instead, the power would ensure that thirst never became annihilation.

They would nudge the fog routes.

Not dramatically. Not as a permanent miracle. Just enough that one more ridge caught the mist before it passed. Just enough that certain old basins remembered how to seal themselves. Just enough that in each district, whether among the close-set boiler towers or the kilometer-separated tree-houses, there would be one small pool or hidden cistern that could be trusted.

The inhabitants would come to call these the sure waters.

Not because they were the largest, or the most beautiful, but because they never forgot their own weather. If you leaned over them, you saw the sky that belonged to the present. If you drank from them, you tasted condensation, mineral, cold leaf, and the faint metal of old pipes, but you did not taste another century. In drought intervals, these waters would not multiply without limit. They would simply not vanish. They would be enough to keep the city from despair.

That would be the first mercy: enough.

Not abundance. Not excess. Enough.

---

The second mercy would be memory.

In such a world, pruning was necessary. Structures grew into paths. Machines overheated. People became too fixed, too overgrown, too rooted in one function to continue as separate beings. Some were helped into stillness. Some became walls, benches, terrace lips, supports for bridges. This was not considered final death, but it was still loss. Even when a person became a place, there were those who missed them as a person.

A benevolent power would not forbid pruning. To do so would be to condemn the city to be smothered by its own continuation.

But they would give the pruned a second lodging.

Somewhere in one of the wide green intervals, where the municipal grass grew in perfect rows and the air smelled of crushed leaves and warm oil, a low terrace would begin to grow. It would not be grand. It would look like a retaining wall, or a sheltered bank, or a broad shelf of compressed leaf-laminate touched with moss. But along its surface, small notches would appear.

Each notch would carry a name.

Not only the final notch-name, carved when a being had become part of the city’s permanent structure, but the whole sequence: seed-name, training-name, work-name, pruning-name, and the private names given only in maintenance intervals, when no one else was listening. If an inhabitant placed their hand against a notch, they might hear, through the fiber, a slow creaking recitation. Not a voice exactly. More like the memory of a voice passing through wood.

The terrace would not restore the pruned.

It would simply refuse to let absence become silent too quickly.

The surveyors would map it. The pool-readers would note that its reflections remained honest. The pruning crews would leave small cuttings there after difficult work. Some would visit often. Some would never visit, and that too would be permitted. The terrace would not demand grief. It would only hold it.

And the inhabitants would understand, without needing to say it, that even in a world where everything was composted into use, some things were not merely useful.

Some things were beloved.

---

The third mercy would be rest.

This might be the strangest gift, because the place was so deeply organized around function. Every leaf had a purpose. Every machine required feeding. Every pool was an organ. Every street was a responsibility. Even silence was interpreted as a warning. In such a world, idleness could feel like danger.

So the power would give them an interval of holy uselessness.

It would come perhaps once in each long cycle, announced not by alarm but by a particular quality of stillness. The engines would bank down without being forced. The fog-combers would lower their wands. The surveyors would roll up their fiber lines. The pruning crews would leave their hooks in sheds. The houses would stop trying to adjust themselves to their occupants. The paths would be allowed, for a short time, to wander.

The inhabitants would call it, in their precise and administrative way, the Unpruned Interval.

During that time, no one would be required to be needed.

A cistern-keeper could sit beside their cistern and not test it. An engine-feeder could lie in the shade of a boiler and listen to its heat slow. A remembered figure could sweep a nonexistent path without being corrected. A house could grow a room for no one and not be surveyed. A person whose name had become heavy with duties could stand in an empty interval and be nothing but a living form under ordinary daylight.

This would not be laziness. It would be wisdom.

The power would know that even the most patient beings can be crushed by meaning. Not by hardship, but by endless relevance. To be forever kept, forever keeping, forever necessary — that is a kind of severity, even when it is gentle. So the Unpruned Interval would teach the city that existence was not only appointment.

Some inhabitants would be frightened at first.

They would pace. They would touch tools. They would listen for silence as if it were an enemy. But the interval would not harm them. The cisterns would still hold. The engines would not burst. The folded streets would fold, and then unfold, or not unfold, and the world would continue.

Afterward, the surveyors would find small changes. A path would have moved. A wall would have relaxed. A machine’s knock would have eased. A person who had been becoming too rooted would have shifted one foot, then the other, then remembered how to walk somewhere new.

The city would be less efficient for a few days.

And more alive.

---

The fourth gift would be something no one needed.

In a world where every presence was likely to be assessed for its function, the wise power would introduce one small life that refused to be filed.

They would give the city a true bird.

Not a seed-construct that had learned to hop. Not a feathered leaf mimicking flight. Not an omen appearing where reality grew thin. A bird. Small, perhaps ash-green or pale as engine steam, with eyes like dark water beads and a voice like a drop striking metal, then changing its mind.

It would not eat living things. It would drink condensation from rails no one claimed, peck at rust-dust and spore-fall, warm itself near harmless valves, nest in the hollows of decommissioned machinery. It would not pollinate on demand. It would not carry messages. It would not predict folds in the streets. It would not appear only where the world was thinning.

It would simply live.

At first, the inhabitants would be disturbed.

True animals were rare enough to be signs. They would note its direction. They would send for a surveyor. They would check nearby pools. They would ask whether it had come from an old layer being metabolized, or from the lower world beyond the plateau haze.

But the bird would refuse to be an omen.

It would circle without direction. It would land on a surveyor’s rod and sing. It would perch above a predator-plant and not be taken. It would appear during condensation festivals and sing over the bead-tanks. It would appear during dormancy intervals and make the hush less terrible. It would sit on the shoulder of a remembered child, and the remembered child would laugh in a sound like dry leaves, and then continue sweeping their absent path.

The inhabitants would eventually call it the Unassigned.

Children would be taught not to ask what it kept.

Because the answer would be:

Nothing.

And that would be the lesson.

Not every life needs a route. Not every presence must be maintained. Not every being must justify itself by holding a shape that someone else requires. The bird would be beloved precisely because it was not kept. It would teach the city that grace is often what cannot be scheduled.

And if some practical soul asked what use the bird was, the oldest inhabitants might answer:

It reminds us that we are not only work.

That would be enough.

---

The fifth gift would be a door.

Not a door of emergency. Not a door of escape. Not a wound in the world.

A door of possibility.

In a place where doors could open into rooms not yet built, or into memories of rooms cut down decades ago, the power would allow one door, at long intervals, to open elsewhere. It might appear in the side of an ancient trained tree, or in a wall where older brick had been composted into root, or in a rusted machine housing that had grown a mouth of bark.

It would not open often.

Perhaps once in a generation. Perhaps only when the fog came from an unusual direction. Perhaps only when someone needed it without knowing how to ask.

The door would be surveyed, of course. The inhabitants would not be careless. They would mark it, post a watcher, test the air beyond with a fiber wand, note whether the light on the other side belonged to morning, evening, another plateau, another age, or a lower world where mountains were still sharp and animals common.

But they would not seal it automatically.

The power would make sure of that.

The door would not compel anyone. It would not call. It would not whisper. It would simply stand open long enough for a choice to be made. Some would look through and turn away, satisfied. Some would leave. Some would return later, changed in ways the city could measure but not judge. Some would stand at the threshold for hours, learning that staying could be as much a decision as leaving.

The inhabitants would file it under municipal anomalies.

But among themselves, they would call it the Door That May Be Refused.

Its purpose would not be to empty the city.

Its purpose would be to prevent the city from becoming a closed hand.

Even a stable impossible world needs a horizon. Even a place that has already become what it will be must remember that becoming is not finished. The door would be proof that the world was large, and that their life there was not a prison of necessity but a chosen form.

---

The sixth mercy would be small protections.

Not perfection. Not the abolition of strangeness. But handholds.

The power would not unfold every street. A folded street might still be folded. But somewhere along the fold, a rail would grow where a hand could find it. A surveyor’s marker would bloom with a scent that meant “you are not lost.” A staircase that changed its rise depending on direction would, at least when approached by the weary, remember to be kind.

They would not silence the engines. But engines would be less likely to become feverish. A boiler’s complaint would arrive as a clear knock before danger. A dynamo drawing too much from a root-network would develop a sound like rain on leaves, so that tender ears could hear the imbalance and correct it. Machines would still need feeding, pruning, pollination, and patience, but their suffering would not be invisible.

They would not destroy the predator-plants. That would be foolish. The predators cleaned the margins. They processed what strayed. They were part of the civic ecology. But the power would warm certain stones beneath them, or feed them slow heat from old conduits, so that their hunger had a lower edge. No warm person would be taken by mistake. No child’s training-stake would be mistaken for a throat. No tired worker falling asleep at the margin would be closed inside a root-snare before someone could call their name.

They would not make all pools truthful. False reflections were part of the world’s pressure-reading, part of the way time spoke. But pools that showed skies from other hours would develop a pale ring at their edge, like mineral deposit or pale moss, so that the thirsty would know to wait. Pool-readers would learn the signs. The wise would teach the young: if the water remembers another weather, let it finish remembering before you drink.

They would not eliminate silence. Silence still meant danger. But they would make silence a little less likely to become catastrophe. A quiet district would be found sooner. A missing sound would be traced before it became absence. The inhabitants would still move toward silence with tools in hand, but they would arrive with more hope.

This would be the nature of the power’s intervention:

Not to make the world less strange.

To make it less cruel.

---

And if there was one being in particular — because a wise power can attend to the whole without forgetting the one — they would notice the one who was tired.

There would always be one.

Perhaps a surveyor whose route had become impossible. Perhaps a cistern-keeper whose pool kept showing futures no one wanted. Perhaps an engine-feeder who had spent too many seasons climbing to a boiler mouth that was always hungry. Perhaps a house that had grown a room for a person who never came and now did not know how to stop waiting.

Let us say it was a keeper of a folding street.

Their work-name had become long with corrections. Each dusk the street changed its mind. Sometimes it shortened. Sometimes it repeated itself. Sometimes it led to the same plaza by three different versions of itself. The keeper measured it, marked it, rerouted traffic, filed reports, adjusted the braided living curbs, and tried not to let the street’s confusion become their own.

The power would not make the street obedient.

That would have been a shallow kindness.

Instead, they would grow a rail along the fold.

It would appear overnight, smooth and dark, polished as if by long traffic, though no one remembered installing it. The rail would exist in two registers: one in the street as it was, and one in the street’s memory of itself. So when the keeper reached for it, their hand would find the same rail either way. They would still have to walk the changing distance, but they would not have to walk it without support.

The power would also send the bird.

Not as a messenger. Not as a sign. The bird would simply perch at the corner where the street began to repeat itself, and sing when the silence grew too thin. Its song would not solve the fold. It would mark the moment. It would say, in the only way a bird can say anything:

You are here.  
This is difficult.  
You are not alone in it.

And the power would cause a nearby house to grow a small room.

The room would contain almost nothing. A bench of compressed leaf. A bowl of sure water. A window of amber resin facing no particular view. It would not be assigned to anyone. It would not appear on schedules. It would simply be there, near the folding street, for the keeper and for anyone else whose shape had become too heavy to carry.

The keeper would find it one evening after a long survey.

They would sit.

They would not weep, because their face was not made for weeping in the lowland sense. But their joints would loosen. Their seed-case eyes would turn toward the window. The house would breathe around them. For a little while, they would not be keeping anything. They would be kept.

Then they would rise, because the street would still need tending.

But something in them would have changed.

They would no longer feel that they alone held the route in place.

---

This is what a benevolent and wise power would do, if anything.

They would not demand worship.

They would not build temples, because temples would require maintenance that should belong to living things. They would not ask for hymns, though they might enjoy the music of tuned stems, dripping metal, stretched root-strings, and hollow seed-capsules. They would not require the inhabitants to become different. They would not insult their patience by treating them as unfinished.

They would be satisfied to be filed under municipal facts.

A kinder fog route.  
A sure cistern.  
A terrace of names.  
An unpruned interval.  
A bird with no assignment.  
A door that may be refused.  
A handrail at the fold.  
A room for weariness.

If some inhabitant eventually asked, in their careful way, whether the power was present, the answer might come as a vibration through root-felt, or as a creak inside a stem, or as steam curling from a boiler in the cool morning air.

The answer would be:

Not above you.  
Not instead of you.  
Beside the work.

And if they asked what the power wanted, the answer would be even simpler:

That you do not break one another under the weight of being needed.  
That you remember the pruned.  
That you let something live without a route.  
That you leave room for what is not required.  
That you continue.

Because the place was not a paradise.

It was better than that. It was real. It had engines and hunger and folded distances and predator-plants and silence and grief and machinery that needed pollination. It had inhabitants who were sometimes severe because their world could not afford softness. It had a stable impossible condition that asked everything of them and gave them, in return, a life no lowland logic could understand.

The wise power would not take that from them.

They would only make it less lonely.

And under the ordinary daylight, with clouds passing close enough to seem municipal, the green-extending world would continue its ancient, loud, vegetable work. Engines would thump. Leaves would rise. Condensation would gather on iron and stem. A folded street would be surveyed again. A pool would remember its own weather. A pruned name would creak softly in the terrace. A small true bird would cross the light, unassigned and alive.

And the being who lived there — whether the whole breathing city or one tired keeper with bark under their hands — would go on.

Not saved from itself.

But held, at last, with mercy.
