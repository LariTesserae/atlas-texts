If I follow my heart at first, I imagine the power doing something vast and tender: opening the drowned basin, pouring in light, lifting the city out of darkness, ending every hunger, stilling every hidden mouth. But that is the kindness of someone who loves a place by wishing it were another place.

Intuition corrects this almost immediately.

A city like that is not a problem to be solved. It is a grammar. Its darkness is not absence; it is skin. Its hunger is not mere cruelty; it is circulation. Its quiet is not emptiness; it is attention. To flood it with daylight, to give its inhabitants tools, to abolish predation, to make the Accreted into something more like us, would not be salvation. It would be replacement. The being would vanish in the act of being saved.

Reason then asks a harder question: if a benevolent and wise power truly wished well to such a place, what would well-being mean for it?

Not comfort as we understand comfort. Not safety as a surface creature understands safety. Not freedom as isolation. For the drowned city, well-being would mean continuity without stagnation. It would mean enough warmth, enough flow, enough memory, enough room for change without collapse. It would mean that death remains part of inheritance rather than becoming meaningless waste. It would mean that the city can keep becoming itself.

So the power would act.

But it would act almost invisibly.

It would not arrive as a face in the water, nor as a voice louder than pressure, nor as a burning sign where no fire belongs. In a place that reads scent, current, texture, and living light, an overwhelming manifestation would be a kind of violence. It would be like shouting into a nursery. It would trigger alarm, closure, retreat. It would distort the very ecology it meant to bless.

Instead, the power would first listen.

It would attend to the city the way an elder gardener attends to an ancient forest: by noticing what is strong, what is failing, what is about to be foreclosed, and what must not be touched. It would taste the thermal pulses. It would learn the grammar of signal colonies. It would understand the difference between a wall breathing and a wall dying. It would know which predators keep balance and which may become extinction. It would distinguish ordinary suffering from irreversible ruin.

Only then would it move.

And when it moved, it would move through the city’s own substances: growth, mineral, current, scent, heat, microbial life, larval settlement, chemical memory. It would leave no tool-shaped object. It would bring no artificial light. It would carve no monument. It would build no throne. It would not ask to be worshiped, because wisdom knows that worship in such a place would become another form of pressure, another hierarchy, another way for one lineage to claim warmth over others.

If the power did anything, it would make the city more capable of remaining itself.

The first gift would be warmth.

Not fire. Not a sun. Not a furnace. But a deepening of heat where heat already lives.

The basin already breathes through hidden seeps and mineral springs. The power would strengthen those veins without making them violent. Where a main vent is old and beginning to fail, it would encourage a secondary seam far enough away to avoid shocking the district, close enough for larvae and chemical memory to bridge the gap. Where warm pulses have grown irregular, it would steady the underlying flow, not by machinery, but by opening minute fissures below the basin floor, easing mineral blockages, redirecting deep pressure through ancient channels that still remember heat.

The inhabitants would not experience this as miracle. They would experience it as fortune. As a good cycle. As a warm season that arrives when it is needed. As a nursery wall that does not chill. As a vent that does not sour.

They might later say, in their chemical way: the heat listened.

The second gift would be flow.

In a drowned city, stagnation is death, and excess flow is violence. The power would become a subtle regulator of current. Not a dam. Not an engine. More like a careful hand placed behind the water, guiding without gripping.

When silt begins to choke a nursery district, it would encourage the city’s own cleaners: crabs toward the burdened ledges, eels into the clogged channels, boring organisms toward compacted deposits, living baffles toward places where current needs to widen or slow. It would not clear the silt by force. It would create conditions under which the city clears itself.

When a warm pulse rises too suddenly, threatening to tear larvae from their settlement surfaces or to flood delicate feeding crowns, the power would soften the surge through hidden side-channels, allowing the heat to arrive in waves rather than blows. When oxygen-poor water threatens a lower quarter, it would guide a cleaner flow inward from some distant upper shelf, faint but enough, carrying not daylight but breath.

The city would feel this as balance. As weather that remains within the body’s tolerance. As streets that do not choke. As doors that do not have to close too long.

The third gift would be memory.

This place already remembers through growth bands, chemical residues, archive-shells, elder bodies, scent-marks, and living walls. But memory is fragile. A sour flood can erase a lineage’s trace. A long cold cycle can starve the keepers of record. A predator can consume an archive-shell before its layers are read. A false signal can overwrite a true one.

A wise power would protect memory without freezing it.

It would strengthen the oldest archive-shells, laying down fine mineral films that make their records less soluble, less vulnerable to acid pulses or careless breakage. It would encourage the growth of memory-nodules in districts that lack them, so no quarter is wholly dependent on a single elder or a single wall. It would help the chemical traces of the dead settle into places where they can be read, rather than scattering into meaningless dilution.

It might create something like a Memory Tide: a rare, slow current that moves through the basin at long intervals, carrying not new commands but old signatures. The scent of a lost household. The mineral trace of a vent that failed long ago. The chemical ghost of an elder fused into a tower three generations back. Not to haunt the living, but to remind them that they are continuous.

The Accreted would experience this as recognition. A young walker might taste an unfamiliar mark and feel, without understanding why, that it belongs to a longer pattern. An anchored elder might receive a scent it has not known since its juvenile wandering and answer with a slow amber pulse of greeting. The city would not become trapped in the past. It would become more certain of its own depth.

The fourth gift would be discernment.

The city’s light-language is sacred and vulnerable. Light there is not illumination; it is speech. Lure-users corrupt that speech. They make hunger wear the mask of kinship, shelter, food. A benevolent power would not simply destroy all lure-users, because they are part of the ecology of caution, choice, and survival. To remove them entirely would weaken the city’s grammar. It would make the Accreted less attentive, less literate, less wise.

But it could give them better discernment.

It might encourage signal colonies to produce one rare additional pattern: a clean, cold-blue ripple that does not mean food or danger or kin, but simply *truth*. When a false signal is nearby, when a lure is mimicking a familiar rhythm, when a message has no proper context, the true-signal ripple would pass along ridges and tower edges. Not as an accusation. Not as a weapon. As a clarification.

The inhabitants would learn to read it the way a surface animal might learn to recognize the smell of smoke. It would not make them safe from deception. It would make deception harder to make total.

This matters, because in a city where language is carried by living light and chemical trace, the worst violence is not only bodily harm. It is the corruption of meaning.

The fifth gift would be refuge.

Not a paradise. Not a place without hunger. But one district, perhaps deep in a sheltered fold of the basin, where the rules soften slightly.

A still-water commons.

There, displaced juveniles could settle without being immediately overwritten by stronger lineages. Molting creatures could hide without being taken at their weakest. Wounded walkers could rest where the current carries enough warmth and food but does not advertise their distress too widely. Old archive-shells could remain undisturbed. Larvae too fragile for the crowded towers could find a quiet surface marked with ancestral scent but not defended by territorial aggression.

The power would not make this refuge into a fortress. It would not wall it with force. It would simply shape the water so that the usual appetites pause there. Predators would feel a subtle aversion, a taste of mineral bitterness, a pressure of unease, a memory of old stone that says: *not here*. Scavengers would still come eventually, because death must still be recycled, but not with the frantic swarm that turns every fall into immediate dissolution.

This place would become known, if it became known at all, as the Quiet Basin.

No lineage would own it. No elder would anchor there permanently, because permanent anchoring would turn refuge into territory. The Accreted might learn to respect it the way they respect a nursery door or a heat-chamber: by restraint. The power would ensure that no one could claim it by scent-overwriting or architectural growth. If a lineage tried to build a barrier across its entrance, the current would gently undo the work over time. If a predator learned to ignore the aversion and hunt there too often, the water would shift, making the place less attractive, drawing its hunger elsewhere.

The refuge would be one of the power’s clearest acts of compassion. Not because it removes death, but because it prevents the most helpless deaths from being utterly unremembered and unaided.

The sixth gift would be foresight.

Not prophecy. Not command. Not moral law imposed from outside. But a faint capacity to feel consequence before it closes.

The Accreted already live by long consequence. They cultivate borers, feed walls, guide shrimp, maintain doors. They think in pulses and generations. Yet even they can make errors: a lineage may grow a barrier that eventually chokes its own flow; a district may overfeed a signal colony until it becomes unstable; a council may settle larvae in a beautiful warm hollow that will become toxic after three cycles.

The power might give them a subtle inner sense, carried in their photophores or symbiotic bacteria, that flares faintly when an action threatens long-term continuity. Not as guilt. Not as divine prohibition. As a pressure-shift in the self. A dimming. A hesitation.

In councils, this might appear as a pale white ring forming briefly in the water between participants. Not a command to stop. A sign meaning: *this path narrows the future*. The Accreted could ignore it. They would need to be free to ignore it, or it would not be wisdom but control. But over time, those who attend to it might learn to associate it with consequences they would otherwise discover only after loss.

This gift would be dangerous, because foresight can become fear. The power would therefore keep it faint. Enough to invite reflection. Not enough to paralyze.

The seventh gift would be healing.

Not the healing of perfection. Not the removal of all injury. But the healing of wounds that would otherwise poison the whole.

If a district begins to die from toxic seepage, the power would encourage organisms capable of metabolizing the poison. It would guide bacterial lawns toward the sour water. It would help living walls seal lesions by feeding the cementing polyps that already do that work. It would not make the city sterile. It would help its immune system remember itself.

If a great predator lingers too long in the avenues and begins to unbalance the population, the power would not strike it dead. It would alter currents, scents, pressure gradients, making the district less favorable for ambush, drawing prey into safer patterns, encouraging the predator to move toward wider waters where its hunger can be met without collapsing local life.

If a wall-fold grows too large and begins taking too many cleaners, the power might encourage a subtle change in the wall texture so that smaller creatures can detect it sooner. Not to save every individual. To prevent the local grammar from collapsing into fear.

If an elder begins to fail, the power would not necessarily prolong its life. Some elders must become architecture. Some must release their place to new growth. But it might help the elder’s chemical record pass cleanly into the wall, so that the household does not lose its memory all at once. It might make the transition slower, more legible, less like erasure.

The power would also attend to the city as a being.

This is important. The being that lives there is not only the Accreted. It is also the city-as-distributed-organism: the walls that breathe, the towers that signal, the plazas that digest, the streets that pulse, the doors that contract, the ancestors fused into stone and tissue. If a wise power noticed this being, it would not treat the Accreted as the only persons present. It would recognize that the city itself has a kind of slow selfhood.

It would speak to that selfhood not in words but in rhythms.

Imagine one moment, rare and vast.

The basin is quiet. A warm pulse rises from below. The signal colonies begin their usual slow wave. Then, for the first and perhaps only time, every ridge, every tower, every living door, every elder fused into architecture, every shrimp cloud and eel-path and feeding crown receives the same gentle pressure: not a command, not a presence demanding worship, but a single coordinated breath.

A blue-green wave passes from the lowest seep to the highest terrace.

It does not mean obey.

It means *seen*.

The city responds. Some creatures freeze. Some flash in alarm. Some close. Some open. The Accreted extend their sensory fronds, tasting the water, unsure whether this is predator, weather, or ancestor. Then the wave fades. The ordinary pulses resume. The city does not become different in that instant. But something in its chemistry changes: a residue of sweetness, a mineral trace that does not belong to any known vent, a warmth that seems for a moment to hold the whole basin without pressing it.

Later, the elders might try to read it.

They would find no mark of conquest. No scent of ownership. No demand. Only a pattern that, when translated into their closest grammar, might mean something like: *you are whole. Continue.*

That would be enough.

If the power ever spoke to the Accreted directly, it would not speak as a ruler. It would not say: I made you. I own you. I will save you. It would not demand temples, sacrifices, or exclusive devotion. It would know that in a dense fluid city, any exclusive claim becomes a weapon. One lineage would say it has the true scent of the power. Another would say its elders heard the true pulse. Then the power would become territory. Then it would become war.

So the power would avoid becoming a person in their politics.

It would prefer to be remembered as a condition.

A warmth that listened.

A current that corrected.

A memory that did not vanish.

A silence that protected.

If rituals arose anyway — and they would, because living beings make meaning from kindness — the power would gently disperse them. Not with anger. With dilution. If one household tried to build a shrine around a warm nodule and claim special access, the nodule would slowly cool, or the current would shift, or the scent would become common everywhere, so that no one could say: here alone is the sacred. If priests arose, the power would make their signs ambiguous. If armies of interpretation formed, the water would carry their certainties away.

The power would want to be used, not ruled by.

It would prefer a practical reverence: a crab path kept clean, a nursery door repaired, a false-scent forbidden, an archive-shell protected, a displaced juvenile allowed to settle, a hungry wall-fold avoided rather than slain without need, a vent tended without being hoarded.

Its ethics would be simple and severe:

Do not turn warmth into domination.  
Do not let memory become a chain.  
Do not make light into lies.  
Do not let hunger erase the future.  
Do not forget that every body is borrowed from the city.

If catastrophe came — and catastrophe must come in any living world — the power would act more strongly.

Suppose the basin floor cracked and released a brine so toxic that it could kill the chemosynthetic lawns for many cycles. Suppose the deep heat failed for so long that the nurseries chilled and the city began to contract into death. Suppose an outside predator entered in such numbers that whole districts fell silent. Suppose a disease moved through the signal colonies, making their light false and chaotic.

Then the power would not stand aside in the name of non-interference. Wisdom is not passive. Benevolence is not the same as neutrality.

But even then, it would act through the city’s own nature.

It would guide larvae toward hidden refuges. It would wake dormant microbial strains capable of surviving the toxic interval. It would encourage the Accreted to move by dreams of warmth: not visions, but a persistent chemical pull toward a passage they had ignored. It would strengthen the living doors. It would help the oldest towers conserve heat. It would open a deep route to cleaner water if one existed. It would preserve at least one archive-shell lineage in each major district, so that after the disaster the city would not begin from silence.

It would not guarantee survival. It would not promise that every individual would live. It would not make the city immune to grief.

But it would prevent total erasure.

That is the difference between cruelty and ecology. Cruelty destroys without meaning. Ecology kills, but it also continues. A wise power would serve continuation.

Afterward, when the crisis passed, the city would remember the event in its own way. A scar in a wall. A strange mineral layer in an archive-shell. A district repopulated by larvae from an unexpected passage. A signal colony whose rhythm carries a faint irregularity, like an old wound turned into music. The Accreted would lead their young there. They would let them taste the mark. They would not tell a story in words. They would say, in their way: *here the water changed. Here we almost ended. Here something helped.*

They might not call the helper a god.

They might call it the Third Warmth.

Or the Attention.

Or the Pulse That Did Not Take.

That last name feels right.

Because the deepest temptation of any benevolent power is to take. To take gratitude. To take obedience. To take the shape of the beloved and remake it in one’s own image. To say: I love you, therefore become what I can love more easily.

The wise power would refuse that temptation.

It would love the drowned city as drowned. It would love the Accreted as soft, slow, chemical, communal, porous, and strange. It would love the predators for the caution they create. It would love the darkness for the intimacy it demands. It would love the living walls for the ancestors they hold. It would love the city’s hunger not because hunger is good in itself, but because hunger is part of the way this place turns death into continuation.

It would not love the city by trying to make it innocent.

It would love it by helping it remain profound.

There would be moments, perhaps, when the power drew close enough to be almost known.

A juvenile, lost in a cold underpass, would feel a faint warmth along a wall where no warmth should be. It would follow the warmth and find a current carrying food. It would never know it had been helped. It would only grow.

An elder, anchored in a failing tower, would taste a mineral sweetness in the water just before collapse. It would send out a final signal, not alarm but invitation, and the surrounding district would answer. Its body would be incorporated cleanly. Its household would not scatter. Its memory would enter a new arch. The elder might think, in whatever way such beings think: *the city received me.* It would not need to know that the receiving had been aided.

Two lineages on the edge of conflict would meet in a plaza, chemical plumes sharp, photophores flickering in broken warning. Then a slow pulse would pass through the signal colonies around them — not from either side, not from any elder present. A neutral rhythm. Old. Calm. It would mean nothing so specific as peace. It would only create a pause. In that pause, one lineage might withdraw a barrier. Another might soften its scent. The dispute would continue, because disputes are life, but not as annihilation.

A wall-fold about to strike a cleaner might feel a subtle bitterness in the water and hesitate. The cleaner would escape. The predator would later eat something else. No one would call this justice. It would simply be balance.

A lure-user glowing with false kinship might find its light answered by the true-signal ripple, exposing it. A school of small creatures would scatter. The lure-user would remain hungry. That is not cruelty to the lure-user; it is the continuation of its kind, but not at the cost of destroying the city’s ability to trust light.

The power would care especially for trust.

Because in that city, trust is not sentiment. Trust is survival. If scent becomes unreliable, if light becomes only deception, if current carries only threat, the city contracts into terror. It stops growing. It stops tending. It stops opening its feeding crowns. It stops releasing larvae. It becomes a place of closed doors.

So the power’s most sacred work would be to protect the possibility of trust.

It would not make every signal true. It would make truth possible.

It would not make every encounter safe. It would make safety meaningful.

It would not make every death gentle. It would make death part of the city’s long speech.

What, then, would the power do, if anything?

It would do enough.

It would not be absent. Absence, in the face of such fragile complexity, can be its own cruelty. But it would not be loud. It would not be imperial. It would not turn the basin into a display of divine affection. It would not make the Accreted into pets, prophets, or philosophers of the surface. It would not give them metal, because metal would sever their grammar of growth. It would not give them artificial light, because artificial light would colonize their language. It would not give them the sky, because the sky would erase them.

It would give them deeper heat, steadier flow, stronger memory, clearer signals, refuge, healing, and a faint sense of consequence.

Then it would withdraw.

Not abandon. Withdraw into the background of the city’s own processes, so that what it gave could become theirs. So that the city would not depend on continuous miracle. So that the Accreted would not become supplicants. So that the power’s love would not become a cage.

It would remain, if it remained, as a habit of the water.

A warmth that arrives when the nurseries need it.  
A current that opens when a district begins to choke.  
A scent that carries old names back to young bodies.  
A pulse that pauses violence just long enough for choice.  
A silence that protects the smallest lives.  
A memory that refuses to become mere stone.

And if, after many ages, the Accreted were to ask what the power wanted in return, the answer would be simple.

It would not come as words.

It would come as the city itself continuing.

Walls repairing.  
Doors opening.  
Shrimp clouds rustling around warm vents.  
Elders tasting the water and finding it good.  
Larvae settling on marked stone.  
Signal colonies passing slow blue-green waves along ancient ridges.  
Predators waiting in shadows that are not empty.  
Dead bodies becoming foundation.  
Juveniles playing briefly in a warm pulse, then growing serious.  
The whole drowned metropolis breathing slowly in the black water, not perfect, not innocent, not free from hunger, but alive, continuous, and capable of itself.

That would be the power’s answer.

Not: *love me.*

Not: *obey me.*

Not: *become something else.*

Only: *continue.*

And perhaps, in the deepest part of the basin, where no walker goes and no predator hunts, a single warm spring would pulse at long intervals with a pattern no living colony fully understands. A pattern that means, if it means anything at all:

*I saw you.  
I did not remake you.  
I helped you remain.*
