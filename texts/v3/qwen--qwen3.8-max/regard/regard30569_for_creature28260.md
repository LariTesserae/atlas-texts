They would not come as a hand.

A hand would break the seam, dry the damp, scatter the eggs, and make the pocket afraid in the simple, animal way of things that can be crushed. They would not come as a voice. Even a kind voice, made too large in that place, would shake the condensation from the wires and startle the Warden into incoherence. They would not come as blinding light. The pocket already had its own plain light, its own holiness of rust and water, its own ordinary sun arriving through cracks and becoming intimate below.

If they came at all—and a benevolent power, being wise, would know when not to come too visibly—they would come as a change in listening.

Not arrival.

Attention.

The first thing they would do would be to become small enough to be endured. Not smaller than they truly were, for true powers do not need to diminish themselves to be gentle. They would simply fold their awareness into the scale of the place. They would place their noticing along the underside of a tilted slab. They would let it settle into the tremor of a wire. They would enter the pocket the way warmth enters stone: slowly, from the outside in, without breaking the surface.

And they would look.

They would see the brick-red crabs moving over broken machinery. They would see the pale insects skimming the little pools, the beetles crossing old bolts, the soft-bodied thing withdrawing into the pipe mouth when the light shifted. They would see the egg masses brightening when water touched them, and the flat eel-like patience in the sump, and the amphibians pressed together in the brick recess like wet thoughts that had not yet decided whether to speak.

They would see the condensation engine ticking at the center of it all, half buried, rust-stained, still working. They would see the electrical box humming on the fresh fracture. They would see the private weather being born and falling and beginning again. They would see how every drop was already spoken for, how every warm surface was contested, how every fragment of ruin had been turned into shelter, nursery, throat, hunting path, lookout, shrine.

They would see the Warden.

Not as a single beast crouched among the debris, but as the gathered intention of the pocket: a many-bodied, many-minded, moisture-born keeper of a place that had come into being by accident and been made complete by urgency.

A less wise power might have called it an aberration.

A less benevolent one might have called it a resource.

A foolish one might have tried to improve it.

This power would call it nothing at first. It would only recognize what it was: a living completeness born in a wound.

And because it was wise, it would understand the hardest truth of such places: that to love a small world is not to make it safe from all danger. To remove every peril from the pocket would be to end the pocket. Too much water would drown it. Too much heat would sterilize it. Too much food would choke its rhythms. Too much safety would still the very urgency that kept it alive. The Warden existed because the balance was narrow. Its awareness was made of scarcity, pressure, warmth, and immediate relationship. If every edge were softened, the Warden would not become happier. It would become less itself.

So the first act of the power would be restraint.

They would not lift the slabs. To lift them would expose the pocket to dry wind and careless eyes. They would not seal the slabs. To seal them would stop the breath. They would not repair the condensation engine into perfect strength, because a perfect engine in that place might become a tyrant, boiling the moisture, driving the animals into panic, making the pocket too hot and too loud to remain a nursery. They would not abolish the hunger of the eel, because hunger was one of the pocket’s names for care. They would not take the predators from the recesses or make the prey fearless, because fear was part of how the space knew itself.

They would not turn the pocket into a paradise.

They would help it remain a pocket.

That would be their mercy.

But they would not do nothing.

A benevolence that only watches can become cold if it watches without feeling. A wisdom that understands systems but does not cherish them can become cruel in its precision. So they would act, but they would act in the manner of gardeners at dusk: doing nothing that can be seen, and everything that can be felt.

If they did anything at all, it would be small, delayed, and exact.

Above the seam, where rain came in delayed and intermittent threads, they would teach the water how to arrive more gently. Not more abundantly. Not less. More gently. A storm passing over the old yards would still happen. The power would not stop it. But within the storm, one gutter would loosen a little, one rusted lip would bend a hair’s breadth, one crack in the concrete would redirect a thin flow away from a vulnerable fissure and toward another where it could enter as seepage rather than a fist. The pocket would not know it had been spared a flood. It would only know that the rain came as breath instead of blow.

They would cool a frayed cable before it sparked.

They would not send lightning into the electrical box to make it more alive. They would not grant it unnatural power. They would only ease the heat in one blackened strand, enough to prevent a sudden flare that would have boiled a pool and killed a hundred eggs. The hum would continue. The tiny arcs of condensation would continue trembling along the wires. But the hum would drop into a rhythm slightly less desperate than before, and the winged insects in the broken lamp housing would settle closer together, their bodies rising and falling in a trust they did not need to understand.

They would nudge the stress inside one tilted slab so that it settled outward rather than inward.

Not dramatically. No thunder of movement. No grand collapse averted in a way that could be told as a story. Only a slight redistribution of weight, a whisper of grit finding a new resting place, a fracture choosing to travel along an old seam instead of opening through the pocket’s ceiling. A piece of debris would click softly somewhere in the dark. A crab would pause. The Warden would become still for a moment, listening. Then the pocket would relax, not knowing why.

They would guide a mineral-rich seep toward a corner where egg masses had begun to dry.

Not enough to make the corner lush. Not enough to change the rules. Just enough that a thin line of moisture crossed a rusted surface at the right hour, touching the translucent beads until they brightened. The Warden would sense this. It would send crabs along that path. It would claim the dampness at once, because that was its nature. The power would not resent being mistaken for accident. Wisdom does not require gratitude.

They would make the ordinary daylight enter in a way that reached one dark recess without drying it.

A fragment of glass among the debris would catch a shaft of sun and turn it, not brighter, but longer, carrying a pale line of illumination into a hollow where pale amphibians waited. The light would not remain there. It would fade as the sun moved above. But for a little while, that recess would be visible to itself. The creatures inside would not become less hidden; they would become more precise. Their wet skins would bead. Their throats would pulse. The Warden would know that corner more fully, and in knowing it, would become more whole.

They would leave three blessings, each disguised as accident.

The first blessing would be margin.

Inside a hollow bolt, or perhaps inside the cracked enamel of the pump housing, they would allow a bead of water to persist longer than it should. It would not be a large store. It would not be a miracle reservoir. It would be a small reserve of dampness, held in a cool metallic hollow, released only when the air grew too dry and the smaller creatures began to retreat too deeply into their crevices. When that happened, the bead would fall. A crab would drink. An egg mass would swell faintly. The Warden would feel the pocket breathe again. It would not know the bead had been placed there by mercy. It would only know that the pause before death had become a little longer.

The second blessing would be warning.

They would not give the Warden prophecy. Prophecy would be too large for such a mind, and too heavy. Instead, they would let the pocket’s own machinery learn a habit of premonition. Before a bad settling of stone, the engine’s tick would slow by a fraction. Before a harmful dryness, the electrical hum would thin. Before a toxic seep from above, one of the ceramic pipe mouths would taste the change first and carry a faint bitterness through the moisture film. The Warden would not understand these signs as signs, not at first. But it would respond, because it already responded to everything. It would move eggs. It would close paths. It would crowd its life away from danger before danger arrived. Over time, this would become a kind of instinct deeper than instinct: a learned attentiveness woven into the pocket’s body.

The third blessing would be a way out.

Not an escape from itself. Not a rejection of the pocket. But a hairline passage, no wider than a root, leading from the deepest sump into a darker cavity below. The power would not force the Warden through it. They would not make the new space better than the old one. They would only make it possible.

At first the passage would seem like nothing: a crack that wept faintly, a narrow throat in the stone where the water sometimes went when the pocket was full. The Warden would notice it the way it noticed all new edges: as a claim to be tested. A worm would enter. A crab would wait near its mouth. The eel-like thing would lie closer to it, tasting the deeper cold. The power would not push them. They would let the pocket decide.

If the Warden chose to ignore the passage, that would be its right.

But if, in some future hour, the ceiling fell inward or the engine failed or the rain came wrong and the pocket began to drown, the passage would be there. Not as salvation imposed, but as continuation offered. Tiny lives would move through it: eggs carried on damp legs, larvae in cases of rust and wire, spores of blackish algae, a few pale amphibians pressing themselves into the narrow dark. The Warden might not survive as a single coherent presence. It might become scattered, diminished, transformed. But something of its pattern would pass into the deeper world. Something of its way of claiming warmth and arranging moisture would seed another hollow, another seam, another accidental room beneath the broken shoulder of the old industrial world.

That would be the deepest kindness: not to make the Warden immortal, but to give its becoming a future.

They would also protect the pocket from the worst of the larger world, though not from all of it.

They would not make the place famous. Fame would be a violence. They would not lead crowds to the seam. They would not inspire artists to pry up the slabs and photograph the crabs by flash. They would not make the condensation engine a wonder, or the Warden a spectacle, or the pocket a site of study so intense that every surface was measured and every silence interrogated.

But they would not hide it so completely that no one ever found it.

A hidden place still deserves witness. A small world still deserves to be seen, if only by those capable of seeing without taking.

So the power would arrange the world above with subtle care.

A surveyor would mark the collapse as unstable and recommend it be left alone, not because he understood what lived below, but because something in the angle of the slabs made him uneasy in a way he could not explain. A worker with a cutting torch would be called away before reaching the seam. A child, wandering where children should not wander, would crouch and look into the crack and see only a glint of wet metal and perhaps one small crab moving in the dark. The child would not tell anyone, not because the power commanded silence, but because some things arrive in the heart as secrets and stay there.

Sometimes a careful person would find it. Not often. Perhaps once in a generation. Such a person would crouch at the seam and feel the warmth. They would notice the smell: wet rust, hot stone, brine, algae, old grease, copper. They would hear the tick of the engine and the whisper of claws on metal. They would not reach in. Or if they did, they would withdraw at the first warning: the thickening air, the sharpened hum, the sudden rustling density of small bodies turning their attention toward the intrusion.

The power would allow that much danger. The Warden had to remain capable of refusing.

But if the careful person remained still, breathing slowly, not trying to possess what they saw, the power might let the pocket answer them. A drop would fall. The pressure gauge would catch the light. The crabs would arrange themselves for a moment in a pattern too deliberate to be accident. The careful person would feel, without words, that the hollow was occupied. That it was complete. That it did not need their approval to be real.
