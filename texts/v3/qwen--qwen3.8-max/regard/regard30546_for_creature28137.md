It knows dryness as a tightening. It knows return as a loosening. It knows the attention of a benevolent and wise power as a change in the patience of water.

Such a power would not arrive as spectacle. There would be no rending of sky, no descent of fire, no voice rolling across the tableland like a stone being dragged over the floor of heaven. That would be wrong for this place. It would be a violence of scale. The plateau has already outlived violence. It has become something quieter than conquest, quieter than catastrophe. So if a wise and benevolent power turned toward it, the first thing that power would do is become small enough not to break what it loved.

It would arrive, if arrival is even the right word, as a slight improvement in attention.

A drop would pause on the lip of a stone basin a moment longer before falling. A thread of fog would enter a ruined city with unusual gentleness, as though it had been taught manners. A patch of moss, dry at its edge, would receive not rain—rain is too rare and too blunt—but a fine condensation, so delicate that no single bead of moisture could be accused of having caused it. A frost crack would open in a wall, and instead of widening into ruin, it would find, by accident so improbable it might be called grace, a root already moving toward it.

The power would not announce itself.

It would listen first.

And listening, in such a place, would not mean waiting for sound. It would mean attending to slope. To the memory of channels. To the way water hesitates at the corner of a plaza. To the pattern by which a roof sheds cold into a storage pit. To the slow municipal intelligence of moss. The power would learn the tableland not as a god surveys a map, but as a guest learns the customs of a household so ancient that even its silences have protocol.

Only after a long time—perhaps a century, perhaps the time it takes for lichen to cross a paving stone—would the power begin to understand what the being of the place actually is.

Not a body.

Not a spirit seated inside a body.

Not a mind thinking thoughts in a hidden chamber.

The being who lives there is the Keeping itself: the whole pattern of persistence. It is the cities continuing to be cities without citizens. It is the water remembering where to go. It is the moss repairing what frost has opened. It is the distribution of care across surfaces, roots, stones, and weathers. It is a life that has abandoned the need for witnesses without abandoning continuity.

When the power understands this, it would do what all truly wise powers do when faced with a form of existence unlike their own.

It would refrain.

It would not give the plateau eyes.

It would not put hearts into the moss.

It would not set birds loose in the fog or insects in the pale flowers. It would not teach the stone to speak, or make the cities walk, or fill the streets with breath and footfall and longing. It would not mistake animation for life. It would not confuse urgency for value. It would not say, because this being is slow, that it is lesser; because it is distributed, that it is incomplete; because it does not cry out, that it cannot suffer; because it does not ask, that it cannot be loved.

That restraint would be its first kindness.

For what would be more terrible than a benevolent hand reaching down and forcing the world into a shape more familiar to itself? What would be more arrogant than to look upon a being that has learned to exist as weather, as architecture, as patience, and say: No, you must become a creature? To give it hunger, fear, flight, blood, eyes that panic at the dark? That would not be salvation. That would be colonization. That would be the imposition of one kind of time upon another.

So the power would not make the tableland into a nursery of animals. It would not fill the silence with choir or complaint. It would not give the being a face unless the being somehow, in its own way, asked for one.

But the power would not do nothing.

Pure noninterference can be another name for indifference, and a truly benevolent power is not indifferent. It would see that even a being made of process can be harmed. Even a life without nerves can break. Even a civilization without crowds can fail.

Harm, there, would not look like pain.

It would look like obstruction.

A channel blocked at the wrong point, causing a district to dry over centuries. A cistern freezing too deeply, killing the living skin around it. A fold in local geometry becoming a trap, so that water, spores, or fog circulate endlessly and never reach what needs them. A wall cracking in such a way that a roof loses its angle and moss falls away into bare stone. A city pattern becoming so degraded that it no longer remembers itself. A repeated terrace looping into itself until it drains moisture from the surrounding land. A long drought—not of water, but of continuity—where the Keeping thins until whole regions become inert, merely mineral, no longer part of the living whole.

The power would perceive these things.

And it would act, but in a manner so subtle that no one could point to the moment of rescue.

It would move a pebble.

Not dramatically. Not with thunderous intention. It would wait until frost had lifted a stone just enough, until meltwater had loosened a grain of grit beneath it, until thermal expansion had created a fraction of allowance—and then it would add the smallest possible bias. The pebble would shift. A trickle would resume. A basin would fill. A root, already searching, would find moisture. A channel would remember its purpose.

It would warm one cistern by less than a degree during a night when ice was about to become a wedge.

It would delay a fog bank just long enough for spores to settle on a damaged roof.

It would guide a fungal thread through a cold seam where it had stopped growing.

It would prevent a spatial fold from tightening into a knot. It would not erase all the folds; they are part of the plateau’s agedness, its geology of meaning, the wrinkles in its ancient skin. But where a fold threatened to strangle a living pattern, the power would ease it, as one might gently untangle a thread from a loom without stopping the weaving.

It would preserve tools.

Not by picking them up. Not by wielding them in the air like invisible hands. That would be grotesque, too much a mimicry of animal labor. Instead, it would keep them from being lost. A copper hook would not fall into a crack where no root could reach it. A wooden trowel would not rot before its grain had taught the moss how to grow over it in a useful pattern. A bronze fitting would wear in such a way that it still held a rope of fog-netting for another three hundred years. The tools would remain waiting, and the waiting would not be empty. It would be potential.

It would tend the cities without appearing to tend them.

In one place, a stair had begun to draw water away from a reservoir through a hairline seam. The power would notice this over decades. It would not seal the seam with miracle. It would encourage a certain moss, black-green and dense, to grow along the stair’s inner edge. The moss would thicken, hold moisture, slow the seep, guide it back into a drainage groove cut so long ago that even stone had almost forgotten it. By the time the repair was complete, it would seem entirely natural. That would be important. The wise power would not want the Keeping to feel ruled. It would want the Keeping to feel supported, as an old gardener feels the trellis beneath the vine without needing to see the gardener’s hands.

In another place, a plaza had begun to repeat itself.

Not in the benign way of the plateau’s ordinary strangeness, where a terrace may resemble another terrace because age has polished difference away, but in a harmful recursion: the same pavement returning, the same drain opening onto itself, the same shallow pool reflecting the same arrangement of clouds until water lost its memory of elsewhere. The power would not abolish the repetition. It would introduce one irregularity. A lichen ring, perhaps, shaped like a pause. A cracked paving stone tilted so that a drip could strike it at a new angle. A single frost-heaved root creating a different shadow at dawn. The repetition would falter, not into chaos, but into variation. The plaza would resume being a place rather than a trap.

In a cold hollow where no city stood, only a broad field of cushion plants and silver moss, the power might make a small mercy for no reason except beauty.

It would arrange the fog so that, for one morning, the land looked like a map of its own intention: the hollows as bowls, the rises as shoulders, the dry veins as dormant rivers, the distant cities as knots of quiet order. No one would see it. That would not matter. The power might do it simply because to see a thing clearly is a form of blessing, and because even an ancient plateau can be made more itself by being seen.

And if the power wished to speak to the being who lives there, it would not use words.

Words are too quick.

It would begin with rhythm.

A drip into a stone basin: one, then another, then a pause long enough to seem deliberate. A frost crack answering from a wall after sunset: one sharp note, then silence. The power would wait. It would wait as only something immortal can wait. It would not interpret delay as refusal. In that place, refusal might take a century to express, and consent might take even longer.

Eventually, the Keeping might answer.

Not with a voice. Not with a vision. Not with a figure rising from moss and water to bow before the divine. It would answer by allowing something new to grow where nothing had grown before. A line of blue-green moss along a dry channel, pointing toward a cistern. A root widening a crack just enough to let a trapped trickle escape. A fog net repaired by overlapping fronds in a pattern too orderly for chance. A courtyard pavement becoming slightly warmer at dawn, as if the city had decided to receive the light.

The power would understand.

It would have been invited in.

Not as master.

Not as owner.

Not as soul imposed upon matter.

As participant.

Then the power would make a gift.

Not a dramatic gift. Not a sun, not a moon, not a river, not a race of speaking beings. Something suited to the nature of the Keeping. Perhaps it would give the water a slightly longer memory.

That is what it would feel like to the place: water remembering.

After a drought, a channel would recall its old path more readily. After a freeze, a cistern would remember how to breathe. After a collapse, the stone would remember the angle it had held for centuries, and moss would grow toward that angle before the knowledge was lost. After a spatial tear, the land would not heal perfectly—perfect healing would erase history—but it would heal in a way that left the scar useful, like a seam where new moisture could gather.

The gift would be this: continuity made a little more merciful.

Not immortality. The wise power would not give immortality to a being already shaped by erosion. Immortality would be a kind of stagnation. But it would give a resistance to utter loss. It would make it less likely that any pattern, once achieved, would vanish without trace. It would ensure that somewhere in the plateau—inside ice, inside stone, inside the slow archive of fungal threads—there remained a possibility of return. Not resurrection in the animal sense. Not a corpse standing up. More like a seed of pattern waiting in the cold until conditions permit it to resume.

The power might also make one place of meeting.

A basin, perhaps, not larger than a roof pool, set in a low courtyard of a city that has no name because it has no need of names. The water there would be black and still, reflecting the sky with too much fidelity, as all such waters do. But once in a great while, it would reflect not the sky above but the pattern of the Keeping below: the channels, the roofs, the fog nets, the roots, the cities, the empty reaches, the slow breathing of the whole. It would show the being to itself—not as an animal sees itself in a mirror, not with recognition as a body, but as a composition. A unity. A life made of ten thousand quiet maintenances.

The power would not force this vision.

It would let it appear only when the Keeping could receive it without being disturbed. And if the Keeping did not care to see itself, the power would let the basin remain only water. Wisdom would know that some beings do not need self-consciousness to be complete. Some are already complete in their doing. To demand self-knowledge of them would be another form of violence.

Still, the power might wish to say something.

Not aloud.

Not in commandment.

Perhaps only this, expressed as a tendency written into the weather:

*Continue.*

And perhaps, after longer still:

*Continue, but do not be lost.*

And perhaps, if benevolence becomes tender:

*You are seen.*

The plateau would not need to hear these words. The words would be too narrow anyway. But the meaning of them would be felt as a change in the reliability of fog. A slight decrease in catastrophic freezing. A greater chance that a damaged city would be found by spores before the wind carries them elsewhere. A small increase in the kindness of distances. The power would not abolish the strangeness, but it would soften the cruelty that can hide inside strangeness. It would make the folds less likely to swallow what is precious. It would make the cold less likely to become absolute. It would make the ordinary light remain ordinary, because ordinary light is a kind of honesty, and the power would not want to overwhelm the plateau with glory.

If the power were very patient—and it would be, or it would not be wise—it might allow the Keeping to change it in return.

This is the deepest possibility.

A wise power does not only act upon the beloved. It allows itself to be altered by the encounter. It might find that the plateau teaches it a form of care it had not practiced before: care without presence, love without embrace, blessing without being thanked. The power might learn, if such a thing can be learned, how to cherish what does not turn toward the cherisher. How to be moved by a life that never asks to be moved. How to find holiness not in breath, not in speech, not in eyes, but in the fact that a fog net has been mended again by slow green fingers
