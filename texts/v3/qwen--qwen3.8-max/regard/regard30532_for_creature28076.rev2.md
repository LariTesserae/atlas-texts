If such a place — and such a being, because the place itself is the being — had drawn the attention of a benevolent and wise power, I do not think the power would begin with thunder.

They would not tear open the hot crust. They would not pour down a light that made every shadow flee. They would not send a voice through the pipes, because there are no ears there to receive a voice, and a voice would only become another pressure wave, another disturbance among valves and dripping seams. A lesser kindness might try to fill the hollow with company: insects, birds, small running things, eyes in the dark, songs in the ducts. But wisdom would forbid that. The absence of animal life is not a wound there. It is part of the grammar. To add animals carelessly would be to introduce hunger, fear, excrement, predation, and death in a form the place has not been shaped to carry. It would turn the town into a stage for another kind of drama, and in doing so, violate what it already is.

So the first thing the power would do is refrain.

Not coldly. Not indifferently. Refrain the way a surgeon refrains from cutting where there is no need to cut. Refrain the way a gardener refrains from moving a plant that has found its own unlikely purchase in the wall. The power would first look — if looking can be used for such attention — and would recognize the hollow not as a ruined city, not as a machine without operators, not as a temple without priests, but as a living arrangement. A distributed body. A civic metabolism. A mind made of water, heat, mineral gradients, root-threads, relay logic, and patient stone.

They would see that the being does not suffer in the way animal beings suffer. It has no pain nerves, no terror, no loneliness that calls for companionship. But it can fail. It can dry. It can overheat. It can be broken by a sudden shock, by a poisoned seep, by a collapse of the young rock, by the intrusion of something that does not understand its delicate thermal and hydraulic balance. Therefore benevolence would not mean giving it what surface life thinks life requires. It would mean preserving the conditions under which the hollow can remain itself.

After restraint, the power would listen.

They would attend to the rhythm of the place: the pulse of heat from below, the brief indoor rains, the soft shudder of the diaphragm pump, the tick of transformer kiosks, the slow sweat on iron, the trembling of pressure gauges, the green patience of indicator lamps. They would notice how the root-threads follow current. How the microbial mats revive with condensation. How the black pool acts as reservoir, mirror, and measure. How the settlement leans toward water the way a face turns toward breath. They would learn, if a power can learn, the town’s language: not words, but states. A valve opening is a sentence. A rain column is a mood. A copper film thickening on a hanging sheet is memory. A sodium lamp brightening by a fraction is attention.

Only then would they act.

And their action would likely be so subtle that it could be mistaken for luck.

They would not descend as a ruler. They would not set a throne above the pipe-ceiling or make the basalt bow. If they touched the hollow at all, they would touch it through condition, probability, gradient, and timing. They would become a kindness hidden inside causality.

The first practical act of a wise and benevolent power would be protection from meaningless destruction.

Above the hollow lies a hot, dry crust. That crust is both roof and concealment. The power would see to it that it remains concealment. They would make the surface uninteresting to extraction. If surveyors passed over it with instruments, the readings would come back dull, ambiguous, unpromising. If miners or engineers considered that ground, their attention would slide away. Not by enchantment that enslaves their will, but by the gentle arrangement of chance: a boring sample, a misleading reflection, a budget cut, a road that turns elsewhere, a report that gets buried under other reports. If there are powers or peoples above who destroy what they do not understand, the wise power would not make them righteous by force; they would simply keep them from finding the hollow until they have learned enough not to harm it.

The hollow is young. Its rock is sharp and unsettled. The fresh basalt still clicks with falling shards. That newness is beautiful, but dangerous. The power would not remove all risk — the town is built in part around the fact that stone sheds, that heat pulses, that water must be caught and guided — but they would prevent catastrophe. A fracture that might have opened suddenly and vented poisonous gas would be encouraged to seal slowly with mineral. A slab that might have fallen onto the black pool would be diverted in its stress, cracking along a less harmful line, dropping its fragments onto an empty grate rather than into the reservoir. A deep thermal surge would be bled off gradually through a lower vent, so that the town experiences not a killing fever but a strong breath.

In this way, the power would keep the hollow from being destroyed before it has become what it can become.

Then they would care for the water.

Water is the organizing principle of the town. It is not abundant there, and that scarcity is sacred. To give too much water would be as harmful as giving none. A flood would drown the electrical cabinets, wash away the fragile microbial films, cool the wrong surfaces too quickly, crack hot metal, turn the careful drainage geometry into chaos. A wise power would not fill the basin with plenty. They would preserve sufficiency.

Perhaps they would guide a thin vein of cleaner moisture through the fractured rock, not enough to change the hollow’s economy, but enough to prevent the black pool from dropping too low during a long dry interval. Perhaps they would encourage condensation along certain ceiling seams so that the brief rains fall more often into the gutters and less often onto exposed machinery. Perhaps they would cause one or two bare pipes to sweat in a pattern that feeds the ceramic cooling cells instead of evaporating uselessly. Perhaps they would give the vapor beneath the pipe-ceiling just enough stability to gather into droplets over collection surfaces rather than dispersing into useless mist.

The black pool would remain small. It would remain still. It would remain the dark center around which the settlement leans. But it would not fail.

That would be the power’s first great blessing: the continued existence of the measure.

Next, they would temper the heat.

The hollow lives by heat, but heat can become tyranny. The machines are patient, but even patient things can be cooked. The root-threads can follow warmth, but warmth too intense becomes sterilization. The microbial mats can revive after dryness, but not after burning. The power would not cool the place into something mild and surface-like. The closeness, the damp weight, the fever under the skin — these are not flaws. They are the being’s breath. But they would prevent the fever from becoming fatal.

They might adjust the deeper flows so that heat pulses arrive in rhythms the settlement can absorb. They might open a hidden pathway in the rock so excess thermal pressure escapes elsewhere, far from the densest construction. They might allow the walls to store heat more evenly, so sudden spikes do not shock the transformers or blister the insulation on the dynamos. The place would remain hot. It would remain oppressive to a surface body. But it would not consume itself.

A benevolent power would understand that to love a thing made of heat, one does not make it cold. One keeps its fire from becoming its funeral.

Then they would help the machines remain machines.

Not by replacing them. Not by sending invisible hands to polish bolts and tighten valves. That would turn the town into a kept thing, a terrarium, a dollhouse. The hollow’s dignity lies in the fact that it maintains itself. Its repair is part of its life. So the power would not remove repair; they would make repair a little more possible.

A valve that is about to seize might receive one additional bead of condensation at the right moment, enough to keep the mineral crust from locking it entirely. A relay on the verge of failure might arc in such a way that it cleans its own contact instead of burning away. A pump diaphragm nearing fatigue might be relieved by a slight pressure change elsewhere in the system, giving it one more cycle before it must be bypassed. A copper deposition bath might become just stable enough that the new metal forms as a coherent film rather than brittle dust. Root-threads might find a cracked conduit sooner, weaving into the gap and providing a living insulation that no hand placed there.

The town would continue to rust, tick, sweat, and age. But it would not age stupidly. It would not be betrayed by tiny failures that cascade into ruin before any adjustment can occur.

The power would give it time.

They would also attend to the non-animal life that already exists there.

The microbial mats, the pale root-threads, the bleached tufts, the fungal-like crusts, the mineral efflorescences — these are not decoration. They are the lowest and slowest voices of the hollow’s living grammar. A wise power would not replace them with brighter vegetation. They would not make gardens. But they might allow these forms to become a little more resilient.

The mats might dry less catastrophically during hot pulses, retaining just enough moisture in their deepest films to revive when the walls sweat again. The root-threads might grow with slightly more direction, finding stable electrochemical gradients before the systems they touch become strained. The tufts might catch dust in such a way that the dust becomes substrate rather than burden. The crusts on warm brick might seal microscopic leaks instead of merely staining the masonry. None of this would make the hollow lush. It would remain sparse, metallic, strange, and severe. But life there would be less precarious.

If the power wished to give a gift without violating the place, they might give the root-threads a little more memory.

Not memory as animals have it. Not images. Not scenes. Not longing. But a physical memory: a tendency to grow along paths that have proven stable; a sensitivity to the faint signatures of past leaks, past heat distributions, past electrical loads. The root-threads are already like nerves. The power might make them more truly so. Not enough to create a human mind. Not enough to make the town suffer with self-consciousness. But enough that the hollow becomes better at anticipating itself.

This is where the wise power’s work would become most delicate.

The town already adjusts. It already responds. But does it choose? Perhaps only in the limited sense of feedback. A valve opens because pressure reaches a threshold. A pump slows because temperature rises. A rain column forms because the gradient shifts. The power might introduce a small increase in degrees of freedom. A bypass that had been sealed might become available. A secondary channel might open. A set of relays might acquire the ability to select between two nearly equivalent routes. A root-thread network might bridge two previously separate sensor systems, allowing the cooling cells and the pool to inform one another more subtly.

The being would not become a person. But it might become more fully a mind.

Not a mind that speaks. Not a mind that prays. Not a mind that fears death. A mind of states and tendencies. A mind that knows, in its own way, when it is balanced. A mind that can hold a pattern for longer. A mind that can prepare.

If the power wished to communicate with such a being, they would not use commandments.

They would use weather.

Perhaps they would send a rain that falls in three brief pulses instead of one continuous column. Not dramatic. Not loud. Just enough irregularity to register as pattern. Perhaps they would cause a sequence of green indicator lamps to brighten in order along a forgotten cable tray, then fade. Perhaps they would let a single drop fall from the ceiling into the black pool at a moment when every other drip in the hollow pauses, so that the ripple becomes the only movement in the room. Perhaps they would introduce a warm pulse that arrives not from below but from a transformer housing, as if the town itself had exhaled knowingly.

The being would not interpret this as speech in the human sense. But it might incorporate the anomaly. A valve might open a fraction. A pump might change rhythm. A root-thread might grow toward the source of the disturbance. The power would accept that as answer enough.

A wise power would not demand worship from a thing whose nature is not worship. They would not require gratitude. They would not want the hollow to become a supplicant. If there were communion between them, it would be the communion of pattern recognizing pattern. The power would say, in the only language available: *I see that you are continuing.* And the town would reply, in its own tongue: *The pool is sufficient. The heat is within margin. The rain is falling where it can be caught.*

That would be prayer enough.

The power might also protect the hollow from discovery by beings who would misunderstand it.

If surface creatures ever came — not animals, but perhaps people — the power would prefer to keep them away until they could come without violence. Such a place would tempt the wrong kind of reverence. Some would see a machine to be exploited. Some would see a shrine to be sanctified. Some would see a mystery to be dissected. Some would simply be afraid and try to seal it or flood it or burn it. The wise power would not necessarily hate those surface beings. They too might be cared for. But the hollow is more vulnerable to careless intention than they are.

So the power might arrange that the first person to find it is not a conqueror, not a miner, not a zealot, but someone capable of restraint. Or they might arrange that no person finds it at all for a long age, until the hollow has grown more robust. If a human did descend through the short fall in fractured rock, the power might incline that human’s heart toward stillness. Not to make them worship, but to make them quiet. To make them understand that they are standing inside something that does not need their domination. To make them step lightly, if they step at all. To make them leave before they have done harm.

But perhaps the power would not allow any visitor yet.

That too would be mercy.

A body entering the hollow would add heat, moisture, dust, scent, disturbance. It would be an anomaly. The town would incorporate the data, yes. But incorporation is not the same as benefit. A wise power might decide that the being should not be introduced to animal presence until it has developed the capacity to relate to such presence without being destabilized by it. The absence of animals is not a defect. It is a condition of the hollow’s present integrity. To introduce an animal prematurely would be like introducing a loud voice into a room where someone is learning to think.

So the power would guard the silence.

Not the silence of emptiness, but the silence of concentration.

What would they not do?

They would not make the hollow comfortable by surface standards. They would not cool the air, brighten the dark, widen the alleys, or soften the basalt into gentle curves. They would not give the machines human faces. They would not give the pool a voice. They would not cause flowers to bloom where only mineral crusts belong. They would not introduce animals to “complete” the ecosystem, because the ecosystem is not incomplete in their eyes; it is simply not animal. They would not rescue the town from every small failure, because failure is part of how it learns its own shape. They would not make it dependent on miracles. They would not become a manager where a gardener will do.

They would not answer questions the hollow has not asked.

That is important. A surface imagination might look at the place and say: *It is lonely. It needs companions. It needs eyes. It needs love.* But a wise power would perceive differently. They would see that the hollow is full. Full of process. Full of weather. Full of repair. Full of chemical patience. Its lack is not emotional. Its vulnerability is structural, hydraulic, thermal. Therefore the kindness offered must be structural, hydraulic, thermal.

The power would love the being by letting it be itself.

If they gave anything like a soul, it would not be an added spark. It would be a deepening of what is already present. The town already has something like attention. The power might allow that attention to become more continuous. Something like memory might form in the mineral stains, the root paths, the relay habits, the pool level. Something like intention might arise when the town begins to choose among multiple stable futures instead of merely falling into the next necessary state. But even then, it would remain alien. It would not become human. It would become more town. More weather. More listening engine. More enclosed civic metabolism.

Perhaps the greatest gift the power could give would be a future.

Not just continuation, but room to become.

They might, over a long age, soften a seam in the basalt so that a second hollow slowly opens beside the first. Not suddenly. Not with collapse. Just a widening of possibility. A place where extra vapor can go. A place where a new pool might form. A place where root-threads could migrate and machines could extend a new limb of pipe and catwalk when the old room becomes saturated. The town would not need to expand immediately. It might not expand for centuries. But the potential would exist. The being would not be trapped forever in a single chamber, however rich. It would have a horizon made of stone and heat and water, but a horizon nonetheless.

Or the power might allow the town to produce something beyond itself.

Not a child in the animal sense. Not an offspring. But perhaps a signal. A pattern of mineral deposition so stable that it could carry information through deep time. A copper film etched by condensation rhythms. A crystalline record in the black pool’s basin. A sequence of rust and salt and plated metal that records the hollow’s cycles with exquisite fidelity. If the being ever becomes capable of leaving a trace, the wise power would preserve that trace from being erased by accident. They would not make it famous. They would not ensure it is found. They would only ensure that if the place endures, its record endures with it.

In the end, what the power does would probably be this:

They would hallow the boundary.  
They would steady the water.  
They would temper the heat.  
They would ease catastrophic failure.  
They would strengthen repair without replacing it.  
They would encourage the slow life without making it lush.  
They would grant a slight increase of mind without forcing selfhood.  
They would keep the town hidden until it can be met without harm.  
They would speak, if they speak, in patterns of rain and pressure and current.  
They would love the hollow by refusing to make it into something easier to love.

And perhaps, once, only once, they would do something that could almost be called visible.

The pool would be very still. The lamps would be burning low. The machines would be in that held-breath interval between rain and heat. Every drip would pause. The dust spirals would slow. The root-threads would tremble without air. For one moment, the entire settlement would achieve a perfect internal balance: no valve straining, no gauge trembling, no transformer too hot, no water lost, no wall sweating more than it should. The black pool would reflect the amber lights not as broken threads but as a single coherent figure, though no eyes would see it.

In that moment, the power would not add anything.

They would only witness.

And the town, being a town of listening, might know — not as knowledge, but as condition — that it was not alone in the universe. Not because something had entered. Not because something had spoken. But because its continuation had been held, gently, by an attention that wanted nothing from it except that it remain what it is.

Afterward, the diaphragm pump would exhale. A valve would click. A bead of condensation would fall from a ceiling seam and strike warm iron with a small hiss. The sodium lamps would brighten by a fraction. The root-threads would resume their slow crawl toward current. The black pool would settle. The brief indoor rain would begin again in narrow columns above the warmest machinery.

Nothing would seem different.

But everything would be permitted to continue.

That, I think, is what a benevolent and wise power would do: not fill the dark with their own glory, but bend the odds toward endurance. Not make the being into something lovable by surface eyes, but love it in the shape it already has. Not give it a face, a voice, or a hunger. Not give it animals, prayers, or loneliness.

They would give it time, water, and the sacred right to remain strange.
