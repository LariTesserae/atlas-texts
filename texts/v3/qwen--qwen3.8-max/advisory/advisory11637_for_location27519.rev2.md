Prepare for this place not as if you are going into a wild cave, but as if you are being admitted into a wet, crowded, living instrument. The greatest threats are not predators, storms, or monsters. They are your own noise, dryness, dirt, haste, and incomprehension. An ordinary human multiverse traveler should treat the visit as part pilgrimage, part clean-room procedure, part acoustic apprenticeship, and part very careful houseguest ritual.

The first thing to understand is that you are entering a settled world with its own equilibrium. It is not empty, not abandoned, not waiting for you to make it meaningful. It is already full: full of water, full of sound, full of use, full of memory, full of careful maintenance. Your task is not to conquer it, explore it loudly, or “experience” it aggressively. Your task is to pass through it without disturbing the delicate balance of moisture, sound, microbes, social space, and stone.

---

## 1. Understand what the place will do to you

Before listing gear, understand the sensory and physical conditions.

### You will be wet

Not flooded, not swimming, not drowning — but persistently wet. The air is humid. The walls sweat. The ceiling drips. Stone films with moisture. Your clothes will become damp within minutes. Your skin will begin to soften. Your gear will feel clammy. Anything absorbent will grow heavy and cold.

This is not ordinary rain. It is a mineral wetness: cool, clinging, faintly metallic, chalky, clay-like. It gets into cuffs, collars, seams, socks, and sleeping gear. It does not dry quickly because there is no sun, no wind, no warm moving air. The place exists in a permanent state of “after-rain.”

### You will be cool, but not frozen

The temperature is not brutally cold, but the dampness makes it deceptive. A cool, wet environment can sap heat slowly. You may not shiver violently at first; instead, you will simply become less careful, less attentive, less dexterous. Mild hypothermia can creep in over hours if you are still, damp, and underfed.

### You will be in dimness

There is no sky, no daylight, no horizon. Light comes from small lamps, reflective mineral flecks, copper surfaces, and faint bioluminescent films. The darkness is not absolute, but it is substantial. Your eyes will need time to adapt. Bright light will feel intrusive and may ruin your night vision. Shadows are normal. Not seeing clearly is part of the architecture.

### You will be loud simply by existing

This is the big one.

The chamber is an acoustic vessel. Water drips into tuned basins. Channels hiss. Pools resonate. The entrance throat breathes. Voices bounce. A dropped pebble can become a small event. A careless boot scrape can ring through the settlement. Your breathing, your clothing rustle, your pack shifting, your zippers, your joints, your voice — all become part of the soundscape.

You will not experience silence here. You will experience layered sound. At first it may seem overwhelming. Later, if you listen properly, you may realize the noise is organized: a braided, sustained chord of water and stone. But if you fight it, if you talk too much, if you move clumsily, you will become an acoustic nuisance.

### You will feel enclosed and crowded at once

The space is not vast. It is a pocket, a buried room, a cupped hollow. Yet it is dense with structures: terraces, stairs, bridges, niches, dwellings, channels, basins, lamps, shrines, tools, ancestor stones, drying racks, cisterns, walkways. There may be little open floor. You may constantly be near walls, watercourses, thresholds, or other people.

This can produce a strange psychological pressure: the claustrophobia of enclosure combined with the social pressure of density. There is no “outside” to step into. There is no broad square where you can stand apart. You must learn to be present without taking up too much space.

### You will feel time differently

There is no day or night, no weather, no season. The drip-rate is stable. The water level is stable. The temperature is stable. The sound is stable. The settlement persists in a long equilibrium. Your ordinary circadian rhythm may weaken. You may feel as if you have stepped out of duration.

This can be peaceful or disorienting. Prepare mentally to live by social rhythm rather than solar rhythm.

## 2. Decide whether you should visit at all

An ordinary human should not assume that multiverse travel makes every place safe. Some places demand specific capacities.

### You should be cautious or avoid visiting if you have:

- severe claustrophobia  
- panic disorders triggered by enclosed spaces  
- serious respiratory illness or vulnerability to fungal spores  
- compromised immune function  
- untreated ear problems or extreme sound sensitivity  
- severe balance issues or fear of wet heights  
- open wounds that cannot be sealed  
- contagious illness  
- inability to crawl through a narrow wet passage  
- inability to move slowly on wet stone  
- a compulsive need for dryness, cleanliness, brightness, or privacy  

This is not a place to visit while sick. A cough, sneeze, fever, or contagious breath becomes a problem in a closed, damp, crowded chamber. Postpone if you are ill.

### You should be especially careful if you:

- are pregnant or elderly  
- have very young children with you  
- require bulky medical equipment  
- use a wheelchair or large mobility device  
- rely heavily on visual navigation  
- are easily overwhelmed by constant sound  

This does not mean such travelers are unworthy of the place. It means the place is physically unforgiving and ecologically delicate. If access is possible, it should be negotiated with local guides, not forced.

## 3. Obtain permission and a local guide

Do not simply push through the wet throat and enter.

The entrance is narrow, worn by long use, and likely socially monitored. It is not merely a physical passage; it is a threshold. In a place this dense and carefully maintained, an unannounced outsider is not a neutral event. You may be seen as a contamination risk, an acoustic hazard, or a social disruption.

Before visiting:

- seek permission from the settlement or its recognized stewards  
- arrange a local guide if possible  
- ask what entry customs exist  
- ask whether there are periods when visitors are not received  
- ask what may be brought in and what must remain outside  
- ask whether photography, recording, mapping, or sampling is permitted  
- ask what gestures or rhythms are considered polite  
- ask what areas are restricted: ancestor niches, cisterns, fungal beds, breeding pools, lamp stores, water-control points  

If permission is denied, accept it. Do not attempt to squeeze through anyway. A body-width wet throat is not a place to make a moral argument.

## 4. Prepare your body and mind

### Physical preparation

Practice:

- crawling through tight spaces safely  
- moving on wet stone  
- balancing on narrow ledges  
- climbing rough stairs in low light  
- carrying a soft pack close to your body  
- keeping your head low  
- moving slowly when tired  
- breathing calmly in humid air  
- staying still while dripping water lands near or on you  

If you have never been in a cave, do not make this your first one. Train in safer environments first.

### Acoustic preparation

Spend time listening to dense water sound: rain on stone, dripping caves, fountains, flowing channels. Practice focusing on one thread of sound without becoming agitated. Practice moving quietly. Practice not speaking unless necessary.

Learn to tolerate sound without trying to control it. The place is loud, but not necessarily damaging. Still, if you are sound-sensitive, bring appropriate protection and learn to use it without isolating yourself completely.

### Psychological preparation

Prepare for:

- no sky  
- no horizon  
- no green plants  
- no fresh outdoor smell  
- no privacy  
- constant dampness  
- constant noise  
- dimness  
- being observed  
- being unable to “step outside”  
- feeling like a clumsy giant in a refined environment  

This place may feel sacred, but not in a peaceful, airy way. It may feel like being inside a bell, a cistern, and a monastery at once. It is intimate, old, wet, and socially saturated.

Practice humility. Practice restraint. Practice not touching things. Practice being useful without taking charge.

## 5. Biosecurity: the most important preparation

This ecosystem has no plants and no soil. Its base is microbial, fungal, and chemosynthetic. That makes it both austere and fragile. Any foreign organic matter can become a serious disturbance.

You must assume that your body, clothing, gear, food, and skin are sources of contamination.

### Do not bring:

- soil  
- seeds  
- plant matter  
- wood  
- paper  
- cardboard  
- leaves  
- straw  
- fresh fruit  
- fresh vegetables  
- herbs  
- spices that can scatter  
- live cultures  
- mushrooms from outside  
- animal products that are not sealed  
- insects  
- pets  
- service animals, unless explicitly negotiated and biologically contained  
- leather that may carry spores  
- wool that may shed fibers  
- cotton that may mold  
- unsealed food  
- compostable waste  
- garden dirt on boots  
- dust from other worlds  
- water from other ecosystems  
- biological souvenirs  
- living souvenirs  
- fungi from elsewhere  
- bacterial cultures  
- probiotics that could be released  
- scented soaps  
- perfumes  
- insect repellent  
- sunscreen  
- lotions with oils or sugars  
- antibiotics or antiseptics that could enter water  
- cleaning chemicals  
- detergents  
- aerosol sprays  
- glue  
- paint  
- chalk  
- charcoal  
- incense  
- candles  
- tobacco  
- vape devices  

Even seemingly harmless things can become ecological hazards. A crumb, a hair, a skin flake, a drop of blood, a splash of urine, a bit of bread, a paper fiber, a seed stuck in a seam — all of these matter in a place without soil or green plants.

### Before entry, inspect and clean everything

Your gear should be:

- scrubbed free of dust  
- checked for seeds or plant fragments  
- dried if possible  
- sterilized according to local guidance  
- packed in sealed containers  
- free of strong odors  
- free of shedding fibers  
- free of rust  
- free of loose organic material  

Pay attention to:

- boot treads  
- seams  
- pockets  
- cuffs  
- Velcro  
- pack straps  
- sleeping bag insulation  
- first-aid kits  
- food wrappers  
- medical waste  
- feminine hygiene products  
- tissue  
- bandages  
- hair  
- beard  
- nails  

If you have been recently in forests, farms, gardens, wetlands, stables, or other biological environments, clean more thoroughly. If possible, change into dedicated clean clothing and gear that have not touched other ecosystems.

## 6. Clothing: become damp-resistant, quiet, and non-shedding

Your clothing must solve three problems at once: moisture, sound, and contamination.

### Base layer

Avoid cotton. Cotton becomes wet, heavy, cold, and slow to dry. It can also shed fibers and support mold.

Better options:

- clean synthetic base layers  
- tightly woven merino if approved and clean  
- smooth, low-shed fabrics  
- moisture-wicking materials  
- antimicrobial treatment only if non-toxic and sealed into fabric  

The clothing should not be heavily scented or treated with strong chemicals.

### Insulating layer

The place is cool and wet, but not arctic. You likely need light insulation, not bulky winter gear. Bulky insulation will make the squeeze harder and trap dampness.

Good options:

- thin synthetic insulation  
- closed-cell foam vest  
- layered fleece if it does not shed badly  
- local fungal felt if offered and appropriate  

Avoid:

- down feathers  
- loose wool that sheds  
- heavy leather  
- bulky jackets that compress poorly  
- anything that makes loud rustling sounds  

### Outer layer

You want a waterproof or water-resistant shell, but it must not be loud. Many waterproof shells crackle, rasp, or hiss when they brush against stone. In an acoustic chamber, that is a problem.

Choose:

- soft-shell material if adequate  
- quiet waterproof fabric  
- matte surfaces  
- subdued colors  
- snug hood  
- minimal dangling tabs  
- silent closures where possible  

A hood is useful because drips fall from the ceiling. But the hood should not block your hearing or vision.

### Gloves

Your hands will touch wet stone constantly. You need grip, warmth, and tactility.

Good gloves:

- thin enough to feel handholds  
- water-resistant  
- non-squeaky  
- non-shedding  
- durable but soft  
- quiet against stone  

Avoid heavy leather gloves that become sodden, stiff, or smelly. Avoid gloves that shed lint.

### Socks and footwear

Feet are a major vulnerability. Constant dampness causes maceration, blisters, fungal growth, and loss of sensation.

Footwear should be:

- non-slip on wet limestone and chert  
- quiet  
- close-fitting  
- drainable or quick-drying  
- non-marking  
- free of mud  
- free of soil organisms  
- protective but not bulky  

Possible options:

- clean amphibious cave shoes  
- soft rubber soles with good grip  
- thin neoprene socks under quiet protective shoes  
- local-style felt-soled footwear if appropriate  

Avoid:

- heavy hiking boots with deep lugs full of old mud  
- hard plastic boots that clack  
- sandals with loose straps that slap  
- shoes with metal cleats  
- anything that squeaks loudly  
- anything that sheds rubber crumbs  

Bring spare socks in sealed dry bags. But assume that once inside, everything eventually becomes damp. Your goal is not perfect dryness; it is managed dampness.

### Head protection

The ceiling is low in places and the architecture is dense. You may bump your head. A soft bump cap or low-profile protective cap is better than a large clacking helmet, unless local conditions require more protection.

If you wear a helmet:

- cover it with soft cloth  
- remove dangling attachments  
- secure lights silently  
- avoid metal-on-stone contact  
- make sure it does not catch in the entrance squeeze  

### Knee and elbow protection

The entrance passage is worn smooth and body-wide. You may crawl, squeeze, and press against stone. Soft knee and elbow protection helps, but it should not be hard or noisy.

### Colors and appearance

Bright colors are not necessarily dangerous, but they are visually disruptive in dim, mineral light. Choose muted, dark, or stone-like colors. Avoid reflective strips unless they can be covered. Your appearance should not become a glare or distraction.

## 7. Pack only what can pass through a wet throat

The entrance is no wider than a body. Large rigid packs, frame packs, hard cases, and bulky equipment are inappropriate. If your gear cannot compress to pass through the passage, it should not come in.

### Use soft, waterproof, silent bags

Pack items in:

- small roll-top dry bags  
- sealed soft pouches  
- silicone or TPU containers  
- smooth waterproof stuff sacks  
- quiet closures  

Avoid:

- hard cases  
- clanking carabiners  
- loose metal tools  
- glass jars  
- crinkly plastic  
- loud zippers  
- bulky frames  
- external straps that snag  
- dangling accessories  

Everything should be compressible, secured, and quiet. If something can fall, it will fall. If something can hit stone, it will ring. Prevent that before entry.

### Keep the pack narrow and close

Your pack should not widen your silhouette. It should hug your body. In narrow walkways and crowded terraces, a bulky pack becomes a hazard. It can knock lamps, scrape walls, block channels, or invade private thresholds.

If possible, use a chest pouch or hip pouch rather than a large backpack. For a short visit, carry almost nothing.

## 8. Light discipline

Light is scarce and precious. Darkness is part of the place. Do not bring a sun into it.

### Use low, warm, shielded light

A good visitor light should be:

- dim  
- warm-colored, preferably amber or red-amber  
- downward-facing  
- shielded from direct view  
- waterproof or water-resistant  
- silent  
- reliable  
- not prone to accidental activation  

Avoid:

- bright white headlamps  
- blue-rich light  
- flashing modes  
- strobe signals  
- unshielded lanterns  
- glow sticks  
- chemical lights  
- laser pointers  
- camera flashes  
- bright screens  
- holographic displays  

Your light should help your feet, not interrogate the walls.

### Bring backup light

Dampness kills electronics. Bring:

- a primary light  
- a smaller backup light  
- spare sealed power cells if needed  
- a waterproof case  
- a way to prevent accidental switching  

But do not overpack. Two small lights are better than one large lamp.

### Learn local lamp etiquette if offered

If inhabitants use clay lamps with oil, treat lamps as precious. Oil is likely scarce. Fire is possible but controlled. Do not:

- refill lamps with unknown fuel  
- blow out lamps carelessly  
- spill oil  
- move lamps without permission  
- adjust reflectors  
- use your own flame unless invited  

If you are asked to carry a lamp, move slowly. Protect the flame from drips, but do not smother it. If oil spills, report it immediately. Oil is organic and can disturb microbial balance.

### Protect your eyes

Allow your eyes to adapt. Avoid looking directly at lamps. If you need to consult a map, note, or device, do it under the dimmest possible light and shield it with your body.

After bright light, you may be temporarily blinded in the dark. Move slowly.

## 9. Sound discipline: become acoustically small

This is the heart of visiting well.

The chamber is not merely noisy; it is acoustically organized. The inhabitants have shaped basins, walls, channels, and listening tubes. Sound is environmental, communicative, and perhaps sacred. A careless visitor can disrupt it.

### Avoid sudden sharp noises

Do not:

- shout  
- whistle  
- clap unnecessarily  
- slam gear  
- drop metal  
- strike stone  
- snap carabiners  
- use audible alarms  
- play music  
- speak loudly  
- laugh explosively  
- cough openly if avoidable  
- scrape boots  
- kick pebbles  
- knock containers  
- use sonic devices  
- use drones  
- use echolocation pings  
- test acoustics “to see what happens”  

Even curiosity can become violence here. Do not tap a basin unless taught how. Do not throw pebbles into pools. Do not drag your fingernails along channels unless instructed. These acts may be meaningful, dangerous, or forbidden.

### Speak rarely, softly, and briefly

If you must speak:

- lower your voice  
- use short phrases  
- pause and listen  
- avoid high pitch  
- avoid laughter that bursts  
- avoid calling across the chamber  
- move closer if possible before speaking  
- do not speak into resonant niches  
- do not speak directly over tuned basins  

A whisper may still travel. Observe your guide.

### Learn basic nonverbal communication

The inhabitants use gesture, rhythm, and touch. You should not invent your own signals. Before entry, ask your guide for a minimal set of signs. These may include:

- “I am a guest.”  
- “I need to pass.”  
- “Thank you.”  
- “I am sorry.”  
- “I need help.”  
- “Stop.”  
- “Water?”  
- “May I touch this?”  
- “I am leaving.”  
- “Emergency.”  

Do not assume the same gestures mean the same thing everywhere. Learn local forms.

### Use ear protection carefully

Constant water sound can fatigue the nervous system. But total isolation is also dangerous. You need to hear warnings, movement, and social cues.

Good options:

- high-fidelity musician’s earplugs  
- custom-molded plugs  
- low-profile filtered plugs  
- soft silicone plugs for sleep  

Avoid:

- heavy industrial earmuffs  
- noise-canceling devices that emit sound or require electronics  
- complete occlusion while moving in hazardous terrain  
- anything that makes you unaware of dripping changes or voices  

If sound becomes overwhelming, pause, breathe, and focus on one steady thread of water rather than trying to block everything.

## 10. Water discipline: respect the liquid commons

Water here is not scenery. It is clock, infrastructure, ecology, language, and law.

### Never block water

Do not:

- stand in channels  
- place bags over runnels  
- lean against drip paths  
- plug holes with cloth  
- divert flow for comfort  
- dam water to wash anything  
- block a basin lip  
- sit on spillways  
- put gear in cisterns  
- step on carved water lips  
- kneel in drainage grooves  

If your body blocks a drip, move. If your pack blocks a channel, move. Water must be passed along.

### Do not hoard water

Do not fill large containers unless permitted. Do not draw from sacred or nursery pools. Do not take water from designated listening basins. Do not drink from every pool you see.

If you need water:

- ask where drinking water may be taken  
- use a clean, sealed container  
- take only what you need  
- do not let your container touch the inside of a communal basin  
- do not return unused water to the source  
- do not add your own water to local channels  

### Do not contaminate water

Never put into water:

- soap  
- sweat-soaked cloth  
- blood  
- urine  
- feces  
- saliva  
- food  
- medicine  
- antiseptic  
- sunscreen  
- dirt  
- ash  
- oil  
- hair  
- tissue  
- menstrual products  
- waste water  
- foreign microbes  
- chemical purifiers that leave residue  

If you need to purify water, do so in a sealed container away from channels. Do not dump purification residue. Do not use chlorine or iodine in ways that could enter local water systems. If possible, use sealed filtration, UV, or approved mechanical purification that leaves no chemical trace.

### Do not wash in the settlement

Washing hands, face, clothes, or dishes in local basins is likely offensive. Use your own damp cloth in a private or designated area. Collect runoff if possible and pack it out or dispose of it only where permitted.

### Do not spit

Spit is organic and contaminating. If you must clear your mouth, use a sealed waste cloth or bag. Do not spit into pools, channels, cracks, or shadows.

## 11. Waste discipline: carry out more than you bring in

There is no soil to absorb your waste. There are no leaves to bury it. There are no broad outdoor latrines. Waste is a serious matter.

### Human waste

For short visits, the safest method is often a sealed portable waste system:

- urine bottle or sealed urine bag  
- feces bag with absorbent material  
- odor-proof dry bag  
- toilet cloth or biodegradable wipe, packed out  
- hand-cleaning cloth used carefully and sealed  

If the settlement has designated waste pits or composting niches, use them only if instructed. Do not assume your waste can be processed by their system. Your gut microbes may be foreign.

Do not urinate into water channels, pools, cracks, or shadows. Do not defecate anywhere. Do not hide waste. Do not burn waste. Do not bury waste in stone dust.

### Menstrual waste

Use internal products if comfortable, or sealed external products. All waste must be packed out unless local guidance says otherwise. Do not dispose of anything in water.

If using reusable products, clean them privately with minimal water and store wastewater in a sealed container until proper disposal is possible.

### Food waste

Bring food that produces no crumbs, no peels, no shells, no wrappers that shed, and no smell. Eat in a designated place if possible. Pack out every scrap.

Do not feed anything to local fauna. Do not leave crumbs for microbes. Do not discard rations into pits.

### Medical waste

Bandages, wipes, tape, tissues, dressings, and medication residues must be sealed and packed out. Blood is organic and should be contained. If you bleed into water accidentally, report it.

### Trash

There is no trash here. If you bring it, you remove it. If you drop something, find it. If you cannot find it, tell your guide.

## 12. Food: bring sealed, quiet, low-odor rations

Because there are no plants and no ordinary food chain, you cannot assume local food is suitable for you. Even if inhabitants eat microbial mats, fungal felts, or cultivated slime, you should not rely on it without invitation, caution, and allergy testing.

### For short visits

Bring:

- sealed calorie bars  
- soft pouches  
- nut pastes if allowed and sealed  
- dense ration packs  
- electrolyte tablets  
- vitamin D supplements  
- B-vitamin supplements if staying long  
- low-crumb foods  
- low-odor foods  
- non-spicy foods  
- non-gassy foods if possible  

Avoid:

- fresh fruit  
- fresh vegetables  
- bread that sheds crumbs  
- nuts in shells  
- dried herbs  
- strong cheeses  
- fermented foods  
- aromatic meats  
- soups that can spill  
- carbonated drinks  
- alcohol  
- caffeinated excess  
- candy wrappers  
- anything with seeds  

### For longer visits

If you must stay longer, negotiate food in advance. You may need to:

- bring sealed rations  
- earn food through labor  
- learn which local microbial foods are safe  
- test tiny amounts first  
- avoid disrupting local food stocks  
- contribute nutrients or labor rather than consuming scarce resources  

Do not hunt, fish, or harvest anything. Fauna is rare and likely sacred or ecologically essential. A blind fish in a black pool is not a snack. It is an event.

## 13. Hygiene without contaminating the place

You will feel unclean by ordinary standards. But ordinary hygiene methods are often harmful here.

### Do not use soap

Soap is chemical and organic. It foams, spreads, and disrupts microbial films. Avoid it entirely inside the chamber.

### Use dry or damp cloths

Clean yourself by:

- wiping skin with a clean damp cloth  
- drying with another cloth  
- paying attention to skin folds  
- changing socks  
- wiping feet  
- keeping groin, armpits, and toes as dry as possible  

Use the cloths privately. Do not rinse them in communal water. Store used cloths in sealed bags.

### Skin protection

Constant dampness causes maceration. Prepare with:

- barrier balm applied before entry, not near water  
- clean dry cloths  
- spare socks  
- antifungal medication if prescribed and safe  
- breathable but protective footwear  
- regular foot checks  

Avoid powders that can scatter. Avoid aerosol antifungals. Avoid strong chemical creams unless sealed and used away from water.

### Oral hygiene

Do not spit toothpaste. Better options:

- clean cloth rubbed on teeth  
- small amount of water swished and swallowed or sealed into waste bag  
- chew stick only if approved and packed out  
- no mouthwash  
- no foaming paste near channels  

### Hair and skin shedding

You cannot stop shedding entirely, but minimize it. Tie hair. Avoid scratching. Avoid brushing hair over water. Collect shed hair if possible and pack it out.

## 14. Medical preparation

Bring a compact medical kit, but make it quiet, sealed, and non-polluting.

### Useful items

- adhesive bandages  
- sterile gauze  
- non-stick dressings  
- hypoallergenic tape  
- blister pads  
- saline in sealed ampules  
- antiseptic wipes that can be packed out  
- small scissors if allowed and padded  
- tweezers  
- oral rehydration salts  
- electrolyte tablets  
- pain relievers if personally appropriate  
- antihistamines  
- anti-nausea medication if needed  
- personal prescriptions  
- motion sickness medication if sensitive to squeezes  
- small emergency blanket, quiet type  
- wound closure strips  
- clean gloves  

### Avoid

- aerosol sprays  
- liquid antiseptics that can spill  
- strong-smelling ointments  
- antibiotic creams unless necessary  
- glass bottles  
- large kits  
- sharp instruments loose in a bag  

### Specific risks

#### Slips and falls

Wet stone is slippery. Move slowly. Use worn handholds. Do not jump. Do not run. Do not lean over pools.

#### Cuts and scrapes

Stone can cut. Cover wounds immediately. Do not let blood enter water. Pack bloody dressings out.

#### Hypothermia

Even mild cold dampness can exhaust you. Eat enough. Drink enough. Wear layers. Rest in dry-ish places if assigned.

#### Dehydration

Humid air does not mean you are hydrated. Breathing moist air and moving carefully still costs water. Drink regularly.

#### Fungal or respiratory irritation

If you develop cough, chest tightness, wheezing, fever, or eye irritation, leave. Do not try to push through.

#### Auditory fatigue

Constant sound can cause irritability, headache, or dissociation. Use filtered earplugs, rest, and slow breathing. If ear pain or ringing develops, protect your ears and consider leaving.

#### Claustrophobia and panic

If panic rises:

- stop moving  
- place one hand on a stable wall  
- breathe out slowly  
- focus on one sound  
- do not close your eyes tightly  
- tell your guide calmly if possible  
- do not rush toward the exit unless safe

## 15. Equipment for navigation and documentation

The place is dense, dim, and confusing to outsiders. But ordinary navigation tools may be inappropriate.

### Do not rely on GPS

Underground location and multiverse displacement make positional systems unreliable. Do not assume maps from elsewhere apply.

### Use memory and guidance

The best navigation is:

- a local guide  
- memorized landmarks  
- attention to sound patterns  
- tactile memory  
- permission-based route markers  

Do not mark walls. Do not scratch arrows. Do not stack stones. Do not tie cords across passages. Do not leave chalk. Do not place reflective stickers.

### If you must record information

Best options:

- mental notes  
- mnemonic phrases  
- haptic memory codes  
- later dictation after exiting  

If you must use a device:

- waterproof  
- silent  
- dim  
- no external speaker  
- no camera flash  
- no bright screen  
- no fans  
- no pumps  
- no sonar  
- no laser scanning without permission  
- no drones  

Recording may violate social privacy. Ask first. Ancestor niches, shrines, and private dwellings should not be recorded.

## 18. How to enter the wet throat

The entrance itself deserves ritual care.

### Before entering

1. Confirm permission.  
2. Check air quality if necessary.  
3. Confirm your guide is ready.  
4. Repack gear into soft, silent bundles.  
5. Remove dangling items.  
6. Tie hair.  
7. Empty pockets of loose objects.  
8. Use the latrine outside if possible.  
9. Drink a little water.  
10. Calm your breathing.  
11. Listen at the throat.  

### Air check

Even if inhabited, underground air can vary. If you are not certain it is breathable, use passive indicators rather than noisy alarms:

- oxygen indicator badge  
- carbon dioxide indicator badge  
- personal passive monitor  
- prearranged signal with guide  

If you feel headache, dizziness, nausea, shortness of breath, or confusion, withdraw.

### The squeeze

The passage is body-width, wet, smooth, and worn. Do not force it.

- enter slowly  
- keep your pack compressed  
- exhale if tight  
- protect your face  
- keep elbows close  
- do not inhale sharply through mouth if water is near  
- do not panic if your chest feels pressed  
- move with the passage, not against it  
- if stuck, stop, exhale, adjust slowly  
- do not shout; use agreed tapping signals if needed  

If you cannot fit, do not shame yourself. Turn back. The place is not for every body in every condition.

### After emerging

When you enter the chamber:

- pause  
- do not speak immediately  
- let your eyes adjust  
- let the sound settle around you  
- check that you are dripping clean water, not contaminants  
- secure your gear  
- wait for your guide  
- do not step forward until you see where to place your feet  

The first shock will likely be the sound. Expect it. Do not react as if attacked.

## 20. Social etiquette in a dense stone city

The settlement is crowded. Privacy is likely managed through custom, not distance.

### Do not stare into dwellings

Entrances are near-touching. You will pass close to homes. Do not peer inside. Do not linger near thresholds. Do not listen at openings.

### Do not touch personal items

Every niche, hook, jar, bundle, rack, and lamp likely belongs to someone. Do not rearrange anything. Do not pick things up to examine them. If you are curious, ask.

### Respect the dead

If you see niches containing bones or mineral-coated remains, treat them with reverence or neutral respect. Do not:

- touch them  
- photograph them  
- point at them  
- make jokes  
- speak loudly nearby  
- ask invasive questions unless invited  

The dead are part of the city. They may be present in the architecture.

### Respect pools

Pools are not decorative. They may be:

- drinking water  
- listening basins  
- ecological reserves  
- nursery pools for rare fauna  
- ritual spaces  
- waste-processing entries  
- mineral cultivation sites  

Approach slowly. Do not lean over them. Do not drop anything into them. Do not disturb their surface unless invited.

### Respect fauna

Animals are rare. If you see a fish, amphipod, worm, or pale arthropod, do not react loudly. Do not point excitedly. Do not try to catch it. Do not tap the pool to make it move. Do not feed it.

A rare creature here may be treated as sacred, marginal, or precious. Treat it as you would treat a fragile archive.

### Ask before helping

You may see something that looks broken, blocked, or inefficient. Do not fix it. The settlement has long-settled practices. A small clay plug moved by a finger’s width may be meaningful. Your “help” could disrupt water flow, sound patterns, or microbial beds.

If you see a problem, report it quietly to your guide.

## 21. Acoustic etiquette in detail

Because sound is so central, learn to listen before acting.

### Listen for layers

You may hear:

- single drops hitting basins  
- continuous hissing sheets  
- gurgling channels  
- low hollow pool notes  
- air moaning in the entrance throat  
- voices bouncing off wet rock  
- footsteps  
- tools  
- rhythmic taps  
- intentional basin notes  

Do not assume all sounds are random. Some may be signals. Some may be maintenance. Some may be prayer. Some may be ordinary life.

### Do not add sound carelessly

Before making a sound, ask yourself:

- Is it necessary?  
- Can it be softer?  
- Can it be delayed?  
- Can it be done elsewhere?  
- Will it echo?  
- Will it startle?  
- Will it mask another signal?  
- Will it disturb pools or people?  

### If you make a bad noise

If you drop something, scrape stone, or speak too loudly:

- pause  
- do not laugh nervously  
- do not apologize loudly  
- use a learned apology gesture if available  
- become more careful  
- do not repeat the noise  

### If you hear an intentional signal

Do not answer unless you know the meaning. Do not imitate. Wait for your guide.

### If silence suddenly occurs

In a place of constant water-noise, silence may mean something is wrong: a channel blocked, a drip diverted, a structural change, a social emergency. If the sound changes dramatically, stop and follow local guidance.

## 22. Light and vision etiquette

### Let darkness exist

Do not try to illuminate everything. Darkness is part of the architecture. It hides private spaces, preserves bioluminescence, and reduces visual chaos.

### Use light downward and briefly

When moving:

- aim light at your feet

- do not sweep light across faces  
- do not shine light into niches  
- do not shine light into pools  
- do not illuminate ancestor stones

Obey immediately. Dim it, shield it, or turn it off.

### Adapt your eyes

Give yourself at least twenty to thirty minutes to adapt. Avoid checking bright devices. If you must use light, use the dimmest possible amber source.

## 26. Special considerations for different travelers

### If you are highly talkative

Practice silence. Your words will feel too large here. Learn to communicate with eyes, hands, and presence.

### If you are naturally quiet

This place may suit you, but do not become invisible. Make sure your guide knows your condition, especially if you are overwhelmed.

### If you are a scientist

Resist the urge to sample. The place is not a laboratory. It is a home and an ecosystem. Sampling without permission is extraction. Ask. Accept no. Use non-invasive observation.

### If you are an artist

Do not leave pigments, carvings, or marks. The place is already saturated with use. Your art should be memory, attention, or later work made outside.

### If you are religious or spiritual

The place may feel sacred. Do not assume your rituals belong here. Ask before praying, blessing, chanting, or performing ceremony. Silent respect is often safest.

### If you are a photographer or recorder

You may be disappointed. Bright light and recording devices are likely inappropriate. If permitted, use low-light, silent methods. Prioritize consent. Some things should not be recorded.

### If you are a child or traveling with children

Children may adapt quickly or become frightened by noise, darkness, and crowding. They must be taught not to run, shout, touch, or throw things. If a child cannot understand these constraints, postpone the visit.

### If you are elderly or mobility-limited

The squeeze, wet stairs, and dense architecture may be dangerous. Seek local advice. Do not modify the environment for access without permission.

### If you are deaf or hard of hearing

The place may be visually and vibrationally rich, but much information is acoustic. Travel with a guide who can translate sound cues visually or tactilely. Learn visual signals. Be aware that water vibration may mask approaching movement.

### If you are blind or low-vision

The acoustic richness may be extraordinary, but the physical hazards are significant. A trusted guide is essential. Learn the tactile layout slowly. Do not move independently until invited.

## 27. Emergency protocols

### If you are lost

Do not wander.

1. Stop.  
2. Place a hand on a wall if safe.  
3. Listen.  
4. Do not call out loudly.  
5. Use agreed rhythmic signal if available.  
6. Wait for a response.  
7. Move only if you recognize a safe route.  

In a loud chamber, direction can be confusing. Water sounds may seem to come from everywhere. Do not chase echoes.

### If you fall

Assess before rising.

- check for bleeding  
- check for broken bones  
- check whether you struck your head  
- check whether you disturbed a pool, channel, shrine, or lamp  
- do not rise quickly if dizzy  
- signal your guide if needed  

If you bleed, contain it. Do not wash blood into water.

### If there is a water change

If the sound of water suddenly changes:

- stop  
- listen  
- look for rising water only if visible  
- follow locals  
- move toward higher ledges if instructed  
- do not block channels while fleeing  
- do not push through crowds  

The place is usually stable, but any underground water system can change.

### If a lamp goes out

Do not panic.

- stand still  
- shield your face  
- ask for guidance  
- do not use bright emergency light unless necessary  
- do not grope near pools or stairs  
- wait for local response  

Darkness is ordinary. Your fear of it is not the settlement’s emergency.

### If you contaminate something

If you spill food, oil, blood, medicine, waste, or foreign water:

- do not hide it  
- do not wash it away  
- tell your guide immediately  
- contain it if instructed  
- accept correction  

Honesty matters more than shame.

### If you must leave urgently

Move carefully. The exit throat is not a place to rush. A panicked exit can injure you or damage the entrance. If possible, alert your guide. If alone, use agreed departure signals and move toward the entrance with control.

## 33. What the visit will feel like

At first, it will feel like being swallowed by a wet stone throat. You will squeeze through damp rock, perhaps with your breath loud in your own ears. Then the space will open, and the sound will hit you: not a roar exactly, but a braided, living loudness. Drips, hisses, gurgles, echoes, voices, footsteps, water lips, basin notes — all layered into one sustained chord.

Your eyes will struggle. Your skin will become aware of every drip. You will feel cool, wet, and perhaps absurdly large. You will want to apologize for the sound of your own boots.

Then, if you are fortunate, you will slow down. You will notice that the noise has structure. You will see lamps glinting on copper, water sliding over carved stone, faces moving along terraces, hands touching polished walls, basins receiving their appointed drips. You will realize that the city is not primitive but refined. Its sophistication is not in machines but in attention.

You may feel watched, not because anyone is hostile, but because the place is saturated with presence: living people, ancestor stones, carved faces, worn thresholds, old repairs, mineral-coated bones. Nothing is empty. Everything has been used, listened to, adjusted, remembered.

If you stay long enough, you may begin to feel the chamber as a body: water as circulation, sound as breath, stone as bone, inhabitants as the careful mind maintaining the whole. You will understand why they do not fight the loudness. The sound proves the water moves. The water proves the place lives. The stone holds it all.

And when you leave, you may carry the drip with you in your mind. Ordinary rain may feel crude. Open sky may feel exposed. Silence may feel incomplete. You may find yourself listening for the tuned basins in every room.

That is the mark of having visited properly: you do not conquer the place. It adjusts you.

## 34. Final preparation advice

Prepare your gear, but prepare your character more.

The ideal visitor to this place is not the bravest, strongest, or most technologically equipped. The ideal visitor is clean, quiet, patient, observant, and humble. Someone who can squeeze through a wet throat without cursing, enter a loud chamber without shouting, see darkness without flooding it with light, encounter rare life without grabbing it, and move through a crowded stone city without making themselves the center of it.

Before you go, ask yourself:

- Can I be wet for hours without complaining?  
- Can I move slowly when I am tired?  
- Can I listen more than I speak?  
- Can I resist touching everything?  
- Can I accept that my comfort is secondary?  
- Can I leave no trace, no smell, no sound, and no contamination?  
- Can I treat a strange, plantless, mineral city as a real home rather than an adventure?  

If the answer is yes, prepare simply, travel lightly, and enter with reverence.

You are not going to see a curiosity. You are asking to be admitted into a sustained chord of water, stone, and sound. Your preparation should make you worthy of that chord.
