## Preparing to Visit the High Weeping Basin

The first thing to understand is that this place does not reward the usual traveler’s instincts. It is not a ruin to be explored, a summit to be conquered, or a picturesque settlement to be admired from a comfortable distance. It is a living, wet, cold, bright, tightly balanced world. Its inhabitants have survived by becoming attentive, quiet, flexible, and extremely careful. An ordinary human multiverse visitor should prepare to become smaller, slower, drier, and less conspicuous.

The core rule is this:

**Do not try to dominate the basin. Learn to move inside its rules.**

That means preparing for altitude, severe cold, constant moisture, blinding light, slippery vertical terrain, strict quiet, delicate ecology, and a social order organized around water. You should expect to be uncomfortable much of the time. You should expect to be watched. You should expect that almost every action has a consequence, because the bowl is small, the surfaces are wet, the channels are connected, and the inhabitants are paying attention.

Below is a detailed preparation guide for an ordinary human traveler who wants to visit responsibly and survive the visit without damaging the place.

---

# 1. Decide Whether You Are Ready

This is not a good destination for a first-time multiverse traveler, a casual tourist, or someone who dislikes being cold, wet, observed, or quiet.

You should be in good physical condition, have some experience with mountain environments, and be emotionally comfortable with restraint. The basin is not dangerous in the same way as a war zone or predator den, but it is unforgiving of carelessness.

## Basic fitness requirements

You should be able to:

- Hike uphill at altitude with a light pack.
- Balance on wet stone.
- Climb short ladders and traverse narrow shelves.
- Crouch, step carefully, and move slowly for long periods.
- Tolerate cold, damp clothing and limited sleep comfort.
- Recover quickly from brief exertion without gasping or panicking.

You do not need to be an elite athlete. In fact, brute speed is less useful than patience. But you must be steady. A clumsy visitor is a hazard to themselves, to the inhabitants, and to the settlement’s fragile water systems.

## Medical considerations

Before going, get a basic medical evaluation, especially if you have any of the following:

- Heart or lung problems.
- Severe asthma.
- Poor circulation.
- Raynaud’s syndrome.
- Balance disorders.
- Vertigo.
- Recent surgery.
- Pregnancy with complications.
- History of severe altitude sickness.
- Cold urticaria or cold-triggered conditions.
- Conditions that impair judgment, coordination, or temperature regulation.

High altitude is not trivial. The air is thin, the light is hard, and dehydration can creep up on you. If you are prone to altitude sickness, do not simply push through. Plan gradual acclimatization and carry emergency medication only under professional guidance.

## Psychological preparation

You must be able to:

- Remain quiet for hours.
- Resist the urge to fill silence with speech.
- Accept constant dampness.
- Move without urgency.
- Watch before acting.
- Follow unfamiliar rules without demanding explanation.
- Tolerate being seen at all times.
- Avoid romanticizing the inhabitants.
- Avoid treating the settlement as a backdrop for your own experience.

The emotional tone of the basin is alert stillness. You should prepare your mind for beauty that is severe rather than soothing. It may feel austere, exposed, and intensely present. If you need comfort, novelty, or constant reassurance, this place will wear you down.

# 2. Understand the Environment Before You Enter

The basin is defined by several overlapping conditions. Each one demands preparation.

## Altitude

The settlement lies far above timberline. The air is thin. Light is stronger. Breath comes shorter. Physical effort becomes costly. Dehydration and altitude sickness are real risks.

## Cold

This is not necessarily storm-cold, but it is persistent, bright, penetrating cold. It settles into joints, metal, rope, and clothing. At night, everything freezes into delicate lace. By day, thaw begins again.

## Wetness

Water is not a feature here; it is a condition of existence. Surfaces are filmed with moisture. Channels shift. Pools form. Ice melts, seeps, climbs, beads, drips, and slides. Your gear will get wet. Your body will get wet. Your ability to stay functional depends on managing moisture.

## Brightness

The basin is almost painfully bright. Wet stone, ice, quartz, pools, and glacier light create glare that can injure the eyes. You must protect your vision. You must also avoid introducing reflective surfaces that could scatter light dangerously or disturb the settlement.

## Silence

Quiet is not just etiquette. It is technique. Loud noise or vibration can disturb wildlife, loosen crusts, signal danger poorly, or betray ignorance of local custom. You should prepare to make your body and equipment nearly silent.

## Instability

The settlement is stable because it changes. Platforms move. Channels shift. Ice releases meltwater. Walls may flex. Structures are repaired constantly. Do not expect fixed ground or permanent arrangements. Expect a world that edits itself.

## Ecological density

The basin is full of animals despite sparse vegetation. Birds, fish, insects, amphibians, small mammals, and larger creatures all have roles. They are not decorative. They are indicators, competitors, food sources, neighbors, and sometimes dangers. You must not disturb them.

## Social density

The bowl is small and visible. Every visitor is obvious. Every mistake can affect someone else’s water, heat, shelter, or food. You must behave like a guest in a household where all the walls are wet, all the floors are stone, and everyone can see you.

# 3. Choose the Right Kind of Visit

An ordinary human should not arrive unannounced and unprepared. If at all possible, arrange the visit through a local guide, intermediary, or multiverse cultural steward who knows the basin.

## Best-case preparation

- Visit with a local guide.
- Arrive after acclimatizing nearby.
- Stay only a short time at first.
- Bring no unnecessary equipment.
- Observe more than you act.
- Ask permission before entering, sitting, touching, or helping.
- Leave nothing behind.

## If you cannot arrange a guide

If you must approach without one, do not enter the settlement immediately. Stop at the rim or on a lower stable ledge and observe. Watch where people walk. Watch how they handle water. Watch what they avoid. Notice which signs are marked with red cord, white stones, feathers, or dark dust. Do not move anything.

If you are not invited in, do not force the encounter. The basin is not yours to enter.

# 4. Acclimatization Plan

Do not step directly from a low-altitude world or comfortable transit station into the basin and expect your body to perform.

## Recommended acclimatization

If possible:

1. Spend at least one night at moderate altitude.
2. Spend another day or two above timberline but below the basin.
3. Make a short, unhurried approach to the bowl.
4. Rest before entering.
5. Enter only when breathing is controlled and you are not sweating heavily.

If multiverse transit deposits you near the basin abruptly, treat the first several hours as a fragile period. Move slowly. Hydrate. Avoid heavy exertion. Monitor yourself for headache, nausea, dizziness, confusion, shortness of breath, or coughing.

## Altitude warning signs

Descend or activate emergency extraction if you experience:

- Worsening headache.
- Nausea or vomiting.
- Severe fatigue.
- Loss of coordination.
- Confusion.
- Shortness of breath at rest.
- Persistent cough.
- Blurred vision.
- Chest tightness.
- Extreme sleepiness or inability to think clearly.

Do not hide symptoms. In a wet, cold, high place, poor judgment can become fatal quickly.

## Oxygen

Carry supplemental oxygen only for emergencies unless you have been trained to use it properly. It can save your life, but it should not become a substitute for acclimatization. If you depend on oxygen just to function at the basin, you are not ready to visit.

# 5. Timing the Visit

The time of day and season matter.

## Best conditions

Choose, if possible:

- Stable weather.
- Low wind.
- Clear but not excessively warm conditions.
- No recent rapid thaw.
- No storm approaching.
- No heavy icefall activity.
- A day when local movement seems calm.

Avoid:

- High winds.
- Whiteout conditions.
- Rapid warming after a cold spell.
- Rain at altitude.
- Visible ice cracking.
- Unusual animal agitation.
- Flooded channels or overtopping sluices.

## Time of day

Morning may be frozen and slick but relatively stable. Midday brings intense glare and melt pulses. Afternoon may bring shifting water and active repair. Evening brings refreezing.

For a first visit, late morning may be better than dawn, because some ice has loosened and movement is visible, but the full midday glare has not yet become overwhelming. Still, this depends on local conditions.

If you arrive at midday and the basin seems to hold its breath, do not mistake that stillness for safety. It is a pause. Something may give soon.

# 6. Clothing: The Most Important Personal Preparation

Your clothing must manage cold, wetness, wind, glare, and silence. It must also be matte, subdued, and free of flapping, clinking, or reflective surfaces.

The basin is bright enough to punish shiny gear. It is wet enough to punish cotton. It is quiet enough to punish loose metal. It is narrow enough to punish bulky clothing that snags.

## General clothing principles

- Wear layers.
- Avoid cotton completely.
- Avoid shiny fabrics.
- Avoid bright red unless you know its local meaning.
- Avoid loose cloaks that can catch on beams or brush channels.
- Avoid noisy shells or crinkly emergency blankets.
- Keep everything fitted enough to move through tight vertical spaces.
- Assume everything will get wet eventually.

## Base layer

Use a wicking synthetic or merino wool base layer. It should move sweat away from your skin. You will be exerting yourself in cold air; if sweat stays against you, you will chill.

Bring at least one spare base layer in a dry bag.

## Insulating layer

Use fleece, wool, or a synthetic insulated layer. Down is excellent when dry but dangerous if soaked. If you use down, keep it protected in a waterproof bag and consider a synthetic alternative for damp conditions.

The insulation should be warm without being bulky. You need freedom of movement for climbing, crouching, and balancing.

## Outer shell

You need a waterproof, windproof, breathable shell. It should have a hood that can be tightened without blocking peripheral vision.

The shell should be:

- Matte.
- Quiet fabric.
- Not overly long.
- Free of dangling drawstrings.
- Equipped with secure but silent closures.

If your shell has loud zippers or hard plastic tabs, muffle them with fabric loops or soft covers.

## Trousers

Wear quick-drying, insulated, water-resistant trousers. Full waterproof trousers may be necessary if you expect to be near heavy drip lines or melt channels.

Avoid wide legs. They can catch on stone, brush, rope, or ladder rungs.

## Headwear

A warm, close-fitting hat is essential. A hood is also useful. Avoid wide-brimmed hats unless they are secure and matte; they can catch wind or bump in narrow spaces.

A face veil or neck gaiter can protect skin from glare, wind, and cold. Choose a matte color.

## Eye protection

This is critical.

Bring smoked goggles or glacier glasses with side protection. They should be dark enough for intense glare but not so dark that you cannot see in shadowed corners. Ideally, bring two pairs:

1. Dark goggles for bright glare.
2. Clear or lightly tinted goggles for shadow, overcast conditions, or night movement.

They should:

- Fit securely.
- Resist fogging.
- Have matte frames.
- Have no shiny metal accents.
- Be strapped so they cannot fall into water.

If your eyes water from brightness, do not remove the goggles. Let your eyes adjust. Snow blindness or photokeratitis can ruin your visit and endanger others.

## Face and skin protection

Use mineral sunscreen on exposed skin, applied away from water sources and allowed to set. Use unscented lip balm with SPF. The bright, cold air can burn and crack skin quickly.

Avoid greasy products that can smear onto goggles, ropes, or stone.

## Gloves

Your hands are vulnerable. Bring multiple glove systems:

- Thin liner gloves for dexterity.
- Insulated gloves for general use.
- Waterproof overmitts for wet work.
- Spare dry gloves sealed in a waterproof bag.

Avoid gloves with loud fasteners. Avoid anything with shiny buckles.

If your hands get wet, dry them inside your jacket or in your sleeping system. Wet hands lose dexterity fast, and cold hands make you dangerous on wet stone.

## Footwear

This is one of the most important choices.

You need boots or approach shoes that are:

- Waterproof.
- Insulated enough for cold stone.
- Supportive but not clumsy.
- Fitted with sticky rubber soles.
- Quiet.
- Matte.
- Free of metal cleats if possible.

Avoid heavy mountaineering boots if they are too rigid, and avoid metal crampons unless absolutely necessary. Metal striking stone violates the quiet of the place and can damage worn surfaces.

If ice is unavoidable, use the least aggressive traction possible and seek local advice. Soft traction may be preferable to sharp metal. The inhabitants understand ice; copy their movement rather than forcing your own solution.

## Socks

Bring several pairs of wool or synthetic socks. No cotton. Change them if damp. Wet feet on stone become cold feet, then numb feet, then injuries.

Consider thin liner socks under thicker socks if your boots allow it without cutting circulation.

## Gaiters

Waterproof gaiters can help keep meltwater, spray, and crushed ice out of your boots. They should be snug and quiet.

## Knee pads

Not essential, but useful if you must kneel on wet stone. Choose matte, soft-covered pads. Do not kneel on lichen, moss, or fragile crusts.

## Helmet

A lightweight climbing helmet may be wise, especially near the cliff face or glacier rim. Falling ice, shifting stone, or low overhangs can injure you. The helmet should be matte and securely fitted. Do not attach shiny accessories.

# 7. Personal Gear: Quiet, Waterproof, Matte, Minimal

Your equipment should be chosen with the same ethics as your clothing: quiet, waterproof, matte, minimal, and non-polluting.

## Pack

Use a medium-sized pack, perhaps 30 to 50 liters. It should be:

- Waterproof or lined with dry bags.
- Narrow enough for tight paths.
- Matte in color.
- Free of external dangling gear.
- Free of loud buckles or metal clatter.

Do not carry a huge expedition pack into the bowl. The settlement is dense and vertical. A large pack makes you clumsy and intrusive.

Pack everything in dry bags. Assume one bag will leak, so use two layers for critical items.

## Silent closures

Avoid Velcro if possible; it is loud. If you must use buckles, wrap them in fabric or choose soft toggles. Test your pack by moving in a quiet room. If you can hear it, the inhabitants can hear it.

## Dry bags

Bring several sizes. Use them for:

- Spare clothing.
- Food.
- Medical kit.
- Electronics.
- Waste bags.
- Note-taking materials.
- Water purification backups.

## Waterproof notebook and pencil

If you want to record observations, use a small waterproof notebook and pencil. Avoid ink that runs. Avoid erasers that leave crumbs. Do not leave notes behind.

A small slate or reusable tablet could work if it is silent and matte, but do not let it become a distraction.

## Rope and cordage

Carry 10 to 30 meters of quiet, matte, high-strength cordage. However, do not rig ropes or anchors without permission. Existing ropes may be part of the settlement’s water management, drying systems, or safety lines. Do not clip into them casually.

If you must use your own rope, keep it coiled in a rope bag to prevent noise and snagging.

## Repair kit

The environment will test seams, straps, gloves, and boots. Bring a small repair kit:

- Needle and strong thread.
- Synthetic sinew or cord.
- Seam sealer.
- Patch material.
- Matte tape.
- Wax for zippers or fabric.
- Spare toggles.
- Soft wire or binding cord.

Avoid large tools. No hammers. No pitons. No drills. No bolts.

## Multi-tool

If you bring a tool, bring a small, matte, sheathed knife or multi-tool. Use it only for repair, food preparation, or emergencies. Do not chop, pry, carve stone, or cut local materials without permission.

## Lighting

Avoid bright artificial light unless absolutely necessary. The settlement uses solar cues and darkness. If you must carry light:

- Use a dim, downward-shielded source.
- Avoid blue-white glare.
- Use red or amber if appropriate, but test it beforehand.
- Keep it silent.
- Do not shine it into faces, pools, animal nests, or dwellings.

A small headlamp can be useful, but only if it can be kept extremely low and controlled.

## Watch

Use a simple, silent watch. Do not rely on beeping alarms. The basin measures time by light and water; you should learn to do the same.

## Navigation

The bowl is small, but it is vertically complex. A map may help, but do not mark it with bright tape or leave physical markers in the environment. Use memory, sketches, and solar cues.

If you use a compass or altimeter, keep them quiet and matte. Do not attach them with metal chains.

# 8. Water Strategy

Water is everywhere, but you cannot assume it is safe for an ordinary human stomach. It may contain rock flour, animal waste, microorganisms, mineral dust, or cross-world contaminants.

## Do not drink casually

Do not drink from pools, channels, cisterns, or weeping cliff strands unless explicitly invited and unless you understand the local water system.

Even clear water can be dangerous. Milky water may contain fine rock flour that upsets digestion. Warm seeps may contain amphibians, insects, or geothermal minerals. Channels may be marked as tainted.

## Water purification

Bring multiple methods:

- A mechanical filter suitable for cold water.
- Chemical purification as backup.
- UV purification only if batteries can survive the cold.
- Pre-purified water for the first day.

Be aware: filters can freeze and crack. Keep the filter close to your body when not in use. Chemical purification is slow in cold water. UV devices may fail if batteries chill.

## Containers

Carry at least two liters of water capacity, preferably three. Use insulated bottles or keep bottles inside your jacket to prevent freezing. Wide-mouth bottles are less likely to freeze shut, but they must be sealed tightly.

Avoid bladders with long tubes unless the tube is insulated; otherwise it will freeze.

## Hydration discipline

High altitude suppresses thirst, but you still need water. Dehydration worsens altitude sickness, cold injury, and poor judgment.

Drink small amounts often. Add electrolytes if you are sweating, but choose low-scent, low-sugar options to avoid attracting animals.

## Respect local water signs

From the description, certain signs matter:

- **Red cord across a sluice:** stop. Do not pass or adjust.
- **White pebble in the flow:** the line is being watched. Do not interfere.
- **Broken feather:** the channel may be tainted. Do not drink from it. If possible, alert a local calmly.
- **Red cloth hung on a line:** likely a signal. Do not touch.
- **Circle of white stones:** likely a marker. Do not disturb.

If you do not understand a sign, assume it is important and leave it alone.

## Do not contaminate water

Never:

- Wash hands directly in a cistern.
- Rinse food in a channel.
- Spit into water.
- Urinate near water.
- Defecate anywhere in the basin.
- Dump greywater into channels.
- Use soap in local water.
- Throw waste into pools.
- Drop trash, wrappers, or fibers into melt lines.

Your waste must be contained and packed out.

# 9. Food Strategy

The basin does not feed visitors by greenness. Do not forage. Do not hunt. Do not fish unless explicitly invited and using local methods.

## Bring food that is:

- High-calorie.
- Low-crumb.
- Low-odor.
- Easy to eat cold.
- Sealed against animals.
- Easy to eat quietly.
- Not dependent on cooking.

Good options include:

- Nut butters in squeeze packets.
- Dense energy bars that do not crumble.
- Soft dried fruit without sticky flakes.
- Cheese or preserved protein if stable.
- Chocolate or high-fat rations kept from freezing.
- Warm food in an insulated container if you can prepare it without fire.

Avoid:

- Strong-smelling food.
- Spicy food.
- Crumbly pastries.
- Loud wrappers.
- Foods that require boiling.
- Foods that leave residue.

## No open flame

Fire is scarce, dangerous, and poorly suited to the basin. Fuel is limited. Smoke is hazardous in enclosed stone. The settlement relies more on insulation, body heat, shelter geometry, fermentation, and geothermal nooks than on open fire.

Do not build a fire.

If you absolutely must heat something for survival, use an enclosed, stable stove only with local permission, away from structures and channels, and pack out all fuel waste. But for an ordinary visit, better to rely on no-cook food and insulated containers.

## Keep food sealed

Small mammals and birds will exploit any mistake. Keep food in odor-proof bags. Do not leave food unattended. Do not feed animals. Feeding them can alter behavior, attract predators, or introduce disease.

## Waste food

Pack out all food waste, crumbs, peels, wrappers, and used utensils. Even “natural” scraps can disrupt the ecology.

# 10. Shelter and Sleep Preparation

If possible, arrange to sleep in a local guest shelter or a lower-altitude camp outside the basin. Sleeping inside the settlement without invitation would be intrusive.

If you must bivouac in the basin:

- Choose a dry upper shelf.
- Avoid melt-lines.
- Avoid channels.
- Avoid the spillway.
- Avoid cliff fall zones.
- Avoid nesting ledges.
- Avoid geothermal vents unless invited.
- Avoid lichen and moss.
- Do not block paths or water routes.

## Shelter type

Avoid large tents. They require stakes, create glare, catch wind, and may block water. If you need shelter, use:

- A small bivy sack.
- A low tarp rigged without stakes if possible.
- A freestanding inner shelter that does not damage stone.
- A matte, quiet fabric.

Do not hammer stakes into stone. Do not move large rocks to create anchors. Do not dig into gravel or crusts.

## Sleeping system

You need:

- A cold-rated sleeping bag.
- A high-insulation sleeping pad.
- A waterproof or water-resistant bag shell.
- A dry bag for spare sleep clothes.
- A small insulated sit pad for resting during the day.

Nighttime freezing means condensation can stiffen into lace. Keep your sleeping bag dry. Breathe outside the bag if possible to reduce moisture buildup.

## Keep essentials warm

At night, keep inside your sleeping bag:

- Water bottle.
- Filter.
- Batteries.
- Spare socks.
- Gloves.
- Next day’s boot liners if removable.

Do not sleep in wet outer layers if you can avoid it. Change into dry sleep clothing.

# 11. Hygiene and Sanitation

This is a place where water is sacred, channels are shared, and ecology is delicate. Ordinary human hygiene must be extremely disciplined.

## Toilet needs

Use a portable waste system.

Carry:

- Waste bags with absorbent gel or sealable liners.
- Unscented toilet paper.
- Hand sanitizer.
- Small trowel only if required elsewhere, but not for digging in the basin.
- Sealable odor-proof bag for all waste.

Do not defecate in the basin. Do not urinate into channels, pools, or seeps. If you cannot pack out urine, ask local guidance; otherwise use a sealable bottle and carry it out.

## Menstruation

Use a menstrual cup or pack-out system. Do not leave waste. Do not wash products in local water.

## Hand hygiene

Use hand sanitizer rather than washing in channels. If you must wash, use a personal basin and contain the greywater. Pack it out or dispose only with local permission far from settlement water systems.

## No soap in natural water

Avoid soaps, shampoos, toothpaste, or detergents entering the basin. Use swallowable or wipe-based oral hygiene if possible. If you use toothpaste, use a tiny amount and pack out residue.

## Body odor and scents

Avoid scented deodorants, perfumes, lotions, or food-smelling clothing. Scents can attract animals or disturb inhabitants. Cleanliness matters, but fragrance does not.

## Skin care

Cold, wet, bright air damages skin. Use:

- Unscented barrier cream.
- Lip balm.
- Blister prevention.
- Foot powder.
- Moisturizer that does not make your hands slippery.

Check fingers, toes, nose, cheeks, and ears for cold injury.

# 12. Movement Through the Basin

Movement here is a skill. The terrain is vertical, intimate, wet, and worn smooth. You must walk as if every step could either preserve your balance or disturb someone else’s water.

## Basic movement rules

- Move slowly.
- Keep your center of gravity low.
- Take short steps.
- Place feet flat on polished stone.
- Avoid heel-striking.
- Keep hands free.
- Do not run.
- Do not slide.
- Do not jump between shelves unless absolutely necessary and trained.
- Watch where water is moving.
- Watch where others are carrying loads.
- Yield to inhabitants.

## On wet stone

Wet stone is deceptive. A dry-looking path may be polished by generations of feet and dangerously slick.

Use:

- Sticky rubber soles.
- Small steps.
- Three points of contact where possible.
- Light touch on walls.
- Gloves to protect hands if you need to steady yourself.

Do not kick stone, scrape boots, or drag equipment.

## On narrow shelves

The shelves are only a few body-lengths wide in places. Keep your pack narrow. Do not swing your shoulders. Do not turn quickly. If you meet someone carrying a load, stop and press against the rock if safe, letting them pass.

## On rope bridges

Rope bridges may sag and shift. Do not bounce. Cross one person at a time if possible. Keep your eyes on the bridge or the far anchor, not on glaring water below. Do not attach your own gear to bridge ropes unless necessary for survival and done without damaging them.

## On ladders

Test ladders before putting full weight on them. Keep three points of contact. Do not carry wide loads. Wait if a local is already on the ladder.

## On chutes

Do not use chutes unless explicitly instructed. They are likely part of water movement, salvage routes, or local transport systems. A visitor sliding down a chute could injure themselves, damage a basin, or trigger a flood pulse.

## Around channels

Never block a channel with your foot, pack, or body. Step over channels, not through them. If a channel is covered by a slab, use the slab. If no crossing exists, wait or ask. Do not create a new crossing by moving stones.

## Around melt-lines

Do not build, rest, or place gear in a melt-line. Anything where water has flowed repeatedly may flood without warning. If you set your pack down, ensure it cannot be taken by water and cannot divert water toward a structure.

## Around structures

Many structures are flexible, hinged, or set on runners. Do not lean on walls unless you know they are fixed. Do not block moving panels. Do not wedge your gear under roofs or platforms. Do not climb storage lofts or fish racks.

# 13. Noise Discipline

The basin is quiet, and your ordinary human body is too loud.

## Sources of noise to eliminate

- Clanking carabiners.
- Buckles.
- Zippers.
- Metal tools.
- Trekking poles striking stone.
- Loud footwear.
- Crinkly wrappers.
- Loud breathing.
- Coughing.
- Sneezing.
- Talking.
- Laughing.
- Alarms.
- Electronic beeps.
- Drones.
- Radios.
- Whistles.

## Practical quieting methods

- Wrap metal items in cloth.
- Use soft toggles.
- Keep tools sheathed.
- Use matte, rubber-coated gear.
- Keep food in silent packaging.
- Practice opening wrappers slowly.
- Turn off all sounds.
- Use hand signals.
- Speak only in low voices if necessary.
- Cough into a thick scarf.
- Sneeze into your sleeve and away from water.

## If you must make an emergency signal

Use visual signals first. If sound is unavoidable, use low taps on wood rather than striking stone. Use a whistle only for life-threatening emergencies, and even then expect it to disturb the basin deeply.

# 14. Light Discipline

Brightness is both useful and dangerous.

## Avoid reflective surfaces

Do not bring:

- Mirrors.
- Shiny metal utensils.
- Glass bottles.
- Polished jewelry.
- Reflective emergency blankets.
- Glossy camera bodies.
- Bright lenses exposed openly.
- Shiny helmets.

Cover necessary metal with matte tape or cloth.

## Use matte gear

Choose dark, stone-colored, grey, blue, or muted tones. Avoid bright colors unless they are required for emergency signaling and kept concealed until needed.

## Manage your own glare

Do not flash light into pools, dwellings, or faces. Do not use camera flash. If you need to signal in an emergency, use a matte panel or a controlled mirror only when necessary.

## Respect solar markers

The inhabitants may use quartz veins, dark dust, and shadow lines to track time and light. Do not move quartz markers. Do not wipe dark dust from surfaces. Do not cast unnecessary shadows across work areas by standing idly in a light path.

# 15. Social Conduct and Etiquette

The settlement is dense, visible, and organized. Your behavior matters more than your intentions.

## Ask permission

Do not enter dwellings, touch tools, adjust sluices, move crates, or join work without permission. If language is a barrier, use slow gestures, open palms, and imitation. If someone turns away or gives a stop signal, withdraw.

## Yield to workers

People carrying fish, hides, water, rope, or stone have priority. They know the basin’s rhythm. Step aside safely. Do not make them alter their route for you.

## Do not touch water controls

Channels, paddles, sluices, troughs, and cisterns are shared infrastructure. Moving one paddle may flood a house, chill an egg nursery, or contaminate a tank. Do not touch them unless asked.

## Do not move markers

Red cords, white pebbles, broken feathers, white stone circles, red cloths, and quartz markers are part of the settlement’s communication. Leave them alone.

## Do not give unsolicited help

The place looks busy, but it is not chaotic. The inhabitants know what they are doing. If you want to help, wait for invitation. If you are invited, follow instructions exactly.

If you are not invited, observe quietly.

## Do not touch children

Children may move with astonishing balance and quiet. Do not approach, photograph, touch, or feed them. Smile if appropriate, but do not stare.

## Do not feed animals

No exceptions. Feeding animals changes their behavior and can harm the ecosystem.

## Do not take souvenirs

Do not take:

- Stones.
- Bones.
- Feathers.
- Ice.
- Lichen.
- Moss.
- Seeds.
- Fish scales.
- Shells.
- Wood.
- Artifacts.
- Tools.
- Water samples.

The place is not a collection. Its materials are part of its system.

## Do not criticize or pity

The settlement may look harsh to outside eyes. Do not express loud pity, horror, or condescension. The inhabitants are not victims of their environment; they are experts in it.

## Do not try to improve them

Do not introduce better tools, machines, fuels, wheels, metals, or “more efficient” methods unless explicitly requested through proper cultural channels. Your technology may be dangerous here.

The sophistication of the place is not obvious to an outsider. A simple bone lever may encode generations of water knowledge.

# 17. Ecological Conduct

The basin’s life is strange, abundant, and tightly connected.

## Birds

Birds nest in cliff seams and under eaves. They may warn of pressure changes. Do not flush them. Do not approach nests. If a flock lifts suddenly, observe what disturbed it.

Do not collect eggs unless invited by local custom, and even then be extremely careful.

## Fish and fish-like creatures

They may respond to mineral shifts in water. Do not disturb pools. Do not cast shadows over fishing areas. Do not introduce bait, hooks, lines, nets, or chemicals.

## Insects

Insects hatch in brief pulses. Do not swat clouds of midges dramatically. Move calmly. Avoid disturbing shallow films where larvae or caddis cases develop.

## Amphibians

Amphibians are especially sensitive. Their skin can be harmed by oils, salts, and chemicals. Do not touch them. Do not approach warm seeps where they gather. If amphibians gather unusually, it may indicate thaw or geothermal change.

## Small white mammals

They may be curious and unafraid. That does not mean they are pets. Keep food sealed. Check your pack before entering dwellings or storage areas. If one enters your gear, do not chase it violently. Encourage it out quietly.

## Larger animals

If a mountain goat-like creature, seal-like pool creature, or another large animal appears at the rim, stop. Do not run. Do not block its path. Watch the inhabitants. If they become still, become still. If they prepare to move, move as directed.

## Things in ice

If you see dark specks suspended in blue ice, or something moving with glacial slowness, do not try to free it. Do not chip ice. Do not melt samples. Some things are better left sealed.

## Opalescent flexible formations

These neither ice nor rock materials may be useful and delicate. Do not flex, break, or harvest them.

# 18. Reading the Basin’s Movement

The settlement is turbulent but patterned. A visitor should learn to read it.

## Watch water

Water tells you what is about to happen.

Signs of change:

- A new bead forming on a lip.
- A thread of water where none existed.
- Darkening stone.
- A channel beginning to whisper more strongly.
- Pools becoming milky.
- Drip-lines quickening.
- Ice ticking.
- A deep pressure sound.
- Birds lifting.
- People pausing.

If water changes suddenly, assume something upstream has released. Move away from lower channels, chutes, and spillways. Do not run. Move deliberately to higher, stable ground.

## Watch animals

Animals are indicators.

- Birds lifting may mean air pressure, ice movement, or a predator.
- Amphibians at seeps may mean thaw.
- Fish rising or sinking may mean mineral change.
- Small mammals hoarding may mean stored food is nearby.
- Larger animals at the rim may mean scarcity, curiosity, or territorial movement.

Do not interpret too quickly. Observe the inhabitants’ response.

## Watch light

The day is measured by light.

Notice:

- When the sun crosses the rim.
- When shadows reach certain shelves.
- When quartz veins ignite.
- When fish racks enter or leave light.
- When glare becomes dangerous.
- When surfaces begin to drip.

Your visit should follow these cues, not your impatience.

# 19. Midday Stillness

At midday, when the basin becomes a cup of glare, it may hold its breath. This is not emptiness. It is tension.

Do not use this time to rush around. Do not initiate unnecessary movement. Sit or stand safely. Observe. Keep your face veiled if necessary. Watch how the inhabitants pause.

Then something may give: ice releases, water overtops, a stone shifts, birds lift. The stillness will break into motion. Do not panic. Do not try to solve everything. Move with the rhythm you see.

If you are unsure, stop and yield.

# 20. Emergency Preparation

An ordinary human visitor must assume that rescue will be difficult, quiet, and local. Do not expect loud extraction vehicles, helicopters, or flashing machines. They would violate the basin and may be impossible in the terrain.

## Emergency priorities

1. Protect the basin from contamination.
2. Protect life.
3. Prevent further instability.
4. Use local knowledge.
5. Leave no trace of the emergency if possible.

## Hypothermia

Signs:

- Shivering.
- Clumsiness.
- Slurred speech.
- Confusion.
- Apathy.
- Pale or cold skin.
- Poor judgment.

Response:

- Get the person dry.
- Insulate them from stone.
- Shield them from wind and water.
- Give warm, sweet fluids only if fully conscious.
- Use body heat or insulated bags.
- Avoid rubbing frostbitten skin.
- Evacuate if worsening.

## Frostbite and frostnip

Signs:

- Numbness.
- White or waxy skin.
- Hardened tissue.
- Tingling followed by loss of sensation.

Response:

- Move to shelter.
- Warm slowly using body heat or insulated contact.
- Do not rub.
- Do not use open flame.
- Prevent refreezing.
- Seek evacuation if tissue is hard or numb.

## Altitude sickness

Mild symptoms:

- Headache.
- Nausea.
- Fatigue.
- Dizziness.
- Poor sleep.

Response:

- Rest.
- Hydrate.
- Descend if symptoms persist.
- Do not ascend further.

Severe symptoms require immediate descent and emergency care.

## Snow blindness

Signs:

- Eye pain.
- Gritty feeling.
- Tearing.
- Sensitivity to light.
- Blurred vision.

Response:

- Get into darkness or shade.
- Cover eyes.
- Avoid further light exposure.
- Descend if severe.

Prevention is far better: goggles at all times in bright conditions.

## Fall injury

If someone falls:

- Do not move them unnecessarily.
- Check breathing and responsiveness.
- Protect them from water and cold.
- Stabilize suspected fractures.
- Avoid loud rescue attempts.
- Use visual signals or low taps to summon help.

## Cold-water immersion

If you fall into a pool or channel:

- Do not gasp loudly if you can help it, but prioritize breathing.
- Get out as quickly as possible.
- Move diagonally out of current, not directly against it if strong.
- Protect your head.
- Once out, remove wet layers if dry replacements are available.
- Insulate immediately.
- Do not assume you are fine after a few minutes.

## Melt pulse or sudden flood

Signs:

- Deep pressure sound.
- Ice crack.
- Birds lifting.
- Water rising suddenly.
- Channels overtopping.
- People moving quickly but quietly.

Response:

- Move away from channels and chutes.
- Get to higher stable shelf.
- Do not cross moving water.
- Do not attempt to retrieve gear unless it blocks water and can be moved safely.
- Follow local movement.

## Icefall or cliff shift

Signs:

- Cracking sound.
- Dust or spray.
- Birds fleeing.
- Animals freezing.
- Sudden silence.

Response:

- Move away from cliff face.
- Avoid spillway and lower cleft.
- Do not run blindly.
- Seek stable overhang only if safe from falling material.
- Follow local cues.

## Animal encounter

If a large animal appears:

- Stop.
- Do not shout.
- Do not run.
- Do not throw objects.
- Avoid direct aggressive staring.
- Back away slowly if safe.
- Let locals handle the situation.

## Emergency signaling

Prefer:

- Matte colored panels.
- Slow arm signals.
- Prearranged hand signs.
- Taps on wood.
- Visual mirrors only as last resort.

Avoid:

- Whistles except life-or-death.
- Shouting except life-or-death.
- Bright flashing electronics.
- Fire signals.

# 26. How to Behave During the Day

## Night

Everything may freeze into lace. The cold deepens. Move only if needed. Keep gear from freezing. Keep water from freezing. Do not use bright light. Listen for deep ice sounds.

# 30. Ethical Multiverse Travel Principles

The highest standard is not merely to leave no visible trace. It is to leave no ecological, social, or hydrological trace.

Your visit should be as close to invisible as possible.

# 31. Specific Hazards Unique to This Basin

## Water climbing upward

## Flexible ice-rock formations

# 34. A Practical Summary of the Five Most Important Rules

If you forget everything else, remember these:

1. **Protect your eyes and stay quiet.**

2. **Keep everything dry and contained.**  
   Wetness will defeat your warmth, your tools, and your judgment.

3. **Do not touch water systems.**  
   Channels, sluices, cisterns, markers, and melt-lines are the settlement’s life.

4. **Move slowly and yield.**  
   The inhabitants know the basin. Your haste is dangerous.

5. **Leave nothing and take nothing.**  
   The basin must remain exactly as it is, except for the brief, careful pressure of your attention.

# 35. What Visiting Would Feel Like

You would feel exposed first. The light would be too sharp, the air too thin, the stone too wet, the silence too large. Your boots would feel clumsy. Your breath would seem loud. Your pack would seem full of unnecessary things.

Then, if you were lucky and careful, you would begin to notice the order beneath the turbulence. You would see how water moves like thought through the bowl. You would see hands adjust a paddle, a platform slide a finger’s width, a net lift silver from a pool, a child cross a beam without hesitation. You would hear almost nothing, yet everything would be communicating.

You would understand that the city is not built against the mountain. It is built into its habits.

The cold would press into you. The brightness would make your eyes water. The damp would find every seam in your clothing. You would want to speak, to point, to ask, to capture, to improve, to explain. The place would ask you not to.

The deepest preparation, then, is not just gear. It is restraint. You must prepare to be a guest in a world where water is law, attention is currency, and silence is a form of respect.

If you visit well, you will not conquer the basin. You will not possess it. You will not even fully understand it.

But you may be allowed to stand for a little while at the edge of a small, crowded, shining world where everything is wet, everything is watched, everything is moving, and almost nothing makes a sound.
