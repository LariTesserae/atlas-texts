An ordinary human should prepare for this place as if crossing three thresholds at once: a high-altitude mountain night, a wet industrial cavern, and a slow, crowded animal city whose architecture is made as much of sound, water, and living bodies as of stone. The preparation should not be only equipment-based. It must also be sensory, medical, ethical, and psychological. This is not a hostile place in the simple sense, but it is profoundly unsympathetic to unprepared human habits. It will not become more human for you. You must become more careful, slower, quieter, and more aware of pressure, moisture, echo, and living traffic.

---

## 1. First attitude: do not expect a “city” in the human sense

Before anything else, the traveler must correct their expectations.

This location is not a ruined city waiting to be explored, nor a finished settlement waiting to be toured. It is closer to a process: rock becoming civic form, water becoming infrastructure, animals becoming maintenance systems, technology becoming geology. The visitor should not look for streets, doors, plazas, or signs in the ordinary sense. They should expect:

- surfaces that are wet and uneven even when engineered;
- walls that may be half cliff, half vault;
- paths that may be used more by fauna than by humanoids;
- machinery that behaves like weather or metabolism;
- navigation based on sound, pressure, texture, and scent rather than visible landmarks;
- dense animal presence at every scale, including underfoot, overhead, inside drains, and along handholds.

The correct mental model is not “urban exploration.” It is more like entering a vast, cold, breathing organ made of stone and water, where human presence is incidental.

Preparation begins with that humility.

## 2. Medical preparation

### High-altitude fitness and screening

The basin is high enough that the body notices before the mind does. Thin, cold, saturated air can produce altitude sickness even in fit travelers. An ordinary human should not arrive directly from low elevation without preparation.

Recommended preparation:

- Obtain medical clearance for high-altitude travel, especially if the traveler has heart, lung, blood-pressure, or circulation issues.
- If possible, spend several days acclimatizing at intermediate elevation before arrival.
- Learn the early symptoms of acute mountain sickness: headache, nausea, poor sleep, dizziness, unusual fatigue, shortness of breath at rest.
- Know the emergency signs of high-altitude pulmonary or cerebral edema: confusion, coughing, chest tightness, loss of coordination, inability to stay awake. These require immediate descent.
- Discuss altitude medication with a qualified physician if such travel is medically appropriate. Do not rely on medication as a substitute for acclimatization.

The saturated cold can make breathing feel harder than the oxygen level alone would suggest. The air may be thin but damp; each breath may feel both insufficient and heavy. A traveler with asthma, cold-sensitive airways, or chronic sinus problems should be especially cautious.

### Cold, wet, and immersion risks

This is not a dry cold. Dry cold can be managed with insulation alone; wet cold attacks judgment, dexterity, and endurance. The traveler should prepare for:

- hypothermia;
- chilblains;
- trench foot;
- skin maceration;
- cold-induced loss of finger dexterity;
- dehydration despite feeling constantly surrounded by water;
- electrolyte imbalance from high-altitude exertion;
- eye irritation from cold mineral aerosols;
- sinus and ear discomfort from pressure, moisture, and sound.

The traveler should not assume that “wet” means “rain.” The danger here is persistent condensation, seepage, film, mist, and contact moisture. Everything may be damp even when nothing is actively pouring.

### Biological and fungal precautions

There are no plants, but there are microbial films, fungal threads, gelatinous mineral feeders, spores, aerosols, animal waste, and living surfaces. These are not necessarily dangerous, but an ordinary human should treat them as potentially irritating, allergenic, or ecologically sensitive.

Prepare by:

- carrying a medical kit that includes wound care, antifungal treatment, antihistamines, eye wash, and skin-barrier supplies;
- avoiding contact between face, mouth, eyes, and local surfaces;
- not touching local water and then touching skin or food;
- keeping any cuts completely covered;
- using gloves for all contact with stone, fauna, or machinery;
- learning basic signs of infection: spreading redness, heat, swelling, unusual pain, fever;
- understanding that mineral-rich water may contain dissolved metals, alkaline crusts, or microbial metabolic byproducts unsuitable for drinking without serious treatment.

The traveler should also consider the reverse risk: human microbes may be dangerous to the place. This is not only about protecting oneself. It is about not contaminating a strange, delicate, mineral-animal ecology.

## 3. Respiratory preparation

The air is thin, cold, and saturated, with mineral fog, microbial aerosols, fine particulates near condensation engines, and possibly metallic dust from construction processes. Breathing should not be left unmanaged.

### Respirator or breathing system

An ordinary traveler should use a comfortable, sealed breathing system capable of:

- filtering fine particulates and aerosols;
- reducing inhalation of spores and microbial mist;
- warming and humidifying air if needed;
- supplying supplemental oxygen at high altitude;
- resisting clogging from condensation and mineral deposits.

A simple mask may not be enough if the fog is engineered with microscopic builders. The traveler should prefer a closed or semi-closed system with heated filters and replaceable cartridges. Spare filters are essential.

### Oxygen

Because the place is high and the air difficult, supplemental oxygen is strongly advisable unless the traveler has proven high-altitude adaptation and the local oxygen partial pressure is confirmed safe.

Preferred features:

- quiet delivery, because loud hissing could disturb fauna or acoustic systems;
- low visual signature, because bright equipment may be inappropriate in a lightless environment;
- long duration or rechargeable/replenishable supply;
- emergency reserve independent from main gear;
- no open flame or combustion-based oxygen generation.

The traveler should not assume that the city’s technologies provide breathable air in every district. Some areas may have warm vents or oxygen-producing systems, but others may be poor in oxygen, high in aerosols, or saturated with mineral fog.

## 4. Clothing: manage water as the primary enemy

The traveler must dress for a place where water has priority. Clothing should not merely be waterproof; it must manage condensation, seepage, sweat, and contact wetness over long periods.

### Layering philosophy

The ordinary human should avoid cotton completely. Cotton becomes cold, heavy, and dangerous.

A better system:

1. **Base layer**: thin, moisture-moving, quick-drying, non-irritating. It should move sweat away from skin without becoming clammy.
2. **Insulating layer**: synthetic or aerogel-type insulation that retains warmth when damp. Down is risky unless exceptionally well protected, because wet down collapses.
3. **Vapor-control layer**: depending on suit design, this may be a smart membrane or active climate layer. In saturated air, passive breathability may fail because the outside is already saturated. Active dehumidification or condensate collection may be needed.
4. **Outer shell**: abrasion-resistant, waterproof, seam-sealed, with a smooth surface that does not snag on stone, shells, filaments, or animal colonies. It should shed water but not be so stiff that it prevents tactile awareness.
5. **Seals**: wrists, ankles, neck, and closures must be secure. Not necessarily airtight, but resistant to seepage, spores, and small crawling fauna.

### The problem of sweat

In saturated cold air, sweat may not evaporate. A traveler who overheats while climbing or moving through dense passages can become soaked from the inside. The clothing system should allow venting into controlled condensate paths, not simply let moisture accumulate.

Good preparation includes practicing in wet-cold conditions while doing slow physical work. The traveler should learn how their body sweats, where moisture collects, and how to adjust layers before becoming wet.

### Hood, neck, and face

The head and neck need special protection. A hood should:

- fit under or over breathing equipment without leaking;
- protect ears from cold and excessive sound;
- not block peripheral hearing completely;
- have a small brim or drip edge to keep condensation off the face;
- avoid loose fabric that could catch on rock or fauna.

A transparent or lightly tinted face shield may protect eyes from cold aerosols and dripping mineral water. It should not be overly reflective or brightly lit.

### Gloves

Hands are vulnerable. The traveler needs:

- thin inner gloves for dexterity and warmth;
- waterproof outer gloves with good grip on wet stone;
- spare gloves, because gloves will become wet;
- tactile fingertips so the traveler can feel edges, rails, walls, and subtle vibration;
- no powders, scents, or loose fibers that could contam surfaces or attract fauna.

Touch is one of the primary senses here. Overly clumsy gloves can be dangerous. The traveler should practice manipulating buckles, ropes, latches, and small devices while wearing the gloves they intend to use.

### Boots

Footwear is critical. The ground is wet, slick, uneven, cold, and often alive with microbial films or animal trails.

Boots should be:

- insulated but not overheated;
- waterproof to at least mid-calf;
- stiff enough for sharp stone but flexible enough to feel footing;
- fitted with soles designed for wet rock and biofilm;
- drainable or sealable depending on strategy;
- compatible with removable traction aids if ice is present;
- quiet, because hard clacking soles may interfere with acoustic orientation.

The traveler should avoid aggressive metal crampons unless necessary, because they can damage surfaces, disturb fauna, and create dangerous sparks or vibrations on certain engineered stone. Instead, use micro-spikes or high-friction wet-rock soles, and remove them when entering delicate areas if local guidance permits.

Trench foot prevention is essential. The traveler should carry:

- spare socks in waterproof bags;
- foot powder or approved drying agents;
- skin barrier balm;
- the ability to dry feet inside a shelter;
- knowledge of how to recognize early skin maceration.

## 5. Personal microclimate: because saturated cold is not solved by insulation alone

An ordinary traveler may eventually need more than passive clothing. In this environment, a personal microclimate system can be the difference between functional and miserable.

Useful features:

- small powered drying loop for gloves and boots;
- heated collar or torso layer to preserve core temperature;
- condensate collection from breath and inner suit layers;
- low-power dehumidification near skin;
- quiet operation;
- no bright indicator lights;
- no high-frequency whines that could disturb fauna or resonant systems;
- redundant battery supply kept warm inside clothing.

The traveler should not become a heat beacon. Heat is valuable here, and fauna may be attracted to warmth. A good system should conserve heat without radiating it excessively. The visitor should be warm enough to function, not so hot that they become an anomaly drawing animals, condensation, or machine attention.

## 6. Light: bring very little, and use it with discipline

This place is lightless or nearly so. An ordinary human will want light. But light must be treated as a disturbance, not a right.

### Why light matters here

Light is rare. Fauna may be sensitive to it. Microbial systems may respond to it. The city’s own navigation may depend on sound and pressure rather than visibility. Bright illumination could:

- startle animals;
- disrupt breeding or roosting behavior;
- interfere with maintenance fields;
- reveal the traveler too abruptly to machines or fauna;
- create glare on wet stone;
- ruin the traveler’s dark adaptation;
- violate local norms if such norms exist.

### What to bring

The traveler should carry:

- a very dim headlamp or chest light with adjustable output;
- red or deep amber modes, if local fauna tolerates longer wavelengths better than blue/white;
- shielded lighting that illuminates only the immediate task;
- backup lights sealed against moisture;
- phosphorescent or tactile markers rather than bright beacons;
- no flashing lights unless emergency only.

### How to use it

Use light sparingly. Use it downward. Use it for short intervals. Let your eyes adjust. Learn to move by peripheral awareness, touch, and sound rather than demanding a visible scene.

A useful discipline:

- light off while moving through open fauna spaces;
- light on only for reading equipment, inspecting footing, or medical need;
- never shine light across rookeries, nests, or aerial creatures;
- never use light to signal casually;
- if something approaches the light, dim it and wait.

The traveler should prepare to see very little. That is not failure. It is the nature of the place.

## 7. Hearing: the most important sense to train and protect

The city is loud, but loud in a slow, layered way. Water, machinery, fauna, echo, and pressure combine into a vast acoustic field. A human who relies on vision will be disoriented. A human who learns to listen may begin to orient.

### Acoustic hazards

Sound can be dangerous here. Not necessarily painfully loud, but persistent and disorienting. Risks include:

- masking important signals;
- creating false echoes;
- causing fatigue;
- interfering with sleep;
- triggering anxiety in darkness;
- misleading navigation if the traveler misreads reverberation;
- damaging hearing if low-frequency machinery or resonant structures are intense.

### Hearing protection and enhancement

The traveler should use adaptive hearing protection:

- reduce harmful volume;
- preserve useful frequencies;
- allow directional awareness;
- damp sudden echoes;
- avoid total silence, which can be psychologically destabilizing;
- enable communication with companions.

Bone-conduction communication is useful because it does not pump sound into the air. Throat microphones or subvocal systems may be better than open speakers.

### Training the ear

Before visiting, the traveler should practice:

- walking blindfolded in safe environments;
- recognizing changes in echo as walls or openings approach;
- identifying surfaces by sound;
- using a cane or probe to test footing;
- distinguishing water sounds: drip, trickle, flow, standing pool, overflow, ice-skin;
- listening to low-frequency vibration through boots and hands;
- remaining still and letting sound settle.

They should learn that echo is information. A change in reverberation can mean an open basin ahead, a low ceiling, a wall, a shaft, or a crowd of bodies.

### Acoustic etiquette

Because sound is architecture here, the traveler must control their own noise.

They should:

- remove jangling gear;
- secure loose items;
- avoid shouting;
- avoid rhythmic tapping unless intentionally signaling;
- avoid ultrasonic devices unless approved;
- avoid playing music or broadcasting sound;
- pause before entering resonant spaces;
- listen for responses from fauna or machinery.

The traveler is entering a place where a poorly timed noise may be interpreted as a signal, a threat, a courtship call, or interference with construction. Silence is not merely polite. It may be safe.

## 8. Navigation: prepare to move without sight

The basin may be kilometers across, but visibility may be only a few meters. There may be no sun, no horizon, no visible landmarks. Navigation must be multi-sensory.

### Essential navigation tools

An ordinary traveler should carry:

- inertial movement tracker;
- compass equivalent calibrated for local conditions;
- altimeter, because vertical position matters;
- barometer, because pressure changes may indicate shafts or weather systems;
- tactile map if available;
- acoustic transponders that do not disturb fauna;
- physical line or guide-rope for retreat in dangerous zones;
- marking method that does not damage surfaces;
- haptic feedback device, such as a wrist compass or vibration cue;
- simple cane or telescoping probe.

Visual maps may be useless unless they include acoustic, pressure, and texture information. The best map would describe:

- echo signatures;
- drip patterns;
- heat gradients;
- animal traffic routes;
- machine hum zones;
- wetness levels;
- slope direction;
- resonant towers or markers;
- quiet zones and loud zones.

### Movement discipline

The traveler should move slowly, pause often, and listen.

Basic protocol:

1. Stop.
2. Listen.
3. Test footing.
4. Sense airflow and pressure.
5. Move a short distance.
6. Pause again.

In open darkness, the temptation is to hurry. That is dangerous. The traveler should expect to cover less distance than they would on the surface. Time estimates should be generous.

### If lost

If the traveler becomes disoriented:

- stop;
- reduce light;
- reduce noise;
- check breathing;
- check temperature and wetness;
- check altitude and direction if instruments function;
- use prearranged recall signal only if necessary;
- do not wander toward unfamiliar warmth or sound without caution;
- if fauna gathers, become still and nonthreatening.

Panic in a lightless, crowded, echoing place can quickly become fatal. The traveler should practice calming techniques before departure.

## 9. Footing, touch, and climbing

The terrain is wet stone, microbial varnish, silt, gravel, ice-skins, polished animal trails, and half-formed architecture. It will be slippery. It will also be crowded.

### Surfaces to distrust

- glossy black rock with water film;
- pale calcium crusts that may be brittle;
- silt terraces that may hide shallow water;
- microbial coatings, which can be extremely slick;
- frozen skin over standing water;
- animal-polished stairs and ledges;
- loose volcanic fragments;
- walls with dense colonies that may shift or excrete cement;
- fog-covered edges where depth cannot be judged.

### Touch as information

The traveler should use hands, knees, and probe deliberately. They should learn to feel:

- vibration from machinery;
- warmth from heat channels;
- pressure change near open drops;
- subtle slope;
- sticky or cement-like residues;
- living surfaces that should not be leaned on;
- engineered smoothness versus raw fracture.

However, touching should be minimal and respectful. This is not a place to grab whatever is nearby. Some surfaces may be active, fragile, warm, sharp, or inhabited.

### Climbing ethics

If climbing is necessary:

- avoid damaging biological crusts;
- avoid nesting colonies;
- avoid placing weight on structures that seem unfinished;
- do not assume a handhold is stable;
- do not hammer anchors into fused or resonant stone unless absolutely necessary;
- prefer natural pressure points and existing worn paths;
- use soft slings rather than sharp metal where possible;
- descend if unsure.

The city may appear climbable because it has terraces, walls, and bridges. But those features may be maintained by pressure fields, sound gradients, or animal activity. They may not behave like ordinary stone.

## 10. Water protocol: never obstruct, never drink casually

Water has priority here. The traveler must act accordingly.

### Do not interfere with flow

Every surface sheds, collects, or channels water. The traveler should:

- not sit or place gear in shallow channels;
- not block drains or gutters;
- not step repeatedly across delicate silt beds;
- not dam condensation trails;
- not lean against weeping walls in a way that redirects flow;
- not disturb ice skins unless necessary for safety.

Blocking water, even briefly, could affect construction, fauna migration, or structural balance.

### Drinking

Do not drink local water without rigorous testing and treatment. It may contain:

- dissolved metals;
- mineral builders;
- microbial films;
- fungal particles;
- animal waste;
- alkaline or acidic crust residues;
- cold-adapted organisms harmful to humans;
- engineered microscopic agents.

The traveler should carry enough safe water and a conservative rationing plan. High altitude increases fluid loss even when the traveler does not feel thirsty.

Useful hydration items:

- insulated reservoir to prevent freezing;
- drinking tube accessible without removing gloves;
- electrolyte tablets;
- warm drinks in sealed containers;
- emergency purification rated for bacteria, protozoa, viruses, metals, and particulates, if local use becomes unavoidable.

But the best rule is simple: bring your own water unless survival requires otherwise.

### Managing external water

The traveler should expect to be wet from contact, condensation, and seepage. Gear should be arranged so that water does not collect against skin.

Useful habits:

- wipe condensation from critical seals;
- keep spare dry layers compressed in waterproof bags;
- store electronics above the wettest zones;
- use drip loops on cords so water does not run into cuffs;
- dry gloves in rotation;
- avoid placing packs directly on wet stone for long periods;
- check boots and socks regularly.

## 11. Fauna preparation: expect to be surrounded, touched, and ignored

The dominant presence is animal life. The traveler should prepare emotionally and practically for an environment where bodies are everywhere.

### Fauna are not scenery

They are infrastructure, labor, population, and ecology. Some graze films. Some filter water. Some deposit cement. Some clean channels. Some incubate in machinery. Some navigate by pressure and sound. Some may be partly engineered. They may be slow, cold, numerous, and unafraid in the way creatures become unafraid when they have no evolutionary memory of humans.

The traveler should not expect them to behave like pets, pests, or predators. They may simply persist.

### Emotional preparation

Many humans will find this unsettling. The traveler may feel:

- watched, though few creatures have visible eyes;
- crowded, though few are aggressive;
- disgusted by films, larvae, egg masses, or clustered bodies;
- anxious about stepping on something;
- claustrophobic when drains or walls contain resting animals;
- overwhelmed by sound and movement in darkness.

The traveler should practice calm, slow movement. Sudden gestures may cause chain reactions. Panic may injure fauna and endanger the traveler.

### Do not feed, touch, or collect

Basic fauna protocol:

- do not feed anything;
- do not touch rookeries or egg masses;
- do not pick up creatures unless necessary for immediate safety;
- do not collect shells, beads, cement, or biological samples without permission;
- do not block animal paths;
- do not disturb resting masses;
- do not use repellents or poisons;
- do not introduce human scents or food particles.

If an animal approaches, remain still. If it climbs onto gear, wait. If it blocks a passage, look for another route or wait until it moves. If many animals become interested in you, check whether you are emitting heat, light, odor, vibration, or food scent.

### Fauna may be attracted to heat and water

Your warmth may be unusual. Your condensation may attract them. Your exhaled moisture may draw small aerial or climbing forms. This does not mean they are attacking. It may mean they are thermotaxising or investigating.

To reduce attraction:

- keep food sealed;
- keep waste sealed;
- avoid spilling water;
- avoid excessive external heat;
- avoid scented products;
- keep suit surfaces smooth;
- do not leave gear unattended in animal-dense areas.

### If fauna enter your equipment

This may happen. Eggs, larvae, or small resting forms may enter packs, boots, hoods, or maintenance spaces. The traveler should:

- stop;
- open the item slowly;
- let the creature leave on its own if possible;
- remove it gently if necessary;
- return it to a suitable surface;
- check for damage or contamination;
- avoid crushing anything.

The traveler should not bring organisms back through multiverse transit. Departure inspection and quarantine may be necessary.

## 12. Biosecurity and contamination control

This place has no green plants, no ordinary soil ecology, and surfaces coated by specialized microbial and fungal communities. Human contamination could be serious.

### Before arrival

All gear should be:

- cleaned;
- sterilized according to approved protocols;
- free of plant matter, seeds, soil, spores, insects, and food residues;
- inspected for lubricants or chemicals that might leach;
- packed in sealed containers until arrival.

Clothing should not have been worn through forests, farms, caves, or other biologically rich environments without cleaning. Boot soles are a major contamination risk.

### During the visit

The traveler should:

- not leave food scraps;
- not pour liquids onto local surfaces;
- not urinate or defecate into the environment without sealed containment;
- not spit;
- not discard wipes, tissue, or medical waste;
- not leave tape, fibers, or plastic fragments;
- not touch microbial films unnecessarily;
- not carve or mark stone;
- not introduce microorganisms from breath or skin into critical systems.

A sealed waste system is essential. All human waste should be contained and removed.

### After departure

The traveler should expect decontamination. They should be prepared for:

- gear inspection;
- boot and sole cleaning;
- biological quarantine;
- filter disposal;
- suit isolation;
- possible health monitoring.

Do not smuggle specimens. Even a small mineral bead, egg, film sample, or fungal thread could become dangerous in another world.
