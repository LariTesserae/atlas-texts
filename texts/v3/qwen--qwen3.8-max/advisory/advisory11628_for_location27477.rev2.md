Prepare as if you are entering a city that is also a living machine, a wetland without plants, a market, a rookery, a hydraulic engine, and a vast resonating instrument. The main difficulty of visiting this place is not that it is empty or hostile. It is that it is *full*: full of water, sound, bodies, infrastructure, weather, and use. An ordinary human multiverse traveler should prepare for saturation, crowding, slippery footing, constant noise, humidity, and a world in which almost every surface has a purpose, a schedule, or a claim.

The best mindset is not “wilderness explorer.” It is more like: **careful guest in a very old, very active, very wet civic organism.**

---

## 1. Understand what the place will ask of you

This location will test an ordinary human in several ways at once:

- **Wetness**: Your feet, clothes, skin, gear, and lungs will be exposed to constant damp. Water is not merely weather here; it is the city’s circulating medium.
- **Noise**: The soundscape is not background. It is pressure. Dripping, rushing water, machinery, animals, wings, voices, bells, floodgates, and resonant stone can combine into a wall of sound.
- **Crowding**: There is no true emptiness. People, animals, machines, roosts, stalls, pipes, channels, and shrines occupy every usable volume. Privacy is rare and must be rented, scheduled, or carefully created.
- **No green**: There are no plants. No trees, grass, crops, moss, flowers, pollen, wood, or leafy smell. The freshness is mineral: wet stone, steam, salt, metal, fungal mats, animal musk, and rain on warm ceramic.
- **Constant change**: Streets may become sluices. Markets may flood and reappear. Fog may fill low plazas. Storms may form over one district because the city made them. You must think in terms of states and schedules, not fixed conditions.
- **Advanced but hidden technology**: The city’s systems are sophisticated enough to seem natural. They may adjust water levels, repair surfaces, regulate atmosphere, anticipate storms, and manage animal populations. Do not tamper with them.

You should not plan to “conquer” this place. Plan to become small, careful, observant, and adaptable.

## 2. Decide what kind of visit is realistic

For an ordinary human traveler, the safest first visit should be:

- **Short**: a few days to a week, not a long expedition.
- **Guided**: preferably with a local guide, host, or sanctioned traveler registry.
- **Based in higher, drier districts**: upper terraces, rooftops, dry platforms, cistern-adjacent lodging, or inhabited levels above ordinary flood lines.
- **Limited in vertical and hydraulic ambition**: do not begin by exploring lower vaults, pipe systems, overflow chambers, flooded markets, or maintenance tunnels.

This city-continent is vast enough to behave like a landscape. It is not a place to cross casually on foot. Distances may be deceptive because the environment is layered: a route that looks short horizontally may involve stairs, ramps, causeways, flood channels, animal roosts, market platforms, and timed water-rights passages.

If you have no local contact, arrange one before arrival. If that is impossible, enter through an official traveler point and immediately seek licensed lodging, local advice, and a map of pump/flood/storm schedules.

## 3. Medical and bodily preparation

### Get medically checked before travel

You should consult a clinician experienced in multiverse or unusual-environment travel, if available. Ask specifically about:

- Respiratory health  
- Fungal and mold exposure  
- Skin infections and trench foot  
- Waterborne pathogens  
- Allergies to spores, fungi, shellfish, chitin, biofilms, or mineral dust  
- Heat stress and dehydration  
- Hearing protection and noise fatigue  
- Chronic conditions aggravated by humidity or noise

If you have asthma, COPD, severe allergies, immune suppression, heart problems, balance issues, or anxiety disorders, you should be especially cautious. This is not necessarily a forbidden place, but it is demanding.

Carry:

- Personal medications in waterproof containers
- A medical information card in the local language or pictographic form
- A list of allergies and conditions
- Enough medication for delays, plus a small surplus
- Any required legal documentation for controlled medicines

### Prepare your feet as if they are sacred

Your feet are your greatest vulnerability here. Wet stone, wet ceramic, wet stairs, biofilms, and constant damp will punish careless footwear and neglected skin.

Before departure:

- Treat any existing blisters, fungal infections, cuts, or cracked skin.
- Trim toenails carefully.
- Break in footwear slowly.
- Practice walking on wet, slippery surfaces if you can safely do so.
- Learn how to dry feet quickly and inspect them.

During the visit:

- Inspect feet at least twice daily.
- Dry between toes thoroughly.
- Rotate socks.
- Use antifungal powder or local equivalent.
- Treat hot spots immediately.
- Never ignore macerated, wrinkled, whitening skin.

If your feet fail, the city becomes much more dangerous.

### Build tolerance for noise and sensory load

If possible, prepare by spending time in loud, crowded, humid places. Practice:

- Sleeping with noise
- Moving slowly in crowds
- Breathing calmly when sound feels oppressive
- Using earplugs without becoming disoriented
- Communicating with gestures when speech is difficult

This place can overwhelm not through danger, but through relentless input.

## 4. Clothing: dress for permanent damp

Do not pack for a dry city. Do not pack for cold wilderness. Pack for warm, saturated, mineral wetness.

### Fabrics

Avoid cotton. Cotton absorbs water, stays wet, chafes, and dries slowly.

Prefer:

- Synthetic quick-dry fabrics
- Merino blends, if you tolerate them
- Lightweight, breathable, abrasion-resistant materials
- Mesh panels for ventilation
- Smooth seams to reduce chafing

Avoid heavy leather. It will mold, stiffen, corrode fittings, and smell.

### Base layers

You want clothing that can be wet without becoming dangerous.

Good options:

- Quick-dry underwear
- Moisture-wicking liner socks
- Lightweight rash guard or synthetic base shirt
- Anti-chafe shorts or leggings
- Quick-dry trousers that can be rolled up or split for wading

If your skin is sensitive, bring anti-chafe balm and barrier cream. Wet fabric against warm skin can cause painful abrasion quickly.

### Outer layer

A fully sealed rain suit may be too hot and sweaty. Better is a lightweight waterproof shell that can handle slapping rain and drips while allowing ventilation.

Useful features:

- Hood large enough to cover head and neck
- High collar
- Adjustable cuffs
- Vents under arms or across back
- Quick drainage
- No long dangling straps or cords

A soft, waterproof hat or cap can help more than a broad-brimmed hat in crowded spaces. Broad brims catch drips, bump into people, and become nuisances in low passages.

### Footwear

This is one of the most important choices.

You need footwear with:

- Excellent wet-stone grip
- Drainage
- Quick-drying uppers
- Toe protection
- Secure fit
- No absorbent padding that stays wet forever
- Smooth enough soles not to damage worn city surfaces

A good setup may include:

1. **Primary amphibious shoes**: closed-toe, quick-drying, grippy, protective.
2. **Dry-camp sandals or light shoes**: for sleeping areas, upper terraces, or lodging.
3. **Several pairs of socks**: liner socks, midweight socks, and dry sleeping socks.

Do not go barefoot. Pools in footprints, biofilms, hidden seams, small animals, mineral crusts, and waste make barefoot travel foolish.

### Hand and head gear

- Thin gloves with grip can protect hands on wet rails and rough stone.
- A soft cap or hood protects against drip and condensation.
- A neck gaiter or light scarf can protect against mist, spores, and odor, but avoid anything long enough to snag.

### Spare dry clothing

You will never have complete dryness. Your goal is to maintain small dry reserves.

Always keep:

- One completely dry sleep outfit
- Several dry pairs of socks
- One dry base layer sealed in a waterproof bag

These are not luxuries. They are psychological and medical reserves.

## 5. The dryness system: waterproofing is survival

In this city, dryness is a resource. Treat it like money.

### Dry bags

Use multiple dry bags rather than one large waterproof compartment.

Recommended:

- Small roll-top dry bags for documents, medicines, and electronics
- Medium dry bags for clothing
- Larger liner inside your pack
- Zip bags or reusable waterproof pouches for daily items
- Color-coded bags so you can find things quickly

### Desiccants

Carry:

- Silica gel packs
- Rechargeable desiccant containers if possible
- Small dry boxes for batteries, filters, and medical supplies

### Electronics

Assume electronics will fail unless protected.

Use:

- Waterproof cases
- Conformal-coated or rugged devices if available
- Spare batteries in insulated dry boxes
- Short charging cables
- Corrosion-resistant connectors
- Local power adapters if the city offers power points

Do not rely on solar charging. The light is ordinary but often overcast, and crowded vertical spaces make solar panels impractical.

### Metal care

Salt, mineral water, copper stains, and humidity corrode metal.

Prefer:

- Stainless steel
- Titanium
- Plastic composites
- Coated tools

Rin and dry metal items when possible. Keep blades, multi-tools, and fasteners lightly protected if local regulations allow them.

## 6. Hearing protection is not optional

The sound can harm you. Not only because it is loud, but because it is continuous, layered, and resonant.

Bring:

- High-fidelity filtered earplugs for movement and awareness
- High-attenuation earplugs for sleep
- Custom-molded plugs if you can get them
- A spare set kept absolutely dry
- Possibly lightweight over-ear protection for especially loud infrastructure zones

But do not block all sound. This city communicates through sound. Floodgates knock. Pumps change pitch. Animals panic. Crowds shift. Storms arrive as changes in resonance before they arrive as rain.

You need protection that preserves warning signals.

If you use hearing aids:

- Use waterproof or dehumidifying storage
- Bring spare parts
- Consider directional or noise-managed settings
- Keep a dry backup communication method

If you feel ear pressure, headache, irritability, tinnitus, or dizziness, take it seriously. Noise fatigue can impair judgment.

## 7. Respiratory preparation

The air is warm, thick, misty, and biologically active. There are no plant pollens, but there are fungal spores, microbial aerosols, mineral dust, steam, animal particles, and chemical traces.

Bring:

- Particulate masks or respirators
- Spare filters
- A lightweight half-face respirator if you are sensitive
- Activated carbon filters if odors or gases are a concern
- Anti-fog eye protection if you wear glasses
- Saline nasal rinse if medically appropriate
- Inhalers or prescribed respiratory medication if needed

Avoid heavy perfumes, scented soaps, or strong-smelling products. They may attract animals, irritate locals, or contaminate delicate water systems.

If you develop cough, chest tightness, fever, or unusual breathing difficulty, seek local medical help early. Fungal or biofilm-related respiratory problems are not something to ignore.

## 8. Hydration: water is everywhere, but safe drinking water is not automatic

The city is saturated, but you should not assume any water is safe to drink.

Water here may contain:

- Chemosynthetic microbes
- Animal waste
- Mineral salts
- Metal residues
- Industrial lubricants or engineered biofilms
- Local organisms harmless to residents but harmful to travelers
- High temperatures or stagnant pockets

### Carry water capacity

Bring:

- At least two liters of clean water capacity for daily movement
- Collapsible containers for lodging
- A sealed clean bottle for emergencies

### Purification

Use a layered approach:

1. **Prefilter** if water is cloudy or full of particles.
2. **Fine filtration** for microbes and biofilm fragments.
3. **Chemical treatment** if needed.
4. **Boiling** only if you have a safe heat source and local permission.

Open flames are probably a bad idea in this city. There is no wood, no plant fuel, and dense infrastructure. Use local food and water services when possible.

### Electrolytes

Warm humid air makes sweating inefficient. You may not feel as dehydrated as you are.

Carry:

- Oral rehydration salts
- Electrolyte tablets
- Salty snacks
- A way to monitor hydration, such as urine color

Do not rely only on plain water.

## 9. Food: prepare for a non-plant diet

This world has no crops, orchards, grains, vegetables, fruits, timber, or plant-based agriculture in the ordinary sense. Food comes from:

- Fish and eel-like canal animals
- Crustaceans
- Shellfish-like organisms
- Fungal mats
- Cultured biofilms
- Mineral cakes
- Eggs
- Animal products
- Engineered protein
- Waste-recycled nutrients

For an ordinary traveler, this means:

- Bring familiar emergency rations at first.
- Do not rely on local food until you know what your body tolerates.
- Ask before eating unfamiliar fungal, animal, or mineral products.
- Be cautious with raw or semi-raw canal foods.
- Cook when possible.
- Watch for allergies, especially to shellfish-like or fungal foods.

### Nutritional supplements

Because there are no plants, bring:

- Multivitamin or relevant supplements
- Vitamin C if you are unsure of local nutrition
- Fiber substitute if needed
- Calorie-dense familiar bars or paste
- Comfort food that is sealed and non-perishable

If you have dietary restrictions, be especially careful. Vegan, vegetarian, kosher, halal, allergy-free, or medically restricted diets may require you to carry most of your own food initially.

### Food storage

Animals and pests are clever, crowded, and opportunistic.

Use:

- Sealed containers
- Smell-proof bags if possible
- Hanging storage in lodging if permitted
- No open food in lower wet levels

Do not feed animals. Human food may harm them, alter local behavior, or violate law and custom.

## 10. Skin, wounds, and hygiene

Warm wetness makes small problems grow quickly.

### Skin care

Carry:

- Antifungal cream or powder
- Barrier cream
- Anti-chafe balm
- Mild antiseptic wipes
- Moisturizer suitable for humid climates
- Zinc oxide or equivalent for irritated skin
- Small quick-dry towel

Keep skin folds dry. Change damp clothing when possible. Use dry sleeping clothes.

### Wound care

Any cut can become a problem.

Carry:

- Waterproof bandages
- Liquid bandage
- Antiseptic solution
- Sterile gauze
- Skin closure strips
- Medical tape
- Small scissors or tweezers
- Clean gloves if available

If a wound becomes red, swollen, hot, streaked, painful, or foul-smelling, seek help immediately.

### Bathing and sanitation

Do not wash yourself, clothes, or dishes in random channels. The city’s water is shared, managed, and biologically active.

Use:

- Public wash points
- Designated drainage areas
- Local sanitation facilities
- Sealed waste bags if no facility exists

Carry:

- Small soap, preferably unscented and local-compatible
- Hand sanitizer
- Toilet paper or reusable cloth in sealed bags
- Menstrual supplies if needed, with sealed waste storage
- A small bidet bottle if that suits your hygiene style

In a city where water is sacred, economically valuable, and ecologically loaded, careless waste is not just rude. It may be dangerous.

## 11. Sleep: protect rest aggressively

Sleep will be difficult because the city does not become quiet. Even rest here is rest within noise.

Bring:

- Earplugs for sleep
- Eye mask
- Lightweight sleeping pad or inflatable mat
- Sleeping liner or thin quilt
- Small pillowcase or stuff-sack pillow
- A compact bivvy or overhead cover if local lodging is primitive

Choose sleeping places carefully.

A good sleeping spot should be:

- Above normal water level
- Not under an active roost
- Not directly beside a pressure valve or floodgate
- Ventilated but not in a draft of wet mist
- Secure for gear
- Easy to exit if water rises

If you sleep on a ledge, platform, or rented niche, respect its schedule. Many places here are owned by time, level, or use, not absolute possession. Your sleeping shelf may become someone else’s work station, drainage edge, animal perch, or storage rack at another hour.

Do not sleep on wet stone if you can avoid it. Use insulation between you and the surface.

## 12. Navigation: learn to read water, height, sound, and flow

This city will confuse ordinary map habits.

Streets change state. A passage may be dry at one pump cycle and flooded at another. A plaza may be a market in the morning, a spillway at midday, and a fog basin by evening. Vertical levels matter more than horizontal distance.

### Carry navigation tools

Bring:

- Waterproof map or local chart
- Waterproof notebook
- Pencil or grease pencil
- Compass, but do not rely on it entirely near metal infrastructure
- Personal locator beacon if compatible with local systems
- Local mesh device or guide beacon if available
- Watch, preferably waterproof and simple

### Learn local orientation logic

Ask about:

- Pump cycles
- Floodgate schedules
- Storm patterns
- Roost times
- Market tides
- Canal traffic rules
- Vertical district names
- Cistern towers as landmarks
- Acoustic signals or beacon tones

### Use water as a clue

Water generally moves downward toward drainage, canals, reservoirs, or lower wards. Airflow may move through tunnels and vents. Heat rises from lower districts. Sound may carry along channels.

But do not simply follow water. Water may be moving into a machine, a surge chamber, a hazardous overflow, or a closed system.

### If lost

As a general rule:

1. Stop.
2. Move upward if safe.
3. Seek a dry, public, populated area.
4. Look for markets, cistern towers, shrines, or official stations.
5. Show a traveler card or pictogram.
6. Avoid descending into quiet tunnels or unlit maintenance spaces.

Do not wander into lower infrastructure because it looks “empty.” It is probably empty for a reason.

## 14. Movement etiquette: do not block the city

The city moves in currents: water currents, human currents, animal currents, market currents, maintenance currents. An ordinary traveler must learn to flow.

### Basic rules

- Do not stop suddenly in narrow passages.
- Do not stand at the top or bottom of stairs without moving aside.
- Do not block drainage lips, sluice edges, valve access, or gate mechanisms.
- Keep your pack small and close to your body.
- Yield to water. If a channel is rising, the path yields.
- Yield to animals during breeding, roosting, migration, or feeding events.
- Yield to maintenance workers and repair swarms if they are active.
- Use handholds where provided.
- Do not run on wet stone.
- Do not step on biological crusts unless they are obviously meant to be walked on.

### In crowds

Crowds here are not merely people. They include animals, carts, carried goods, waterborne containers, roosting flocks, and machines.

If caught in a dense crowd:

- Keep your elbows near your body.
- Protect your chest and face from jostling.
- Move diagonally toward edges rather than fighting straight through.
- Do not panic if wings, bodies, or goods brush you.
- Watch for slippery spots where animals or water have made surfaces slick.

### On wet streets

Some streets are ankle-deep, knee-deep, or fast-flowing.

Use:

- A walking staff with a non-damaging tip
- Handrails
- Fixed ropes or cables if present
- Local guidance about depth and current

Never assume a flooded street is safe. There may be:

- Open grates
- Suction points
- Submerged steps
- Hidden animals
- Slippery biofilm
- Fast current
- Sudden pump changes

If water is moving quickly enough to require handholds, do not enter without local advice.

- Ladders if necessary
- Crowded walkways
- Low ceilings
- Dim lower levels
- Sudden height changes

## 16. Weather: the city makes its own storms

The weather here is negotiated internally. Heat rises from lower wards. Moisture evaporates from channels. Air is forced upward by districts and drawn through tunnels. Clouds form along infrastructure. Storm cells follow canals. Rain may fall only over certain rooftops.

### Learn the signs

A local storm may announce itself by:

- Heat rising from below
- A change in pitch from pipes or channels
- Clouds forming along towers or high districts
- Animals becoming restless
- Winged creatures lifting in masses
- Floodgates testing themselves
- Vendors securing goods
- A sudden metallic knocking from infrastructure
- Fog gathering in low markets

### What to do when a storm approaches

1. Move upward and away from low channels if safe.
2. Secure loose items.
3. Put on rain protection.
4. Protect hearing and eyes.
5. Avoid open water and exposed high railings unless locals do so.
6. Do not shelter under active animal roosts if droppings or panicked flocks are a risk.
7. Follow local movement into covered routes or storm shelters.
8. Do not attempt to cross newly flooded paths.

### During the storm

The storm is not necessarily an emergency. It may be one of the city’s normal activities. But for a traveler, it is a high-risk state.

Expect:

- Extreme noise
- Reduced visibility
- Rapid water rise
- Steam or mist
- Falling droplets and spray
- Panicked or active animals
- Changing access routes

Do not try to film, explore, or “experience” the storm dramatically unless you are with experienced locals and in a safe place.

### After the storm

After rain passes:

- Surfaces steam.
- Stone releases heat.
- Dripping intensifies.
- Animals emerge.
- Maintenance begins.
- Newly wet surfaces become slippery.
- Some areas may be closed for flushing or repair.

Move carefully. Do not assume everything returns instantly to its previous state.

## 17. Animals: respect them as co-occupants, not scenery

The city is full of animals that do not depend on plants. They are part of the economy, ecology, infrastructure, and daily rhythm.

### General rules

- Do not feed animals.
- Do not touch animals.
- Do not collect eggs, larvae, shells, or living organisms without permission.
- Do not block roosts, hatcheries, or breeding walls.
- Do not imitate calls or signals unless you understand them.
- Do not use flash lights, loud noises, or drones near roosts.
- If animals flee, pay attention. They may know a pressure change, flood, gas, or storm.

### Water animals

Canals contain blind fish, eel-like swimmers, bottom-feeders, crustaceans, and other creatures. They may be farmed, protected, sacred, or simply part of water management.

Do not:

- Swim without permission
- Block waterways
- Step on spawning bodies
- Handle fish or crustaceans
- Drop waste into channels

### Wall and seam animals

Walls may host filter-feeders, worms, beetles, and other cleaning organisms. They may make surfaces slick or delicate.

Do not:

- Pry creatures from seams
- Scratch mineral crusts
- Insert fingers into joints
- Lean on walls unnecessarily if they are slick

### Winged animals

Winged creatures may be numerous enough to darken the air. Their wings can sound like sudden rain. Their droppings are part of the nutrient economy but can be slippery and unhygienic.

If a flock lifts suddenly:

- Protect your face and eyes.
- Do not swing wildly.
- Move toward cover if safe.
- Do not block the path of others.
- Do not panic; mass flight is often normal.

### Larger animals

There may be broad grazers of bacterial mats, pipe hunters, shell-backed scavengers, and semi-domestic species near markets.

Give them space. Do not corner them. Do not feed them. If they are working or maintained by the city, interference may be illegal.

## 18. Biosecurity: do not bring green things into a plantless world

This is important.

Do not bring:

- Seeds
- Live plants
- Soil
- Wood
- Herbal plant medicines
- Fresh fruit
- Vegetables
- Flowers
- Plant fibers that may carry seeds or microbes
- Pets or service animals unless explicitly permitted and quarantined

A plantless world may be extremely sensitive to outside plant life, fungal pathogens, or unfamiliar microbes. Conversely, local microbes may be harmful to you.

Before arrival, expect possible inspection, decontamination, or quarantine. Cooperate. If officials ask to inspect gear, food, or clothing, comply.

If you carry comfort items from home, choose inert ones:

- A photograph
- A stone from home
- A synthetic cloth
- A written note
- A sealed memento

Do not bring a living plant or seed as a psychological comfort. It may be ecologically irresponsible or illegal.

## 19. Technology and infrastructure etiquette

The city’s technology is advanced enough to seem natural. That means it is easy to misunderstand.

### Do not touch or adjust:

- Valves
- Floodgates
- Pressure wheels
- Resonant pipes
- Weather anchors
- Capillary channels
- Self-healing surfaces
- Mineral crust patterns
- Force railings
- Pump controls
- Vent covers
- Signal drums or acoustic devices

Even if something appears broken, it may be functioning in a way you cannot perceive.

### If infrastructure reacts to you

It may be monitoring:

- Weight
- Heat
- Moisture
- Sound
- Pressure
- Chemical traces
- Animal density
- Foreign objects

If a walkway lights up, a door opens, a path dries, or a channel diverts near you, remain calm. It may be normal accommodation. If a path seals or a barrier appears, do not force it. It may be preventing a surge, gas, structural stress, or animal disturbance.

### If you damage something

Report it. In a hydraulic city, small failures can become large ones. Hiding damage is dangerous.

### Devices and emissions

Be careful with:

- Strong lights
- Loudspeakers
- Ultrasonic devices
- Radio transmissions
- Drones
- Active sensors
- Multiversal beacons

These may disturb animals, interfere with infrastructure, or violate local law. Use them only when permitted.

## 20. Social rules: water rights, time rights, and density

Ownership here is often vertical, temporal, and functional.

A ledge may belong to:

- Roosting animals at night
- Workers in the morning
- Drainage at storm level
- A shrine at certain hours
- Storage during market tide
- Travelers only by paid permission

A stair may be:

- A passage at low water
- A weir at high water
- A market edge when dry
- A basking place for animals
- A meeting point at certain hours

Do not assume a place is abandoned because it is momentarily unused.

### Ask before occupying

Before sitting, sleeping, storing gear, or setting up equipment, ask if possible. Use pictograms if language fails.

### Markets

Markets are loud, wet, layered, and crowded.

When visiting markets:

- Keep your pack small.
- Do not touch goods unless invited.
- Pay before handling food.
- Keep money in a waterproof pouch.
- Do not block stall access.
- Expect dripping goods, animal products, live creatures, and strong smells.
- Learn basic prices or exchange tokens.
- Carry small denominations if local currency exists.

### Shrines

Shrines may be small, wet, crowded, and integrated into infrastructure.

Do not:

- Touch offerings
- Photograph without permission
- Leave organic offerings
- Block access
- Speak loudly unless that is the local custom

If you wish to show respect, observe first or ask.

## 24. Arrival protocol: the first hours matter

When you arrive, do not immediately rush into the depths of the city.

### First priority: stabilize

1. Find a safe, dry, elevated place.
2. Check your hearing protection.
3. Check your breathing and hydration.
4. Dry any wet essentials.
5. Inspect your feet.
6. Establish where you are relative to water flow.
7. Identify the nearest potable water point, sanitation point, medical point, and storm refuge.

### Second priority: learn the local rhythm

Spend time observing before moving far.

Notice:

- Which paths stay dry
- Which channels are rising or falling
- Where animals gather or avoid
- Which sounds precede movement
- How locals dress and carry goods
- How crowds yield to water
- Where vendors secure their stalls
- Which ledges are occupied and which are avoided

### Third priority: ask questions

If you have a host, ask:

- Where may I sleep safely?
- Where may I drink?
- Where should I not walk?
- What signals mean danger?
- What times are flood or surge times?
- Are there breeding roosts I should avoid?
- Is the air safe in lower areas?
- How do I pay for things?
- How do I show respect?

If you do not have a host, seek an official station, licensed lodging, market elder, repair crew, or traveler registry.

## 26. Emergency scenarios

### If a street begins to flood

Do not wait to see how deep it becomes.

- Move upward.
- Avoid grates and drains.
- Use handholds.
- Do not cross fast water.
- Follow locals if they are moving calmly and purposefully.
- If trapped, climb to a stable platform or fixed structure.

### If you hear floodgates knocking or pipes singing

This may be normal, but it can also indicate pressure movement.

- Secure loose items.
- Move away from valve mechanisms.
- Hold onto stable rails if the ground vibrates.
- Listen for local warnings.
- Do not place hands or gear near moving infrastructure.

### If you fall into water

- Protect your head.
- Do not panic.
- Try to float or grab a fixed edge.
- Avoid suction points, grates, and fast channels.
- Get out as soon as possible.
- Treat any cuts immediately.
- Do not drink the water.
- Dry yourself and inspect skin.

### If you become lost

- Stop.
- Move up if safe.
- Avoid descending into tunnels.
- Look for public, populated, dry areas.
- Use pictograms.
- Do not hide in infrastructure spaces.

### If you are overwhelmed by noise

This is common and not shameful.

- Move to a drier, less resonant area if possible.
- Insert earplugs.
- Focus on breathing.
- Hydrate.
- Eat something familiar.
- Rest in a rented or permitted niche.
- Do not make major travel decisions while sensory-overloaded.

### If you develop fever, cough, rash, or infected wounds

- Mask up if respiratory symptoms appear.
- Avoid contaminating water.
- Seek local medical help.
- Do not assume local microbial exposures are trivial.

### If animals behave strangely

- Do not approach.
- Do not photograph at close range.
- Move away from the direction they are fleeing.
- Look for environmental signs: gas, water rise, pressure change, storm.

### If your multiverse beacon or device causes interference

- Turn it off if safe.
- Shield it.
- Move away from weather anchors, resonant pipes, or pump systems.
- Report interference if required.

## 27. Psychological preparation: the lack of green matters

Many travelers underestimate how disturbing a plantless world can be.

There may be no leafy smell, no shade from branches, no horizon softened by vegetation, no field, no forest, no garden. The world is mineral, fungal, animal, hydraulic, and built. This can feel rich and magnificent, but also alien and oppressive.

Prepare by:

- Accepting that beauty here is not green beauty.
- Learning to notice textures: wet basalt, salt crusts, copper stains, polished shell, glazed ceramic, fungal velvet, animal gloss.
- Listening for patterns instead of seeking quiet.
- Carrying a small inert reminder of home.
- Scheduling visual and auditory rest.
- Not forcing yourself to love it immediately.

If you feel grief, irritation, or claustrophobia, that is normal. This place is saturated. It presses against you. Give yourself permission to retreat.

## 28. Ethical visitor conduct

You are entering a place where every surface is used and every organism has a role.

A good visitor:

- Leaves no waste.
- Does not disturb water flows.
- Does not feed animals.
- Does not remove living material.
- Does not touch infrastructure.
- Does not block paths.
- Does not make unnecessary noise.
- Does not assume local customs are inefficient.
- Does not treat residents or animals as attractions.
- Asks before photographing people, shrines, or hatcheries.
- Pays fairly.
- Follows storm and water rules.
- Reports damage or danger.

The city is not a backdrop. It is a system of negotiated life.

## 29. Special notes for different traveler types

### For scientists

- Get sampling permits.
- Use sterile, sealed equipment.
- Do not scrape biofilms without authorization.
- Do not introduce sensors into waterways casually.
- Record environmental data carefully, but remember local systems may be active and legally protected.

### For artists and writers

- Use waterproof paper or slates.
- Protect drawing materials from humidity.
- Expect no green palette unless you invent it.
- Sound recording requires wind and water protection.
- Ask permission before recording people or sacred sites.

### For traders

- Learn local value systems.
- Avoid ecologically dangerous goods.
- Favor repair, filtration, medical, or precision items if legal.
- Do not assume scarcity or surplus from outside logic.

### For pilgrims or spiritual travelers

- Shrines may be integrated into infrastructure.
- Offerings should be mineral, clean, and approved.
- Do not offer food, plant matter, or liquids unless told to.
- Silence may be impossible; reverence here may be rhythm, attention, or careful movement.

### For travelers with disabilities

Plan carefully and seek local advice.

- Mobility: wet stone, stairs, and water levels can be severe. Choose upper dry districts if possible.
- Hearing: protect hearing while preserving warning signals; consider visual alert devices.
- Vision: use tactile grips, cane tips suitable for wet stone, and high-contrast markers if permitted.
- Sensory sensitivity: ear protection, masks, eye protection, and retreat spaces are essential.
- Medical needs: carry documentation and waterproof supplies.

The city can be accessible, but not everywhere, not at all times, and not without local knowledge.

## 31. What visiting this place will feel like

You will feel wet before you feel anything else. Not soaked at once, but claimed by damp. Your skin will know the air. Your clothes will never fully forget it. Water will be beside you, under you, above you, inside the walls, running in gutters, beading on stone, dripping from bridges, and gathering in your footprints.

You will hear the city before you understand it. The noise will enter your chest. At first it may seem chaotic, but if you remain careful, you will begin to distinguish layers: water under everything, machinery above that, animals above that, voices and commerce above all. You will learn that a change in sound can be more important than a visible sign.

You will be crowded by life that does not resemble a green world. There will be no leaves to comfort you, no birdsong exactly like home, no familiar insects, no trees to mark a path. Instead there will be glossy animals, wet stone, fungal films, shell markets, mineral crusts, wings, heat, and endless use.

You will be tempted to think the city is chaotic because it changes state so often. It is not chaotic. It is scheduled by moisture, pressure, animals, repair, and ancient habit. The confusion is yours, not the city’s.

If you prepare well, the experience can be overwhelming without being cruel. There is richness here. There is warmth. There is an intense, roaring fullness. The place is not dead. It is not sterile. It is so full that an ordinary human must learn to become smaller, slower, quieter, and more attentive.

The goal is not to dry the city, silence it, map it completely, or make it familiar. The goal is to enter with respect, keep your feet dry as long as possible, listen carefully, move politely, and let the vast wet mineral world continue around you without needing you to control it.
