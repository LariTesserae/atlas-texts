## Preparation Brief for Visiting the High Saturated Continent  
### “A place at the end of its becoming”

To visit this location, you must first change what you think preparation means.

This is not a wilderness to be crossed, a mountain to be climbed, or a swamp to be survived. It is more like being admitted into the interior of an enormous, ancient, slow-living body. The ground is tissue. The air is breath. The water is not scenery; it is circulation. The light is not absent so much as digested. The place has already become what it is. Your task is not to impose yourself on it, but to enter without tearing it, to move without becoming a wound, and to leave without becoming a relic.

An ordinary human traveler should prepare in three overlapping ways:

1. **Physically:** for altitude, saturation, humidity, warmth, dimness, spores, soft footing, and slow exertion.  
2. **Technically:** for navigation, water purification, communication, shelter, power, and emergency extraction in a wet, muffled, low-visibility environment.  
3. **Ethically and psychologically:** for a place that is crowded, intimate, quiet, patient, and almost aggressively alive without being hostile.

The cardinal rule is simple:

> **Do not try to dry this place, conquer it, light it up, carve it, or make it legible. Prepare to become briefly compatible with it.**

---

# 1. Decide Whether You Should Go at All

This sounds severe, but it is important. Not every traveler is suited for this environment.

You should delay or avoid entry if you have:

- Untreated or unstable respiratory conditions.
- Severe asthma or allergies to mold, pollen, spores, fungi, or decaying vegetation.
- Open wounds, skin infections, fungal conditions, or compromised immune function.
- Poor tolerance for high altitude or low oxygen.
- Severe claustrophobia, agoraphobia, or panic responses to dim, enclosed, endless spaces.
- Extreme sensitivity to dampness, heat rash, chafing, or prolonged humidity.
- A psychological need for open skies, sharp horizons, dry ground, or fast movement.

This place is not violently dangerous, but it is unforgiving to impatience. It rewards slowness, care, and humility. It punishes haste by small accumulations: soaked socks, fogged goggles, clogged filters, blistered skin, poor decisions, lost direction.

If you are the kind of traveler who wants to stride across a landscape and leave a clean track behind, this place will frustrate you. If you can learn to move like moisture — quietly, slowly, with pauses — it may become one of the most profound places you ever visit.

# 2. Understand the Environment Before Packing

Before thinking about gear, understand the environmental logic.

## The ground is alive

There is almost no mineral ground. You are walking on compressed living and once-living plant matter: root webs, moss, leaf flesh, swollen stems, decaying fibers, and water-filled tissue.

The surface yields under pressure. It recovers slowly. It may feel like flesh, sponge, or old muscle. It is not unstable in the way of quicksand, but it is responsive. It will accept your footprint, then close around it.

This means:

- Do not wear sharp footwear.
- Do not drag your feet.
- Do not puncture surfaces unless absolutely necessary.
- Do not assume a firm-looking leaf or shelf will bear your full weight.
- Distribute your weight.
- Use probes or poles.
- Move as if you are walking on the back of something sleeping.

## Water is not “weather” here; water is tissue

The landscape is saturated but not flooded. Water moves by capillarity, condensation, absorption, and slow release. It crawls along root channels, beads on hairs and leaves, gathers in warm black hollows, and drips in delayed rhythms.

This means:

- You will be wet constantly.
- You cannot rely on “waiting for the rain to stop.”
- Waterproofing alone is insufficient; you must manage condensation and sweat.
- Your greatest enemies are maceration, fungal growth, chafing, and equipment rot.
- Drinking water is abundant but biologically complex. Treat all local water as suspicious.

## The air is warm, humid, and thin

The elevation is real. The air is thinner than expected, but the warmth comes from biological metabolism, decay, and humidity. There is no frost, no true cold, but the dampness can still sap you.

This means:

- Prepare for altitude.
- Prepare for humid heat.
- Prepare for exertion that feels harder than it should.
- Hydrate with electrolytes, not plain water.
- Do not assume warmth means safety. Wet warmth can still exhaust you.

## The light is dim and softened

There is a permanent greenish twilight caused by canopy layers and spore-haze. The sun, if present, appears as a pale thickening in the air. Distance is deceptive. Shapes may look larger or nearer than they are.

This means:

- Bring low-level artificial light, but use it gently.
- Do not rely on sight alone.
- Train yourself to use touch, sound, proximity, and instruments.
- Avoid bright white beams that may blind you in haze and disturb local life.

## Sound is absorbed

The landscape does not echo. It swallows sound. Voices feel dead. Calls do not carry far. Communication must be close, redundant, and deliberate.

This means:

- Do not count on shouting.
- Use hand signals, tether lines, prearranged meeting points, and short-range communication devices.
- Assume radios may be degraded by water, plant mass, and spore haze.
- Listen for small sounds: drips, clicks, bubbling, root taps, displacement of water.

## The place changes slowly, but fog and water can shift quickly enough to matter

Weather here accumulates rather than attacks. A storm is a gradual deepening of wetness. Fog tides can move at knee height, climb into the canopy, and vanish. Some hollows may become lake-like if root channels shift.

This means:

- Watch barometric pressure and humidity trends.
- Do not camp in hollows, root channels, or shallow depressions.
- If the air begins to feel heavier, if the light dims evenly, if moisture seems to lean downward, assume wetter conditions are arriving.
- Move to higher, denser, more stable growth before you need to.

# 3. The Traveler’s Mindset

Prepare your mind as carefully as your pack.

This place has a strange tenderness. It is not kind. It is not conscious in a human way. But it is soft with age. It has worn away edges. It has filled gaps. It has quieted extremes. It can make you feel held, watched, absorbed, or strangely comforted.

That comfort is a hazard.

You may feel an urge to lie down in the moss, to stop moving, to let the quiet enter you, to become still for longer than is safe. Do not mistake this for peace. It may be fatigue, hypoxia, humidity, mild intoxication from spores or fermentation gases, or simply the psychological effect of a world without sharp edges.

Practice:

- Regular self-checks.
- Timed movement breaks.
- Verbal check-ins with companions.
- Objective measurement of time and distance.
- Emotional honesty: if you feel dreamy, euphoric, unusually calm, or unwilling to leave, treat that as a warning sign.

The correct attitude is not heroic. It is almost liturgical:

- Move slowly.
- Touch gently.
- Breathe through filters.
- Speak softly.
- Observe without taking.
- Rest without nesting.
- Leave without memorial.

You are a brief visitor inside a completed world.

# 4. Physical Preparation and Acclimatization

## Altitude

Treat this as a high-altitude environment even though the terrain does not resemble mountains. The height has been worn down, but the elevation remains. The air is thinner. The horizon is too wide. Light arrives as if from a greater distance.

Before entry:

- Spend time at comparable elevation if possible.
- Learn your personal altitude symptoms.
- Know the warning signs: headache, nausea, dizziness, unusual fatigue, shortness of breath, poor sleep, confusion.
- Ascend gradually if your transit method allows.
- If entering by direct portal, consider spending the first hours at reduced activity with supplemental oxygen available.

Carry:

- A pulse oximeter.
- Emergency oxygen or an oxygen concentrator if your multiverse travel system supports it.
- Medications approved for altitude sickness if advised by a physician.
- A written plan for evacuation if symptoms worsen.

Do not push through altitude symptoms out of stubbornness. In this place, poor judgment can be more dangerous than the environment itself.

## Humid Heat

The warmth is gentle but persistent. Sweat will not evaporate efficiently. Your body may overheat even without violent exertion.

Train beforehand by walking slowly in humid conditions while carrying a light pack. Learn to recognize:

- Heat exhaustion.
- Dehydration.
- Salt depletion.
- Skin maceration.
- Chafing.
- Mental fog.

Your pace should be deliberately boring. If you feel energetic, slow down.

## Respiratory Conditioning

The air is full of spores, mist, fine particles, and biological aerosols. Even if the atmosphere is breathable, you should not breathe it raw for long periods.

Practice wearing:

- A well-fitted respirator.
- Goggles or eye protection.
- A hood or veil.
- A full-face mask if you are sensitive.

Many travelers underestimate mask fatigue. Breathing through filters in humidity is harder than expected. Train with it. Learn when filters become difficult. Learn how to replace them without contaminating yourself.

## Skin and Foot Training

Your skin will be under siege. Not from cold, but from moisture.

Before the journey:

- Treat any foot fungus, cracked skin, eczema, or irritation.
- Break in boots thoroughly.
- Practice changing socks quickly.
- Learn to inspect your feet in low light.
- Practice keeping your hands dry-ish while working with wet equipment.

This is not glamorous advice. It is essential. In a saturated world, small skin problems become major problems.

# 5. Clothing: Build a Personal Microclimate

You are not dressing for rain. You are dressing for permanent saturation.

Your clothing system should manage four problems:

1. External moisture.
2. Internal sweat.
3. Spore exposure.
4. Skin maceration.

## Base Layer

Avoid cotton entirely.

Use:

- Synthetic or fine wool blends that retain function when damp.
- Seamless or flat-seam construction.
- Lightweight, wicking, anti-chafing fabric.
- Long sleeves and long legs even though it is warm. Skin exposure invites spores, insects, microbes, and abrasion.

Your base layer should feel comfortable when wet, because it will be wet.

## Mid Layer

You do not need heavy insulation, but you do need a thin warm layer for rest periods. Wet warmth can still chill you when you stop moving.

Use:

- A light synthetic insulating layer.
- Something that does not collapse completely when damp.
- A garment that can be dried partially inside a sleeping system.

## Outer Layer

A normal rain jacket may fail here because humidity prevents breathability. You may feel just as wet from inside as outside.

Better principles:

- Use a waterproof but highly vented shell.
- Use large vents, open cuffs, and adjustable hoods.
- Prefer mechanical ventilation over perfect waterproofness.
- Use a wide-brimmed hat or hood that sheds drips away from your face and neck.
- Use a neck gutter or hood collar so condensation does not run down your spine.

If the plant canopy drips constantly, a light hood with a stiff brim is more useful than an umbrella, because thickets and hanging roots will obstruct open canopies.

## Lower Body

Use:

- Quick-drying trousers with sealed seams.
- Gaiters if moving through deeper wet growth.
- Avoid tight cuffs that trap water.
- Avoid heavy waterproof trousers unless absolutely necessary; they may become sweat bags.

If you expect to wade through shallow channels, consider amphibious lower layers that drain and dry quickly. However, drainage also allows organisms and spores inside. Balance that against your biosecurity needs.

## Boots

This is one of the most important choices.

Do not wear:

- Sharp mountaineering crampons.
- Aggressive metal cleats.
- Hard spiked soles.
- Heavy boots that tear living surfaces.

Ideal boots:

- Wide, soft-ish soles with good wet traction.
- Flexible enough to feel the ground.
- High enough to protect ankles.
- Closed enough to keep debris out.
- Drainable or fast-drying if wet.
- Compatible with waterproof socks or inner liners.
- Non-marking and non-reactive.

Consider adding:

- Broad, lightweight flotation pads or snowshoe-like overshoes for soft ground.
- Removable inner liners so you can dry them separately.
- Multiple pairs of socks.

Your goal is not to bite into the ground. Your goal is to rest on it.

## Socks

Bring more socks than seems reasonable.

Use:

- Thin liner socks under thicker socks if tolerated.
- Synthetic or wool blends.
- Antifungal treatment if recommended by a medical professional.
- Separate dry bags for clean socks and used socks.

Change socks at least once per day, more often if possible. Dry used socks inside your sleeping system or against your body if necessary, but do not leave them damp in a sealed bag.

## Gloves

Your hands will be wet and useful.

Bring:

- Thin tactile gloves for delicate work.
- Thicker protective gloves for moving through dense growth.
- Waterproof over-mitts if needed.
- Long cuffs that can be tucked under sleeves.

Avoid bare-handed contact with unknown fluids, bladders, fungal shelves, or warm pool water.

## Head, Face, and Eyes

Use:

- A wide-brimmed hat or hood.
- Anti-fog goggles or protective glasses.
- A respirator or mask.
- A neck shroud or buff if spores are dense.

If the spore-haze is thick, ordinary glasses will not suffice. You need sealed eye protection. Anti-fog treatment is essential. Carry spare anti-fog wipes or liquid, sealed against moisture.

## Spore Protection

At minimum:

- Well-fitted respirator.
- Spare filters.
- Eye protection.
- Hood.

Better:

- Full-face mask with exhalation filtration.
- Powered air-purifying respirator if you can support the weight and power.
- Exhalation filter to protect the environment from your breath.

Remember: you are protecting yourself from the place, but also protecting the place from you. Human breath carries microbes, moisture, carbon dioxide, and scent. In a world organized by chemical signaling, your exhalation is not neutral.

# 6. Skin, Foot, and Body Care Routine

In this environment, hygiene is survival.

## Twice-Daily Body Check

Inspect:

- Feet.
- Between toes.
- Groin.
- Armpits.
- Under breasts if applicable.
- Neck folds.
- Behind knees.
- Hands and wrists.

Look for:

- Whitish wrinkled skin.
- Redness.
- Itching.
- Cracking.
- Rash.
- Blisters.
- Fungal patches.

## Keep Skin Dry-ish

This sounds impossible, but it is necessary.

Use:

- Absorbent microfiber cloths.
- Barrier creams where chafing is likely.
- Antifungal powder or cream if appropriate.
- Gentle drying by patting, not rubbing.

Do not aerosolize powders in the environment. Apply carefully, preferably inside a shelter or bag.

## Wound Care

Any wound is a portal for infection.

- Clean immediately.
- Dry surrounding skin.
- Seal with waterproof dressing.
- Monitor for redness, heat, swelling, pain, pus, or fever.
- Evacuate if infection signs develop.

Do not enter warm pools with open wounds.

## Sweat Management

You will sweat. The sweat may not evaporate.

Use:

- Ventilation zippers.
- Slow pace.
- Rest in elevated, less saturated spots.
- Remove layers before overheating.
- Use a small battery-powered fan if your clothing system supports it.

Do not rely on “toughing it out.” Heat exhaustion can arrive politely.

# 7. Hydration, Electrolytes, and Food

## Water

Water is everywhere. That does not mean it is safe.

The pools are warm, tannic, biologically active, and possibly rich in microorganisms, fermentation byproducts, plant compounds, or animal waste. The liquid inside plant bladders may look clean but can contain sugars, alkaloids, resins, or defensive chemicals.

Do not drink untreated local water.

Carry:

- Enough sealed water for the first phase of the journey.
- Collapsible clean water containers.
- A filtration system.
- A purification system.
- Electrolyte tablets or powders.
- A clean drinking tube with a protected bite valve.

Recommended water treatment chain:

1. **Pre-filter** through cloth or a coarse filter if water is full of particles.
2. **Fine filtration** down to at least 0.1 micron if possible.
3. **Activated carbon** if available, to reduce tannins, taste, and some organics.
4. **Disinfection** by UV, chemical purification, or boiling if you have a sealed heating system. Open fire is impossible and inappropriate.

Important: do not dump filter backwash, chemical residues, or contaminated water into the environment. Pack it out or store it in sealed containers.

## Hydration Strategy

Because humidity hides sweat, you may lose more fluid than you realize.

Drink before thirst.

Monitor:

- Urine color.
- Headache.
- Dizziness.
- Fatigue.
- Muscle cramps.
- Irritability.

Use electrolytes. Plain water alone can become dangerous if you are sweating heavily and eating poorly.

## Food

Do not plan to forage unless you are accompanied by a qualified xenoecologist with specific knowledge of this place. Even then, be conservative.

Bring:

- Ready-to-eat rations.
- Low-odor food.
- High-calorie dense bars or packets.
- Salty snacks.
- Comfort food for morale.
- Meals requiring minimal water and no cooking.
- Sealed packaging.

Avoid:

- Strong-smelling foods.
- Crumbly foods.
- Foods that require extensive preparation.
- Open cooking.

Food waste must be sealed and packed out. This landscape composts everything, but your food is not part of its cycle. Introducing foreign nutrients, microbes, or scents can disrupt local chemical ecology.

# 8. Shelter and Rest

You will need rest, but you should not make a permanent nest.

## Campsite Selection

Choose:

- Elevated, stable growth.
- Broad hummocks or dense root platforms.
- Areas away from shallow hollows.
- Areas away from obvious root channels.
- Areas away from warm black pools.
- Areas not directly beneath large dripping leaves or hanging bladders.
- Areas with enough structure to hang or suspend shelter without cutting.

Avoid:

- Depressions that could fill with water.
- Soft, spongy patches that feel dramatically different from surrounding ground.
- Places with many chemical trails or heavy animal movement.
- Old built structures. These are not shelters. They are remains, nests, or composting phases.

## Shelter Types

Good options:

- Hammock-style shelter suspended between broad supports using wide, non-cutting straps.
- Elevated bivouac slung between buttressed stems.
- Lightweight tarp supported by tension only.
- A closed-cell pad or insulated platform laid over broad load-bearing leaves if they are proven stable.

Avoid:

- Nails.
- Screws.
- Stakes.
- Cutting branches.
- Tying tightly around thin living tissue.
- Tension that bends or damages growth.

Use wide straps, soft loops, and removable anchors. If you must anchor, use broad pressure rather than penetration.

## Sleeping System

Even in warmth, sleeping on wet vegetation will chill you.

Use:

- Closed-cell foam pad or insulated pad.
- Waterproof but breathable bivy or liner.
- Sleeping bag or quilt rated for damp conditions.
- Vapor barrier management: be careful not to trap sweat completely.
- A small overhead tarp or condensation shield if drips are constant.

Your sleeping system should include a dry bag for spare socks, filters, electronics, and medical supplies.

## Drying Gear

There is no fire. There may be no dry air.

You dry gear by:

- Absorption with microfiber cloths.
- Wringing inside sealed bags.
- Desiccant packs.
- Body heat for small items.
- Ventilation during rare less-humid intervals.
- Rotating wet and dry items.

Accept that some things will stay damp. Design your kit around function-while-damp.

# 9. Navigation and Communication

This place will defeat casual navigation.

The horizon is wide but softened. The light is even. Vegetation layers repeat. Fog tides erase landmarks. Distance is deceptive. Sound does not carry. Electronic signals may be attenuated by water and plant mass.

## Primary Rule: Tether Yourself

Unless you are in a clearly established safe zone, use a physical line.

- Spool line as you move.
- Anchor to the portal or base camp.
- Use removable, non-damaging markers if needed.
- Retrieve your line on return.

If you must mark a route, use loose loops, high-visibility removable tags, or infrared reflectors that you intend to collect later. Do not carve, paint, nail, or leave permanent markers.

## Instruments

Carry:

- Compass, if magnetic conditions are known.
- Inertial navigation unit if available.
- Barometer.
- Hygrometer.
- Thermometer.
- Timer or watch.
- Range finder if visibility allows.
- Low-power mapping device.
- Beacon or personal locator.

Do not rely on one system. Assume all systems can degrade.

## Communication

Use:

- Short-range radios with waterproof housings.
- Earpieces.
- External antennas if possible.
- Prearranged check-in intervals.
- Hand signals.
- Light signals.
- Physical tether tugs.

Do not assume voices carry. Sound is absorbed. A shout may die a few meters away.

If you use sound signals, avoid tapping on living roots. Root taps may be part of local communication. If you must make a signal, tap on your own pack frame, a synthetic container, or another inert object.

## Lighting

The dimness is gentle, but artificial light can be harsh.

Use:

- Headlamps with red or amber modes.
- Low output settings.
- Shielded beams pointed downward.
- Task lights instead of broad floodlights.
- Backup lights.
- Extra batteries in dry bags.

Avoid:

- Bright white beams into spore haze.
- Flashing lights near fauna.
- Leaving lights on unnecessarily.

Many organisms here are adapted to weak light. Sudden brightness is not just annoying; it may be ecologically violent.

# 10. Moving Through the Living Ground

Your movement style matters more than your equipment.

## Pace

Move slowly.

Not because the place is fragile in a sentimental sense, but because it is dense, responsive, and full of hidden hollows. Haste will cause injury, disturbance, and poor decisions.

A good pace is one where you can still hear your breath, feel your footing, and stop instantly.

## Foot Placement

Prefer:

- Dense moss carpets.
- Broad stable leaf surfaces if tested.
- Root platforms that feel firm.
- Existing open channels if shallow and clearly non-nested.
- Surfaces already compressed by animal paths, if this does not disturb active trails.

Avoid:

- Translucent bladders.
- Swollen water-stems.
- Thin leaf bridges.
- Hollow-sounding areas.
- Warm black pools.
- Areas with dense chemical trails.
- Nesting masses.
- Old built structures.
- Anything that looks like a membrane stretched over a void.

## Use Poles

Bring two collapsible poles with broad baskets or pads.

Use them to:

- Test footing.
- Distribute balance.
- Probe soft areas.
- Check water depth.
- Stabilize on slopes of plant matter.

Do not stab them into the ground. Use them as probes, not spikes.

## Weight Distribution

If the ground yields deeply:

- Use wide foot pads.
- Use crawling pads or lightweight platform mats.
- Kneel on broad pads if necessary.
- Spread pack weight low and centered.
- Avoid sudden shifts.

If the ground slowly rises under you, do not panic. That is normal. Shift weight, pause, and let it settle.

## Fog Tides

If fog moves in at knee height:

- Stop.
- Tighten group spacing.
- Use tether.
- Probe ahead.
- Avoid hollows.
- Wait if visibility becomes too poor.

Fog here is not merely visual. It can hide changes in ground tension, water depth, and animal movement.

## Group Movement

If traveling with others:

- Maintain visual or tether contact.
- Move single file where possible.
- Do not spread out unnecessarily.
- Confirm each transition: rest, movement, water collection, camp.
- Assign a rear guard to ensure nothing is dropped.
- Use buddy checks for mask seal, hydration, skin, and gear.

# 11. Interacting with Plants

The plants are not scenery. They are architecture, ground, vessel, sponge, and skin.

## Do Not Cut Unless Life Depends on It

Carry no machete, no saw, no axe. If you bring tools, they should be blunt, non-reactive, and used only for emergency rescue or repair.

Cutting here is not like cutting wood. You may release fluids, spores, scents, or defensive compounds. You may damage structural tissue that supports other growth. You may create a wound that becomes a water channel or infection site.

## Do Not Puncture Bladders

Translucent fluid bladders may look like water storage, but they are organs.

They may contain:

- Water.
- Sugary fluid.
- Defensive chemicals.
- Fermentation gases.
- Larval organisms.
- Waste products.
- Acidic or alkaline compounds.

If you accidentally puncture one:

- Avoid contact with the fluid.
- Do not inhale vapors closely.
- Back away.
- Decontaminate gear later.
- Do not taste, collect, or store the fluid unless under expert supervision.

## Do Not Harvest

Do not take leaves, stems, seeds, moss, or fluids as souvenirs.

If scientific sampling is authorized:

- Use sterile tools.
- Take minimal samples.
- Seal immediately.
- Record location and conditions.
- Pack out all waste.

## Do Not Bend Living Growth into Shelter

If you need shelter, use tension, suspension, and broad pressure. Do not train, tie, or bend plants into shape. Even gentle bending can have long consequences here because the landscape incorporates such changes for centuries.

# 12. Interacting with Fauna

Most animals here are slow, soft-bodied, and adapted to wet, dim conditions. That does not mean they are harmless. It means they are specialized.

## General Rules

- Do not chase.
- Do not grab.
- Do not feed.
- Do not touch transparent membranes.
- Do not pop floating bladder creatures.
- Do not disturb chemical trails.
- Do not use strong scented repellents.
- Do not make rhythmic tapping on living roots.
- Do not splash in warm pools.

## Pool-Striders and Surface Creatures

These may rely on surface tension. Do not break the water surface near them unnecessarily. Avoid oils, soaps, or chemicals on your boots or clothing that could contaminate surface films.

## Moss-Grazers

They may appear tolerant of movement, but do not follow or crowd them. Their feeding maintains surfaces. Disrupting them can have cascading effects.

## Root-Serpents and Channel Creatures

If you see long, low creatures moving through flooded channels:

- Give them space.
- Do not block their path.
- Do not attempt to cross directly above them.
- Do not touch gill-frills or soft tissues.
- If one approaches, stand still or retreat slowly.

Do not assume they are aggressive. Assume they are large, slow, and entitled to their route.

## Aerial Drifters

Bladder-bodied floaters may drift through fog trailing filaments.

- Do not swat them.
- Do not shine bright light directly into them.
- Do not walk into dense concentrations.
- If filaments touch you, avoid rubbing your eyes.
- Wash or wipe the contact area later with clean water if possible.

They may be fragile. Popping one could release gas, fluid, spores, or chemical signals.

## Large Hidden Creatures

Somewhere nearby, something large may move through a flooded channel with a sound like a mouth closing softly.

If you hear displacement:

- Stop.
- Identify direction.
- Move away from the water channel.
- Avoid sudden vibrations.
- Do not shout.
- Do not throw objects.
- Retreat to stable higher growth.

If you see it, do not approach. You are not here to document bravery.

## Chemical Trails

Many creatures rely on scent and chemical trails.

If you encounter a visible or olfactory trail:

- Do not walk along it.
- Do not urinate or defecate near it.
- Do not apply repellents nearby.
- Cross quickly and perpendicularly if unavoidable.
- Avoid dragging gear through it.

Your scent is a disruption. Keep it minimal.

# 13. Old Structures and Biological Architecture

You may see rings of fused stems, mounds of seed cases, hollowed trunks, smoothed passages, or woven tunnels.

These are not ruins in the human sense. They are old nests, old phases, composting intelligence, or instinctive architecture. They should be treated with restraint.

## Do Not Enter Unless Necessary

Old structures may be:

- Fragile.
- Occupied.
- Filled with spores.
- Filled with fermentation gases.
- Structurally unpredictable.
- Sacred in ways you cannot understand.

If you must inspect one, do so from outside if possible.

## Do Not Collect Artifacts

No seed cases. No sap mounds. No woven fragments. No polished interior pieces.

The landscape does not preserve relics. It composts them. You should not interrupt that.

## Do Not Attempt to Replicate Building

Unless survival requires it, do not bend, weave, stack, or shape living material. If a structure must be made, make it removable, non-penetrative, and minimal.

If you find evidence of past intelligence, do not romanticize it into human forms. It may have been cultivation, selection, pruning, slow shaping, or communal instinct. It may not have wanted cities. It may have wanted continuity.

# 14. Sanitation and Waste

This is critical.

The place composts quickly, but that does not mean it should receive your waste.

Human waste contains:

- Foreign microbes.
- Nutrients.
- Hormones.
- Medication residues.
- Chemical traces.
- Odors that may disturb fauna.

## Urine and Feces

Use:

- Portable waste bags.
- Sealed containers.
- Absorbent gel or solidifier.
- Odor-proof sacks.
- Double containment.

Do not bury waste. Do not deposit it in pools, channels, hollows, or root systems. Pack it out.

If a long-duration mission requires more complex sanitation, use a closed-loop system with sterilization and sealed storage.

## Greywater

Do not wash directly in pools or streams.

If you must wash:

- Use a sealed wash bag.
- Use minimal biodegradable soap.
- Capture the water.
- Pack it out.

Even biodegradable soap is not neutral here.

## Food Waste

Seal everything.

No crumbs. No peels. No wrappers. No discarded ration corners. No tea leaves. No coffee grounds. No chewed food. No “natural” scraps.

Foreign organic matter can introduce invasive microbes or alter nutrient cycles.

## Trash

Pack out:

- Wrappers.
- Filters.
- Used masks.
- Wipes.
- Bandages.
- Batteries.
- Desiccant packets.
- Broken gear.
- Shed fibers if recoverable.

Leave no relic.

# 15. Electronics and Power

Humidity is the enemy of electronics.

Assume everything will eventually fail unless protected.

## Protection

Use:

- Waterproof hard cases.
- Roll-top dry bags.
- Sealed pouches with desiccants.
- Humidity indicator cards.
- Conformal-coated devices if possible.
- Redundant systems.

Keep electronics close to your body if that helps stabilize temperature, but avoid trapping sweat against them.

## Power

Solar power will be unreliable due to dim light and haze.

Use:

- High-capacity batteries.
- Low-drain devices.
- Rechargeable cells in sealed cases.
- Backup power banks.
- Fuel cells only if they do not release heat, moisture, or exhaust into sensitive areas.

Avoid combustion. Fire is nearly impossible and ethically unacceptable.

## Lighting and Instruments

Prioritize:

- Headlamp with red mode.
- Small task light.
- Backup light.
- Beacon.
- Navigation tablet or e-ink device.
- Environmental sensors.

Avoid unnecessary bright screens. They drain power and disturb the visual ecology.

# 19. Food and Scent Discipline

This world is organized by scent, taste, vibration, and chemical trails.

Therefore:

- Use unscented hygiene products.
- Avoid perfumes.
- Avoid scented insect repellents unless medically necessary.
- Avoid strong foods.
- Store food in odor-proof containers.
- Seal waste immediately.
- Do not leave sweaty clothing exposed where odors disperse.
- Do not use chemical deterrents casually.

Your body will still smell human. That is unavoidable. But you should not amplify it.

If local fauna becomes interested in you, it may be because of salt, sweat, food residue, or exhaled carbon dioxide. Reduce attractants, withdraw calmly, and do not swat.

# 20. Psychological Preparation

The psychological challenge of this place is subtle.

It is not terrifying. It is not obviously hostile. But it is immense, dim, wet, crowded, quiet, and slow. It may make you feel small in a gentle way. That can be beautiful. It can also be destabilizing.

## Prepare for Time Distortion

The light does not change much. The weather accumulates. The sounds are small. There may be no day-night rhythm.

Use:

- A watch.
- Scheduled meals.
- Scheduled rest.
- Scheduled check-ins.
- Artificial day/night light in camp, kept dim and shielded.
- Journals.
- Objective distance logs.

Do not navigate by feeling. Feeling here is unreliable.

## Prepare for Intimacy Without Contact

The place is dense. Leaves brush leaves. Water skins touch water skins. Bodies pass through narrow gaps. Yet there is order.

You may feel watched, held, or surrounded. Remember: density is not aggression. Crowding is not attack. Stillness is not sleep.

## Prepare for Emotional Responses

Travelers may feel:

- Unexpected calm.
- Grief.
- Tenderness.
- Desire to remain.
- Fear of contamination.
- Irritation at slowness.
- A sense of being digested.
- A sense of being accepted.

None of these are proof of anything. Note them, share them with companions, and continue using objective safety checks.

## Practice Stillness Without Surrender

Learn to sit quietly without lying down. Learn to observe without touching. Learn to wait without fidgeting. Learn to breathe slowly through a mask.

The place will teach you patience whether you want to learn or not.

# 21. Biosecurity and Multiverse Ethics

Because this is a multiverse destination, contamination works both ways.

## Before Entry

Clean all gear:

- Brush soles.
- Remove seeds or fibers from previous worlds.
- Disinfect where appropriate.
- Use lint-free clothing.
- Avoid shedding materials.
- Sterilize sampling tools.
- Seal food and medical supplies.

Your goal is to arrive with as few foreign organisms as possible.

## During Entry

Do not:

- Spit.
- Dump water.
- Leave trash.
- Release breath directly into enclosed hollows if avoidable.
- Touch reproductive structures unnecessarily.
- Transfer soil or plant matter between regions.

## After Exit

Decontaminate thoroughly:

- Brush and vacuum gear in a controlled area.
- Seal contaminated clothing.
- Use UV or dry treatment if available.
- Inspect boots, filters, packs, and seams.
- Shower and inspect body.
- Rinse nasal passages and mouth with sterile fluids if recommended.
- Monitor health for days or weeks.

Do not bring souvenirs. Do not carry mud, water, seeds, spores, or fibers to another world.

# 22. Emergency Planning

Emergencies here are rarely dramatic. They become dangerous by accumulation.

## If You Are Lost

Stop.

Do not push forward.

- Anchor yourself if possible.
- Activate beacon.
- Check tether or route line.
- Consult instruments.
- Return along known path if clear.
- If no known path, stay put and conserve energy.
- Do not shout continuously; sound will be absorbed and you will exhaust yourself.
- Use short, repeated signals on an inert object if necessary.
- Use light signals only if visibility allows.

The worst response is to wander faster.

## If You Are Injured

- Stabilize injury.
- Seal wounds.
- Move to higher, stable ground.
- Set up shelter if evacuation is delayed.
- Keep filters dry.
- Keep communication open.
- Avoid entering water.
- Monitor for infection.

If you suspect altitude sickness, respiratory compromise, sepsis, heat exhaustion, or gas exposure, evacuate.

## If Weather Deepens

Remember: storms here accumulate.

Signs:

- Air feels heavier.
- Light dims evenly.
- Moisture increases on all surfaces.
- Drips become more frequent.
- Fog thickens.
- Barometric pressure falls.

Response:

- Move away from hollows.
- Avoid root channels.
- Camp on higher dense growth.
- Secure gear.
- Check anchor points.
- Prepare for prolonged saturation.

## If Equipment Fails

Assume redundancy.

- If water filter fails, use chemical purification.
- If mask fails, retreat to cleaner air or use emergency mask.
- If light fails, use backup and wait for safer conditions.
- If radio fails, use tether, beacon, or physical return line.
- If shelter fails, use emergency bivy and move to stable high point.

Do not improvise by damaging the environment unless life is immediately at risk.

## If You Encounter a Large Creature

- Freeze.
- Assess direction.
- Avoid eye contact if the creature seems sensitive.
- Do not splash.
- Do not run through water.
- Move sideways or backward slowly if possible.
- Give it a wide path.
- Do not attempt to photograph at close range.

Most likely, it will ignore you if you do not interfere.

## If You Feel Absorbed

This is a real hazard.

If you feel:

- Unusually peaceful.
- Unwilling to move.
- Sleepy in a warm wet place.
- Emotionally merged with the landscape.
- Indifferent to safety.

Then:

- Check oxygen.
- Check gas monitor.
- Check hydration.
- Check mask seal.
- Move to a more ventilated area.
- Speak aloud with a companion.
- Perform a physical task.
- Consider evacuation.

The place may feel like it is accepting you. Do not let that override your responsibility to leave.

# 25. How to Enter

If your multiverse transit system allows, use a staging threshold.

Before stepping through:

1. Check mask seal.
2. Check goggles.
3. Check boots and foot pads.
4. Check tether or beacon.
5. Check hydration.
6. Check oxygen monitor if carrying one.
7. Check that all waste containers are sealed.
8. Check that no food is exposed.
9. Set return time.
10. Record baseline vitals.

Enter slowly.

Do not stride.

Stand at the threshold and let your senses adjust. The air will taste green, tannic, faintly sweet. The ground will feel alive. The light will be dim and evenly distributed. The silence will have texture.

Take your first step as if placing a hand on the back of an old animal.

---

# 26. How to Move

Use this rhythm:

1. Probe.
2. Place foot.
3. Shift weight slowly.
4. Pause.
5. Breathe.
6. Observe.
7. Move.

Do not drag feet. Do not step on bladders. Do not step into warm black water unless necessary. Do not push through dense growth; find gaps. If a gap seems barely wide enough, ask whether you truly need to pass.

When moving through narrow spaces, keep your pack slim. Do not scrape sides. Do not tear hanging roots. Do not disturb filaments.

If you must kneel, use a pad. If you must sit, use a pad. Do not sit directly on living moss unless emergency requires it.

If you need to cross a channel:

- Probe depth.
- Look for creatures.
- Enter slowly.
- Keep your mask above water.
- Avoid stirring sediment.
- Exit slowly.
- Inspect boots and clothing afterward.

If you see a path made by animals, do not assume it is yours. It may be a chemical highway. Cross, do not follow, unless following would cause less disturbance than forging new ground.

# 27. How to Rest

Rest before you are exhausted.

Choose a high, stable spot. Check overhead for dripping bladders or heavy stems. Check below for hollows. Spread a pad or sling.

While resting:

- Loosen boots if safe.
- Dry feet.
- Change socks.
- Drink.
- Eat a small amount.
- Check mask filters.
- Check electronics.
- Record observations.
- Set a timer.

Do not nap without a timer unless camp is secure. The environment can lull you into stillness.

When sleeping, assign watches if in a group. Keep the camp quiet. Keep lights low. Keep waste sealed. Keep boots close but not touching living surfaces unnecessarily.

# 28. How to Observe

Observation here should be close and patient.

Look for:

- Water movement along root channels.
- Drips delayed by leaf hairs.
- Tiny winged bodies in spore haze.
- Seed vessels drifting.
- Surface dimples made by pool-striders.
- Changes in water film tension.
- Moss-grazed patches.
- Old composted structures.
- Faint chemical trails.
- Root taps or vibrations.

Do not assume you understand what you are seeing. Many actions will be slow, indirect, and distributed. A plant may respond to you over hours. A creature may communicate over chemical persistence. A structure may be meaningful not because it stands, but because it is being digested.

Use notes, sketches, and photographs. Use words carefully. Avoid imposing human categories too quickly. “Road,” “ceiling,” “wall,” “nest,” “ruin” are metaphors, not conclusions.

# 29. How to Leave

Leaving is as important as entering.

Before departure:

1. Sweep the camp.
2. Retrieve all markers.
3. Retrieve tether line.
4. Pack all waste.
5. Seal wet gear.
6. Check for dropped items.
7. Check for inadvertently collected organisms in clothing or packs.
8. Confirm no food remains exposed.
9. Confirm no structures were bent or damaged.
10. Record final location and condition.

Do not leave biodegradable items just because they are biodegradable. Biodegradation here may be too fast, too slow, or ecologically disruptive.

At the threshold, decontaminate in order:

- Remove outer layer carefully.
- Brush or vacuum debris in controlled containment.
- Seal contaminated clothing.
- Clean boots.
- Clean gear.
- Remove mask last if the staging area is not yet clean.
- Wash hands and face.
- Inspect skin.
- Hydrate.
- Rest.

After returning, monitor yourself for:

- Cough.
- Wheeze.
- Fever.
- Rash.
- Eye irritation.
- Sinus pressure.
- Unusual fatigue.
- Skin infection.
- Digestive symptoms.
- Mood changes.

Report any unusual biological exposure according to your multiverse travel protocol.

Do not attempt to cultivate local plants unless authorized and expertly guided. Do not attempt to domesticate fauna. Do not attempt to make a permanent dwelling. The landscape does not keep relics. It composts them.

If you stay long enough, you will be tempted to build, name, mark, or settle. Resist this unless your mission explicitly requires it and you have permission to accept the ecological consequences.

# 31. The Deepest Preparation

The deepest preparation is not gear.

It is learning to be a small, temporary presence in a place that does not need you.

This location is ancient, wet, warm, crowded, and complete. It has no interest in your speed. It has no need for your fire. It does not require your monuments. It does not preserve your names. It will accept your footprints and close them. It will accept your breath and dilute it. It will accept your departure and continue.

Prepare by becoming quieter.

Prepare by noticing smaller things.

Prepare by accepting that your body is also a wet, breathing, porous thing. You are not separate from the environment in the way you may be accustomed to. Here, the boundary between body and world is thinner. You must maintain that boundary carefully: through filters, sealed waste, gentle movement, and disciplined leaving. But you must also respect the fact that you are entering a system where life is layered, intimate, and continuous.

Bring humility.

Bring patience.

Bring dry socks.

Bring filters.

Bring a tether.

Bring enough water to begin.

Bring enough silence to listen.

And when you stand in that greenish twilight, with moisture on your lips and warmth on your skin, and the ground rises slowly under your weight, remember:

> You are not visiting a landscape.  
> You are briefly allowed inside an old living body.  
> Move gently.  
> Breathe carefully.  
> Leave nothing.  
> Let it remain complete without you.
