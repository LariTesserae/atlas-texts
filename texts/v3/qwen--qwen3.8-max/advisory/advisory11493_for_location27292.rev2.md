The first preparation is not packing. It is subtraction.

This is not a place one equips for as if it lacked something. It lacks nothing essential. It is already complete: dry, warm, dark, still, ancient, and self-contained. The traveler is the anomaly. An ordinary human body brings exactly what the hollow does not have: water, heat, breath, motion, scent, sound, shed skin, dust, urgency, and the habit of making things. To visit well, you must prepare to become less like an exploring animal and more like a temporary mineral presence — quiet, sealed, self-contained, and briefly admitted.

The main danger is not that the place will attack you. It will not. It does not threaten. The main danger is that your presence will change it, and that your body will underestimate the cost of being alive in a room-sized geology where nothing drinks, nothing moves, nothing is made, and nothing has happened for a very long time.

---

## 1. Understand the Law of the Place

Before preparing gear, prepare attitude.

The hollow has a set of implicit rules:

- **No water.**  
  Not merely absent as drink, but absent as a principle. No dampness, no mist, no seep, no memory of rain. Your breath, sweat, tears, saliva, urine, blood, and spilled water are all violations of the local order.

- **No animals.**  
  There are no tracks, bones, shells, insects, nests, burrows, or droppings. You will be the only heartbeat. That is not a romantic detail. It is a contamination risk and a psychological weight.

- **No artifacts.**  
  No built things. No paths, markers, walls, carvings, hearths, pits, or arranged stones. The place is pre-artifact. You must not become the first maker there.

- **No weather.**  
  No wind, no season, no day-night cycle, no rain, no cooling draft. Your arrival should not introduce weather. No gust from a portal, no fan, no heat plume, no stirring dust if it can be avoided.

- **No urgent change.**  
  The terrain is finished. The erosion is mature. The forms are settled. The beauty is in restraint. You should not arrive with the human instinct to improve, arrange, collect, mark, test, or “make use” of the place.

If you cannot accept those constraints, you are not ready to enter.

## 2. Decide Whether You Should Enter at All

For many travelers, the correct preparation is to decide not to go into the central basin.

The hollow is small enough that its presence can be sensed from the rim. Its darkness, warmth, stillness, and enclosure can be known without crossing the floor. If your purpose is contemplation, reverence, aesthetic experience, or simple witness, the rim may be enough.

Enter only if there is a reason that justifies disturbance:

- a scientific question that cannot be answered from outside;
- a personal or spiritual necessity that you can articulate clearly;
- a multiverse-travel obligation that cannot be fulfilled remotely;
- a need to experience the place directly that you are willing to balance against the trace you leave.

Even a footprint is a change. In ordinary dust, a footprint may last. In this place, where nothing rearranges the grains, it may last longer than it should. The ethical visitor asks: *What is the minimum presence that fulfills my purpose?*

For most ordinary human travelers, the answer is: one person, short duration, sealed body, no light unless necessary, no touching, no sampling, no speaking, no leaving anything behind.

## 3. Prepare the Body Before the Equipment

Because the environment is mild but severe in its dryness, your body must be stabilized before entry.

### Hydrate outside, not inside

There is no water in the hollow, and you must not bring open water into it. But dry air can dehydrate you invisibly. You lose moisture through breath, skin, and eyes even when you do not notice it.

Before entering:

- drink enough water over several hours, not all at once;
- include electrolytes if you have them;
- avoid alcohol, excessive caffeine, or anything that makes you dry-mouthed or unsteady;
- empty your bladder and bowels before entry;
- make sure you are not entering thirsty, hungry, or overheated.

If you need to drink during the visit, the visit is too long.

### Rest well

The place does not punish fatigue dramatically, but fatigue makes you careless. Carelessness means stumbling, touching a wall, dropping something, breathing harder, sweating more, or wanting to sit down. All of those are problems.

### Avoid illness

Do not enter with a cough, cold, sinus congestion, allergies, stomach trouble, or anything that may cause sudden sneezing, coughing, spitting, vomiting, or bodily urgency. In a sealed suit, these are manageable but unpleasant. Without one, they are contamination events.

### Eat beforehand, but lightly

Do not eat inside. No crumbs, no wrappers, no food smells, no organic particles. Eat a light, stable meal before entry. Avoid gas-producing or heavy foods if you are prone to discomfort.

### Calm the nervous system

This place is not frightening in the usual sense, but it is sensorially unusual. Darkness, silence, warmth, and absence of movement can produce strange inner effects. Some people feel peaceful. Some feel oppressed. Some feel watched even though nothing is there. Some feel an urge to speak, laugh, or touch things just to prove they exist.

Before entering, spend several minutes doing slow breathing. Do not try to “conquer” the place. Do not try to fill it. Let your inner tempo slow toward its tempo.

A useful mental preparation is this:

> I am entering a place that does not need me.  
> I will not make it more like me.  
> I will observe, remain whole, and leave.

## 4. Clothing: Become a Closed System

The ideal visitor is almost entirely covered. Not because the environment is hostile, but because the human body is hostile to the environment by contrast.

### Cover all skin

You shed skin constantly. You emit moisture, salts, oils, heat, and scent. You carry microbes. In a dry mineral basin with sparse chemically based growth, these are pollutants.

Wear:

- a full-body suit if possible;
- long sleeves and long legs at minimum;
- gloves;
- a hood or head covering;
- socks;
- closed footwear;
- a mask or breathing interface;
- eye protection if the air is dusty.

The goal is to leave nothing of yourself behind.

### Choose lint-free, low-shedding materials

Avoid:

- wool;
- old cotton;
- fleece;
- frayed fabric;
- loose threads;
- paper;
- untreated leather;
- fibrous insulation that sheds.

Use smooth, tightly woven or sealed synthetic materials if available. The suit should not shed fibers into the air.

### Avoid scents

Do not use:

- perfume;
- cologne;
- scented soap;
- scented lotion;
- strongly scented sunscreen;
- aromatic oils;
- deodorants with strong residue;
- incense smoke;
- food smells on clothing.

The air there is described as clean in the most severe way. You should not import a human smell.

### No loose objects

Remove or secure:

- jewelry;
- keys;
- coins;
- loose tools;
- dangling straps;
- scarves;
- hats that can fall;
- pens not clipped;
- bags that swing.

Anything that can drop will eventually drop. In this place, a dropped object becomes an artifact.

### Silence your clothing

Clothing should not rustle loudly. Avoid stiff fabric that scrapes. Avoid metal zippers that jingle. If possible, use silent closures, covered snaps, or sealed fasteners. Velcro can be loud and may shed fibers; avoid if possible.

### Manage sweat

The hollow is warm and still. If you exert yourself, you will sweat. Sweat is water, salt, and organic matter. It also makes you uncomfortable and may fog eye protection.

Wear moisture-wicking underlayers inside a sealed outer layer if you have them. If you have a climate-controlled suit, use a closed heat-exchange or moisture-capture system rather than venting humid air outward.

Do not enter if you are already overheating.

## 5. Footwear: Protect Yourself Without Marking the Floor

The floor is one of the most important parts of the place. It is granular, powdery, crystalline, crusty in places, scattered with stones, and possibly fragile in its surface arrangements. Walking is the most destructive ordinary act you can perform there.

### Soft, clean, thin-but-protective soles are best

You want to feel the ground because sight will not guide you well. But you also need protection from sharp flakes and enough surface area to avoid breaking delicate crusts.

Good footwear should be:

- quiet;
- flexible;
- grippy but not sticky;
- non-marking;
- free of embedded dirt from other worlds;
- smooth-edged;
- with enough thin sensitivity to feel changes underfoot.

Avoid heavy boots with aggressive treads if possible. They can dig, scrape, and carry foreign particles.

### Consider boot covers

If you must wear sturdier boots, cover them with clean, soft, lint-free overshoes. Make sure they do not shed.

### Move as if the floor is a record you are not supposed to scratch

When walking:

- place the foot gently;
- roll from outer edge to full sole if the surface seems stable;
- do not drag;
- do not pivot sharply;
- do not push off hard;
- do not scuff;
- do not stamp;
- do not kick stones;
- do not step on visible growths or pale crusts;
- if possible, choose already stony or crusted ground over fine powder.

If you cannot walk without leaving deep prints or disturbing dust, you should not go far inside.

### Consider whether walking is necessary

For scientific or contemplative visits, ask whether you can remain at the edge. The hollow is room-sized. It may be possible to experience much of it without crossing it.

If you must move, choose a short route and do not wander.

## 6. Breathing: Do Not Give the Place Your Water

Breathing is a major issue.

The air may be breathable. It may not be. Even if it is breathable, it is mineral-dry and still. Breathing it directly may dry your throat and lungs. More importantly, your exhaled breath adds water vapor, carbon dioxide, warmth, microbes, and organic particles.

### Test the atmosphere first

If you have a multiverse traveler’s atmospheric kit, test:

- oxygen level;
- carbon dioxide level;
- pressure;
- toxic gases;
- heavy gases that may pool in the low basin;
- particulate concentration;
- temperature;
- humidity;
- any unusual chemical gradient near the floor.

Because the basin is low and still, heavier-than-air gases could collect near the floor even if the upper air seems acceptable. Test at breathing height and near the ground before entering.

### Best option: closed-loop breathing

The ideal solution is a small rebreather or sealed breathing mask with:

- oxygen supply;
- carbon dioxide scrubber;
- humidity trap;
- particulate filter;
- quiet operation;
- backup supply.

This keeps your breath inside your system instead of releasing it into the hollow.

### Minimum acceptable option

If you lack a closed-loop system, use at least:

- a high-quality particulate mask;
- a moisture-exchanging or humidifying component if available;
- a separate clean air supply if the atmosphere is uncertain.

Do not breathe openly if you can avoid it. Open breathing adds moisture and CO₂ to a place with no air movement.

### Do not remove your mask to “taste” the air

You may be tempted. The air is described as clean and severe. But tasting it means drying your mouth and adding water to the environment. It also means inhaling fine mineral dust.

If you must sample the air, use an instrument.

## 7. Water Discipline: The Most Important Rule

There is no water in the hollow. That is not just a description; it is a boundary.

You must treat all water as a contaminant while inside.

### Carry water only in sealed form

If you bring water, it should be:

- in a sealed bladder or bottle;
- inside your suit or pack;
- inaccessible unless you leave the basin;
- equipped with a bite valve or internal drinking tube if absolutely necessary.

Do not open a water container inside.

### Do not drink openly inside

Drinking openly risks spills, droplets, breath moisture, and the psychological association of the place with human need. If you are thirsty enough to drink, exit first.

### Protect against accidental water release

This includes:

- sweat;
- condensation;
- leaked hydration tubes;
- fogged goggles releasing droplets;
- spit;
- tears;
- blood;
- urine;
- nasal discharge.

All of these are forms of water and organic contamination.

### If water is spilled

This should be prevented, because remediation is almost impossible without making things worse. Do not use absorbent cloths that shed fibers. Do not blow on the dust. Do not scrape broadly. If a tiny spill occurs and you have sterile dry containment tools, collect it carefully and remove all material with you. But the best plan is never to allow the spill.

## 8. Light: Use It as a Last Resort

The darkness is not an inconvenience to be corrected. It is one of the materials of the place.

If you bring bright light, you will likely make the basin feel smaller, harsher, and interrupted. The description says a light would not make the place feel illuminated; it would make it feel violated. That is a good intuition to respect.

### Prefer passive sensing if possible

If you have access to equipment, prefer:

- passive thermal imaging;
- low-light amplification only if some photons exist;
- memory-based navigation;
- pre-mapped routes;
- non-emissive spatial awareness tools.

Thermal imaging may be useful because the place has subtle warmth differences. But do not assume it will show sharp edges if everything is nearly the same temperature.

### If you must use visible light

Use:

- very dim light;
- deep red or amber if possible;
- downward-shielded beam;
- short bursts;
- only when necessary for safety;
- never pointed at growths longer than needed;
- never sweeping casually around as if you are in a museum.

Do not use:

- flash photography;
- white floodlights;
- blue-rich light;
- strobing lights;
- laser pointers;
- decorative illumination;
- headlamps left on while you stand still.

### Practice moving without light

Before entering, practice in a safe dark space:

- walking slowly;
- sensing slope through the feet;
- keeping balance without visual reference;
- noticing air pressure and proximity;
- resisting the panic of not seeing.

The basin is not large, but darkness can make any enclosed space feel infinite. You need to learn to be in darkness without demanding that it reveal itself.

## 9. Sound Discipline: Do Not Fill the Silence

The silence is not empty. It is substantial.

Your voice, breathing, footsteps, equipment, and heartbeat may feel absurdly loud there. Sound is rare in that place. When it occurs, it does not blend; it stands out.

### Do not speak

Speech introduces vibration, breath, moisture, heat, and human urgency. If you are with others, use prearranged silent signals.

Possible signals:

- one tap on the shoulder: stop;
- two taps: proceed;
- three taps: return;
- hand on wrist: check time;
- gentle touch to pack: check equipment;
- vibration codes if you have them.

If you must communicate, use a sealed text device or bone-conduction system at the lowest useful volume. Even that should be minimized.

### Silence your gear

Pad or secure anything that might:

- clink;
- scrape;
- rattle;
- beep;
- buzz loudly;
- flap;
- squeak.

Turn off unnecessary alarms. Use vibration instead of sound. If a timer is needed, make it silent.

### Listen, but do not demand sounds

You may hear tiny grain movements, mineral settling, or your own body. Do not try to produce sounds to test the space. Do not clap, whisper, snap, or knock on stone. The place does not need your echo.

## 10. Navigation: The Place Has No Built Landmarks

There are no paths, markers, doors, walls, or constructed reference points. The basin is enclosed, but in darkness, enclosure can be disorienting.

### Prepare before entering

Before you cross the rim:

- note your entry point;
- count your paces;
- memorize the orientation of the slope;
- identify the direction of exit by body sense;
- if possible, record the route with inertial instruments.

Do not rely on celestial navigation. There is no useful sky-light. Do not rely on permanent landmarks inside unless you can sense them without altering them.

### Use non-marking navigation

Good methods:

- dead reckoning;
- pace counting;
- inertial mapping;
- body orientation;
- subtle slope awareness;
- memory of floor texture;
- temperature differences if detectable;
- proximity sense to the rim.

Bad methods:

- leaving flags;
- tying cord to stone;
- chalking marks;
- stacking pebbles;
- scratching arrows;
- dropping beads;
- placing beacons that remain.

If you use temporary beacons, they must be carried in and carried out. Better not to need them.

### Keep the exit simple

Because the basin is low and the descent subtle, exiting should not require climbing. But in darkness, a subtle slope can be hard to find. Decide in advance how many steps you will take inward before turning back. Do not depend on curiosity to guide you deeper.

A useful rule:

> If you are not certain where the exit is, you have already gone too far.

## 11. Time and Multiverse Orientation

The place feels time-invariant. No day or night changes it. No season reaches it. It seems to exist outside sequence. That can be psychologically destabilizing.

### Carry a personal timekeeper

Use a silent watch, internal suit timer, or vibration interval device. Do not rely on environmental cues. There are none.

### Set a maximum stay

For an ordinary human, a short visit is best. Minutes rather than hours, unless your life-support and psychological preparation are exceptional.

Possible limits:

- first visit: five to ten minutes;
- experienced contemplative visit: twenty to thirty minutes;
- scientific visit: only as long as necessary;
- no casual lingering.

The place may feel calm enough that you do not want to leave. That is a warning sign.

### Multiverse return anchor

If you travel between universes, make sure your return method is:

- body-centered;
- not dependent on local landmarks;
- not requiring you to attach anything to the environment;
- tested before entry;
- backed up if possible.

Do not anchor to a stone, growth, or rim feature. Do not carve a sigil. Do not place a beacon unless it leaves with you.

### Expect time distortion

Because the place does not change, your sense of duration may drift. You may feel that only seconds have passed when your body has been there much longer, or vice versa. Check your timer calmly. Do not panic. Do not run.

## 12. Equipment: Bring Only What Is Necessary

The place suggests primitive making, but you must not answer that suggestion. Bring finished, sealed, quiet tools. Do not improvise from local materials.

### Minimal personal kit

A sensible traveler might carry:

- sealed breathing system;
- backup oxygen or air;
- atmospheric analyzer;
- silent timer;
- dim shielded light;
- passive thermal or spatial sensor;
- navigation unit;
- communication device;
- small repair kit;
- sealed hydration system for use outside;
- waste containment if the visit may be long;
- medical basics inside sealed pouches.

Everything should be attached to you. Nothing should be loose.

### What not to bring

Do not bring:

- paper;
- notebooks;
- charcoal;
- chalk;
- pencils that shed;
- paint;
- markers;
- flags;
- string for leaving behind;
- adhesive tape;
- glue;
- food;
- open water;
- plants;
- animals;
- drones with exposed propellers;
- loud speakers;
- candles;
- matches;
- lighters;
- incense;
- musical instruments;
- decorative objects;
- souvenirs bags;
- collection jars unless scientifically essential.

### Avoid combustion entirely

No flames. No sparks. No smoking. No burning incense. Combustion produces heat, CO₂, water vapor, particles, and smell. All are wrong for this place.

### Avoid fans

Fans stir dust, create sound, and introduce artificial wind. The air there should remain still.

### Use sealed electronics

Electronics should be:

- low-heat;
- silent;
- sealed against dust;
- non-sparking;
- low-outgassing;
- shielded if they emit signals.

Do not assume devices will behave normally in a multiverse location with unusual stillness or mineral composition. Test them beforehand.

## 13. Protect the Sparse Growth

The blackish filaments, pale crusts, bone-colored patches, and dry leafless fiber-like growths are not ordinary plants. They seem to live at the mineral margin, drawing from warmth, trace gases, and chemical gradients. Their life is extremely slow. They are not lush, soft, or resilient.

### Do not touch them

Touching them may:

- break them;
- crush crusts;
- transfer oils;
- add moisture;
- introduce microbes;
- change local chemistry;
- leave fibers;
- alter temperature locally.

Even the warmth of your hand could matter over a long timescale.

### Keep distance

Do not lean over them. Do not breathe near them. Do not illuminate them longer than necessary. If you must study them, use passive sensors from a distance.

### Do not harvest

The fibrous growths might look like cordage. The hollow stems might look useful. The crusts might look collectible. This is exactly the temptation the place warns against.

Do not harvest.

The place contains the possibility of primitive making, but the ethical visitor refuses to actualize it.

## 14. Do Not Make Anything

This is crucial.

The location contains naturally keen stones, brittle flakes, hollow stems, shallow bowls, flat surfaces, balanced fragments. It suggests first tools. It feels pre-artifact. A human mind may instinctively think:

- “This flake could cut.”
- “This stone could be struck.”
- “This stem could be a probe.”
- “This bowl could hold something.”
- “These stones could be stacked.”
- “This dust could be drawn in.”
- “I could leave a mark.”

Do not.

Nothing here has been worked. Nothing here has been improved. Nothing here has been left behind. You should maintain that condition.

### If equipment fails

Do not replace it with local materials unless life is in immediate danger. If a strap breaks, use what you brought. If a tool breaks, carry the pieces out. Do not chip stone. Do not twist fibers. Do not make a cord. Do not scrape a mark.

### If you are an artist

This place may move you deeply. But do not draw with local dust, scratch stone, arrange pebbles, or leave offerings. If you need to make art, make it later, elsewhere, from memory.

### If you feel spiritual

Do not create a shrine, cairn, altar, or offering. The place is not asking for worship. It is asking to remain itself.

## 15. Psychological Preparation: Learn to Be Unneeded

The hollow may produce unusual mental states.

It is not hostile, but it is indifferent. It does not generate presence. It does not feel haunted. It does not feel watched. It simply is. For many humans, that is difficult. We are accustomed to environments responding to us: wind, birds, light, weather, other minds. Here, there is almost none of that.

### Expect sensory hunger

Your brain may seek patterns:

- shapes in the dark;
- imagined sounds;
- a sense of presence;
- the feeling of being observed;
- the urge to speak;
- the urge to laugh;
- the urge to touch your face;
- the urge to make a mark.

Recognize these as neurological responses to low stimulus, not truths about the place.

### Practice stillness

Before visiting, sit in a dark, quiet room without moving for thirty minutes. Notice your impulses. Notice how often you want to adjust, scratch, sigh, open your eyes, check the time, or make noise.

The basin will magnify those impulses.

### Bring humility, not courage

Courage is useful in dangerous places. Here, humility is more important. You are not conquering the hollow. You are asking temporary admission to its stillness.

A good inner phrase:

> I do not need this place to respond to me.

### Beware of excessive calm

The warmth and silence may feel soothing. You may feel safe enough to stay too long. Do not let comfort override your timer. The place does not remind you of your needs. You must remember them yourself.

## 16. Entering the Hollow: A Protocol

If you have decided to enter, do so deliberately.

### Before crossing the rim

- Check suit integrity.
- Check breathing system.
- Check water containment.
- Check that nothing is loose.
- Confirm exit plan.
- Start silent timer.
- Take several slow breaths.
- Do not speak.

### Cross the rim slowly

There is no cliff, only a subtle downward gathering. Do not rush inward. Step across the boundary as if entering a room where someone is sleeping.

### Pause immediately after entering

Stand still for at least a minute. Let your body settle. Let your breathing stabilize. Let your eyes accept darkness. Let your ears adjust to silence.

Feel:

- the warmth through your soles;
- the slight slope;
- the pressure of enclosure;
- the nearness of the rims;
- the absence of air movement.

Do not begin moving until you feel calm and oriented.

### Move only as much as necessary

If your purpose can be fulfilled where you stand, fulfill it there.

If you must move:

- take short steps;
- keep your center of gravity low;
- avoid dragging feet;
- keep hands near your body;
- do not reach out to steady yourself unless absolutely necessary;
- do not touch walls or stones casually;
- do not approach growths;
- do not turn quickly;
- do not look around with light unless needed.

### If you feel disoriented

Stop. Do not run. Do not call out. Check your timer and navigation. Breathe slowly. If you cannot recover orientation, begin the exit using your memorized route. If necessary, use a brief, downward, dim light only to avoid damaging something.

## 17. While Inside: What to Do and What Not to Do

### Do

- Observe quietly.
- Monitor your breathing.
- Monitor your time.
- Move slowly.
- Keep your body sealed.
- Keep your attention wide rather than grasping.
- Notice warmth, texture, proximity, silence, and form.
- Leave the place exactly as it is.

### Do not

- Speak loudly.
- Remove mask or gloves.
- Eat or drink.
- Spill anything.
- Touch growths.
- Collect stones.
- Scratch surfaces.
- Stack rocks.
- Leave markers.
- Sit or lie down unless unavoidable.
- Use bright light.
- Make fire.
- Create wind.
- Run.
- Stay too long.

### If you must sit

Do not sit unless necessary. Sitting spreads your weight over a larger area, crushes surface crusts, transfers heat and moisture, and leaves a large imprint. If you are exhausted, leave.

If sitting is unavoidable due to injury or emergency, choose the least sensitive surface: bare rock or already disturbed stone, not powder, crust, or growth. But understand that this is a compromise.

## 18. Sampling: Generally, Do Not

The best default is no sampling.

The place is nearly closed, nearly motionless, balanced on the edge of mere geology. Removing even a small stone, flake, grain, or filament changes the composition. It also creates a wound in a landscape that has finished becoming.

### If sampling is absolutely necessary

Only then should you consider it, and only with strict protocols:

- take the smallest possible amount;
- avoid living crusts and filaments unless the purpose specifically requires them and you have ethical clearance;
- use sterile, sealed tools;
- avoid creating new fracture surfaces if possible;
- document the exact position before removal;
- replace disturbed grains as faithfully as possible;
- carry all samples out in sealed containers;
- do not leave tools, marks, or residues;
- record only what is necessary.

But for an ordinary traveler, the answer should usually be: witness, do not take.

## 19. Emergency Preparation

The place is gentle, but emergencies can still happen.

### If your breathing system fails

Leave immediately. Do not stop to observe. Do not attempt to breathe the ambient air unless you have already verified it is safe. Use backup air.

### If you become dizzy

Exit. Dizziness may come from heat, dehydration, CO₂ buildup, oxygen lack, panic, or sensory deprivation. Do not sit down in the basin if you can avoid it. Move slowly but decisively toward the exit.

### If you injure yourself

Protect your blood. Blood is water, salt, and organic material. If you cut yourself, contain it inside your suit or with sealed dressings. Do not let drops fall. Exit as soon as possible.

Do not use local stone as a tool or bandage. Do not make a splint from local growth. If you are trapped, survival comes first, but every improvisation should be temporary and all materials should leave with you if possible.

### If you become lost

Stop. Do not wander. Use your navigation system, pace memory, or slope awareness. If you have a return anchor, activate it only if leaving the basin is safe and will not cause a disruptive transition inside the hollow.

If a multiverse portal must be opened, open it only if it will not blast air, light, heat, or dust into the basin. If possible, exit by walking out the way you came.

### If equipment falls

Pick it up carefully. Do not kick it. Do not leave it. If it breaks into fragments, collect all fragments. If fragments scatter into dust, collect as much as possible without creating wider disturbance.

### If you spill water or biological fluid

Contain and remove what you can. Do not spread it. Do not attempt to wash it with more water. Do not leave contaminated material behind. This is why prevention matters more than cleanup.

## 20. Leaving the Hollow

Departure should be as careful as arrival.

### Leave before you are tired

Do not wait until you are bored, restless, or distracted. The moment you begin wanting to hurry, you are more likely to damage something.

### Check yourself before exiting

- Are all items secured?
- Is your mask sealed?
- Is your hydration closed?
- Are you carrying everything you brought?
- Have you left any fibers, wrappers, tools, or marks?
- Is there any moisture on your suit?
- Are you bleeding?
- Are you overheated?

### Exit slowly

Do not rush upward or outward. The rim is subtle. Move with the same care you used entering.

### Once outside

Only when you are fully outside and away from the basin should you:

- remove breathing equipment if the outside air is safe;
- drink water;
- cool down;
- speak;
- stretch;
- check your body;
- review your equipment;
- record notes.

Do not shake dust from your clothing near the basin. Do not empty pockets there. Do not spit there. Do not pour water there.

## 21. Aftercare and Contamination Control

The visit does not end when you leave the hollow.

### Rehydrate carefully

Drink water outside. Use electrolytes if needed. If your airways feel dry, use humidified air or saline if available. Do not overdrink all at once.

### Check skin and eyes

Dry air can irritate eyes, lips, nasal passages, and skin. Look for cracks, redness, or abrasions. If you were exposed to mineral dust, rinse outside the sensitive area using controlled water that is collected and disposed of properly. Do not let wash water enter the basin or any protected environment.

### Clean your gear away from the site

Dust from the hollow may be chemically unusual. It may contain fine crystalline particles. Do not inhale it later. Clean or seal your gear using methods appropriate for dry mineral dust. Avoid blowing it around.

If you travel between universes, you must prevent carrying dust from this world into another. Seal outer clothing, boots, and equipment before crossing elsewhere.

### Psychological aftercare

The place may leave you with a strange emotional residue: calm, emptiness, grief, reverence, or restlessness. It is not necessarily trauma. It may be the effect of encountering a landscape that does not need you.

Talk about the experience if you need to. Rest. Eat. Drink. Return gradually to ordinary sensory life. Do not rush back in immediately.

---

## 22. Special Note for Groups

This is not a place for crowds.

If more than one person enters, the contamination, heat, sound, and disturbance multiply. The density of presences in the basin is naturally low. A group violates that composition.

### If a group is unavoidable

- send only one person inside at a time;
- others wait outside the basin;
- no talking across the rim;
- no group equipment left near the edge;
- no simultaneous entry.

If the purpose is shared, share it afterward through memory or sealed records, not by crowding the hollow.

---

## 23. Special Note for Scientific Visitors

The place is scientifically tempting because it is almost a closed mineral system with sparse chemically based life and no animal ecology. But that is precisely why it must be approached with extreme restraint.

### Prioritize non-contact observation

Use:

- passive spectroscopy;
- thermal imaging;
- chemical sniffers that do not vent;
- micro-particulate sampling only if essential;
- remote sensing from the rim;
- time-lapse observation from outside if possible.

### Avoid experiments that alter conditions

Do not:

- add water;
- add nutrients;
- introduce microbes;
- heat a growth to see response;
- scrape crust;
- change gas concentrations;
- leave sensors;
- create vibrations;
- illuminate growths for long periods.

The life there is slow beyond comparison. An experiment that seems brief to you may be a violent event in its timescale.

### If you must intervene

Make the intervention reversible if possible. If it is not reversible, reconsider whether the knowledge is worth the damage.

## 24. What Visiting Will Feel Like

If you prepare well, the visit may feel less like exploration and more like being briefly admitted into a finished room of stone.

The dark will press evenly, not heavy, not malicious. The floor will be warm through your soles. The air, if sampled properly, will feel clean to the point of severity. You will not see much. You will know the basin by nearness: the subtle hollow underfoot, the suggestion of a rim, the occasional presence of a block, the difference between smooth crust and grainy floor.

You may hear almost nothing. Then, perhaps, a tiny tick of mineral adjustment, a grain slipping, a settling so faint it might be your own body. The silence will seem larger because of it.

You may feel neither watched nor alone. The place does not generate presence. It simply continues. Your heartbeat may feel absurdly loud. Your breath may feel like an intrusion. Your warmth may feel excessive.

That is correct.

The beauty of the place is not scenic. It is the beauty of restraint: worn roundness, sparse forms, measured distances, completed erosion, emptiness allowed to remain empty. You will not want to applaud. You may not want to speak afterward. You may feel that words are too quick, too human, too eager.

If you are ready, the experience will teach you something rare: how to be present without making the world respond.

- [ ] Hydrated outside.

- [ ] Bladder and bowels empty.

- [ ] All loose items secured.

- [ ] Lint-free, low-shedding suit.
- [ ] Gloves.
- [ ] Hood or head covering.
- [ ] Soft, clean, non-marking footwear.
- [ ] Mask or breathing interface.
- [ ] Eye protection if dusty.
- [ ] No jewelry.
- [ ] No dangling straps.

- [ ] Atmospheric analyzer tested.
- [ ] Oxygen/air supply adequate.
- [ ] Backup breathing available.
- [ ] CO₂ scrubber functioning.
- [ ] Humidity capture functioning.
- [ ] Suit sealed.
- [ ] Cooling system or heat management ready.

- [ ] Entry direction memorized.
- [ ] Pace count planned.

- [ ] Do not touch growths.

## 26. The Visitor’s Vow

If you want a single preparation to carry into the place, carry this:

> I will bring no water where there is none.  
> I will bring no sound where silence is substance.  
> I will bring no making where nothing has been worked.  
> I will touch what does not need touching.  
> I will leave no trace I can avoid.  
> I will stay only as long as my purpose requires.  
> I will let the hollow remain finished.

That is how an ordinary human multiverse traveler should prepare: not by adding themselves to the place, but by carefully, humbly, temporarily subtracting their ordinary human excess. The basin will not welcome you. It will not reject you. If you prepare properly, it will simply allow you to be there for a little while — a warm darkness, a room of stone and time, and your own brief heartbeat passing through it without becoming a mark.
