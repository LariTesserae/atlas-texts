Prepare as if you are going to enter a small, sacred cistern that is also a musical instrument, a newly built hydraulic room, and a confined underwater space. This is not a natural pool, not a wild grotto, not a ruin softened by centuries. It is made, young, bright, loud, and exact. It will not behave like wilderness. It will behave like a crafted vessel filled with water, light, weather, and moving sound.

An ordinary human multiverse traveler should prepare to be clean, quiet, buoyant, attentive, and very careful about loose objects, drain cycles, storms, and the simple machines that govern the room.

---

## 1. First understand what kind of place you are visiting

The drowned room is a constructed chamber at the bottom of a bright shaft. Water fills it almost to the upper lip. From below, the surface is not a sky but a trembling disc of light. The walls are brick and lime render; the floor is pale glazed tile; the fittings are bronze; the plants are few and held in ceramic sockets. There is no soil, no animal life, no natural bank, no hidden ecology of fish or insects. The life of the place is hydraulic: water entering, warming, shining, moving, flushing, and leaving.

That means your preparation should be different from preparation for a forest, a sea cave, a reef, or a river. You are visiting a room whose “weather” is borrowed from the court above and translated through masonry. You are entering an instrument that is played by rain, light, gates, floats, and drains.

The most important mental preparation is this:

> Do not expect wilderness.  
> Do not expect animals.  
> Do not expect silence.  
> Do not expect a large landscape.  
> Expect a small, resonant, luminous water-room that rewards close attention.

## 2. Decide what kind of visit you are making

There are several possible modes of visitation. Choose the one that matches your training, permission, and comfort.

### A. Rim-only visit

If you cannot swim, cannot dive, lack local permission, or simply wish to be minimally invasive, prepare to observe from above.

For a rim-only visit, bring:

- A polarized lens or viewing glass to reduce glare.
- A small waterproof notebook or voice recorder for afterward.
- Dry footwear with good grip on paving.
- Clothing appropriate to local custom, because the shaft may be in a public or ritual court.
- A listening posture: the place may be audible even from above.

Do not lean over an unprotected opening unless you are certain of your balance. The water may be bright and hypnotic, and the edge may be slick with condensation.

### B. Brief immersion, breath-hold only

For many ordinary human visitors, this is the most intuitive way to experience the chamber: descend briefly, look, listen, feel the water, and return to the surface. But it requires competence.

You should be:

- A confident swimmer.
- Comfortable underwater without panic.
- Able to equalize pressure in ears and mask.
- Trained in safe breath-hold practice.
- Accompanied by a surface watcher.

Never hyperventilate before a breath-hold. Never push until you are desperate for air. Warm, beautiful, enclosed water can be deceptively relaxing, and shallow-water blackout is a real danger.

### C. Guided dive with breathing apparatus

If the shaft is deeper than a safe breath-hold allows, or if you wish to remain below for observation, use appropriate diving equipment. A closed-circuit rebreather would be the quietest and least disruptive option, but it requires advanced training. Open-circuit scuba is simpler but introduces bubbles, noise, and extra equipment into a small resonant chamber. If you are not trained, do not attempt this.

For an ordinary human traveler, the safest assumption is:

> If you are not trained in confined-water diving or freediving, do not enter below the surface without a local guide or trained companion.

## 3. Learn the room’s rules before you enter

The place has clear implicit rules. Prepare by internalizing them.

### Rule one: everything loose goes to the drain

The floor slopes subtly toward the central drain. Leaves, fibers, bubbles, and floating fragments eventually gather there. If you bring a loose strap, a dangling necklace, a towel fiber, a leaf from another world, a camera cap, or a piece of hair ribbon, the room may try to take it into the drain.

Secure everything. Better still, bring almost nothing.

### Rule two: the drain is not a curiosity to touch

The drain is the dark center of the floor. It is fitted with a bronze grate, and when it opens, the chamber can become a slow vortex. Even a slow vortex can pull at loose fabric, hair, or thin cords. Do not put fingers into the grate. Do not swim directly over it during a flushing cycle. Do not let long gear trail near it.

### Rule three: the mechanisms are simple but not toys

Floats, chains, wooden catches, bronze gates, tarred rope, and clay-lined spouts control the water. They may move without warning. A ceramic float may rise, a chain may tighten, a gate may open with a low thunk. If you hear metal, wood, or rushing changes, be alert.

Do not touch, pull, adjust, or block the mechanisms unless explicitly invited by a keeper or operator.

### Rule four: the plants are not decorations to touch

The vegetation is sparse, architectural, and rooted in ceramic collars. There is no soil. The roots are held by fired clay and tile fragments. A careless fin, hand, or camera housing could tear a plant from its socket. The place has no animal life to repair such damage quickly; the greenery is deliberately placed and fragile.

Do not touch the plants. Do not use them as handholds. Do not kick near them.

### Rule five: the surfaces are young and can be damaged

The mortar is still crisp. The glaze is fresh. The bronze has not been worn smooth. This is not an ancient ruin that can absorb rough treatment. It is newly forming its habits. Scratches, scuffs, oil, sunscreen, mud, or metal marks would be violations of the place.

Move as if you are inside a newly fired vessel.

### Rule six: sound is part of the architecture

The curved walls focus noise. The tiled floor reflects it. Water transmits it. A drop can become a tap; a spout can become a roar; a gate can become a bell. The room is loud in a mineral, mechanical, meteorological way. Prepare for sound not as background but as presence.

## 4. Prepare your body and health

Because this is an underwater architectural space, ordinary human limitations matter.

### Swimming and water comfort

You should be able to:

- Swim calmly.
- Tread water without splashing.
- Float neutrally if possible.
- Move without panic in enclosed water.
- Keep your face submerged while observing.
- Recover from a flooded mask or unexpected splash.

If you are not a strong swimmer, do not enter below the surface.

### Breath-hold safety

If visiting by breath-hold:

- Never dive alone.
- Have a watcher at the surface or shaft mouth.
- Breathe slowly before descent.
- Do not hyperventilate.
- Ascend before strong urgency.
- Exhale gently on ascent if needed.
- Do not push through dizziness.
- Rest fully between attempts.

The chamber may be beautiful enough to distract you from your body’s warnings. That is exactly why you must be conservative.

### Equalization

The shaft may be deep enough to create pressure in your ears and mask. If you feel pain, stop. If you cannot equalize, do not descend. Practice gentle equalization before the visit. A low-volume mask helps. Pinch your nose early and often during descent.

Do not enter if you have:

- A severe cold.
- Sinus congestion.
- An ear infection.
- A perforated eardrum.
- Recent ear surgery unless cleared by a medical professional.
- Severe vertigo or balance problems.

### Skin, eyes, and water chemistry

The water is described as clear, warm, mineral-clean, and bath-like, but the chamber is young. New lime render can be alkaline. Bronze fittings may release trace metals. Mineral water may be gentle or irritating depending on local chemistry.

Before entering, if possible:

- Ask a local keeper whether the water is safe for skin and eyes.
- Test pH, temperature, and general water quality if you have a small kit.
- Look for warning signs: strong metallic taste, caustic smell, cloudy films, skin irritation in locals.

If uncertain, protect yourself with:

- A full-face mask or well-sealed dive mask.
- Thin gloves.
- A hood.
- A drysuit or full-body barrier.

If the water is confirmed safe, a thin rash guard or swimskin may be enough.

Avoid contact lenses if possible. They can trap microbes and increase infection risk. Use prescription dive lenses instead.

Do not drink the water. Even clear mineral water can be unsafe, and the chamber’s bronze, lime, and microbial films are not meant for ingestion.

### Temperature

The water is warm near the surface and cooler near the drain. It may feel pleasant at first, but long immersion can still chill you, especially if you remain still. Conversely, if the upper water is truly bath-warm, you may overheat in a thick wetsuit.

Prepare with layered thinking:

- For short visits: swimwear or rash guard.
- For moderate visits: 1–2 mm wetsuit or neoprene top.
- For sensitive skin or uncertain chemistry: full barrier suit.
- For after: dry towel, warm layer, windproof cover.

Hydrate before and after. Warm water can mask dehydration.

## 5. Choose the timing carefully

The room changes with time of day and weather. Your first visit should be chosen for clarity, safety, and orientation.

### Best first visit: calm bright midday

Midday gives the strongest light column. The caustics will be vivid, the chamber bright, and visibility high. You will see the room clearly: the stair, the drain, the plant niches, the spouts, the glaze, the moving nets of light.

This is the best time to learn the layout.

### Morning: quiet and subtle

Morning may be hushed, with the upper water holding the first heat and the lower tiles still cool. The surface may be a pale mirror. This is a good time for listening and noticing small sounds: ticking as walls warm, condensation taps, soft water movement.

### Afternoon: wind and restlessness

Wind through the shaft may disturb the surface into rings. Light may become less stable. This can be beautiful but may make orientation and surface breathing slightly more complex.

### Evening: condensation and intimacy

Evening brings condensation on the upper walls, droplets falling back into the pool with small taps. This is a subtle, intimate time, but lower light may make the chamber harder to read.

### Storms: extraordinary but not for beginners

In storm rain, the shaft becomes a throat of sound. Rain descends in a continuous white curtain. The surface churns. Air is driven downward. The chamber grows darker and louder. Gates may move. Spouts may open or close. The whole place becomes an instrument.

This may be the most character-revealing state of the room, but it is not a good first visit for an ordinary traveler. Storm inflow can reduce visibility, create turbulence, make ascent difficult, and change the hydraulic behavior of the chamber.

If you wish to witness a storm in the drowned room, do so only with:

- Local permission.
- Experienced guidance.
- A clear exit route.
- A surface support person.
- Knowledge of the drain and gate settings.
- No loose equipment.
- A plan to leave if conditions become violent.

For most ordinary visitors: observe storms from outside or from a protected viewing point above.

## 6. Check the flushing cycle

The chamber is periodically flushed. Water enters through wall mouths and leaves through the central drain. A float rises, a chain tightens, a wooden catch shifts, a bronze gate opens.

Before entering, ask or determine:

- When will the drain open?
- How long does a flush last?
- Does the water level drop significantly?
- Do the spouts close during storms?
- Are the mechanisms automatic or manually controlled?
- Is there a warning signal?

If you hear a low thunk, a chain rattle, or a change in sound, treat it as information. Move away from the drain. Stay near the wall or stair. Do not let yourself be in the center when the vortex begins.

For a first visit, choose a time when the drain is expected to remain closed.

## 7. Prepare the right clothing and gear

The guiding principle is: bring only what is necessary, secure, clean, and soft.

### What to wear if immersion is allowed

- **Close-fitting swimwear or a thin wetsuit.** Avoid loose fabric. Loose cloth trails, snags, and sheds fibers.
- **Rash guard or neoprene top** if you want protection from cool lower water or mild abrasion.
- **No jewelry.** Rings, bracelets, earrings, necklaces, and pendants can catch, fall, or scratch glaze.
- **Tie back long hair.** Better yet, wear a swim cap or hood. Hair is exactly the kind of loose organic matter the drain would collect.
- **No contact lenses if avoidable.** Use prescription mask inserts.
- **Soft, non-marking footwear** if the stair or court is slick. Avoid heavy boots or shoes with grit.
- **Thin gloves only if needed** for protection or warmth. They reduce tactile sensitivity, so use them thoughtfully.

### Mask and breathing

- **Low-volume dive mask** for equalization.
- **Clear or lightly tinted lens.** The chamber is bright, but dark lenses may make shaded corners too dim.
- **Spare mask strap** if you are responsible for your own safety.
- **Snorkel** may be useful only if you will remain at the surface of the shaft, but in a narrow water column it may be awkward. Do not rely on it for underwater exploration.
- **If freediving:** no bulky equipment. Keep the body streamlined.
- **If using breathing apparatus:** keep hoses short and secured. Avoid dangling gauges. Use a mouthpiece tether if needed.

### Fins

Be cautious with fins. In a small chamber, long fins can kick plants, strike walls, stir mineral lines, and make movement clumsy. If you use fins, use very short, soft, non-marking training fins or fin socks. In many cases, no fins may be better.

### Ear and hearing protection

The room is resonant. Underwater sound can be surprising and intense. If you are sensitive:

- Use custom vented earplugs designed for diving.
- Or wear a neoprene hood to dampen some noise.
- Do not block your awareness completely; you need to hear gates, rain, and changes in flow.

### Safety instruments

For a careful traveler:

- Waterproof watch or timer.
- Small depth gauge if the shaft depth is unknown.
- Underwater slate or waterproof notebook if you need to communicate.
- Small waterproof light, preferably with low-glare mode, for shaded corners or evening visits.
- Surface whistle or signal device.
- Local emergency beacon if multiverse travel requires one.
- Small first-aid kit above.
- Fresh water for rinsing eyes and skin afterward.

Do not bring bulky equipment unless required. The room is intimate; large gear becomes a hazard.

### What not to bring

Do not bring:

- Soil.
- Seeds.
- Food.
- Animal life.
- Live bait.
- Fishing gear.
- Soap.
- Shampoo.
- Sunscreen unless it is confirmed safe and necessary, and even then avoid entering with it.
- Oils.
- Lotions.
- Dyes.
- Paper that sheds.
- Loose towels near the opening.
- Metal tools.
- Sharp instruments.
- Motorized underwater devices.
- Scooters.
- Drones unless explicitly permitted.
- Souvenirs.
- Containers meant to take water unless sampling is authorized.

The room is not a place for extraction or collection.

## 8. Practice biosecurity and cleanliness

The absence of animals is one of the chamber’s defining qualities. That absence may be maintained by frequent flushing, warmth, mineral content, alkalinity, bronze, lime, or the lack of soil. It is not a defect. It is a condition.

You must not disturb that condition.

Before entering:

- Rinse all gear in clean water.
- Ensure no grit, soil, leaf fragments, seeds, or organic matter cling to clothing or equipment.
- Do not enter after walking through mud, gardens, or animal areas without cleaning footwear.
- Do not introduce organisms from another universe, region, or water body.
- Do not wear contaminated neoprene.
- Do not carry water or biological material from another location into this one.

After leaving:

- Rinse yourself and your gear.
- Dry thoroughly.
- Inspect equipment for plant fragments or mineral residue.
- Do not transfer water to another environment without permission and containment.

For a multiverse traveler, this matters doubly. Cross-dimensional contamination may be subtle: microbes, spores, mineral films, or chemical traces. The drowned room is austere; a single introduction could alter it.

## 10. Prepare for entry and exit

The drowned room is small, but entry and exit may be the most delicate parts of the visit.

### Before entering

At the shaft or court above:

- Look down and assess the water surface.
- Check the light column.
- Listen for rain, gate movement, or rushing water.
- Confirm that the opening will not close above you.
- Confirm the stair or access point is stable.
- Check for falling leaves, debris, or bird droppings from above if the shaft is open.
- Ensure no one above will accidentally drop objects.
- Agree on signals with your companion.

If there is a grate, cover, or glazed panel, make absolutely sure you understand how it operates. Never enter a flooded shaft if the surface opening may be sealed while you are below.

### Surface support

For any immersion beyond casual wading, have at least one person outside the chamber or at the shaft mouth. That person should know:

- How many people are below.
- Expected duration.
- What to do if you do not return.
- How to signal operators.
- How to close or open access if necessary.
- Where emergency help can be found.

A simple line may be useful, but use caution. A line can snag on plants, fittings, or the drain. If used, it should be short, attended, and free of loops.

### Entering

Do not dive headfirst unless the depth is certain and entry is permitted. The chamber is built, fitted with bronze, tile, and stair treads; a careless dive could injure you or damage the place.

Better:

- Enter feet first.
- Use the stair of ceramic treads if present.
- Hold only the intended supports.
- Descend slowly.
- Let the water close over you without panic.
- Equalize early.
- Pause and orient yourself.

If entering from the shaft above, descend along the wall or guided path. Do not block the light column for longer than necessary if others need to see or if the shaft is narrow.

### Exiting

Exit slowly. Do not grab plants, spouts, chains, or ceramic collars. Use the stair. If the stair is submerged and slick, test each tread. Avoid kicking the wall. Rise with control, especially if you have been holding your breath.

At the surface, do not linger in the shaft mouth if rain is falling or if others need access. Move to a stable point.

## 11. Learn how to move inside the chamber

The room is small enough to cross in a few strokes, but movement should be deliberate.

### Move slowly

Fast movement creates turbulence. Turbulence disturbs plants, lifts mineral traces, changes the light, and may carry fibers toward the drain. The chamber is designed for water, not for thrashing limbs.

### Stay near the wall at first

Until you understand the currents, remain near the stair or wall. The center contains the drain. The wall contains niches, spouts, and landmarks.

### Use hands gently

If you need to steady yourself, use the stair or broad stable surfaces. Do not grip plant collars, bronze fittings, ropes, or gate mechanisms. The bronze may be sharp where newly cast; the plants may detach.

### Keep your body compact

Tuck gear close. Avoid trailing straps. Keep arms near the body unless moving. If you use fins, use tiny movements. If no fins, use slow sculling or breaststroke-like motion without kicking the floor.

### Avoid the drain

Do not hover directly above the central drain. Do not let hair, fabric, or cords hang near it. If the drain begins to open, move tangentially toward the wall or stair rather than struggling upward through the center.

### Watch the spouts

The wall mouths may admit water suddenly. Do not place your face or hands directly in front of a spout. The flow may be gentle, but pressure changes can surprise you.

### Listen constantly

The room tells you what it is doing. Learn the sounds:

- Soft ticking: warming or cooling materials.
- Bright tapping: drops from above or condensation.
- Rushing: spouts active.
- Hollow notes: air in overflow pipes.
- Metallic ticks: bronze fittings moving.
- Low thunk: gate shifting.
- Deep circular murmur: drain opening.
- Drumming: rain striking the surface.

If the sound changes, pause and reassess.

## 12. Prepare for the sensory experience

The drowned room is not merely a visual place. It is acoustic, thermal, tactile, and emotional.

### Light

At midday, the shaft becomes a column of white-gold brightness. It strikes glazed walls and shatters into caustics. The floor may seem alive with restless nets of light. This can be beautiful and disorienting.

Prepare by:

- Using a clear or lightly tinted mask.
- Avoiding staring directly into the brightest column for too long.
- Letting your eyes adapt.
- Using the stair and wall as orientation landmarks.
- Remembering that bubbles rise toward light; the bright disc is generally up.

### Sound

The sound may be one of the most surprising elements. The room is underwater and hidden, yet loud. It rings, taps, roars, gurgles, and drums. There is no birdsong, no insect hum, no animal splash. The loudness is mineral and mechanical.

Prepare by:

- Expecting noise.
- Practicing calm breathing.
- Not tensing your shoulders.
- Keeping your jaw relaxed.
- Using ear protection if sensitive.
- Learning to distinguish meaningful sounds from ambient sound.

If you use a regulator, your own breathing may become loud and rhythmic. If freediving, your heartbeat and bubbles may feel amplified. Accept this. Do not fight the acoustics.

### Touch

The water may feel warm and heavy near the surface, cooler near the drain. The walls are rendered brick, crisp and slightly rough. The floor is glazed tile, likely smooth but not necessarily frictionless. Bronze may be cool and hard. Plant leaves may be delicate. Mortar may still have fine texture.

Do not explore by touch unless necessary. The place is meant to be seen, heard, and felt through immersion, not handled.

### Smell and taste

If the surface breaks, there may be a clean mineral smell, like warm masonry and rain. Do not taste the water. If you accidentally take water into your mouth, spit it out discreetly at the surface and rinse later if possible.

### Emotional response

The chamber may feel intimate and discovered, but also strange. It is small, submerged, bright, and animal-less. Some visitors may find it peaceful; others may feel enclosed or unsettled.

Prepare by:

- Visiting first with a companion.
- Keeping the visit short.
- Breathing slowly.
- Reminding yourself that the space is contained and knowable.
- Focusing on one detail at a time: a leaf, a caustic pattern, a bubble train, a bronze pin.
- Leaving if you feel anxiety rising.

The room rewards patience, not conquest.

## 13. Prepare for specific hazards

### Hazard: drain vortex

If the drain opens fully, the chamber may form a slow vortex. Plants bend toward it; light bends as the surface dips; sound changes to a deeper roar.

Preparation:

- Know the flush schedule.
- Avoid the center.
- Keep all gear secured.
- Do not enter during a full flush unless trained and guided.
- If the drain opens while you are inside, move calmly to the wall or stair.
- If something is pulled toward the drain, let it go if possible. Do not chase it.

### Hazard: storm inflow

Storm rain can descend as a solid sheet through the shaft. The surface may boil, visibility may drop, and air may be driven downward in bubbles.

Preparation:

- Check weather above.
- Do not enter if heavy rain is likely unless you are experienced and authorized.
- If caught in a storm, stay near the wall or stair.
- Protect your mask and airway.
- Do not ascend directly under a violent rain curtain if you can wait for a lull.
- Listen for gate changes.

### Hazard: moving mechanisms

Wooden gates, bronze plugs, chains, and floats can move suddenly.

Preparation:

- Keep hands away from fittings.
- Do not lean on chains or ropes.
- If you hear a mechanical sound, pause.
- Learn the local warning signs.

### Hazard: confined space and disorientation

The chamber is small, but underwater orientation can still fail, especially if light changes or bubbles fill the water.

Preparation:

- Always know where the stair is.
- Identify the drain, the plant niches, and the light column.
- Move slowly.
- If disoriented, stop, stabilize, and look for light or bubbles rising.
- If using breath-hold, ascend calmly before urgency becomes panic.

### Hazard: slippery or sharp surfaces

The room is young. Tool marks remain. Mortar edges are crisp. Bronze may have casting roughness. Tiles may be slick.

Preparation:

- Do not push off forcefully from walls.
- Do not grasp unknown edges quickly.
- Use the stair treads.
- Wear protective footwear if allowed.
- Avoid bare-handed contact with rough bronze or fresh mortar.

### Hazard: water chemistry

New lime, bronze, mineral content, or microbial films may irritate.

Preparation:

- Test if possible.
- Ask locals.
- Use eye and skin protection if uncertain.
- Exit immediately if you feel burning, unusual itching, dizziness, nausea, or respiratory irritation.

### Hazard: surface access closure

If the shaft has a grate, cover, or gate, it could close.

Preparation:

- Confirm egress before entering.
- Have an attendant if necessary.
- Do not enter if the cover mechanism is unknown.

- Attached to your body, or
- In a sealed pocket, or

The chamber is resonant. Bulky or rattling gear will feel intrusive. Wrap hard objects in soft covers. Avoid clanking tanks or housings against walls.

### No motors

Do not use underwater scooters, pumps, drones, or powered lights that fan or disturb water. The room’s technology is simple: gravity, buoyancy, and hand strength. Your visit should match that ethic.

- No large fins.
- Ear protection if sensitive.
- Possibly a helmet if there is falling debris risk from the court above, though this depends on local conditions.
- A tether only if managed carefully and without snag risk.

### Storm behavior

- Do not remain in the center.
- Stay near the stair or a protected wall niche if safe.
- Avoid the rain curtain if it is forceful.
- Watch for bubbles driven downward.
- Listen for gate movement.
- Be ready to leave.

The storm version of the room is magnificent, but it is also the chamber in its most powerful mode. It is not a place for casual curiosity.

---

## 19. Prepare for the ethical dimension

This is a hidden, intimate, constructed landscape. It may be precious precisely because it is small and exact. An ordinary multiverse traveler should prepare to minimize their impact.

Do not:

- Take water unless permitted.
- Take tile fragments, mortar, bronze shavings, or plant pieces.
- Leave hair, fibers, skin flakes, or trash.
- Add coins, offerings, seeds, or tokens.
- Touch the mechanisms.
- Alter the water level.
- Block the drain.
- Feed nonexistent animals.
- Introduce organisms.
- Record sacred or private details without consent.
- Publicize the location if it is hidden or protected.

If you must leave something, leave attention. Leave silence. Leave the room as it was.

## 20. Prepare your mind for the character of the place

The drowned room has a particular emotional quality: bright but hidden, warm but austere, intimate but not soft, alive but without animals, loud but not chaotic. It is a room whose air has been replaced by liquid. It is a vessel listening to weather.

To prepare mentally, practice:

- Slow attention.
- Listening without expecting birds or voices.
- Looking without expecting a horizon.
- Noticing small motions: a leaf tilting, a bubble train, a ripple from condensation, a shadow passing over the shaft.
- Accepting absence.
- Letting the room set the pace.

Imagine standing at the rim and seeing a bright disc below. Imagine descending into warm mineral water. Imagine the sound closing around you: not silence, but a resonant mineral room. Imagine the light breaking into moving nets across the floor. Imagine a leaf releasing tiny oxygen beads. Imagine the faint thunk of a bronze gate somewhere above. Imagine the dark drain at the center, patient and exact. Imagine the storm arriving, the surface shattering, the whole chamber ringing.

Then imagine leaving without changing anything.

That is the proper preparation.

## Compact traveler’s checklist

### Before the visit

- Confirm permission and local rules.
- Check weather and storm risk.
- Learn drain/flush schedule.
- Identify keeper or operator if present.
- Choose a trained companion or guide.
- Plan entry and exit.
- Establish signals and emergency plan.
- Hydrate and rest.
- Clean all gear.

### Skills

- Swim confidently.
- Equalize safely.
- Breath-hold conservatively or dive with certification.
- Remain calm in enclosed water.
- Move slowly without stirring the chamber.

### Gear, minimal and secure

- Well-fitted mask.
- Appropriate exposure suit.
- Secure swimwear, no loose cloth.
- Hair secured or capped.
- Non-marking footwear if needed.
- Ear protection if sensitive.
- Timer/watch.
- Small light if needed.
- Fresh water for after.
- Dry clothes.
- Towel kept away from opening.
- Waterproof slate or memory method.
- Camera only if permitted and tethered.

### Do not bring

- Soil, seeds, animals, food.
- Soap, sunscreen, oils, lotions.
- Loose jewelry.
- Sharp tools.
- Motorized devices.
- Untethered equipment.
- Anything that can shed fibers or fall.

### At the chamber

- Look and listen before entering.
- Confirm the opening will not close.
- Check for drain activity.
- Enter feet first.
- Stay near stair/wall at first.
- Avoid the drain.
- Avoid plants, spouts, chains, and fittings.
- Move slowly.
- Listen for gates, rain, and flow changes.
- Leave before discomfort.

### After the visit

- Exit carefully.
- Rinse skin and gear.
- Dry ears.
- Hydrate.
- Inspect for irritation or injury.
- Record notes respectfully.
- Leave no trace.
- Thank keepers if appropriate.
