Prepare as if you are going to become a temporary aquatic organism: quiet, sealed, buoyant, patient, and almost entirely self-contained.

This is not a place where an ordinary human can arrive as a walker, a climber, or even a conventional sailor. There is no shore, no dry camp, no high viewpoint, no refuge of exposed rock. The world is already inside water. To visit it, you must enter the medium completely and accept that your body is the foreign object. The basin is warm, beautiful, and outwardly gentle, but it is also sharp, young, entangling, deep in places, high in altitude, and ecologically fragile. The greatest preparation is not merely survival gear; it is restraint.

A good rule for this place is:

**Bring closed systems. Move slowly. Touch almost nothing. Leave no artifact. Let the world continue as if you were not there.**

Below is a full intuitive preparation guide for an ordinary human multiverse traveler.

---

## 1. Understand what you are entering

The essential facts of the location are simple, but each has practical consequences.

### It is submerged entirely

There is no ordinary land surface. The mountains are drowned. Even the highest ridges are barely beneath or dimpled by water. You cannot stand, rest, survey, or retreat to dry ground. You must be prepared to be immersed for the entire visit unless you bring a floating rest habitat or surface shelter.

**Implication:**  
You need diving life support, buoyancy control, and a plan for rest that does not depend on shore.

### It is high altitude

The sky above is bright and thin. The water is clear and sunlit, but the surface environment may involve low oxygen, strong UV, cool air, wind, rain, and rapid weather changes. High altitude also affects diving physiology: gas expansion, decompression risk, and bubble behavior all differ from sea-level diving.

**Implication:**  
Treat this as an altitude dive, not a tropical swim. Use altitude-adjusted dive planning, conservative ascent profiles, and surface protection.

### It is warm

The water is comfortably warm, close to skin temperature, heated partly by sunlight and partly by geological warmth. This reduces cold shock, but it does not eliminate thermal risk. Long immersion can still cause heat loss, skin maceration, dehydration, or fatigue. Warm water may also make you overconfident.

**Implication:**  
Use a thin full-body protective suit, monitor core temperature, hydrate, and do not assume comfort equals safety.

### The stone is young and sharp

This is not a rounded ancient seabed. The rock is fresh basalt, fractured cliffs, glassy seams, loose talus, pumice, ash pockets, and crisp rims. It cuts. It snags. It may be unstable. Young terrain can shift, settle, crack, or shed fragments.

**Implication:**  
Wear abrasion- and cut-resistant protection. Avoid touching, leaning, kneeling, anchoring, or finning too close to rock. Do not assume a surface is stable.

### The vegetation is dense, layered, and slow

Life covers nearly every stable surface. There are crusts, films, ribbons, blades, tubes, beaded strands, transparent membranes, gas bladders, floating canopies, and drifting spores. The system is lush but fragile. It is not a kelp forest to push through. It is a saturated living architecture that can entangle, obscure, and be damaged by careless movement.

**Implication:**  
Move with excellent buoyancy, keep limbs close, avoid wide strokes, and do not grab vegetation for support.

### Fauna is rare

There are few animals. No swarms, no predators, no obvious grazers. The place is quiet and mostly sessile. This is not a wildlife-rich reef. It is a slow primary-growth world. The rare animals should not be approached, sampled, or disturbed.

**Implication:**  
Do not expect food, companionship, or ecological spectacle. Bring all nutrition. Observe rare fauna from a distance.

### There are no artifacts

No tools, structures, paths, shelters, or signs of making intelligence exist here. Your gear will be an anomaly. Every object you bring is a foreign artifact in a world that has never known artifacts.

**Implication:**  
Minimize your material footprint. Use sealed, retrievable, non-shedding equipment. Leave nothing behind. Avoid altering the environment even temporarily if possible.

## 2. Choose the entry point with great care

Because there is no shore, entry is a major decision.

If your multiverse transit can be aimed, do not arrive blindly. Choose an entry point that gives you open water, stable visibility, and easy orientation.

### Best entry zones

Look for:

- A broad stony shelf with sparse canopy.
- A mid-slope area where the terrain is visible but not densely packed with floating growth.
- A place just below the surface where light is good but not painfully bright.
- A location away from drifting pumice banks, thick spore clouds, bubble fields, and loose talus.
- A spot near a distinctive ridge or saddle that can serve as a natural reference, but without requiring contact.

### Avoid

- Dense canopy directly below the surface, where bubbles can collect and movement becomes entangling.
- Narrow cracks or fresh fractures, where rock may shift or roots may be damaged.
- Ash-sealed depressions, where a fin stroke could create a long-lasting plume.
- Deep valleys if you are not trained for altitude deep diving.
- Areas with active geologic warmth, unusual bubbling, or mineral staining that may indicate vents or gas seeps.

### Surface entry

If you arrive at the surface, do not immediately remove your mask or breathe the air unless you have confirmed it is safe and you are acclimatized to the altitude. The air may be thin, cold, bright, and low in oxygen. Even if breathable, it may not be comfortable for an unacclimatized traveler.

At the surface you should have:

- A flotation rest option.
- Thermal protection against wind and cool air.
- Eye protection against UV and glare.
- A surface oxygen option if the altitude is significant.
- A way to signal or anchor your return point without damaging the environment.

### Submerged entry

If you enter directly below the surface, be ready to equalize immediately. Pressure changes may feel unusual because of altitude. Your mask, ears, sinuses, and buoyancy compensator will behave differently than at sea level.

Enter slowly. Do not plunge. The water is clear, but depth perception can be deceptive. You may think you have more clearance than you do.

## 3. Breathing and pressure: the core life-support problem

An ordinary human cannot breathe this water, no matter how warm and oxygen-rich it may seem. You must bring your own breathing system.

### Best option: closed-circuit rebreather

For this location, a closed-circuit rebreather is ideal if you are trained to use one.

Reasons:

- It is quiet.
- It produces few or no bubbles.
- It reduces disturbance to the canopy and bubble pools.
- It allows longer submerged presence with less gas waste.
- It helps you move more like a slow organism and less like an industrial diver.

However, rebreathers require discipline. You must monitor oxygen partial pressure, scrubber life, carbon dioxide levels, gas dilution, and bailout supply. Do not use one unless trained.

### Acceptable alternative: open-circuit scuba with careful gas planning

If you use ordinary open-circuit scuba, choose a small-bubble, low-noise regulator setup if possible. But bubbles are a problem here. They rise into the canopy, collect under broad leaves, disturb gas films, and announce your presence in a world otherwise full of slow biological silence.

If open-circuit is your only option:

- Avoid lingering beneath broad floating leaves or canopy membranes.
- Do not exhale into bubble pools intentionally.
- Keep ascents slow and controlled.
- Use a conservative gas reserve.
- Prefer short visits.

### High-altitude dive planning

Because the basin is high, ordinary dive tables and computers may not be adequate unless they are altitude-compensated.

You need:

- An altitude-capable dive computer.
- A plan for surface pressure lower than sea level.
- Conservative decompression margins.
- Careful ascent rates.
- Awareness that gas volumes expand more as pressure is lower.
- Extra caution if you will ascend through the water column to a lower-pressure surface.

Do not push depth here. The beautiful upper ridges and mid-slopes are enough. Deep valleys are darker, cooler, more mineral, and less forgiving. Unless you are technically prepared, set a personal depth limit and obey it.

### Equalization

Warm water does not prevent ear or sinus injury. Equalize early and often. If your full-face mask does not allow easy equalization, practice with a safe alternative. Never force an equalization. If you cannot clear your ears, abort the descent.

### Backup breathing

Carry:

- A bailout bottle.
- A backup regulator or emergency breathing source.
- A spare mask or emergency visual reference if your primary mask floods.
- A redundant oxygen source if you are using a rebreather.

This world offers no local air, no pockets of guaranteed breathable gas, and no help. Do not rely on plant bladders. They may contain oxygen, but they may also contain other gases, and puncturing them harms the environment.

## 4. Exposure protection: protect your body from water, stone, light, and life

The water is warm, so you do not need heavy thermal armor. But you do need protection.

### Suit

Use a thin, full-body suit that covers:

- Arms.
- Legs.
- Torso.
- Neck.
- Ankles.
- Wrists.
- Hood or head covering if possible.

The suit should be:

- Abrasion-resistant.
- Cut-resistant.
- Flexible.
- Non-shedding.
- Non-toxic.
- Smooth enough to reduce snagging.
- Sealed or tight at openings to prevent spores, films, and small organisms from entering.

A warm-water dive skin may not be enough against sharp basalt. A lightweight wetsuit with reinforced panels is better. A drysuit may be too warm underwater but could be useful for surface rest if the air is cold. A hybrid approach may be best: aquatic protection below, insulated surface layer above.

### Gloves

Gloves are important, but they must balance protection and dexterity.

You need:

- Cut resistance.
- Puncture resistance.
- Enough tactile sensitivity to manage gear.
- Smooth exteriors that do not catch on filaments.

Do not use loose or bulky gloves that make you clumsy. You should not be touching much anyway.

### Boots or foot protection

If you use fins, wear protective boots with firm soles but avoid heavy treads that trap sediment or crush fragile surfaces. You should not plan to walk on the bottom. If you must place a foot, it should be rare, gentle, and on bare stable rock, not on living crust.

### Helmet or head protection

A low-profile water helmet is wise. The rock is angular, overhead terrain exists, and loose stone may shift. A helmet protects against bumps, falling fragments, and accidental contact with ridges.

It should be:

- Rounded.
- Smooth.
- Free of external snag points.
- Equipped with low-light mounts if needed.
- Not fitted with loud or protruding devices.

### Eye and face protection

Use a full-face mask if possible. It protects your eyes and face from mineral water, spores, accidental contact, and UV. It also helps with communication and drinking.

The mask should have:

- UV protection.
- Anti-fog treatment that is non-toxic.
- A prescription insert if needed.
- A secure seal.
- A low internal volume for easier clearing.

Do not wear ordinary contact lenses if you can avoid it. They increase infection risk and can be dangerous in unfamiliar water.

### Skin care

Even warm water can cause skin breakdown over long immersion. Prepare for:

- Maceration.
- Chafing.
- Minor abrasions.
- Irritation from minerals or biological films.
- Sun exposure in shallow water or at the surface.

Use sealed clothing rather than chemical sunscreens where possible. If you must use skin products, choose inert, non-leaching, non-toxic options and keep them contained under your suit.

## 5. Movement: become slow, neutral, and harmless

Movement is one of the most important preparations. This place punishes haste.

### Buoyancy first

You should be able to hold neutral buoyancy effortlessly. If you cannot hover without touching the environment, you are not ready for this basin.

Practice:

- Still hovering.
- Slow turns.
- Minimal fin strokes.
- Breath-controlled buoyancy.
- No-hand stops.
- Controlled descents without dropping.
- Controlled ascents without ballooning.

### Finning

Use slow, compact fin techniques. Avoid flutter kicks that stir sediment or strike plants. Frog kicks or modified shuffles are better in tight growth.

Your fins should be:

- Moderately sized.
- Not overly stiff.
- Free of sharp edges.
- Securely attached.
- Easy to control.

Avoid finning near:

- Crusts.
- Ash seals.
- Pumice banks.
- Spore clouds.
- Bubble films.
- Transparent membranes.
- Root mats.
- Fresh talus.

### Hands

Keep your hands near your body. Do not reach out to steady yourself unless absolutely necessary. If you must touch, use the back of a gloved hand on bare rock, not on living growth.

Do not:

- Grab fronds.
- Pull roots.
- Push through canopy.
- Part membranes forcefully.
- Lean on pumice.
- Kneel on living skin.
- Stand on ridges unless in emergency.

### Entanglement awareness

The vegetation is not aggressively tangled, but it is dense. Threads, beaded cords, trailing root-like strands, and floating canopy undersides can catch loose gear.

Prevent entanglement by:

- Tucking hoses.
- Securing gauges.
- Avoiding dangling straps.
- Using smooth gear profiles.
- Keeping arms close.
- Moving slowly through thickets.
- Not entering dense canopy unless necessary.

If entangled:

1. Stop.
2. Do not pull.
3. Breathe slowly.
4. Identify the point of contact.
5. Back out along the path of entry if possible.
6. Use gentle manipulation, not force.
7. Only cut as a last resort, and only the minimum necessary.

### Do not use force

This world solves problems by growth, drift, absorption, and patience. You should borrow that ethic. If something is in your way, go around, over, or below it. Do not move it. Do not bend it. Do not clear a path.

## 6. Navigation: prepare for a beautiful, deceptive, trackless medium

The basin is open enough to invite wandering, but the water distorts perception. Distance, shape, and direction can mislead.

### Visual problems

Expect:

- Shifting caustics.
- Moving cloud shadows.
- Green-gold color filtering.
- Softened edges.
- Reduced contrast at distance.
- Illusory depth due to clarity.
- Bright reflections from glassy stone.
- Canopy shadows that change with the sun.

Your eyes may tell you that a ridge is close when it is not, or that a pass is wide when it is narrowing.

### Instrument navigation

Carry:

- Depth gauge.
- Timer.
- Altitude-compensated dive computer.
- Inertial navigation unit if available.
- Compass, but do not rely on it completely.
- Terrain-mapping sonar or lidar at low power if permitted.
- Acoustic ranging beacon if you can deploy and retrieve it without harm.

Basalt can create magnetic anomalies. A compass may be unreliable. Use inertial or terrain-based navigation when possible.

### Natural navigation

Learn the terrain slowly.

Useful features:

- Ridge direction.
- Saddle shape.
- Peak spacing.
- Slope angle.
- Depth changes.
- Canopy density.
- Light direction.
- Distinctive fracture patterns.
- Changes in growth form.

Do not mark the environment by scratching, tying, painting, or leaving line unless absolutely necessary. If you must mark a route for safety, use fully retrievable, non-damaging markers and remove them all when leaving.

### If lost

If you become disoriented:

1. Stop.
2. Stabilize buoyancy.
3. Avoid touching anything.
4. Check depth, gas, and time.
5. Look for broad light direction and slope.
6. Use instruments rather than intuition.
7. Ascend only if you know the surface is clear and you can manage ascent safely.
8. Activate a passive locator if needed.

Do not swim faster. Fast movement increases gas use, stirs debris, and worsens disorientation.

## 7. Communication and sound

Sound is muffled and hard to locate. The basin is quiet, and you should keep it that way.

### Voice communication

If traveling with others, a full-face mask with bone-conduction or throat communication is useful. Practice speaking clearly while breathing from apparatus. Voices may sound strange, and speech may be delayed by equipment.

### Nonverbal signals

Prepare:

- Hand signals.
- Light signals.
- Tactile signals if visibility drops.
- Pre-agreed emergency signals.
- A simple signal for “stop,” “look,” “danger,” “ascend,” “descend,” and “return.”

Keep gestures small. Wide arm signals can strike plants or stir water unnecessarily.

### Sound discipline

Avoid:

- Loud mechanical noises.
- Striking rock.
- Clanking gear.
- High-power sonar unless necessary.
- Underwater speakers.
- Unnecessary vocalization through open regulators.

The place has a low, diffuse hush. Your goal is not to fill it with human noise.

## 8. Light and vision

Light is one of the basin’s great beauties, but it is also tricky.

### Natural light

At midday, the upper water fills with moving caustics. Clouds send broad shadows across ridges. The canopy filters sun into green-gold shade. This is visually magnificent but can be disorienting.

Prepare for:

- Glare at the surface.
- Flickering patterns.
- Reflections from glassy stone.
- Sudden dimming under cloud or rain.
- Reduced contrast in deeper water.

### Artificial light

Use artificial light sparingly. The world is sunlit, so you rarely need strong illumination except in shadowed valleys or if delayed.

If you bring lights:

- Use low intensity.
- Prefer diffused beams.
- Avoid strobing.
- Do not shine directly into plant membranes for long periods.
- Do not use lights to startle or attract animals.
- Use red or amber modes if available, but remember that color perception changes underwater.
- Keep backup lights sealed and non-reflective.

Bright lights can disturb photosensitive growths and rare drifting fauna. Use them as tools, not decoration.

## 9. Sustenance, hydration, rest, and waste

Because there is no dry land, all human needs must be handled as sealed systems.

### Water

Do not drink the basin water. It contains dissolved minerals, organic films, spores, and unknown chemistry. Even if it looks clear, it is not safe without testing and purification.

Bring:

- Potable water.
- Electrolyte mix.
- A drinking system compatible with your mask.
- A sealed waste system.

If you must produce water, use a closed distillation or purification system brought with you. Do not improvise filters from local materials unless in dire emergency, because that would alter the environment and may harm you.

### Food

Bring compact, low-residue food. You will not find food here, and you must not take any.

Good options:

- Gel packs.
- Nutrient bars that do not crumble.
- Sealed pouches.
- Hydration bladders.
- Easy-to-eat rations accessible without removing your mask.

Avoid foods that make you thirsty, gassy, or prone to nausea.

### Rest

For a short visit, you may not need rest. For longer visits, you must solve the problem of no shore.

Options:

- A small floating rest platform with a sealed floor.
- A one-person surface shelter.
- A buoyant rest bladder that does not anchor to living tissue.
- A dry chamber or transition habitat if your multiverse gear supports it.

Never sleep immersed without life support. Even warm water can become dangerous over time, and your gear may drift or snag.

If using a float:

- Keep it compact.
- Do not anchor to plants.
- Do not rest over dense canopy.
- Keep it away from sharp ridges.
- Retrieve or deflate it when leaving.

### Waste

Pack out all waste.

This includes:

- Urine.
- Feces.
- Vomit.
- Food wrappers.
- Wipes.
- Medical waste.
- Condensation or contaminated water from your gear.

Do not release human waste into the basin. The ecosystem is young, slow, and nutrient-sensitive. Introducing nitrogen, bacteria, or organic waste could alter local growth in ways that are invisible at first but long-lasting.

Use sealed relief systems. If your suit has a relief valve, it should drain into a contained collection system, not into the water.

## 10. Health and medical preparation

This is not a violently dangerous world, but it is physically demanding. The main risks are quiet ones.

### Primary medical risks

1. **Drowning or life-support failure**
2. **Decompression illness**
3. **Altitude sickness**
4. **Cuts and punctures from young rock**
5. **Entanglement**
6. **Dehydration**
7. **Thermal stress despite warm water**
8. **Eye, ear, or sinus injury**
9. **Allergic or toxic reaction to spores, films, or minerals**
10. **Psychological stress from immersion, isolation, and strangeness**

### Cuts and wounds

Basalt and glassy stone can cause clean, deep wounds. Underwater, bleeding may be harder to control, and infection risk increases.

Carry:

- Hemostatic dressings.
- Sterile gauze.
- Wound closure strips or sutures if trained.
- Antiseptic suitable for alien-contamination risk.
- Bandages that adhere when wet.
- Trauma shears.
- Small trauma kit in a sealed case.

If cut:

1. Move away from the source.
2. Apply pressure.
3. Control bleeding.
4. Clean the wound with sterile water or saline, not basin water.
5. Seal or dress it.
6. End the visit if the wound is serious.

Do not continue exploring with an open wound exposed to unfamiliar biology.

### Decompression and altitude illness

If you feel:

- Joint pain.
- Dizziness.
- Nausea.
- Unusual fatigue.
- Tingling.
- Shortness of breath.
- Confusion.
- Severe headache at surface.

treat it seriously. Return to a safe pressure environment if possible. Carry emergency oxygen if your transit allows.

### Allergies and spores

Spore clouds, films, and reproductive particles may be harmless to the basin but irritating to you.

Prepare with:

- Full-face mask.
- Sealed suit.
- Antihistamines if approved for your physiology.
- Eye wash or sterile rinse.
- Respiratory filtration in your life-support system.
- A plan to exit if visibility or breathing becomes compromised.

If caught in a dense spore cloud:

- Do not panic.
- Keep your breathing system closed.
- Protect your mask seal.
- Move slowly out of the cloud.
- Avoid inhaling unknown particles at the surface.

### Heat and dehydration

Warm water can make you sweat, and immersion can still dehydrate you. Drink regularly. Watch for:

- Dry mouth.
- Headache.
- Fatigue.
- Cramping.
- Reduced concentration.

### Skin and immersion injury

Long immersion can soften skin, especially around hands and feet. Inspect skin before and after. Treat maceration with drying, barrier care, and rest. Do not return immediately to abrasive water if skin is compromised.

## 11. Ecological preparation: become biologically clean

This is one of the most important parts of the preparation. The basin is young, isolated, and artifactless. It may be vulnerable to contamination in ways that are not obvious.

### Sterilize everything

Before entering, clean and sterilize:

- Suit.
- Mask.
- Fins.
- Gloves.
- Boots.
- Helmet.
- Hoses.
- Tanks.
- Buoyancy gear.
- Tools.
- Sensors.
- Rest habitat.
- Any surface that will contact water.

Use sterilization methods that leave no toxic residue. Rinse with sterile or controlled water. Do not introduce Earth microbes, algae, spores, or organic residues.

### Do not shed

Avoid materials that:

- Shed fibers.
- Flake.
- Corrode.
- Leach plasticizers.
- Release oils.
- Release sunscreen or repellent.
- Lose small parts.

Use inert materials where possible: sealed polymers, glass, titanium, stainless steel, ceramic, or other stable composites. Avoid lead weights if possible. If ballast is necessary, use fully sealed, retrievable, non-toxic ballast.

### No local water contact with personal items

Do not rinse your face, wash gear, clean wounds, or dispose of fluids in the basin. Anything that touches you becomes part of the basin.

### No feeding, touching, or sampling without strict cause

This is not a place for casual collection.

Do not:

- Pick plants.
- Pop bladders.
- Peel crusts.
- Break rock.
- Collect pumice.
- Capture spores.
- Chase ribbon drifters.
- Approach veil-forms.
- Take water samples unless necessary and with sterile containment.

If scientific sampling is part of your purpose, use the smallest possible method:

- Swabs.
- Micro-suction.
- Optical imaging.
- Passive environmental sensing.
- Minimal water draws.
- Sealed containment.
- Full documentation.
- Restoration of the site if possible.

### Do not introduce energy or disorder

Avoid:

- Prop wash.
- Sediment plumes.
- Bubble clouds.
- Bright continuous lights.
- Loud sound.
- Heat sources.
- Chemical discharge.
- Physical trails through growth.

The basin’s ecology is based on patience. Your visit should be a brief, contained interruption.

## 12. The “no artifacts” ethic

The absence of built things is not just a detail. It is part of the nature of the place. Everything there grows, waits, absorbs, drifts, or settles. Nothing is made.

You should prepare mentally to avoid imposing a maker’s mindset.

Do not think:

- “I will clear a path.”
- “I will build a perch.”
- “I will anchor here.”
- “I will mark this for later.”
- “I will collect a souvenir.”
- “I will improve the view.”

Instead think:

- “I will pass without altering.”
- “I will observe without taking.”
- “I will rest without attaching.”
- “I will navigate without scarring.”
- “I will leave nothing.”

If your gear fails, solve the problem with sealed systems, buoyancy, and retreat. Do not improvise tools from the environment unless survival absolutely requires it, and even then, understand that you are violating the character of the place.

## 13. Terrain-specific precautions

### Basalt ridges

Beautiful but sharp. They may be stable, but fresh joints can be unstable.

Precautions:

- Keep distance.
- Do not lean.
- Do not fin against them.
- Watch for loose blocks.
- Avoid overhangs.

### Glassy stone seams

These can flash light and create visual distractions. They may also be brittle.

Precautions:

- Do not stare into reflections.
- Do not touch.
- Avoid striking them with gear.

### Pumice banks

Pale, porous, loosely held, and potentially mobile.

Precautions:

- Do not anchor.
- Do not stand.
- Do not push through.
- Expect them to drift slightly.

### Ash-sealed depressions

These are fragile archives of the basin’s early state.

Precautions:

- Avoid finning near them.
- Do not disturb the living skin.
- Do not sample unless essential.
- Leave immediately if you create a plume.

### Root-filled cracks

Plants follow cracks with precision. Roots may be slowly settling into the stone.

Precautions:

- Do not wedge hands or tools into cracks.
- Do not pull roots.
- Do not use cracks as handholds.

### Canopy layer

The upper canopy is bright and beautiful, but it is also a complex of floating tissues, bubble films, and trailing strands.

Precautions:

- Do not ascend blindly into it.
- Avoid exhaling large bubble clouds beneath broad leaves.
- Keep lights low.
- Move slowly.
- Do not become entangled in trailing root-like strands.

### Deep hollows

Cooler, darker, more mineral, quieter.

Precautions:

- Only enter with gas, training, and light.
- Watch for reduced visibility.
- Be aware of slower temperature.
- Do not linger if you feel unease or hear remote ticking or groaning.

### Rain columns

Rain enters as cooler, fresher descending fingers. This is usually gentle, but it may change local buoyancy, visibility, and plant behavior.

Precautions:

- Expect slight thermal changes.
- Avoid being directly under heavy surface rain if surface conditions worsen.
- Do not assume rainwater is safe to drink without purification.
- End the visit if rain becomes storm-like.

## 14. Fauna etiquette

Animals are rare. When they appear, treat them as events, not attractions.

### Tiny grazers and pale specks

They are part of the living skin. Do not touch or scrape them.

### Ribbon-shaped drifters

Slow, translucent, likely fragile.

Do not:

- Approach quickly.
- Shine bright light on them.
- Attempt to capture them.
- Block their path.

If one passes near you, remain still.

### Broad veil-forms

These are rare, quiet, and perhaps colonial. Their appearance may feel momentous, but do not chase them.

Do not:

- Follow them into canopy.
- Try to sample their tissue.
- Use lights to track them.
- Cast shadows repeatedly over them.

If one appears, stop moving, observe, and let it pass.

## 15. Weather and surface conditions

Although the water is warm, the sky matters.

### Bright high-altitude sun

Prepare for:

- UV exposure.
- Glare.
- Eye strain.
- Surface heat.
- Rapid light changes.

Use a mask with UV protection and a hood if exposed at the surface.

### Rain

Rain can be beautiful but may reduce surface visibility, create chop, and change surface entry/exit. From below it is gentle; from above it may be less so.

If rain intensifies:

- Avoid being caught at the surface far from your exit.
- Descend to a calmer depth if safe.
- Monitor for lightning or wind.
- Consider ending the visit.

### Wind and waves

Some high points are dimpled by waves. Even small waves can make surface rest difficult. If the basin is large, wind can create surface motion that is tiring over time.

Use a low-profile surface rest system if needed. Do not rely on swimming indefinitely.

### Storms

Avoid visiting during storms if possible. A high mountain sea with no shore is a poor place to be caught in lightning, cold wind, or roughened surface water.

If caught:

- Seek portal exit immediately if possible.
- Avoid being the highest point at the surface.
- Descend slightly below the surface if safe and your life support allows.
- Do not shelter under unstable overhangs.
- Do not anchor to living growth.

Your equipment should be quiet, sealed, smooth, and retrievable.

### Essential personal gear

- Full-face mask or high-quality diving mask with backup.
- Breathing system: rebreather preferred, open-circuit acceptable with caution.
- Bailout gas.
- Altitude-compensated dive computer.
- Depth gauge.
- Timer.
- Compass/inertial navigation.
- Low-profile helmet.
- Thin full-body protective suit.
- Cut-resistant gloves.
- Protective boots.
- Fins.

## 17. If you bring devices or drones

Avoid drones if possible. They are loud artifacts in a quiet world.

If a drone is necessary:

- Use a small, shrouded, low-thruster design.
- Keep speed very low.
- Do not enter dense vegetation.
- Do not approach rare fauna.
- Do not use bright lights.
- Do not sample without sealed tools.
- Retrieve it completely.
- Ensure it cannot shed or sink.

A drone should act like a cautious eye, not a probe.

## 18. Training before the visit

An ordinary human should not enter this basin casually. The required skill set is closer to technical diving, cave discipline, and ecological fieldwork than tourism.

Train in:

- Open-water diving.
- Advanced buoyancy.
- Altitude diving.
- Rebreather use if applicable.
- Entanglement response.
- Low-visibility navigation.
- Emergency ascent procedures.
- Mask flooding and clearing.
- Regulator recovery.
- First aid for cuts and immersion.
- Decompression recognition.
- Surface survival without shore.
- Slow, no-touch observation.

Practice in environments that resemble aspects of the basin:

- Warm-water reefs with dense growth.
- Kelp forests, but with stricter no-touch discipline.
- Flooded quarries with sharp rock.
- High-altitude lakes if safe.
- Cave or overhead environments for psychological calm.
- Sensory-deprivation water environments to adapt to muffled sound and slowed movement.

You should be comfortable doing almost nothing while maintaining perfect control.

## 19. Psychological preparation

The basin’s strangeness is quiet. It may affect you more than expected.

### What may be unsettling

- No dry land.
- No horizon.
- No animal sounds.
- No signs of intelligence.
- Constant immersion.
- Muffled sound.
- Slow motion.
- Dense life but rare movement.
- A feeling of being watched by nothing in particular.
- The sense that the world is unfinished.

### What may be comforting

- Warm water.
- Clear light.
- Slow rhythms.
- Lack of predators.
- Gentle currents.
- Beauty of plants and caustics.
- Deep quiet.

Prepare mentally by accepting that you are not conquering this place. You are visiting a state of becoming.

Useful mental habits:

- Slow breathing.
- Deliberate movement.
- Patience.
- Non-reactive observation.
- Comfort with silence.
- Resistance to the urge to touch or fix.
- Willingness to abort if conditions change.

If you are prone to claustrophobia, the water may feel enclosing despite its openness. If you are prone to agoraphobia, the lack of shore may unsettle you. Practice beforehand. Bring calming routines, but do not use music or sound unless it is private, minimal, and does not distract you.

## 20. Arrival procedure: a model first entry

A good first visit should be short, conservative, and mostly observational.

### Before entry

1. Confirm your return method and timing.
2. Check weather, surface conditions, and altitude.
3. Inspect all seals.
4. Sterilize external gear.
5. Confirm gas supply and bailout.
6. Test communication and lights.
7. Secure all loose items.
8. Review hand signals and emergency procedures.
9. Choose an entry point away from dense canopy and fragile growth.
10. Set a conservative turn-back limit.

### Entry

1. Enter feet-first or with controlled immersion, not headfirst.
2. Equalize immediately.
3. Pause just below the surface.
4. Orient yourself.
5. Check buoyancy.
6. Look for ridgelines, depth slope, and light direction.
7. Descend slowly if all is well.

### First movement

1. Move toward an open mid-slope, not a dense thicket.
2. Keep hands close.
3. Fin slowly.
4. Watch your clearance from rock and growth.
5. Breathe calmly.
6. Do not rush to explore.
7. Spend the first minutes simply adapting to the medium.

### During the visit

1. Stay above fragile crusts.
2. Avoid touching anything.
3. Keep an eye on gas, depth, and time.
4. Observe light patterns, plant layers, and stone geometry.
5. If you see rare fauna, stop and let it pass.
6. If you hear ticking or groaning, move away from unstable rock.
7. If visibility drops, slow down and navigate by instruments.
8. If you feel too warm, too cold, tired, anxious, or dehydrated, begin returning.

### Exit

1. Ascend slowly if ascent is required.
2. Avoid rising into canopy.
3. Check for bubbles collecting overhead.
4. Reach your planned exit point.
5. Surface cautiously if exiting at the surface.
6. Protect your return device from water.
7. Decontaminate outside the basin, not inside it.
8. Inspect yourself for cuts, irritation, or equipment damage.
9. Log observations and any ecological contact.

## 21. What to observe if you want to understand the place

If your purpose is not merely survival but appreciation, prepare your attention.

Notice:

- How the living skin changes color over different stone.
- How roots follow fresh fractures.
- How transparent tissues transmit light.
- How gas bladders lift fronds in slow balance.
- How bubbles collect under canopy leaves.
- How rain enters as cooler descending columns.
- How the water thickens with distance into pale suggestions of terrain.
- How plant forms change with depth and light.
- How silence is not empty but full of tiny events.
- How rare animals appear like passing organs of the basin rather than inhabitants in a familiar sense.

Do not try to dominate the experience with data. Record, but also allow the place to impress itself on you.

## 22. Emergency scenarios and how to respond

### Regulator or rebreather failure

- Switch to bailout immediately.
- Begin controlled exit.
- Do not attempt repairs in dense growth.
- Keep buoyancy stable.
- Ascend only if safe.

### Mask flood

- Stop.
- Use clearing procedure.
- If full-face mask fails, switch to backup mask if available.
- Do not remove protection in unfamiliar water.

### Entanglement

- Stop.
- Breathe.
- Back out slowly.
- Cut only if necessary.
- Repair damaged growth if possible? Usually impossible; avoid damage beforehand.

### Cut or puncture

- Move away from hazard.
- Control bleeding.
- Clean with sterile supply.
- Exit if serious.

### Disorientation

- Stop.
- Check instruments.
- Use terrain and light direction.
- Do not swim frantically.
- Activate locator if needed.

### Sudden geological sound or tremor

- Move away from cliffs, overhangs, and fresh cracks.
- Avoid talus.
- Watch for falling fragments or sediment plumes.
- Exit if the sound repeats or water becomes turbid.

### Unusual heat or non-biological bubbling

- Retreat.
- Do not investigate.
- Check sensors if available.
- Assume possible vent or gas seep.

### Surface storm

- Exit if possible.
- Avoid surface exposure.
- Descend to a safer depth if life support permits.
- Do not shelter under unstable terrain.

## 23. After the visit

Preparation does not end when you leave.

### Decontaminate

Clean all gear outside the basin. Do not rinse in the basin or near its edge if that could return contaminants.

### Medical check

Inspect for:

- Cuts.
- Punctures.
- Skin irritation.
- Eye irritation.
- Ear pain.
- Sinus pressure.
- Unusual fatigue.
- Dizziness.
- Allergic symptoms.

Seek care if symptoms develop later. Some reactions to unfamiliar biology may be delayed.

### Ecological report

If you are part of a multiverse travel system, record:

- Entry point.
- Duration.
- Depth range.
- Any contact with life.
- Any damage, however small.
- Any unusual fauna seen.
- Any geological activity.
- Any contamination risk.

This helps protect the basin from cumulative harm.

### Psychological integration

Give yourself time to adjust. The basin’s slowness may make ordinary life feel harsh afterward. You may miss the quiet, the warmth, the green-gold light, the sense of a world still becoming. That is normal. But do not return impulsively. Repeated visits can become a burden to such a place.

## 24. A final intuitive summary

To visit this submerged highland country, you should prepare as though you are entering a warm, living, unfinished cathedral of water and young stone. You must be sealed against it and gentle within it. You must bring your own air, your own warmth margin, your own navigation, your own ethics, and your own exit. You must not expect the world to accommodate you. It will not. It will simply continue around you.

The ideal traveler in this place would be almost invisible:

- A quiet diver in a smooth, dark suit.
- Moving with barely any wake.
- Touching nothing.
- Leaving no bubbles under the canopy if possible.
- Leaving no mark on the stone.
- Watching light move over black ridges.
- Letting spores drift past.
- Letting rare transparent animals pass without pursuit.
- Listening to the muffled hush of water against itself.
- Feeling the warmth press close, but not claiming it.

If you can prepare to be that kind of visitor—technically competent, medically cautious, ecologically humble, and emotionally patient—then this place can be one of the most profound multiverse destinations imaginable. It is not a world of tools or voices. It is a world of water, stone, growth, light, and long time. Your preparation should make you worthy of that silence.
