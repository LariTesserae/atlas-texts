Prepare as if you are entering a finished interior of the world, not a wilderness, not a ruin, and not a cave. This place is small, sealed, dry, luminous, crowded, and loud in a way that has nothing to do with weather or machinery. It is more like stepping inside a vast, preserved organ than visiting a landscape. An ordinary human should not prepare mainly for monsters, storms, heat, cold, or difficult terrain. The real dangers and demands here are dryness, sound, fragility, sensory distortion, contamination, and the fact that your body — wet, warm, moving, breathing, shedding — is profoundly out of place.

The best preparation is to become less like an explorer and more like a careful guest inside a completed biological architecture.

---

## 1. Understand what you are entering

This pocket is not an open environment. It is a constructed chamber beneath a pale mineral plain. It is room-sized, intimate, hidden, and saturated with built material. There is no water, no fauna, no technology, no ordinary geology. Everything is grown, secreted, layered, woven, crystallized, resinous, waxy, saline. The space is bright without a clear source, cool without being freezing, dry in a way that feels hard and mineral, and loud with a continuous resonant chord.

That means your preparation should focus on five major problems:

1. **Your body contains and releases water.**  
   The chamber has no water. Your breath, sweat, tears, saliva, urine, and even the moisture in your skin are foreign substances here.

2. **The sound is physically intrusive.**  
   The room’s constant hum is not background noise. It is a standing resonance felt in teeth, chest, and bone. It can disorient, nauseate, or psychologically overwhelm an unprepared person.

3. **The space is packed and fragile.**  
   The chamber is not empty. Every interval is occupied by constructed forms. You cannot move normally. Touching, leaning, brushing, stepping, or forcing your body through gaps may damage irreplaceable structures.

4. **The light and surface texture can deceive your senses.**  
   Sourceless brightness, reflective salt, wax, resin, and crystal can flatten depth perception and make the space feel unreal. You may misjudge distances, footing, and clearance.

5. **The environment may be stable only because nothing disturbs it.**  
   No animals, no flowing water, no machines, no weather. Your presence may be the first mobile disturbance in a long equilibrium.

Do not think of this as “surviving” the location. Think of it as **not ruining it, not losing yourself in it, and not becoming a contaminant.**

## 2. Decide whether you should enter at all

Before preparing gear, ask whether entry is justified.

This place is hidden, sealed, and complete. It does not invite casual visitation. If your purpose is mere curiosity, consider remote observation first. If your multiverse transit method allows non-invasive scanning, do that before committing a human body to the chamber.

An ordinary human should not enter if:

- They cannot enter without breaking or widening an opening.
- They cannot move without touching many surfaces.
- They have severe claustrophobia, tinnitus, vertigo, panic disorders, or respiratory fragility.
- Their equipment requires venting moisture, dust, heat, or exhaust into the chamber.
- They intend to sample, cut, drill, mark, or collect anything.
- They are alone and inexperienced.

If there is no natural or existing aperture, **do not force entry.** This is not a cave that can be opened for convenience. Breaking into the wall would violate the place and may release sealed gases, alter humidity, fracture crystalline lattices, or destabilize the resonance.

If you are using multiverse travel, do not materialize blindly inside. The chamber is packed. There may be very little open volume. Arrival inside solid or semi-solid construction would be catastrophic. Set your arrival point just outside the pocket, in the pale plain above or beside it, and enter by physical passage only if one exists or can be used without damage.

## 3. Train your body and mind before the visit

You do not need extreme athletic preparation, but you do need specific discipline.

### Practice stillness

The chamber is motionless and timeless. Ordinary humans are used to changing environments: wind, light shifts, animal movement, temperature variation, sound sequences. Here, the environment does not “happen.” It persists. That can be unsettling.

Before entering, practice:

- Sitting quietly for 20–40 minutes.
- Breathing slowly without fidgeting.
- Keeping eyes open without seeking novelty.
- Tolerating a steady drone or tone without becoming irritated.
- Moving slowly and deliberately in cramped spaces.

### Practice restrained movement

You must move as if every inch around you is brittle, precious, and older than your understanding of it. Practice:

- Crawling without dragging knees.
- Turning without swinging shoulders.
- Keeping elbows close.
- Looking without leaning.
- Stopping immediately when uncertain.

### Prepare for sensory strangeness

The room is bright without a sun, loud without a source, crowded without creatures, and alive without obvious motion. It may feel sacred, medical, architectural, or dreamlike. It may feel as if the space is aware, even though there are no eyes. Do not let that feeling lead to panic or reverence so deep that you forget practical safety.

A good mental rule is:

> Move less, observe more, touch nothing, and remember that you are mostly water in a place without water.

## 4. Assume the atmosphere is not safe until proven otherwise

The description suggests cool, clean, dry air, but “clean” does not mean breathable for an ordinary human. It may be dry enough to injure mucous membranes. It may contain fine salt dust, resin particles, crystalline aerosols, volatile organic compounds, or unusual gas ratios. It may have oxygen, but it may also have low humidity so extreme that breathing it directly is harmful.

### Preferred breathing setup

Use a **sealed full-face helmet or mask** with independent life support.

The safest system is:

- Closed-circuit rebreather or sealed supplied-air system.
- Carbon dioxide scrubbing.
- Oxygen regulation.
- Internal humidity control.
- No venting of exhaled moisture into the chamber.
- Backup air supply.

If you must use a simpler system, at minimum use:

- Full-face respirator.
- Particulate filtration.
- Organic vapor/acid gas cartridges if resin or salt chemistry is suspected.
- A humidifying inlet or internal moisture trap.
- A sealed exhaust path that does not blow onto surfaces.

Do not enter relying on “it smells clean.” Smell is not safety.

### Why moisture containment matters

Your breath contains water vapor. In ordinary environments, this is harmless. Here, it is a contaminant. Water could:

- Dissolve or weaken salt structures.
- Activate unknown crystalline chemistry.
- Encourage microbial contamination from your own body.
- Alter waxy or resinous surfaces.
- Disturb the chamber’s long dryness.

Your exhalation should be captured, condensed, scrubbed, or otherwise prevented from entering the room.

## 5. Protect yourself from the dryness

Even if the air is not toxic, it is aggressively dry. It is described as mineral, cool, clean, and almost hard against the skin. That is not ordinary desert dryness. It is dryness like salt, wax, sealed pores, and stable chemistry. An unprotected human would lose moisture quickly from eyes, lips, throat, nasal passages, and skin.

### Skin preparation

Do not enter with exposed skin if you can avoid it. Wear a sealed suit. If any skin must be exposed:

- Do not apply perfumed lotions, oils, or creams that could shed volatile compounds.
- Use only minimal, approved barrier products if they will remain inside your suit.
- Expect tightness, itching, or cracking if exposure is prolonged.

Your body is a water source. Your skin sheds. Your oils are foreign chemistry. Cover yourself.

### Eyes

The dryness and brightness can strain the eyes rapidly.

Use:

- Sealed goggles or a full-face helmet.
- Anti-fog treatment, because your own breath or sweat may fog the inside of your visor.
- Clear or very lightly tinted lenses at first. The chamber is bright, but you do not want to darken it so much that you misjudge depth.
- Anti-glare coating if available.
- No contact lenses unless your helmet is perfectly sealed. Contacts may dry painfully and increase eye irritation.

The light is sourceless and reflective. It may not cast strong shadows. This can make surfaces appear flatter than they are. Be careful judging relief.

### Mouth and throat

If your helmet fails or you must briefly breathe local air, do not expect comfort. The dryness may feel sharp or hard in the throat. Do not try to relieve it by drinking openly. Water should remain sealed inside your hydration system.

---

## 6. Carry water, but treat water as hazardous material

You must hydrate. Cool dry environments are deceptive; you can dehydrate without feeling hot. But in this chamber, free water is an alien substance.
