## Preparation for Visiting the Green Engine Hollow  
### A practical guide for an ordinary human multiverse traveler

Treat this place as what it is: not a ruin, not a garden, not a swamp, but a slow, saturated, living machine-room. It is wet, warm, enclosed, patient, and extremely good at absorbing things. That includes water, sound, vibration, abandoned objects, and — if you are careless — the small traces you leave behind.

An ordinary human traveler can visit it safely, but only if prepared for humidity, soft footing, low visibility under dense canopy, faint bioelectric strangeness, old machinery that is not entirely dead, and an ecology in which stillness invites colonization. The goal is not to conquer the hollow, map it aggressively, or harvest it. The goal is to pass through with minimal disturbance and maximum self-control.

The best preparation can be summarized in four rules:

1. **Stay as dry as possible.**  
2. **Move slowly and deliberately.**  
3. **Leave nothing still, nothing loose, and nothing behind.**  
4. **Respect the place as an integrated organism-machine system.**

Below is a detailed preparation dossier.

---

# 1. Understand What You Are Entering

Before choosing gear, understand the logic of the location.

It is a low basin. Water has no clear outlet. The ground is not ordinary soil but a thick, spongy mat of roots, peat, leaf mould, moss, rhizomes, and softened old construction material. Press a foot down and water rises. There are black pools, dripping leaves, weeping brick, warm humid air, and a canopy so complete that daylight becomes green-gold and particulate.

Everything is vegetal, but not in a wild, chaotic way. The plant-life is established, settled, architectural. It forms walls, vaults, screens, cushions, ropes, shelves, and living machinery. Old industrial equipment — pumps, boilers, pipes, flywheels, chimneys, gauges, valves — has been incorporated into this system. The machines are not active in the normal sense, but they are not fully dead either. Some parts move very slowly. Water moves through old tubing and living stems. There is faint electricity in mossy conduits, leaves, and damp metal. There is a low, wet hum. Somewhere, an engine or pump keeps time with a deep thump and a long sigh.

Fauna is rare. The soundscape is made by water, leaves, wood, metal, pressure, and hidden flows. The place is loud in a layered, continuous way, but not sharp. It feels inhabited without being populated by animals.

Most importantly, the hollow seems to operate by a rule of adoption: anything that remains still long enough becomes substrate. Metal is furred with moss. Stone is entered by rootlets. Water is lifted, held, exhaled, condensed, and drunk again. The place does not attack. It incorporates.

So preparation is not only physical. It is behavioral.

You are entering a system that rewards patience and punishes carelessness by quietly absorbing your mistakes.

# 2. Decide the Kind of Visit You Should Make

For an ordinary human, this is best treated as a **short, carefully controlled visit**, not a casual stroll and certainly not an overnight expedition unless absolutely necessary.

## Recommended visit profile

- **Duration:** 1–3 hours for a first visit.  
- **Party size:** 2–3 people. Solo entry is not recommended.  
- **Insertion point:** the rim or one of the drier brick/concrete shelves, not the central chamber.  
- **Objective:** observation, orientation, quiet study.  
- **Avoid:** prolonged stays near the central engine, camping, sampling machine parts, forcing paths, or leaving equipment unattended.

If you have multiverse travel capability, establish your staging area **outside** the hollow if possible. If the transition point must be inside, choose the driest available rim and create a clean, temporary threshold. Do not open a portal, doorway, or transit field deep in the wettest center unless emergency requires it. The central chamber is humid, acoustically resonant, electrically faint, and filled with water-moving machinery. It is not a good place for delicate transit equipment.

## Before entering, check:

- Return device or recall anchor.
- Time limit.
- Buddy system.
- Emergency extraction signal.
- Dry staging area.
- Decontamination plan.
- Air and environmental monitors.
- Communication method that does not rely on loud sound.

If your return mechanism depends on electronics, assume humidity, condensation, and faint bioelectric fields will be hostile to it. Keep it sealed, insulated, and on your body. Do not set it down. Do not hang it on a pipe. Do not leave it in a pocket that may be pressed against wet vegetation.

# 3. Physical and Medical Preparation

The hollow is not described as overtly lethal, but it is physically demanding in quiet ways. Warm humid air, saturated ground, uneven footing, and low-level sensory pressure can fatigue an ordinary human faster than expected.

## You should be reasonably prepared for:

- Heat stress.
- Dehydration.
- Fungal skin irritation.
- Trench foot or macerated skin.
- Slips and falls on wet roots.
- Minor cuts from old metal or cracked masonry.
- Respiratory irritation from spores, pollen, mist, or metallic humidity.
- Claustrophobia or disorientation in an enclosed green chamber.
- Time distortion caused by slow rhythms and lack of ordinary landmarks.

## Medical precautions

Before visiting, an ordinary human should ideally:

- Be up to date on **tetanus** vaccination.
- Have no open wounds. If you have cuts, cover them with waterproof dressings and avoid immersion.
- Carry antihistamines if prone to plant allergies.
- Carry antifungal powder or cream.
- Carry basic antibiotics or antiseptic wipes, but do not use them casually in the environment.
- Avoid entry if you have severe asthma, uncontrolled heart conditions, or a compromised immune system unless you have specialist protection.
- Be cautious if you have a pacemaker, implant, neurostimulator, or other sensitive medical electronics. The place has faint distributed current and damp conductive surfaces. It is probably weak, but unknown fields in a wet environment deserve respect.

If you begin to feel dizzy, headachy, unusually euphoric, overly calm, or strangely reluctant to leave, treat that as a warning. It may be heat, humidity, mild gas exposure, psychological entrainment, or something subtler. Signal your partner and move toward the rim.

# 4. Clothing: Dress for Warm Saturation

The wrong clothing will make this place miserable. Cotton becomes wet and stays wet. Leather absorbs water and may become food for slow colonization. Loose straps and rough fabrics give rootlets and tendrils things to catch. Heavy boots sink. Thin shoes offer no protection.

You want clothing that is:

- Waterproof or highly water-resistant.
- Quick-drying.
- Smooth-surfaced.
- Breathable where possible.
- Sealed at ankles, wrists, and neck.
- Not overly insulated, because the air is warm and humid.

## Recommended clothing

### Base layer

Use a thin, moisture-wicking synthetic layer. Avoid cotton entirely. You will sweat, and in this humidity sweat will not evaporate easily.

A lightweight long-sleeved shirt and long underwear or leggings are useful because they reduce skin exposure to sap, condensation, spores, mites, and faint electrical tingling.

### Mid layer

Minimal. The hollow is warm. If you chill easily, bring a very light synthetic or fleece layer in a dry bag. Do not wear heavy insulation.

### Outer layer

A lightweight waterproof-breathable shell is useful, but understand its limits: in saturated air, breathable membranes may not breathe well. You may end up wet from inside as much as outside.

Choose a smooth jacket with sealed seams. A hood is essential. The canopy drips constantly even when there is no rain.

A wide-brimmed hat with a drip skirt or neck cover is extremely useful. It keeps falling condensation off your face and neck.

### Legwear

Long, quick-drying pants with a smooth exterior. Gaiters are strongly recommended. They keep root tips, moss fragments, mites, and water out of your boots.

Avoid baggy fabric that can snag on stems.

### Footwear

This is one of the most important choices.

The ground is soft, wet, and uneven. There are hidden roots, cracked bricks, old metal, and black pools. You need:

- Waterproof boots.
- Good ankle support.
- Puncture-resistant soles if possible.
- Broad, stable soles to distribute weight on peat.
- Lugs enough for grip, but not so aggressive that they tear the root-felt.
- Smooth enough uppers that roots and moss do not cling excessively.

Avoid open sandals. Avoid canvas shoes. Avoid heavy metal cleats that bite and scar the living floor.

If the ground is extremely soft, consider wide bog shoes, mud shoes, or removable traction pads that spread your weight. However, do not bring anything with sharp spikes.

Bring spare socks in a sealed dry bag. Changing socks halfway through the visit can be a major comfort and health benefit.

### Gloves

Wear thin, tough gloves if you expect to touch anything. Nitrile gloves are useful under lightweight work gloves if you are sensitive to sap, rust, or biological films.

Do not go barehanded unless necessary. The surfaces are wet, old, and sometimes faintly charged.

### Eye and face protection

The air drips. Spores, pollen, fine plant particles, or condensation may fall from above. Old metal may shed mineral dust.

Use:

- Safety glasses or clear goggles.
- Anti-fog treatment.
- A buff or light respirator mask if the air feels heavy.

If you wear contact lenses, consider switching to glasses for this visit. Humidity and airborne droplets can make contacts uncomfortable and increase infection risk.

### Respiratory protection

You do not necessarily need a full respirator, but carrying a compact P100 or organic-vapor/particulate mask is wise. The smell is pleasant but close: warm leaves, wet bark, rust, peat, and a faint metallic or electrical sharpness.

Use respiratory protection if:

- The air feels thick.
- You see drifting spores or fine mist.
- You feel chest tightness.
- You are near bubbles rising from black pools.
- You are near old vents, gauges, or dilating flower-like structures.

# 5. Carry System: Everything Must Stay Dry and Attached

The hollow is hard on equipment. Moisture enters seams, condenses inside electronics, beads on lenses, and creeps into folds. The faint electrical presence and conductive wet surfaces also argue for careful protection.

## Backpack or harness

Use a small, clean, waterproof pack with smooth fabric. Avoid external straps that flap or drag. Everything should be internal or secured.

Line the pack with a dry bag. Then put individual items in smaller dry bags. Clear bags are useful because they let you see contents without opening them in humid air.

Use desiccant packs inside sealed bags, but do not rely on them completely. In this environment, the best protection is not opening the bag until you are out.

## Essential rule: nothing touches the ground unattended

If you set a tool, camera, sensor, or pack down, do not leave it. The place adopts still objects. A metal tool left on moss may be furred with rootlets or surrounded by condensation within a short time. A forgotten strap may become a trellis. A dropped wrapper may be folded into the floor.

If you must rest your pack, place it on a small closed-cell foam pad or a dry shelf of old brick, and keep it attended. Even then, do not leave it for long.

## Return device and critical electronics

Your multiverse return anchor, recall beacon, emergency communicator, or equivalent should be:

- Carried on your body.
- Kept in a waterproof case.
- Shielded if possible.
- Insulated from direct contact with wet vegetation and metal.
- Checked before entry.
- Set to an automatic fail-safe recall if your vital signs or timer trigger it.

Do not depend solely on radio beacons. The canopy, water, and conductive plant mass may interfere. Do not assume ordinary GPS-like systems will work. The hollow is enclosed and may have magnetic anomalies from buried iron, pipes, and faint current.

# 6. Navigation: Do Not Trust the Path

There are paths, but they are not human paths. They are channels of least resistance maintained by water flow, heat currents, and the slow negotiation of plants. They close if ignored and widen if used. They respond to pressure and persistence, not intention.

This means navigation must be conservative.

## Navigation problems to expect

- The horizon is close.
- The walls look similar.
- The canopy breaks daylight into shifting green-gold patches.
- Magnetic compasses may be disturbed by iron and faint current.
- Footprints may fill with water or be absorbed quickly.
- Paths may narrow behind you.
- Sound may distort orientation.
- The place has a slow, hypnotic rhythm that can make you lose track of time.

## Navigation preparation

Use multiple redundant methods:

- Inertial or gyroscopic compass if available.
- Visual compass if the sky is partially visible.
- Dead reckoning.
- Step counting or distance estimation.
- Short-range tether if moving into dense areas.
- Temporary markers that you remove completely.
- A timer.
- A buddy whose job is to watch the return route.

Do not tie ribbons, strings, or markers to living stems. If you must mark a route, use removable, highly visible, non-persistent markers and take them all back. Better still: use a tether that one person manages.

If you use a tether:

- Use smooth line, not fibrous rope.
- Do not tie it to plants or pipes.
- Do not leave it unattended.
- Keep it from touching wet ground for long stretches.
- Retrieve it fully.

## If a path begins to close

Do not panic. Do not hack. Do not force yourself deeper.

Stop. Breathe. Check your timer and compass. Back out slowly along the line of least resistance. If you have a tether, follow it. If not, move toward the memory of brighter air, drier footing, or the rim.

The hollow is small enough that the boundary is near, but panic can make you push inward instead of outward.

# 8. Light and Sight Preparation

The canopy admits daylight only as broken shafts. It is beautiful, but it can be disorienting. Shadows shift. Green dominates. Depth perception may suffer. Condensation may bead on lenses.

Bring a soft, low-heat light. Do not use bright floodlights unless necessary. They may startle you, distort the environment, and perhaps affect dilating plant structures.

A headlamp with red or amber mode is useful for preserving night vision and reducing visual harshness, but keep intensity low.

Avoid flash photography if it causes visible movement in nearby flowers or valve-like structures. If a plant dilates in response to light, heat, or vibration, step back.

If you wear glasses, bring anti-fog treatment and spare cloths. If you bring a camera, keep it in a sealed housing with a clear wipe. Expect condensation. Do not change lenses or open cases inside the wettest areas.

# 9. Sound Preparation

The hollow is louder than expected. Not sharp, but full. Dripping, ticking, gurgling, rasping leaves, creaking stems, muffled knocks, low electrical hum. This can become psychologically enveloping.

Prepare by:

- Bringing low-profile ear protection if you are sound-sensitive.
- Avoiding total noise cancellation; you need to hear environmental changes.
- Learning to distinguish important sounds.
- Keeping your own voice low.
- Avoiding percussion, shouting, metal striking, or heavy footsteps.

## Sounds to notice

- Regular dripping: normal.
- Soft leaf rasp: normal.
- Occasional distant knock: likely the old engine or pump.
- Low wet hum: likely distributed moisture-electricity or water movement.
- Sucking or gurgling: hidden flow through pipes or roots.
- Hissing: possible pressure release; move away.
- Sudden change in rhythm: warning.
- New metallic strain: warning.
- Silence where there was sound: warning.

The place uses sound as part of its organization. Do not add unnecessary noise.

# 10. Movement: How to Walk in the Hollow

Movement here is not hiking. It is closer to wading through a living architecture that notices pressure.

## Basic movement rules

- Walk slowly.
- Place feet deliberately.
- Test uncertain ground with a staff.
- Avoid stepping directly into black pools.
- Avoid stepping on mossy pipes, valves, gauges, or old metal plates.
- Do not kick stems or push roughly through leaves.
- Do not climb on boilers, engines, or root buttresses.
- Do not lean on suspicious structures.
- Keep your center of gravity low.
- Keep your pack from brushing vegetation.

## Foot placement

The ground yields. That is normal. But if water rises quickly around your foot or you feel suction, shift weight and choose another step.

Prefer:

- Older brick or concrete shelves, if stable.
- Dense root mats that feel firm.
- Areas where water flow has maintained a shallow channel.
- Places where the plant cover is less thick.

Avoid:

- Perfectly black pools.
- Glossy depressions.
- Moss over unknown holes.
- Cracked metal plates.
- Bulged boiler surfaces.
- Areas where roots are tightly coiled around pipes.
- Places where steam, warmth, or humming is concentrated.

## If you must pass through dense growth

Do not tear. Use gentle persistence. Part stems slowly, wait, and let the mass accommodate you. If it does not accommodate, do not force it. Find another angle.

The paths respond to pressure and persistence over time. A single traveler cannot command them, but a careful traveler can move through them with less damage.

# 11. Water, Pools, and Wet Hazards

Water is the hollow’s medium. It is near, under, above, and inside everything.

## Drinking water

Do not drink water from the hollow.

Even if it looks clear in thin streams or collected droplets, it may contain:

- Root exudates.
- Tannins.
- Rust.
- Metal ions.
- Microbial films.
- Unknown spores.
- Faint electrical charge.
- Organic decay products.

Bring your own drinking water.

## Immersion

If you fall into a pool or sink into soft peat:

1. Do not thrash.
2. Spread your weight.
3. Use a staff or pole horizontally.
4. Move slowly toward firmer ground.
5. Avoid swallowing water.
6. Exit as soon as possible.
7. Rinse exposed skin with clean water later.
8. Dry thoroughly.
9. Treat any cuts.
10. Watch for signs of infection or skin irritation.

Black pools are not necessarily deep, but they may hide roots, old debris, suction pockets, or hollows. Treat them as dangerous mirrors.

## Wet feet

In warm saturated conditions, feet can deteriorate quickly. Bring spare socks and foot powder. If your skin begins to wrinkle deeply, sting, or soften, take a break, dry your feet, and change socks if possible.

Do not walk barefoot.

# 12. Machinery: Old Iron with a Living Pulse

The machines are among the most fascinating and most hazardous elements. They are not merely ruins. Some are incorporated into water movement, warmth, and perhaps the hollow’s slow metabolism.

## Rules for machinery

- Do not touch moving parts.
- Do not touch parts that hum or tingle.
- Do not force valves.
- Do not turn wheels.
- Do not climb boilers.
- Do not lean on pipes.
- Do not insert tools into openings.
- Do not stand over vents, grates, or dilating structures.
- Do not block water channels.
- Do not remove machine parts.

Even a flywheel turning very slowly can pinch. Even a quiet pipe can hold warm water, pressure, or weak current. Even a gauge twitching with air pressure may be connected to something larger.

## Central engine chamber

The chamber-like clearing with the old pump or engine is the heart of the hollow. It is beautiful, but it is not a lounge. It is a center of organization.

If you enter it:

- Keep your visit brief.
- Do not touch the engine.
- Do not stand in the trough.
- Do not put hands in the water flow.
- Do not place instruments inside the mechanism.
- Do not synchronize your breathing too deeply with its rhythm unless you remain consciously in control.
- Count the thump-pause-sigh cycle from a distance.
- Leave if the rhythm changes.

If the engine’s sound becomes faster, shorter, or irregular, or if the sigh becomes a hiss, withdraw. The system may be shifting pressure, water flow, or heat. You do not want to be inside that adjustment.

# 13. Electricity and Bioelectric Phenomena

The place has faint distributed electricity. It is not described as violently dangerous, but it is present: hum in wet conduits, tingling in leaves, weak charge along moss-covered cables, glittering droplets.

An ordinary human should treat this with caution.

## Precautions

- Avoid touching moss-covered cables with bare skin.
- Avoid touching metal and water at the same time.
- Remove rings, bracelets, watches, and conductive jewelry before entry.
- Keep tools insulated.
- Do not let wet clothing bridge metal surfaces.
- Do not lean on pipes while touching leaves or water.
- If you feel tingling, move away.
- If your hair rises or you smell ozone strongly, leave the area.
- If medical implants behave oddly, exit immediately.

Do not try to harvest current. Do not attach wires. Do not attempt to power your equipment from the hollow. That is exactly the kind of behavior that turns a visitor into part of the system.

# 14. Air, Gas, and Breathing

The air is warm and full. It feels inhabited. It may be perfectly safe, but a low basin with peat, standing water, decay, old machinery, and no clear outlet deserves caution.

## Possible air hazards

- High humidity.
- Condensation droplets.
- Spores or pollen-like particles.
- Tannic or metallic mist.
- Methane from anaerobic peat.
- Carbon dioxide pooling in low spots.
- Hydrogen sulfide in black sediment.
- Ozone-like sharpness near electrical moisture.
- Warm vapor from engine-heated vessels.

## Prepare by bringing

- A compact multi-gas detector if possible.
- A particulate respirator.
- A small organic-vapor filter if metallic or electrical sharpness becomes strong.
- A bandana or buff for casual moisture filtering.

## Warning signs

Leave or move to higher, drier air if you notice:

- Sudden headache.
- Dizziness.
- Nausea.
- Chest tightness.
- Unusual breathlessness.
- Rotten-egg smell.
- Sour or sulfurous smell.
- Bubbles rising continuously from pools.
- Air that feels thicker than before.
- A change in sound, as if the hollow is inhaling and not exhaling.

Do not use open flame to test air. Ever.

# 15. Fire and Heat Discipline

No fire. No smoking. No sparks. No hot work.

The hollow contains peat, dried leaf layers under canopy, old wood, moss, woody stems, possible methane, old oil residues, and electrical moisture. Even if the environment is wet, fire in peat can smolder invisibly. Heat may also trigger pressure changes in old sealed vessels.

Use low-heat LED lighting. Avoid devices that become hot. Keep batteries sealed and protected. If a battery becomes warm, disconnect it if safe, place it in a fireproof sealed bag, and prepare to leave.

# 17. Waste and Contamination Discipline

This is crucial for multiverse travelers.

The hollow absorbs. If you leave organic waste, it may be folded into the floor. That sounds natural, but you are introducing foreign material into a finely balanced system. If you leave plastic, metal, or electronics, the place will adopt them, but they may disrupt its slow chemistry. If you carry something out of the hollow into another world, you may carry spores, rootlets, charged moisture, or machine-plant symbionts where they do not belong.

## Rule: pack out everything

That includes:

- Food wrappers.
- Crumbs.
- Used tissues.
- Wipes.
- Bandages.
- Gloves.
- Broken gear.
- Battery casings.
- Hair.
- Nail clippings if you are that careless.
- Bodily waste if unavoidable.
- Contaminated water used for rinsing.
- Debris brushed from clothing.

Do not bury waste. Do not burn it. Do not feed it to the hollow.

## No souvenirs

Do not take:

- Leaves.
- Seedpods.
- Moss.
- Root fragments.
- Rust flakes.
- Machine parts.
- Water samples unless scientifically necessary.
- Fungal-like growths.
- Gauges or valves.
- “Small harmless” artifacts.

If you must sample for serious study, take the smallest possible amount, seal it triple, document it, and prepare to quarantine it. Better: rely on photographs, sound recordings, sketches, and passive sensor data.

# 18. Psychological Preparation

The hollow is not frightening in a conventional way, but it can be psychologically heavy. It is enclosed, green, wet, loud in a soft way, and slow. There are almost no animal voices. The machines count time in deep knocks. The vegetation seems attentive. You may feel watched, though not by eyes.

Prepare your mind.

## Mental hazards

- Claustrophobia.
- Time distortion.
- Over-fascination.
- Desire to linger.
- Hypnotic entrainment to slow rhythms.
- Sensory monotony.
- Feeling absorbed into the environment.
- Mild dissociation.

## Mental preparation techniques

- Set a clear time limit.
- Use a timer that vibrates gently.
- Carry an object from a dry, inert, ordinary world as a grounding token.
- Speak quietly with your companion at intervals.
- Do not stare at the central engine for too long.
- Keep your name, purpose, and return plan in mind.
- If you feel unusually peaceful to the point of not wanting to leave, treat that as a warning.

The place is patient. You should not be. You have a return window.

# 19. Ethical Preparation

An ordinary human traveler should prepare ethically as well as practically.

This location is not a resource depot. It is not a ruin to be scavenged. It is not a machine to be restarted. It is not a garden to be pruned. It is a settled integration of living tissue and old industry. It has achieved a balance through patience.

Your visit should add as little as possible.

## Ethical rules

- Do not restart machines.
- Do not clear paths.
- Do not cut living tissue unless emergency requires it.
- Do not remove artifacts.
- Do not introduce organisms.
- Do not change water flow.
- Do not make permanent marks.
- Do not create noise pollution.
- Do not try to “wake” the hollow.
- Do not attempt to harvest atmosphere, heat, sugar, electricity, or time.

If you find yourself thinking, “What would happen if I turned this valve?” the answer is probably: you would become part of the story the hollow tells next. Do not.

# 20. Entry Procedure

A good entry routine reduces risk.

## At the threshold

1. Check all seals on dry bags.
2. Confirm return device is active and sealed.
3. Set time limit.
4. Check gas monitor and environmental sensors.
5. Confirm communication plan.
6. Put on gloves, eye protection, and face covering if needed.
7. Tighten straps so nothing dangles.
8. Remove jewelry.
9. Test footing on the first soft ground.
10. Enter slowly.

Do not rush in. Stand at the edge and listen. Notice the drip rhythm, the warmth, the smell, the light. Let your body adjust to the humidity.

Choose a route along the drier rim if possible. Avoid heading straight into the deepest center.

# 22. Observing the Central Engine

The central chamber is the hollow’s heart. If you go there, treat it like a mix of chapel, pump room, and sleeping animal.

## Observation protocol

- Approach from a stable route.
- Stop at a respectful distance.
- Do not circle it rapidly.
- Do not touch water channels.
- Do not place sensors into the engine.
- Observe the rhythm: thump, pause, sigh.
- Note whether nearby leaves angle toward warmth.
- Note whether moss thickens near vibration.
- Note whether vines tighten near pipes.
- Leave before the rhythm changes.

If the engine makes a new sound, if water begins to rise in the trough, if nearby plants suddenly tighten, or if the air becomes warmer, withdraw.

Do not attempt to communicate with it. It may be part of a larger intelligence, or it may simply be a mechanism integrated into ecology. Either way, your attempts to interact are likely to be interpreted as pressure, vibration, or substrate.

# 23. If You Must Camp

Do not camp if you can avoid it.

If an emergency forces an overnight stay, keep it as short and non-invasive as possible.

## Minimum camp rules

- Choose the driest rim.
- Do not cut vegetation.
- Do not stake into roots.
- Do not build a fire.
- Do not leave gear on the ground.
- Do not sleep directly on peat.
- Do not tie shelter to living stems unless absolutely unavoidable; if unavoidable, use wide soft straps and remove without damage.
- Use a free-standing shelter if possible.
- Keep return device sealed and on your body.
- Set automatic recall if available.
- Keep a watch if there are multiple people.
- Leave at first light or earlier if conditions change.

A better option is to retreat to the threshold or another less saturated environment rather than sleep inside the hollow.

---

# 24. Emergency Procedures

## If you become lost

Stop. Do not run. Check timer, compass, and last known route. Listen for the engine or water flow. Move toward drier footing and the rim. If you have a tether, follow it. If not, choose a direction and move slowly, marking only temporarily and retrieving markers.

The hollow is enclosed. The boundary is near. Panic is more dangerous than the plants.

## If a path closes behind you

Do not force forward. Back up slowly. Use your hands to gently part stems, but do not tear. If the mass resists strongly, stop and try another angle. Loud noise or aggressive cutting may only make the environment less cooperative.

## If you sink in soft peat

Do not jerk. Spread your weight. Lean back if possible. Place a staff, pole, or pack horizontally under you. Move limbs slowly and broadly. Crawl toward firmer material. Once out, check footing before standing.

## If you fall into water

Get out quickly. Do not drink or swallow water. Rinse later. Dry skin. Treat wounds. Inspect for mites or root fragments. If you feel chilled or ill, end the visit.

## If you feel electrical tingling

Move away from cables, pipes, wet metal, and mossy conduits. Remove conductive items if safe. Dry your hands and gloves. If tingling spreads or becomes painful, leave the hollow.

## If you smell gas or feel dizzy

Leave immediately. Do not test with flame. Do not linger to collect samples. Activate emergency recall if necessary.

## If machinery activates more strongly

Withdraw. Do not investigate. Do not touch. Do not photograph at close range. A slow system can still release pressure, hot water, steam, or mechanical motion.

## If you are injured

Control bleeding with waterproof dressings. Avoid immersion. Keep the wound sealed. Exit as soon as possible. Seek medical attention afterward, especially for puncture wounds, rust exposure, or infection signs.

# 25. Exit Procedure

Leaving is not just walking out. You must also prevent carrying the hollow elsewhere.

## At the exit threshold

1. Stop before crossing into your staging area.
2. Inspect clothing for rootlets, moss, seeds, spores, or green film.
3. Brush debris into a sealed bag, not onto the ground.
4. Wipe boots and gear with a damp cloth, then bag the cloth.
5. Check seams, gaiters, pack straps, and treads.
6. Seal wet clothing in a dry bag.
7. Protect return electronics from moisture before transit.
8. Hydrate and cool down if needed.
9. Record observations while fresh.
10. Leave before fatigue makes you careless.

If possible, do not transport directly into a clean multiverse hub while still wearing hollow gear. Use an antechamber, airlock, or decontamination stage.

# 26. Post-Visit Decontamination

The place manufactures atmosphere and integrates matter. You should assume that tiny fragments, spores, root hairs, or charged moisture may cling to you.

## Gear decontamination

- Dry everything thoroughly.
- Inspect electronics for condensation.
- Open devices only in a controlled dry area.
- Look for green staining, fine root threads, or mineral crust.
- Clean smooth surfaces with wipes, but bag the wipes.
- Quarantine gear if anything seems to be growing.
- Do not compost debris from the hollow in another world.

## Personal decontamination

- Shower.
- Wash hair.
- Clean under nails.
- Dry feet carefully.
- Apply antifungal powder if prone to skin issues.
- Inspect skin for rash, bites, or irritation.
- Monitor breathing for 24 hours.
- Seek care if cough, fever, rash, dizziness, or infection develops.

## Psychological aftercare

You may find ordinary dry environments feel thin or too fast after the hollow. That is normal. The place has a long patience. Give yourself time to re-acclimate. Drink water, eat something ordinary, and do not make major decisions while still mentally saturated by its rhythm.

If you keep hearing the thump-pause-sigh long after leaving, rest. If it persists, consider whether it was real sound, memory, or something acquired. Report it if your multiverse travel authority tracks such effects.

# 28. The Visitor’s Attitude

The final preparation is internal.

You should enter the hollow with the attitude of someone stepping into a cathedral that is also a machine, a forest that is also a factory, and a slow animal made of leaves, water, and old iron.

Do not try to dominate it. Do not try to speed it up. Do not try to take it apart. Do not assume that because it is quiet, it is inactive. Do not assume that because it is green, it is soft. Do not assume that because it is small, it is simple.

Be clean. Be dry. Be slow. Be attentive.

If you do only one thing right, make it this: **leave no stillness behind.** Everything you abandon, forget, or allow to remain too long may be adopted. That includes tools, wrappers, footprints, vibrations, heat, noise, and perhaps even the habit of standing too long in one place.

Visit lightly. Observe closely. Record carefully. Leave before the hollow begins to include you in its next cycle.

And when you return to drier worlds, remember the sound: drip, rasp, hum, thump, pause, long wet sigh. That is the place counting time without needing you to witness it.
