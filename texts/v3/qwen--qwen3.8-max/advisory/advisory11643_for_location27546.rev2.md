Prepare as if you are entering a living machine, not a scenic cave.

This place is small enough to be taken in at a glance, but dense enough to punish carelessness. It is hot, wet, dim, loud, animal, industrial, and still unfinished. Its dangers are not dramatic monsters or overt magic. They are saturation, heat, bad air, slick footing, electricity near water, moving machinery, startled animals, and the slow accumulation of small mistakes. An ordinary human multiverse traveler should therefore prepare with humility, redundancy, and a strong bias toward non-interference.

The central rule is simple:

**Stay cool enough, dry enough, breathing well, on stable footing, electrically safe, and socially unobtrusive.**

Everything else follows from that.

---

## 1. Understand what kind of place you are entering

This is not a wilderness cavern and not a normal town. It is a utility-shaped settlement: a compact underground room organized around water movement, heat, ventilation, and maintenance. It has become town-like because people must live near the machines, but its real inhabitants are water, pipes, fans, pumps, insects, bats, rats, fungi, rust, and the slow pressure of stone.

You should expect:

- No sky, no horizon, no natural day-night cycle.
- Low but not cramped ceilings; intimate, but not necessarily claustrophobic.
- Constant heat and high humidity.
- Wet rock, wet metal, wet concrete, wet clothing.
- Dim amber lighting and large shadowed zones.
- Loud layered sound: fans, pumps, water, machinery, insects, bats, frogs.
- A strong smell of wet stone, iron, grease, guano, mold, and stagnant condensation.
- Sparse human presence; the place may feel larger than its population.
- Fauna that is not merely present but dominant.
- Construction and erosion happening at the same time.
- Floors that may be solid in one spot and only a crust over saturated ground in another.
- Water that does not rush dramatically but persistently intrudes.

The psychological effect can be strange. It may feel both enclosed and exposed: you can see most of the settlement at once, yet every shadow has life or machinery in it. It is loud but not hurried. It is inhabited but not crowded. It is built but not finished. Prepare mentally for a place that is not trying to be comfortable for you.

## 2. Decide whether you should go at all

An ordinary human can visit, but not every ordinary human should.

Reconsider or shorten the visit if you have:

- Severe claustrophobia or panic responses in windowless spaces.
- Serious respiratory illness, poor tolerance for humid heat, or heart problems.
- Open wounds, skin infections, or compromised immunity.
- Strong aversion to insects, bats, rats, or confined industrial spaces.
- Poor balance or difficulty moving on wet uneven surfaces.
- Sensitivity to noise, especially continuous mechanical drone and sudden metallic sounds.
- Dependence on precise circadian cues; there is no daylight here.

If you go, treat the first visit as a short reconnaissance, not a long residence. The environment is survivable, but it will wear on you through heat, damp, noise, and sensory monotony.

## 3. Prepare your body before arrival

### Heat and humidity acclimation

The air is thick enough to feel like a damp cloth. Sweat will not evaporate well. This means ordinary exertion becomes more dangerous than it looks.

Before visiting:

- Spend time in warm humid environments if possible.
- Practice light work while carefully monitoring hydration.
- Learn your own early signs of heat stress: headache, nausea, irritability, dizziness, cramping, excessive fatigue, confusion, or sudden chills.
- Avoid arriving sleep-deprived, hungover, dehydrated, or overheated.

### Hydration discipline

Bring water and electrolytes. Do not assume local water is safe.

You should be able to drink small amounts frequently without being told. In hot humid spaces, thirst is a late signal. If you wait until you are desperate for water, you are already behind.

### Foot and skin preparation

This environment attacks skin through dampness. Blisters, fungal infections, chafing, and macerated feet are mundane but serious risks.

Before arrival:

- Make sure boots are broken in.
- Trim toenails.
- Treat any existing cuts or abrasions with waterproof dressings.
- Bring antifungal powder or cream.
- Bring spare dry socks in sealed waterproof bags.
- Avoid cotton next to skin if possible.

### Respiratory caution

The air may contain:

- Dust from rock and construction.
- Fungal spores.
- Bat guano particles.
- Ammonia-like smells.
- Grease and machine fumes.
- Possible stagnant-air pockets.
- Possible geothermal or decomposition gases, depending on local conditions.

Even if the air seems breathable, a traveler should be ready to protect the lungs. A wet, guano-rich, fungal, industrial underground space is not a place to breathe casually if you have any doubt.

## 4. Medical preparation

Consult a travel medicine or occupational health professional if possible. Because this is a multiverse destination, do not assume local pathogens match your home biology. Treat unknown organisms as potentially hazardous.

Consider discussing:

- Tetanus protection.
- Hepatitis A and B protection.
- Typhoid or other enteric precautions if food/water contamination is possible.
- Leptospirosis risk in wet rodent-contaminated environments.
- Fungal exposure risks, especially around guano and damp organic matter.
- Skin and wound care in humid environments.
- Antidiarrheal and oral rehydration supplies.
- Broad-spectrum first aid supplies appropriate for remote industrial travel.
- Personal medications, including antihistamines if insect contact is likely.

Do not rely on local medicine unless you understand its compatibility with your body and your multiversal travel conditions.

### Carry a compact medical kit including:

- Antiseptic wipes.
- Wound irrigation syringe or clean squeeze bottle.
- Waterproof dressings.
- Blister care.
- Antifungal powder or cream.
- Burn dressings.
- Eye wash or sterile saline.
- Tweezers.
- Nail clippers or small scissors.
- Electrolyte packets.
- Anti-chafe balm.
- Pain and fever relief suitable for you.
- Any personal emergency medication.

Because humidity and infection are the quiet enemies, small wounds deserve immediate attention. A tiny scratch from rusty metal, rat claws, or insect bites should not be ignored.

## 5. Clothing: dress for wet heat and industrial hazards

You want clothing that protects skin while allowing sweat to move away. You also want to avoid anything that can snag on machinery, catch fire easily, or stay wet forever.

### Base layer

Use moisture-wicking fabric, not cotton. Long sleeves and long trousers are preferable because they protect against:

- Insects.
- Abrasive rock.
- Hot metal.
- Drips of unknown water.
- Guano and fungal spores.
- Sharp construction debris.

A thin long-sleeved shirt and lightweight long underwear or work trousers may be better than heavy layers. The environment is hot, but the surfaces and splashes are not clean.

### Outer layer

A tough, breathable work shirt and trousers are better than a fully waterproof shell. In high humidity, waterproof clothing can trap sweat until you are soaked from inside. Use splash protection only where needed.

A light hood or hard hat with neck protection may help under dripping areas.

### Footwear

Boots are critical.

Ideal boots:

- Fit well and are already broken in.
- Have ankle support.
- Have slip-resistant soles for wet stone, wet concrete, and metal grating.
- Are resistant to oil, grease, and mild heat.
- Have some puncture resistance.
- Can be dried or at least drained.
- Do not conduct electricity unnecessarily through exposed metal parts.

Avoid:

- Sandals.
- Canvas shoes.
- Smooth-soled fashion boots.
- Heavy mountaineering boots that cannot dry.
- Boots with damaged soles.

Bring:

- At least two pairs of dry socks, preferably three.
- Foot powder.
- A small towel.
- Spare bootlaces.
- Gaiters if there is much mud or shallow water.

If you expect to cross wet areas, consider boots that can drain, but understand that drainable boots may also let contaminated water in. Better to avoid standing water whenever possible.

### Gloves

Bring at least two kinds:

- Thin nitrile gloves for biological contamination, dirty water, guano, dead insects, or suspect surfaces.
- Work gloves for hot valves, rough metal, scaffolding, and equipment handling.

Do not touch unknown pipes, cables, or machinery bare-handed. Some metal may be hot, electrically live, sharp, or contaminated.

### Head protection

A hard hat or sturdy bump cap is wise. The ceiling is not necessarily low everywhere, but there may be:

- Pipes.
- Cable trays.
- Dripping ledges.
- Scaffolding.
- Timber braces.
- Low-hanging fixtures.
- Falling droplets or flakes of mineral crust.

If there is active construction or fresh blasting work, head protection becomes essential.

### Eye protection

Clear, sealed safety glasses or goggles are useful. They protect against:

- Dripping water.
- Rust flecks.
- Insects flying into the face.
- Dust from guano or dry construction debris.
- Accidental snags near pipe racks.

Anti-fog treatment is valuable. In hot humid air, goggles can fog quickly.

### Hearing protection

The noise is not a single sharp blast but a layered industrial and animal drone. It can become exhausting.

Bring:

- Foam earplugs.
- Reusable filtered earplugs if you need to hear alarms or voices.
- Over-ear muffs if you will be near pumps or fans for long periods.

Be careful not to isolate yourself completely. In this environment, hearing a pump start, a fan change, a warning shout, or a new water sound may matter. Level-dependent hearing protection is ideal if available.

### Face and breathing protection

At minimum, carry a compact particulate mask. Better: a half-face respirator with appropriate filters for particulates, organic vapors, and acid gases if local advice supports it.

Potential airborne hazards:

- Fungal spores.
- Guano dust.
- Mineral dust.
- Ammonia-like odors.
- Machine fumes.
- Possible sewer or decomposition gases.
- Smoke or burnt insulation in a fault.

If you enter and the smell is strong enough to make your eyes water or your throat tighten, do not simply endure it. Protect your airway and reassess.

## 6. Essential gear

This is not a place to travel ultralight. However, it is also not a place to overload yourself with clumsy equipment. You want gear that is waterproof, rugged, simple, and easy to keep attached to your body.

### Lighting

Local lighting is dim, amber, and incomplete. Do not depend on it.

Carry:

- Primary headlamp.
- Secondary handheld light.
- Backup pocket light.
- Spare batteries in waterproof container.
- Amber or red mode to reduce insect attraction and avoid startling animals.
- A small diffuser or bounce method if you need to inspect something without glaring into others’ eyes.

Use light discipline. Bright white beams in this space will attract insects, disturb bats, annoy inhabitants, and make your own vision worse by reflecting off wet surfaces.

### Air monitoring

If possible, carry a small four-gas monitor:

- Oxygen.
- Carbon dioxide.
- Carbon monoxide.
- Hydrogen sulfide or combustible gases, depending on configuration.

This may seem excessive, but the place has stagnant corners, machinery, water, guano, heat, and possible geothermal or mine-related gases. Air that smells bad is not always the most dangerous air; some hazards are odorless.

If you cannot carry a full monitor, at least bring a compact oxygen/combustible gas detector and an escape respirator suitable for the likely hazards. Know its limits.

### Water and food

Carry your own water. Do not drink from the pool, drains, condensation, pipes, or taps unless you have verified local safety and treated the water appropriately.

Bring:

- At least two liters for a short visit; more if staying.
- Electrolyte tablets or powder.
- Sealed, low-crumb food.
- Food storage bags that close tightly.

Avoid messy, smelly, or crumbly food. Insects and rats will notice.

### Navigation and recording

The chamber is small, but disorientation is still possible because:

- There is no daylight.
- Sound echoes strangely.
- Multiple levels and catwalks create vertical confusion.
- Machinery noise can mask direction.
- Some passages look similar.
- Wet surfaces reflect light deceptively.

Carry:

- Waterproof notebook or synthetic paper.
- Grease pencil or all-weather pen.
- Simple map if available.
- Compass, but expect interference from iron and machinery.
- Pacing count.
- Small non-permanent marking tags if permitted.
- Camera in waterproof case, if documentation is needed.

Do not rely solely on memory. The place may feel easy to grasp at first, but fatigue and heat degrade judgment.

### Communication

Sound carries, but machinery may swallow voices.

Useful options:

- Hand signals.
- Small whistle, though noise may already be high.
- Local radio if permitted and safe.
- Prearranged check-in times if your multiverse anchor supports them.
- A written note system if you are working with local operators.

If you are alone, establish a check-in plan with someone outside the location or at your entry point.

### Tools

You are not going there to operate the settlement unless invited, but small practical tools can help.

Carry:

- Multitool.
- Small insulated screwdriver or probe.
- Zip ties.
- Waterproof tape.
- Small roll of duct tape.
- Foldable probe pole or walking stick if the floor is uncertain.
- Small dry bag of spare fasteners or fuse types if you know local standards.
- Non-sparking tools if gas risk is suspected.

Do not tamper with valves, switches, pumps, or fan controls unless you know exactly what you are doing and have permission. In this environment, one careless adjustment can flood a walkway, stop ventilation, or overload a circuit.

### Electronics protection

Humidity is hostile to electronics.

- Keep devices in sealed waterproof cases.
- Use desiccant packs.
- Avoid charging electronics in damp areas.
- Do not place devices on wet metal.
- Expect condensation when moving between temperatures.
- Prefer rugged, simple devices.
- Consider conformal-coated or waterproof gear if visiting often.

If your multiverse travel equipment is sensitive to moisture or electromagnetic interference, treat this location as high-risk. Keep the anchor or return device in a dry, shielded container and test it before settling in.

## 7. Prepare for the fauna

The animals are not incidental. They are part of the operating system of the place.

### Insects

Expect clouds of midges and moisture-flies around lamps, moths beating against shades, beetles in mud, cockroaches in dark seams, and crickets in cracks. Insects are not usually the primary danger, but they are constant.

Preparation:

- Use insect repellent suitable for your biology.
- Consider permethrin-treated clothing if allowed and safe.
- Keep sleeves and trousers closed.
- Use a fine head net if lamps draw dense clouds.
- Do not leave food exposed.
- Check clothing and bags before leaving.
- Avoid crushing insects on machinery or electrical surfaces.

Lamps become insect traps. Do not assume a lamp shade is clean or safe to touch.

### Bats

Bats roost in high recesses. They should be respected, not disturbed.

Do not:

- Shine bright lights directly into roosts.
- Make loud noises beneath them.
- Throw objects.
- Try to touch or collect them.
- Stand under heavy guano accumulation for long periods.
- Breathe guano dust if it is being stirred.

Disturbing bats may cause panic, noise, falling guano, and animals flying into machinery. In a confined industrial space, that is hazardous.

If you must pass near a roost, move quietly, use dim amber or red light, and avoid lingering.

### Guano and fungal hazards

Guano supports entire communities of flies, larvae, beetles, mites, fungi, and scavengers. It is fertile, smelly, and potentially hazardous.

Avoid:

- Stirring dry guano dust.
- Sitting or sleeping under roosts.
- Touching guano without gloves.
- Eating near guano deposits.
- Storing gear directly beneath bat clusters.

If guano dust is unavoidable, wetting the surface lightly may reduce airborne particles, but do not alter the habitat unless necessary and permitted.

### Frogs, toads, and pool life

The pool may contain frogs or toads, water beetles, larvae, crustaceans, and perhaps blind pale fish or detritivores. These animals are part of the local balance.

Do not:

- Drink from the pool.
- Wash your water containers in the pool.
- Dump waste near it.
- Introduce organisms from another world.
- Remove animals unless authorized.
- Shine bright lights into the water for long.

If something moves under the surface, do not reach in. The water is dark, the bottom uncertain, and the pool may conceal pump intakes, sharp debris, or electrically hazardous fittings.

### Rats

Rats are numerous, cautious, and intelligent. They are not usually bold, but they will investigate food, insulation, and unattended gear.

Preparation:

- Store food in sealed containers.
- Do not leave bags open.
- Check boots before putting them on.
- Keep cords and soft items away from dark corners.
- Do not leave waste behind.
- Do not feed them.

If rats are chewing near your sleeping area, relocate. Do not leave poison casually; it can harm other animals and create secondary hazards.

### Birds

If upper shafts connect to the surface, birds may enter. They are usually confused by the artificial light and heat. Do not chase them. They may injure themselves or contaminate machinery. If local inhabitants have a method for guiding birds out, follow their lead.

## 8. Prepare for the machinery and infrastructure

The technology is industrial, heavy, and practical. It is not decorative. It exists to move water, move air, generate power, and keep the place from becoming uninhabitable.

### Fans and ventilation

Ventilation is survival. If fans fail, heat and bad air can accumulate quickly.

Prepare to notice:

- Fan sound.
- Air movement on skin.
- Changes in smell.
- Changes in condensation.
- Sudden stillness.

If the fans stop and the air begins to feel heavier, warmer, or foul, do not wait to see what happens. Move toward known exits and fresh airflow.

### Pumps

Pumps may cycle without warning. You may hear a cough, a shudder, a rising whine, or a thump through the iron before water moves.

Rules:

- Learn the pump rhythm if possible.
- Do not sleep or linger on the lowest path until you understand flood pulses.
- Keep gear secured; vibration can move loose items.
- Do not touch pump intakes or grates.
- If water begins to rise quickly, move to the ledge or higher walkway.

### Pipes

Pipes are everywhere. Some are cold and sweating. Some are hot. Some may be under pressure. Some may carry water, steam, air, grease, or waste.

Do not:

- Lean on pipes unnecessarily.
- Grip pipes without checking temperature.
- Hang heavy gear on valves.
- Force stuck wheels.
- Open unknown fittings.
- Assume a pipe is safe to climb.

Test hot surfaces with the back of your hand before full contact.

### Electrical hazards

Water and electricity coexist here. That means caution should be extreme.

Assume:

- Wet floors may be electrically hazardous.
- Cables may be damaged.
- Junction boxes may be damp.
- Metal railings could be inadvertently live.
- Switchboards are not for visitors.

Keep gear dry. Do not touch electrical equipment with wet hands or while standing in water. If you see sparking, scorched insulation, or smell burning plastic, report it if possible and move away.

### Walkways, ladders, and grating

The paths are iron walkways, grated stairs, ledges, and concrete patches. They may be slick with algae, mineral slime, condensation, or oil.

Movement rules:

- Use handrails when available.
- Take stairs slowly.
- Keep one hand free if possible.
- Do not run.
- Watch for missing grating or loose boards.
- Avoid stepping on unknown crusts over saturated ground.
- Use three points of contact on ladders.
- Keep boot soles clean; algae and mud reduce grip.

A fall here is more dangerous than it looks because of hard rock, hot pipes, moving machinery, and contaminated water.

## 9. Prepare for water

Water is the persistent actor.

It weeps from walls, beads on pipes, drips from cable trays, runs through gutters, slickens drains, collects in pools, and rises after delayed rainfall from far above. The place may not know rain immediately, but it remembers it.

### Flooding and pulse events

Even if the chamber seems stable, pump cycles or delayed surface water can change the lower areas.

Before going low:

- Ask locals if there is a flood cycle.
- Observe water stains.
- Look for tidelines or mineral marks near the pool.
- Listen for pump intakes.
- Identify higher ledges and stairways.
- Do not assume the lowest route is always passable.

If the pool begins to creep over the lower path, do not cross casually. The water may be deeper than it appears, electrically hazardous, or hiding displaced debris.

### Condensation

Condensation is not just annoyance. It makes metal slick, drips into gear, fogs lenses, and promotes corrosion.

Protect critical items:

- Keep electronics in sealed bags.
- Use lens cloths and anti-fog.
- Store dry clothing in waterproof bags.
- Wipe down tools after use.
- Avoid leaving paper or cardboard exposed.

### Wet surfaces

Every surface may be wet, but different wet surfaces mean different things:

- Wet concrete: slippery, may crack.
- Wet limestone: sharp, uneven, slick in places.
- Wet metal grating: treacherous if algae-coated.
- Wet timber: possibly rotting.
- Wet shotcrete: new and perhaps unstable.
- Wet mud: may hide saturation beneath.

Move with deliberate attention. Your feet are your first instruments.

## 10. Prepare psychologically

The environment’s strangeness is not spectacular; it is cumulative.

You may feel:

- Watched, though few humans are visible.
- Oppressed by the heat.
- Irritated by constant dripping.
- Disoriented by lack of daylight.
- Tired from noise.
- Unsettled by insect clouds and bat sounds.
- Claustrophobic despite being able to see the whole chamber.
- Oddly comforted by the intimacy and rhythm.

### Mental habits that help

- Slow down.
- Narrate your observations quietly or in notes.
- Focus on layers: rock, water, machine, animal, light.
- Treat discomfort as information, not insult.
- Do not fight the place; adapt to it.
- Take scheduled rest breaks.
- Keep a watch or independent timepiece.
- Bring a small personal comfort object if it helps: a smooth stone, a folded photo, a familiar tea bag.

The place may feel “heavy,” not because of gravity but because every detail insists on itself. You are inside a system that does not care whether you are comfortable. Calmness is a survival skill.

## 11. Social preparation: how to behave around the sparse inhabitants

The settlement feels built for more people than it holds. You may see only a figure on a catwalk, someone working by the pumps, or hear voices without seeing anyone. Do not assume the few inhabitants want company, conversation, or help.

### First contact principles

- Be visible without being intrusive.
- Move deliberately.
- Do not touch machinery or infrastructure.
- Ask before entering inhabited rooms.
- Respect closed doors and sealed hatches.
- Keep weapons out of sight unless absolutely necessary; better still, avoid bringing them if possible.
- Avoid loud speech; sound carries and may disturb animals or workers.
- Learn basic local signs: arrows, hazard marks, valve numbers, painted names.

If you meet workers, present yourself as a careful traveler, not an inspector, tourist, or conqueror.

### Useful gifts

If gift-giving is appropriate, choose practical, low-risk items:

- Waterproof tape.
- Spare gaskets or O-rings if you know sizes.
- Ceramic fuses if you know standards.
- Fine mesh for lamp cages.
- Clean work gloves.
- Antifungal soap if culturally acceptable.
- Sealant suitable for damp stone.
- Spare lamp bulbs compatible with local fixtures.
- Dry socks.
- Clean water purification tablets.

Avoid:

- Food that attracts pests.
- Advanced technology that may destabilize local practice.
- Weapons.
- Strong perfumes.
- Biological materials.
- Unfamiliar chemicals.
- Trinkets with no use.

Sometimes the best gift is competence: helping move something, keeping watch, carrying a tool, or simply not creating more work.

### If there are no visible people

Even if no one appears, behave as a guest. The settlement may be monitored, or people may be sleeping, working behind doors, or absent temporarily. Do not loot, rearrange, or occupy sealed spaces. Leave no trace.

## 12. Arrival protocol

Your first minutes matter.

### Choose the arrival point carefully

If your multiverse travel method allows some control, avoid arriving:

- In the pool.
- On wet electrical floors.
- Under a bat roost.
- Beside a fan intake.
- Near a pump intake.
- Under dripping cable trays.
- On fresh unstable rock.
- In a construction zone.
- Inside a sealed room.
- Directly under lamps that will attract insects to your entry.

Aim for:

- A ledge or walkway.
- A dry-ish corner.
- Good airflow.
- Visible exits.
- Stable footing.
- Some shadow, so sudden light does not alarm animals.

### Stop and assess

Do not immediately rush forward.

Check:

1. Air monitor.
2. Breathing comfort.
3. Heat level.
4. Footing.
5. Sound environment.
6. Nearby water level.
7. Location of fans and airflow.
8. Nearest safe exit.
9. Nearest high ground.
10. Any immediate hazards: exposed cables, hot pipes, moving machinery, insects, falling drips.

Keep your mask or respirator available and, if uncertain, on.

### Identify the water logic

Look for:

- Drains.
- Gutters.
- Slopes.
- Pool edge.
- Pump intakes.
- Wet marks on walls.
- Lowest walkway.
- Overflow channels.

The place is designed to guide water. Do not stand where water wants to go.

### Identify the air logic

Feel for airflow. Listen to fans. Notice which doors or ducts seem important. If a door is sealed or marked, do not open it casually. Airflow routes may be deliberately arranged.

### Make contact or remain neutral

If inhabitants are present, seek permission or at least acknowledge them. If none are present, avoid settling into private or operational spaces.

## 13. Moving through the settlement

### Move slowly

The chamber is small, so the temptation is to cross it quickly. Resist that. Wet grating, hot pipes, low cables, and uneven rock punish haste.

### Keep your center of gravity controlled

- Bend knees slightly.
- Take short steps.
- Avoid sudden turns.
- Do not jump between levels unless absolutely necessary.
- Use rails.
- Test uncertain surfaces with a probe or walking stick.

### Watch up and down

There are hazards above and below.

Above:

- Drips.
- Cables.
- Pipes.
- Bats.
- Loose mineral crust.
- Scaffolding.
- Timber braces.
- Hanging lamps.

Below:

- Puddles.
- Algae.
- Broken slabs.
- Cracked concrete.
- Drain channels.
- Saturated crust.
- Rodent runs.

### Keep your light low and directed

Do not sweep bright beams across the whole chamber. Point light at the path or task. Use peripheral vision when possible. Amber light helps with insects and animals.

### Avoid creating new drips or leaks

Do not move tarps, gutters, or drip pans unless necessary. Local water management may look improvised but can be carefully balanced.

### Do not sit or lean casually

Before resting or leaning:

- Check the surface for heat.
- Check for wetness.
- Check for sharp edges.
- Check for droppings.
- Check for insects.
- Check stability.

Use a small closed-cell foam pad if you need to sit. It insulates you from damp, hot, or contaminated surfaces.

## 14. Eating, drinking, and sanitation

This is difficult in a hot, insect-filled, animal-filled place. Be strict.

### Eating

- Eat only in clean-ish, elevated areas if possible.
- Avoid eating near guano, drains, or food waste.
- Keep food sealed until the moment you eat.
- Finish quickly.
- Pack out crumbs and wrappers.
- Do not drop food scraps.

If rats or insects notice your food, you have created a problem.

### Drinking

- Use your own water.
- Keep the container closed.
- Do not let the mouthpiece touch contaminated surfaces.
- Wash hands before drinking if possible.

### Sanitation

Do not relieve yourself casually. In a small underground settlement, waste becomes a severe hygiene and smell problem.

If facilities exist, use them. If not:

- Use a portable waste bag or bottle.
- Pack out waste unless local custom provides a safe method.
- Do not contaminate water channels.
- Do not leave toilet paper or wipes.

The place already smells fertile; do not make it worse.

## 15. If you must stay overnight

Sleeping here requires choosing your spot as carefully as choosing a campsite in a floodplain.

### Choose a sleeping place that is:

- Elevated if possible.
- Away from the pool and lowest path.
- Away from active drips.
- Away from bat roosts and guano deposits.
- Away from electrical panels and hot machinery.
- Close enough to an exit to reach it quickly.
- In airflow but not directly under a loud fan if avoidable.
- Not blocking a walkway or valve access.

### Sleeping gear

- Closed-cell foam pad or cot.
- Sleeping bag liner suitable for heat.
- Mosquito or insect net if possible.
- Earplugs.
- Eye mask.
- Dry clothes sealed until bedtime.
- Boots stored closed or upended and checked.
- Mask within reach.
- Light within reach.
- Water within reach.

Do not sleep directly on damp concrete or bare rock if you can avoid it. Dampness will chill you despite the heat, and fungal exposure increases.

### Food storage

Hang or seal food. Do not keep it in open bags near the floor. Rats and insects will find it.

### Timekeeping

There is no dawn. Use a watch, pump cycles, or agreed local signals. Do not rely on your body’s sense of time.

### Night hazards

At night, the place may feel louder because human movement is reduced. Bats may shift. Drips may seem closer. Machinery may become the dominant presence. That is normal, but stay alert to changes:

- New water sounds.
- Fan stoppage.
- Alarms.
- Unusual animal panic.
- Smell changes.
- Temperature changes.

If something changes significantly, wake fully, dress, and reassess before going back to sleep.

## 16. Emergency preparation

Prepare for the most likely failures, not the most dramatic fantasies.

### Fan failure

Signs:

- Reduced airflow.
- Heat becoming heavier.
- Smell changing.
- Condensation increasing.
- Air feeling stale or foul.

Response:

- Stop exertion.
- Check air monitor.
- Put on respiratory protection if needed.
- Move toward exit or known fresh air route.
- Do not linger in low corners.

### Water rise

Signs:

- Pool edge creeping outward.
- Drains gurgling.
- Pump starting more frequently.
- New trickles from seams.
- Lower walkway becoming slick or submerged.

Response:

- Move to higher ledge.
- Avoid crossing unknown water.
- Do not touch electrical equipment while wet.
- Wait for pulse to pass if local pattern is known.

### Gas or bad air

Signs:

- Dizziness.
- Headache.
- Nausea.
- Eye irritation.
- Sudden smell of rotten eggs, ammonia, or fuel-like gas.
- Air monitor alarm.
- Animals behaving strangely.

Response:

- Leave immediately.
- Do not search for the source unless trained and equipped.
- Avoid low areas.
- Use escape respirator if you have one.

### Electrical fault

Signs:

- Sparks.
- Burning smell.
- Flickering lights.
- Tingling when touching metal.
- Hissing from a panel.
- Water dripping onto electrical equipment.

Response:

- Do not touch suspect metal.
- Step away carefully.
- Warn inhabitants if present.
- Do not throw water on electrical fires.
- Use appropriate extinguisher only if trained.

### Injury

For cuts, burns, sprains, or falls:

- Stop bleeding or contamination.
- Clean wound as best possible.
- Seal with waterproof dressing.
- Monitor for infection.
- Leave if injury affects balance, judgment, or breathing.

In hot damp environments, infection risk rises quickly.

### Animal disturbance

If bats panic or rats scatter:

- Lower light.
- Stop moving.
- Do not shout.
- Protect face and eyes if animals fly.
- Wait for calm.
- Check that nothing has entered your gear.

Do not chase animals. They know the place better than you do.

## 17. Multiverse-specific precautions

Because you are traveling between worlds, contamination and exit reliability matter more than usual.

### Prevent biological cross-contamination

This environment has spores, larvae, mites, fungi, bacteria, insects, and perhaps small animals that could become invasive elsewhere. Likewise, your home biology could harm this place.

Before leaving:

- Brush and clean boots.
- Check cuffs, hoods, bags, and seams for insects.
- Empty water from gear away from sensitive areas and in a safe disposal zone.
- Seal biological samples if authorized.
- Do not carry mud, guano, or organic matter across worlds.
- Launder clothing as soon as possible.
- Inspect portal equipment for stowaways.

### Protect your travel anchor

If your multiverse device, anchor, spell, or mechanism is sensitive:

- Keep it in a waterproof, shock-resistant container.
- Use desiccant.
- Shield it from electromagnetic noise if necessary.
- Test it early and often.
- Do not activate it while standing in water.
- Avoid using it near fan motors, transformers, or steam pipes if interference is possible.

### Have a secondary extraction plan

If your normal exit fails due to moisture, interference, or local conditions, you need alternatives:

- A known safe waiting point.
- A timed check-in.
- A physical route toward surface access if one exists.
- Local emergency contacts if available.
- Supplies for an unexpected extended stay.

Do not assume you can simply step out whenever you wish. Wet, iron-rich, machine-filled underground spaces can be hostile to delicate transit methods.

## 21. How to read the place

The settlement gives signs if you pay attention.

### Water signs

- Fresh stains: recent flow.
- Darkened concrete: persistent damp.
- Mineral crusts: slow evaporation and deposition.
- Algae lines: regular moisture.
- Brown slime: nutrient-rich water.
- Mud footprints filling quickly: saturated ground.
- Drips in rhythm with machinery: pump-related condensation or pulses.

### Air signs

- Moving dust or mist: airflow.
- Still pockets: poor ventilation.
- Stronger smell near a seam: possible gas or stagnant air.
- Insects avoiding an area: possibly foul air or heat.
- Bats shifting restlessly: disturbance or air change.

### Structural signs

- Cracked concrete: movement.
- Fresh pale rock: recent cutting.
- Rust streaks on new stone: water already working.
- Shored timber: unstable passage.
- Abandoned scaffolding: unfinished work.
- Shotcrete still damp: recent stabilization.

Do not assume new construction is safe. New openings may still shed rock or shift under water pressure.

## 22. Ethical preparation

This place is fragile despite its industrial hardness.

A traveler should avoid:

- Moving animals.
- Taking living specimens without permission.
- Altering water channels.
- Feeding wildlife.
- Leaving waste.
- Damaging fungi or moss out of curiosity.
- Disturbing bat roosts.
- Removing artifacts, tools, or signs.
- Marking surfaces permanently.
- Introducing outside organisms.
- Creating noise that disrupts animals or inhabitants.

The settlement is not a museum, but it is also not a dump or playground. It is someone’s working home and an ecosystem built from accidental opportunities.

The ethical rule is:

**Pass through without making the place more difficult for those who remain.**

## 23. Prepare for the smell

Do not underestimate odor.

The smell is layered: wet stone, iron, warm grease, guano, fungal rot, stagnant condensation, algae, and perhaps ammonia. At first it may be overwhelming. After a while your nose may dull, but that does not mean the air is safe.

If the smell changes suddenly:

- Check air monitor.
- Check ventilation.
- Look for new water flow.
- Look for overheating machinery.
- Consider leaving if the change is accompanied by headache, nausea, or eye irritation.

A small amount of scent tolerance is necessary, but smell should be treated as information, not merely discomfort.

## 24. Prepare for the sound

The sound may be more difficult than the heat.

Layers include:

- Ventilation fans.
- Pump hammering.
- Motors whining.
- Metal expanding.
- Drips hitting tin.
- Water in gutters.
- Insects buzzing.
- Moths hitting lamps.
- Bat squeaks.
- Frog calls.
- Rat claws on grating.
- Voices echoing strangely.

Hearing protection should reduce fatigue while preserving awareness. If you remove protection entirely, the noise may become abrasive. If you block everything, you may miss warnings.

Good practice:

- Use filtered earplugs in normal movement.
- Add muffs near loud machinery.
- Remove one side briefly if you need to listen carefully, then restore protection.
- Pay attention to changes in sound more than single noises.

A pump engaging, a fan slowing, or a new trickle can be more meaningful than a shout.

## 25. Prepare for time distortion

There is no dawn, noon, dusk, or night. Time is marked by:

- Switch rotations.
- Pump cycles.
- Feeding calls.
- Animal behavior.
- Maintenance rhythms.
- Lamp changes.

Carry an independent timepiece. Do not rely on your internal sense of time, especially if you are tired or overheated.

If you are conducting observations or waiting for a portal window, set alarms and backups. Heat and monotony can make people underestimate elapsed time.

## 27. If you are traveling with others

Small groups are better than large ones. A large group creates heat, noise, disturbance, and logistical strain.

If with others:

- Agree on hand signals.
- Set turn-back criteria.
- Do not spread out too far.
- Keep sight or sound contact in risky areas.
- Share water and light resources.
- Check each other for heat stress and contamination.

The chamber is intimate, but one person’s mistake can affect everyone.

## 28. Departure and decompression

Leaving is not complete until you have checked yourself and your gear.

### Before exiting

- Ensure no insects, larvae, or seeds are in clothing or bags.
- Empty and clean boots if necessary.
- Seal wet or contaminated items.
- Check that no local animal is near your portal or exit point.
- Confirm you have not left waste, food, or gear.
- Note any changes in the environment that inhabitants should know about.

### After exiting

- Dry out thoroughly.
- Launder clothes.
- Inspect skin, feet, and any wounds.
- Clean ears and eyes if contaminated water touched them.
- Monitor health for days: fever, cough, rash, digestive illness, unusual fatigue, swollen glands, or breathing problems.
- Report any serious contamination or invasive-species risk according to your travel protocols.

The place may cling to you: smell, damp, spores, and memory. Give yourself time to recover from the heat and noise.

## 29. A simple preparation ritual

If you want a practical mental checklist before entering, run through this:

1. **Air:** Do I have breathing protection and a way to detect bad air?
2. **Water:** Do I have enough clean water, and do I know not to drink local water?
3. **Heat:** Am I hydrated, rested, and able to rest again soon?
4. **Footing:** Are my boots secure, dry enough, and appropriate for wet metal and stone?
5. **Light:** Do I have three light sources and spare power?
6. **Exit:** Do I know where I can leave quickly?
7. **Machines:** Do I know enough not to touch controls, valves, or cables?
8. **Animals:** Am I prepared not to disturb bats, rats, insects, frogs, or birds?
9. **People:** Will I behave as a guest, not an owner?
10. **Return:** Is my multiverse exit protected from water, heat, interference, and contamination?

If you cannot answer these reasonably, delay entry or reduce the scope of the visit.

## 30. The traveler’s attitude

The best preparation is not arrogance or excessive fear. It is attentive respect.

You should enter as if stepping into a room that is also a body: breathing through fans, sweating through pipes, digesting water, hosting animals, repairing itself slowly, and tolerating your presence only if you do not make trouble.

Do not expect grand strangeness. The wonder here is material and close: a rust stain spreading on fresh limestone, a spiderweb beaded with condensation, a bat shifting in the dark, a pump coughing into motion, a frog calling from black water, a lamp turning amber on wet brick.

Prepare to be hot. Prepare to be damp. Prepare to hear metal and water arguing all night. Prepare to share the place with creatures that were here before you and will be here after you. Prepare to leave without leaving a mark.

If you do that, the settlement will not become comfortable, but it will become legible. You will begin to understand it not as a grim hole or a strange underground city, but as a working compromise between stone, water, machinery, animals, and the few people stubborn enough to live among them.
