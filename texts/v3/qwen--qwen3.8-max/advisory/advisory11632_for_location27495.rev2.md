Prepare as if you are entering a cold, high-altitude acoustic organism rather than a city. This place is not hostile in the manner of predators or traps; it is hostile in the manner of pressure, damp, darkness, altitude, and indifference. It is full of life, but not life arranged for human comfort. It is built, but not built for eyes. It is loud, but not in a way that will immediately make sense to a human ear. An ordinary traveler should assume that they will be a clumsy, warm, noisy, fragile presence in a system that is slow, wet, mineral, animal, and only partially artificial.

The first rule is simple: **do not try to conquer the place with light, speed, or noise.** You must become small, quiet, warm, dry, oxygenated, and patient.

## 1. Understand what the environment will do to an ordinary human body

Before packing anything, internalize the main pressures of the location.

### Thin, cold, saturated air

The air is thin and difficult, suggesting high altitude or low pressure, but it is also saturated with moisture. This combination is deceptive. It may feel thick, close, and heavy, but your body may still be starving for oxygen. You may experience:

- Shortness of breath on minimal exertion.
- Headache, nausea, poor judgment, or euphoria from hypoxia.
- Cold stress from wet air and stone.
- Rapid heat loss when you stop moving.
- Condensation inside masks, goggles, helmets, and clothing.
- Frozen regulators, valves, or zippers if moisture accumulates.
- Chapped skin, cracked lips, and irritated airways.

Saturated cold air can make mild hypothermia sneak up on you. You may not feel soaking wet in the usual sense; you may simply feel heavy, chilled, and increasingly clumsy.

### Darkness

The darkness is not merely absence of light. It is the primary medium of the place. There may be faint microbial sheen, cold sparks, or distant glints, but not enough for human vision to rely on. You should expect:

- Loss of horizon.
- Loss of depth perception.
- Difficulty judging distances.
- Visual anxiety or panic.
- Tendency to imagine shapes where there are none.
- Dependence on sound, touch, vibration, and memory.

If you arrive expecting to “just see a little,” you will be disappointed. The place must be navigated like a cave, a night sea, or a dream of architecture.

### Sound

Sound is immense. Water, machines, animals, stone, and resonance create a layered acoustic environment. This is both your greatest danger and your greatest navigational aid.

You may feel sound in your chest before you understand it. Subsonic machinery may produce dread, pressure, or unease. Echoes may distort your sense of space. Animal calls may seem like signals. Construction tones may seem like warnings. The city may appear to be speaking, but not necessarily to you.

You must protect your hearing without deafening yourself. Total silence is dangerous here. You need filtered, controlled access to the soundscape.

### Wet stone and mineral surfaces

Everything is slick, cold, and alive with microbial films, fungal threads, mineral crusts, and moisture. Surfaces may be:

- Polished by grazing fauna.
- Slimy with microbial varnish.
- Sharp with newly exposed rock.
- Unstable where construction is incomplete.
- Coated with calcium, silica, or metallic deposits.
- Slick with condensation or thin ice.

Falls are a major risk. Not because the place is dramatic, but because the ground is patient and indifferent. A slip on wet basalt in darkness can be fatal even if the fall itself is short.

### Fauna

Animals are not background scenery. They are population, infrastructure, maintenance system, and perhaps citizenship. They occupy the ground, walls, water, air, pipes, drains, roofs, shafts, and possibly the technologies themselves.

You should assume that many of them are not aggressive, but that does not mean they are safe to touch, block, imitate, or disturb. Some may be heavy. Some may be numerous. Some may be sensitive to vibration, heat, pressure, or sound. Some may be partially engineered. Some may be so integrated into the city’s function that harming them is equivalent to damaging infrastructure.

### Living or geological technology

The technologies here do not behave like ordinary machines. They behave like weather, slow animals, pressure systems, or mathematics. Bridges may be held by sound. Stairs may be stabilized by fields. Mineral fog may carry microscopic builders. Machines may pause for animals. Walls may grow. Arches may be supported by temporary forces.

Do not assume you can see what is structural. Do not assume you can touch what is safe. Do not assume that something humming, warm, or vibrating is meant for your interaction.

## 2. Arrange for local knowledge before entering

An ordinary multiverse traveler should not enter this place casually. The correct preparation begins before arrival.

### Seek a local guide, custodian, or acoustic interpreter

If any sapient or semi-sapient custodians exist, contact them before entering. If there are no visible humanlike inhabitants, there may still be:

- Maintenance intelligences.
- Animal-handlers.
- Resonance wardens.
- Construction choirs.
- Pressure-field shepherds.
- Acoustic archivists.
- Ecological managers.
- Automated visitor protocols.

If the local civilization uses sound as primary architecture, your best preparation is to learn the correct acoustic etiquette. You do not want to accidentally broadcast a territorial challenge, a mating call, a construction command, or a distress signal.

If no guide is available, at least obtain:

- Acoustic maps.
- Migration schedules.
- Construction cycles.
- Quiet zones.
- Dangerous resonance areas.
- Thermal vent locations.
- Flood or condensation surge patterns.
- Known stable routes.
- Emergency extraction points.

### Ask what not to do

In this environment, prohibitions may matter more than instructions. Ask:

- Which sounds must not be made?
- Which districts are incubation zones?
- Which animal migrations must not be interrupted?
- Which fields are dangerous to enter?
- Which water channels are active?
- Which fog periods are hazardous?
- Which structures are unstable?
- Which organisms must not be touched?
- Which technologies respond to human breathing, footsteps, or heartbeats?

### Register your presence

If multiversal travel is possible, assume that local authorities or systems may track visitors. Registering yourself may be essential for:

- Rescue.
- Medical emergency response.
- Avoiding being treated as a contaminant.
- Preventing accidental construction around you.
- Ensuring machines pause rather than incorporate you.
- Allowing fauna to recognize you as a temporary presence.

If there is no formal registry, leave a detailed itinerary with someone outside the location. Include expected duration, route, emergency signals, and extraction conditions.

## 3. Acclimatize before doing anything else

Do not step directly into this place and expect to function. You need a transition period.

### Altitude acclimatization

If the basin is truly high, spend time in a staging environment with similar pressure and oxygen levels. If possible:

- Arrive gradually.
- Hydrate.
- Avoid alcohol or sedatives before entry.
- Monitor blood oxygen saturation.
- Carry medication for altitude sickness if medically appropriate.
- Know the symptoms of hypoxia and cerebral/pulmonary edema.
- Be willing to withdraw early.

An ordinary traveler should not rely on willpower. Thin air impairs judgment before it impairs movement.

### Cold and damp acclimatization

Before entering, spend time in a cold, wet, dark controlled environment if possible. Learn how your body reacts when:

- Your gloves become damp.
- Your feet stay cold for hours.
- Your breath fogs.
- Your clothing stiffens.
- Your fingers lose dexterity.
- Your motivation drops.

Most people underestimate how quickly wet cold erodes morale.

### Sensory acclimatization

Practice spending time in darkness. Practice moving slowly without vision. Practice listening without trying to translate every sound into a visual image. Practice remaining still while surrounded by unfamiliar noise.

If you can, train with:

- Blindfolded walking.
- Cane use.
- Echo awareness.
- Slow breathing.
- Noise tolerance.
- Tactile navigation.
- Guided meditation in loud environments.

The goal is not to become blind-adapted instantly. The goal is to stop panicking when your eyes stop being useful.

## 4. Protect breathing first

Breathing is the highest priority. The air is thin, cold, wet, mineral-laden, and possibly full of spores, aerosols, microbial particles, and microscopic construction agents.

### Use a serious respiratory system

A simple cloth mask is inadequate. You need a cold-rated, pressure-compensated breathing system.

Ideal components include:

- Full-face mask or hood with sealed interface.
- Heated humidification to prevent airway irritation and freezing.
- Oxygen enrichment for altitude.
- CO₂ scrubbing if using a rebreather.
- Particulate filtration for spores, dust, and microbial aerosols.
- Chemical filtration for metallic or mineral vapors if present.
- Anti-fog visor or face shield.
- Low breathing resistance.
- Quiet exhalation routing.
- Spare filters.
- Ice-resistant valves.

Do not rely on local air unless verified safe. Even if oxygen levels are acceptable, the mineral fog and microbial aerosols may be dangerous to human lungs.

### Be careful with mineral fog

The engineered fog that deposits calcium, silica, and metallic compounds is not ordinary weather. It may coat your lungs, filters, mask, suit joints, and instruments. If fog thickens:

- Seal your breathing system.
- Reduce exertion.
- Protect exposed equipment.
- Avoid speaking unnecessarily.
- Leave the fog bank if possible.
- Wait for it to pass if you cannot navigate safely.

Do not assume the microscopic builders are harmless to human tissue just because they build walls.

### Manage exhalation

Your breath is warm, moist, and chemically distinct. In a place where organisms feed on dissolved minerals, exhaled heat, or chemical gradients, your breath may attract attention. Use a mask that directs exhalation away from sensitive surfaces. Do not exhale into crevices, nests, machinery, or colonies.

## 5. Stay warm, dry, and mobile

Cold and wet are the slow enemies here. You need a clothing system that works even when everything around you is damp.

### Base layers

Use moisture-wicking, non-cotton base layers. You want sweat moved away from the skin before it becomes a chilling layer.

Recommended:

- Synthetic or wool base layer.
- Fitted but not tight.
- No cotton anywhere.
- Spare base layer in a dry bag.
- Antifungal powder or wipes for feet and skin folds.

### Insulation

Insulation must work when damp. Avoid unprotected down.

Use:

- Synthetic insulation.
- Aerogel panels if available.
- Heated underlayers.
- Localized heating for hands, feet, and core.
- Thin, flexible insulation around joints.

You do not want to become a bulky, clumsy bundle. You need enough warmth to remain still for long periods, but enough mobility to climb, crouch, and balance.

### Outer shell

Your outer layer should be waterproof, windproof, abrasion-resistant, and quiet.

Important features:

- Sealed seams.
- Hood compatible with helmet and breathing apparatus.
- High collar.
- Long cuffs with gauntlets.
- Waterproof zippers or storm flaps.
- Reinforced knees, seat, and shoulders.
- Ventilation options that do not flood.
- Matte, non-reflective surface.
- Soft exterior that does not clank or rasp loudly.

Avoid gear that makes sharp synthetic rustling with every movement. You are entering a sound-sensitive ecosystem.

### Hands

Your hands will be needed for balance, touch, and inspection, but they cannot be numb.

Bring:

- Thin waterproof liner gloves.
- Insulated outer gloves.
- Spare dry gloves.
- Grip-enhancing palm surfaces.
- Touch-compatible fingertips if using tactile interfaces.
- Wrist straps so gloves are not dropped.
- Hand warmers that do not require open flame.

Do not sacrifice all dexterity for warmth. You need to feel handholds, railings, stones, and haptic controls.

### Feet and boots

Feet are critical. Wet cold stone will punish poor footwear quickly.

Boots should have:

- Insulation.
- Waterproof construction.
- Aggressive but non-damaging tread.
- Good ankle support.
- Stiff enough sole for sharp rock.
- Flexible enough forefoot for stairs and ledges.
- Drainage prevention unless you expect deliberate wading.
- Removable liners for drying.
- Compatible gaiters.

Avoid metal spikes unless necessary. On polished stone, some hard spikes can reduce friction. Micro-textured rubber, siping, or soft grippy compounds may work better. Test footwear on wet stone before the visit.

Bring:

- Multiple sock layers.
- Vapor barrier socks if appropriate.
- Antifungal foot care.
- Blister treatment.
- Spare insoles.
- Boot dryers or absorbent liners.

### Helmet and head protection

You need head protection from falling fragments, low ledges, and accidental contact with unseen structures.

Helmet should include:

- Chin strap.
- Mounting for acoustic equipment.
- Space for breathing apparatus.
- Anti-fog face shield.
- Ear protection integration.
- Insulation without overheating.
- No dangling straps.

A hood alone is not enough. The place is rocky, unfinished, and three-dimensional.

You may bring light, but you should not depend on it.

### Use light only as emergency or task illumination

Carry:

- Dim, shielded red or amber light.
- Downward-facing headlamp with diffuser.
- Low-intensity task light.
- Backup light.
- Glow markers that do not spill light.
- Light-proof storage to prevent accidental illumination.

Use light sparingly. If you use it, shield it. Do not sweep beams across the basin. Do not flash animals. Do not shine light into crevices, nests, or machinery.

### Prioritize non-visual sensing

Your primary navigation should be:

- Sound.
- Touch.
- Pressure.
- Vibration.
- Air movement.
- Smell, if safely filtered.
- Haptic feedback.
- Inertial tracking.
- Acoustic beacons.
- Local guide cues.

You are preparing to move through a space where a wall may be known by the way it changes the sound of dripping water.

## 7. Build a navigation system for a loud, dark, wet city

Navigation here is not “follow the road.” It is more like learning the shape of a living instrument.

### Passive listening first

When you arrive, do not immediately emit loud sonar or calls. Listen.

Try to separate the layers:

- Water: drips, trickles, flows, laps, hisses.
- Machinery: drones, hums, pulses, pressure tones.
- Fauna: calls, thumps, rasps, rustles, wingbeats.
- Architecture: echoes, hollow spaces, resonant markers.
- Your own body: breathing, footsteps, gear noise.

The city may already contain navigational information. Certain walls may return calls in recognizable patterns. Certain towers may act as resonant markers. Certain plazas may echo in characteristic ways.

### Use approved acoustic tools

If local authorities provide acoustic navigation tools, use them. If not, choose tools that are quiet and respectful.

Possible tools:

- Passive acoustic mapper.
- Haptic sonar belt.
- Directional microphones.
- Bone-conduction audio.
- Low-power echo emitter.
- Soft clicker for personal echolocation.
- Haptic compass.
- Inertial navigation unit.
- Acoustic transponders placed with permission.
- Tactile map encoded in raised patterns.

Avoid high-powered sonar unless emergency. In a place organized by echo, a loud pulse may be equivalent to shouting in a cathedral or setting off an alarm in a hospital.

### Use tethering and markers carefully

In darkness, a physical guide line can be lifesaving. But do not leave permanent markers, block channels, or disturb fauna.

Use:

- Thin, strong tether.
- Removable anchors.
- Soft line that does not cut into colonies.
- Local-approved temporary acoustic tags.
- Biodegradable or fully retrievable markers.
- No adhesive on living or growing surfaces.

If you place a marker, be prepared to remove it. If you cannot remove it, it should not be there.

### Do not trust visual memory

You will not remember this place like a normal city. Do not rely on “I’ll recognize that tower.” Instead, record:

- Echo signatures.
- Surface textures.
- Slope angles.
- Air pressure changes.
- Temperature changes.
- Sound landmarks.
- Water direction.
- Vibration patterns.
- Step counts.
- Turn sequences.

Use redundant systems. Electronics may fail in moisture and fields. Carry analog backups.

## 8. Manage sound like a resource and a hazard

You need hearing, but too much sound can overwhelm you. You also produce sound, and your sound may matter to the city.

### Use filtered hearing protection

Do not block your ears completely. You need environmental awareness. Instead use:

- Level-dependent ear protection.
- Acoustic filters that reduce harmful volume but preserve directionality.
- Subsonic damping if the machinery causes panic or nausea.
- External microphones with adjustable gain.
- Bone-conduction output if masks interfere with hearing.
- Ability to quickly reduce input if overwhelmed.

If you feel dread, pressure, or nausea from low frequencies, reduce subsonic exposure. That feeling may be environmental, not psychological.

### Reduce your own noise

An ordinary traveler is louder than they realize.

Quiet your gear:

- Pad metal objects.
- Secure straps.
- Avoid clinking tools.
- Use soft bags.
- Muffle buckles.
- Wear soft outer fabric.
- Move slowly.
- Breathe steadily.
- Avoid shouting.
- Avoid ultrasonic devices.
- Avoid music, alarms, or ringing electronics.

Your heartbeat, breathing, and footsteps may become part of the local soundscape. That is unavoidable, but you should avoid adding unnecessary noise.

### Learn the acoustic grammar if possible

If the city uses sound architecturally, there may be patterns:

- Calls that mark safe paths.
- Resonances that warn of unstable stone.
- Quiet zones for incubation.
- Loud zones for construction.
- Rhythms that open channels.
- Tones that indicate machinery is active.
- Echo shapes that identify districts.

Do not imitate these sounds unless trained. A wrong call could attract animals, halt machines, trigger construction, or cause a territorial response.

## 9. Move slowly and deliberately

This is not a place for speed. The pace of the city is slow. Water arrives by condensation. Stone changes by shedding. Construction advances by accretion. Animals move in patient masses. You should match that pace.

### Walking technique

Use short steps. Keep your weight centered. Test footing before committing. Keep one hand free if possible. Do not trust polished surfaces.

Recommended movement habits:

- Slide or place feet rather than stomping.
- Feel for slick films.
- Avoid thin ice skins.
- Stay away from weeping ledges.
- Do not step into standing water unless necessary.
- Use walls only if stable and not alive with colonies.
- Keep knees slightly bent.
- Avoid sudden turns.
- Pause often to listen.

### Use a cane or staff

A tactile probe is invaluable. Use a lightweight staff or cane to:

- Test edges.
- Detect pools.
- Find steps.
- Check stability.
- Sense changes in surface texture.
- Warn you of low obstacles.
- Reduce foot reliance.

Use it gently. Do not jab into soft biological surfaces, drains, nests, or machinery.

### Avoid unfinished architecture

The city is still being born. Do not lean on embryonic walls. Do not shelter under unsupported arches. Do not cross bridges unless confirmed stable. Do not enter areas where rock is actively shedding.

Signs of danger:

- Fresh sharp fragments.
- Hollow sounds underfoot.
- Dust or mineral grit falling.
- Temporary supports.
- Fields that feel heavy or uneven.
- Acoustic dead zones.
- Sudden changes in water flow.
- Animal avoidance behavior.

If animals avoid an area, take that seriously.

### Beware pressure and sound fields

Some stairs, bridges, and structures are stabilized by gradients of pressure and sound. These fields may feel like invisible water, heaviness, or subtle resistance.

If you encounter such a field:

- Do not run.
- Do not fight it.
- Move at a steady rhythm.
- Keep breathing normal.
- If your chest feels compressed, retreat slowly.
- Do not place your head or mask into the strongest gradient.
- Do not insert tools or probes unless authorized.
- If your equipment begins to hum strangely, withdraw.

These systems may be designed for fauna, not humans. They may pause for large bodies, but they may not anticipate your shape or density.

## 10. Prepare for water everywhere

Water has priority. If you ignore water, the place will correct you.

### Understand water behavior here

Water arrives slowly but constantly:

- Condensation from dark air.
- Beading on cliffs.
- Threadlike runoff.
- Seepage from joints.
- Upward moisture from ground.
- Shallow pans.
- Channels with ice skins.
- Cisterns and reservoirs.
- Drains lined with animals.

A route that seems dry may become slick or flooded over hours. A shallow pan may hide ice, silt, or organisms. A channel may be part of both hydrology and fauna migration.

### Do not block water

Never dam, divert, or cover a water channel without local permission. Water management may be essential to:

- Construction.
- Animal drinking.
- Sediment transport.
- Heat distribution.
- Microbial growth.
- Cistern filling.
- Pressure stabilization.

Blocking even a small trickle may have downstream effects.

### Avoid standing water when possible

Standing water may contain:

- Fine silt.
- Mineral precipitates.
- Blind filter-feeders.
- Larvae.
- Eggs.
- Microbial films.
- Cold enough to numb feet.
- Thin ice over deeper water.

If you must cross water, test depth, temperature, and bottom stability first. Protect your breathing system from splashes. Dry and warm your feet afterward.

### Hydration

Do not drink local water without testing and treatment. It may contain dissolved metals, mineral builders, microbial spores, animal waste, or unknown chemicals.

Carry enough safe water for the entire visit plus emergency reserve. Cold suppresses thirst, but altitude and exertion increase dehydration.

If you must treat local water:

- Filter particulates.
- Use chemical or physical purification.
- Test for heavy metals if possible.
- Avoid water from stagnant pools.
- Prefer flowing, filtered, or cistern water only if approved.
- Boil only with sealed heating systems, not open flame.

### Protect gear from water

Moisture will attack zippers, electronics, filters, joints, and fasteners.

Use:

- Dry bags.
- Sealed cases.
- Hydrophobic coatings.
- Conformal-coated electronics.
- Spare seals.
- Absorbent cloths.
- Zipper lubricant compatible with cold and mineral exposure.
- Silica or desiccant in sealed containers.
- Protective covers for microphones and sensors.

Expect to perform maintenance after exposure. Mineral films may crust over equipment.

## 11. Treat fauna with extreme respect

The animals here are overwhelming in number and integrated into the city. They are not pests. They may be labor, infrastructure, citizens, or components of a living system.

### General fauna rules

- Do not feed.
- Do not touch.
- Do not chase.
- Do not imitate calls.
- Do not shine light on them.
- Do not block movement paths.
- Do not enter rookeries.
- Do not disturb eggs, larvae, or molting clusters.
- Do not remove animals from walls or pipes.
- Do not kill unless immediate life-threatening emergency.
- Do not assume they are unintelligent.

### Grazing flat-bodied creatures

These slow creatures graze bacterial films on wet gravel. They may move in broad fronts.

Precautions:

- Do not walk through grazing fronts.
- Avoid polished trails they leave; they may be extremely slick.
- Yield to them if they move toward you.
- Do not step on their carapaces.
- Do not startle them with sudden vibrations.

They are likely fragile despite their dull appearance.

### Blind eel-like filter feeders

These live in deeper pools and canals. They may form chains and filter mineral-rich water.

Precautions:

- Do not enter their pools.
- Do not dangle limbs in canals.
- Do not disturb sediment near them.
- If you fall in, move calmly to the edge.
- Do not grab their bodies for support.
- Avoid creating suction or turbulence near them.

They may not hunt, but their bodies could entangle or obstruct you.

### Wall colonies

Some creatures cling in layered rookeries, excrete mineral cement, or trap heat with filaments.

Precautions:

- Do not touch colonies.
- Do not scrape walls.
- Do not lean on ribbed or knobby biological formations.
- Do not remove cement beads.
- Watch for falling debris or excretions.
- Avoid breathing directly onto colonies.

These organisms may be part of wall maintenance. Damaging them may weaken structures.

### Eyeless climbing creatures in crevices

These masses may rest under eaves or in crevices. They may be sensitive to heat, vibration, or pressure.

Precautions:

- Do not reach into crevices.
- Do not shelter in occupied recesses.
- If they shift around you, remain still.
- Do not panic if they investigate you.
- Do not make high-frequency sounds nearby.

If they cluster on you due to heat, do not thrash. Move slowly toward a cooler area if possible and allow them to disperse.

### Aerial forms

Wide-bodied gliding creatures may move between towers using thermal leaks. They navigate by sound and pressure.

Precautions:

- Do not linger under thermal shafts.
- Keep helmet and face protection secure.
- Do not imitate wing-thumps or calls.
- Do not release drones.
- Do not raise poles or antennas unexpectedly.
- If one approaches, lower your profile and remain still.

Their movement may be slow, but their mass and membranes could knock you off balance.

### Swarms near condensation engines

Dense clouds may feed on aerosols, spores, or particles. Their rushing sound may be beautiful, but they can clog filters and obscure breathing equipment.

Precautions:

- Seal mask and suit.
- Protect intake valves.
- Avoid walking through dense swarms if possible.
- If engulfed, stop moving and breathe steadily.
- Do not swat.
- Check filters afterward.

### Animals in infrastructure

Drains, pipes, reservoirs, stairwells, and machines may contain animals. This is normal.

Do not clear a drain because it is full of resting bodies. Do not open a pipe because it contains eggs. Do not enter a reservoir because it looks like shelter. These are functioning systems.

If a machine pauses because of animals, wait. Do not force it to continue.

## 13. Food, waste, and scent discipline

You must bring all nutrition and carry out all waste.

### Food

Bring high-calorie, low-odor, sealed food. You need calories for warmth and altitude, but you do not want scent spreading.

Good options:

- Sealed calorie bars.
- Nutrient gels.
- Vacuum-packed rations.
- Thermos of warm drink.
- Electrolyte mix.
- Easy-to-eat foods that do not crumble.

Avoid:

- Strong-smelling food.
- Crumbly snacks.
- Open containers.
- Spices that aerosolize.
- Food that requires cooking in the open.

Do not cook with open flame. Use sealed heaters if necessary.

### Waste

All human waste must be contained. Do not use drains, pools, channels, or crevices as toilets. The local ecosystem processes waste in its own way, and foreign waste may disrupt chemical balances.

Carry:

- Waste bags.
- Urine bottles with secure seals.
- Solid waste containment.
- Odor-proof pouches.
- Hand sanitation that does not leave scented residue.
- Menstrual waste containment if applicable.

Leave nothing.

### Scent

Scent may matter here, even if vision does not. Your smell could attract fauna, trigger microbial responses, or mark you as an anomaly.

Reduce scent:

- Use unscented hygiene products.
- Keep food sealed.
- Store waste in odor-proof containers.
- Avoid perfumes.
- Avoid strong medicines or balms unless medically necessary.
- Keep exhalation filtered or directed.

## 14. Prepare your mind for a non-human environment

This place can be psychologically difficult not because it is evil, but because it does not center you.

### Accept visual helplessness

You may feel blind. You may feel watched. You may imagine shapes. This is normal. Do not chase visual hallucinations. Trust your instruments, your guide, and your slow movement.

### Accept slowness

You will not move quickly. You will not “push through.” The environment punishes impatience. If you feel irritated by the pace, that is a warning sign that you are rushing.

### Accept noise

The sound may feel overwhelming. Water never stops. Machinery may vibrate in your chest. Animals may call across the darkness. You may not be able to find silence.

If you feel panic rising:

- Stop moving.
- Reduce auditory input slightly, but do not deafen yourself.
- Breathe slowly.
- Check oxygen.
- Check warmth.
- Focus on one sound layer at a time.
- Remind yourself that the noise is structure, not threat.

### Accept crowding

The place is dense. There are bodies everywhere: on walls, in water, in air, in drains. If you have a fear of insects, worms, bats, eels, or crawling things, you must prepare carefully.

You are not required to love the fauna. You are required to remain calm and non-destructive.

### Accept that you may not be the protagonist

The city may not respond to you. It may not acknowledge you. It may simply continue. That is not hostility. It is scale.

Do not attempt multi-day stays without:

- Local guidance.
- Secure shelter.
- Reliable oxygen.
- Heating.
- Waste system.

## 17. Health monitoring

Monitor yourself constantly, but calmly.

### Hypoxia

Watch for:

- Headache.
- Confusion.
- Poor coordination.
- Euphoria.
- Irritability.
- Nausea.
- Blue lips or fingertips.
- Unusually slow decisions.

If symptoms worsen, use oxygen and descend or extract.

### Hypothermia

Watch for:

- Shivering.
- Clumsiness.
- Slurred speech.
- Apathy.
- Poor judgment.
- Cold hands that do not recover.
- Wet clothing that no longer feels wet.

If shivering stops in a cold environment, treat as emergency.

### Dehydration

Watch for:

- Headache.
- Dry mouth.
- Dark urine.
- Fatigue.
- Dizziness.
- Reduced urine output.

Cold altitude can hide dehydration. Drink on schedule, not only when thirsty.

### Trench foot and skin damage

Watch for:

- Pale, wrinkled skin.
- Numbness.
- Burning or tingling.
- Blisters.
- Cracks.
- Fungal irritation.

Change socks before problems develop. Dry feet carefully.

### Sensory overload

Watch for:

- Panic.
- Irritability.
- Nausea.
- Desire to flee.
- Inability to parse sounds.
- Feeling of pressure in chest.
- Paranoia.

Stop, reduce input, check oxygen, and withdraw if necessary.

### Falls and trauma

In darkness and wet stone, even minor injuries can become serious. Carry trauma supplies and do not rely on walking out quickly.

## 18. Emergency preparation

Assume that ordinary emergency methods may not work here.

### Extraction

Your multiverse return or extraction device must be tested for:

- Moisture.
- Low pressure.
- Acoustic interference.
- Pressure fields.
- Mineral fog.
- Electric fauna.
- Lack of visual landmarks.
- Subsonic noise.

Set extraction triggers based on:

- Time.
- Oxygen level.
- Body temperature.
- Heart rate.
- Manual activation.
- Buddy signal.
- Failure to check in.

Do not rely only on “I’ll leave when I feel tired.”

### Beacon

Carry an emergency beacon that does not rely solely on light or radio. Consider:

- Acoustic emergency pulse.
- Pressure-wave signal.
- Thermal signature, if safe.
- Local-approved distress tone.
- Inertial location tag.
- Multiversal anchor ping.

Do not use distress signals that mimic animal calls or construction commands unless approved.

### If lost

If you are lost:

1. Stop.
2. Breathe.
3. Check oxygen and warmth.
4. Do not wander.
5. Activate passive listening.
6. Compare current sound landmarks to your map.
7. Use haptic or inertial backtrack if reliable.
8. If uncertain, stay put and beacon.
9. Avoid moving toward unfamiliar loud sounds.
10. Avoid entering quiet zones or animal clusters for shelter.

Moving blindly through an unfinished acoustic city can turn a minor navigation error into a fatal one.

### If equipment fails

Priorities:

1. Breathing.
2. Warmth.
3. Stability.
4. Communication.
5. Navigation.
6. Light, only if necessary.

If your mask fogs, stop and clear it. If your suit leaks, protect core and extremities. If your navigation fails, do not improvise rapidly. Use backup systems. If your oxygen fails, use reserve and withdraw immediately.

### If injured

- Stop bleeding.
- Insulate from ground.
- Prevent further wetting.
- Stabilize limbs.
- Avoid shock.
- Use oxygen.
- Call for extraction.
- Do not attempt heroic movement through dark stone.

## 19. Interacting with local technologies

Do not assume advanced technology is user-friendly. It may not recognize you.

### Passive observation first

When encountering humming walls, pressure fields, resonant towers, or moving construction systems:

- Stop.
- Listen.
- Observe from a distance.
- Do not touch.
- Do not speak loudly.
- Do not emit competing tones.
- Do not place tools inside channels.
- Do not cross barriers just because they are invisible.

### Do not feed the machines

This sounds absurd, but in this place, machines may respond to:

- Heat.
- Sound.
- Pressure.
- Chemical gradients.
- Animal bodies.
- Moisture.
- Mineral concentrations.

Your body may look like a minor geological event. Do not encourage machines to build around you, deposit minerals on you, or route water toward you.

### If a machine pauses for you

It may be designed to yield to fauna. Do not take that as invitation. Wait, then move predictably and slowly. Do not dart.

### If a machine responds to your voice

Stop speaking. If you must communicate, use local-approved methods. Your voice may accidentally function as a command.

### Drones

Avoid drones unless absolutely necessary and locally permitted. Flying machines may:

- Collide with aerial fauna.
- Disrupt pressure fields.
- Create unacceptable noise.
- Be treated as prey, rival, or contaminant.
- Trigger construction responses.

If you must use a drone, make it slow, quiet, shielded, and biomimetic in behavior. Keep it low and tethered if possible.

## 24. Arrival protocol

When you first enter, do not immediately move into the basin.

### Step one: pause

Stand at the threshold or edge. Let the environment hit you. Feel the altitude, cold, moisture, and sound. Do not try to interpret everything at once.

### Step two: check life support

Confirm:

- Oxygen flow.
- Mask seal.
- Visor clarity.
- Suit integrity.
- Hearing protection level.
- Warmth.
- Hydration.
- Beacon status.
- Navigation system.

### Step three: listen

Identify at least three sound layers:

- Water.
- Machinery.
- Fauna.
- Echo architecture.

Do not move until you can distinguish them.

### Step four: locate your route

Use local guide, acoustic map, or approved beacons. Do not rely on “that looks like a path.” In darkness, apparent paths may be water channels, animal trails, or construction zones.

### Step five: move slowly

Take the first steps with exaggerated care. Test surfaces. Keep your body low. Avoid touching anything unless necessary.

### Step six: monitor yourself

Check:

- Breathing rate.
- Heart rate.
- Oxygen saturation.
- Finger and toe warmth.
- Anxiety level.
- Clarity of thought.

If anything is wrong, fix it early or withdraw.

## 25. How to behave once inside

### Yield to water

If water is moving, do not oppose it. If a route crosses a channel, wait for a safe moment or find another way. If the sound of water changes, pay attention.

### Yield to animals

If fauna is moving, let it move. Do not insert yourself into a migration. Do not step over dense colonies if you can avoid it. If animals surround you, become still and boring.

### Yield to machines

If a construction field, pressure gradient, or resonant system is active, do not interrupt it. If it pauses for you, do not exploit the pause. Move predictably and clear the area.

### Avoid touching unless necessary

Touch can transfer heat, microbes, oils, pressure, or vibration. Use vision substitutes first. If you must touch, use gloves and do so lightly.

### Keep your profile low

Do not stand exposed on ledges. Do not silhouette yourself against faint luminescence. Do not become a large acoustic or thermal object. Stay close to stable surfaces.

### Speak rarely

If you must speak, speak softly. If using communication devices, use bone conduction or haptic signals. Avoid broadcasting.

### Do not create false echoes

Do not tap walls, throw objects, or produce rhythmic noise unless using an approved navigation method. False echoes may confuse fauna or machines.

## 26. Special hazards to remember

### Polished trails

Trails polished by grazing fauna may be dangerously slippery. Avoid them or cross them carefully.

### Ice skins

Water may stand just above a thin skin of ice. Do not assume it is solid. Do not step on it to test it.

### Falling rock

Newly exposed stone sheds fragments. Avoid raw escarpments and listen for small impacts or scraping sounds.

### Invisible supports

Embryonic arches and stairs may be held by temporary fields. Do not lean, sit, or hang gear from them.

### Thermal leaks

Warm shafts may attract gliding fauna and create air movement. Do not camp beneath them. Do not use them as navigation markers unless approved.

### Reservoir masses

Floating masses of filter-feeders may look like solid mats. They are not floors. Do not step on them.

### Drain bodies

Drains lined with resting animals are not clogged. They are occupied. Leave them alone.

### Mineral fog

Fog may deposit material on your gear and lungs. Seal up and wait it out.

### Subsonic dread

If you feel sudden fear, grief, or pressure, check whether it is environmental. Low-frequency sound can produce emotion. Do not let it drive you to run.

## 29. The deeper preparation: becoming an acceptable visitor

The most important preparation is psychological and ethical. You must learn to be present without dominating.

An ordinary human visitor should aim to be:

- Warm enough to think clearly.
- Oxygenated enough to make good decisions.
- Quiet enough not to disturb acoustic systems.
- Dark enough not to disrupt fauna.
- Clean enough not to contaminate.
- Slow enough not to trigger defenses.
- Humble enough to yield.
- Patient enough to listen.
- Observant enough to notice changes.
- Disciplined enough to leave before conditions worsen.

You are not going to a city built for you. You are entering a place where water has priority, animals are infrastructure, sound is architecture, light is subordinate, and construction is a geological process.

The best traveler in this place is not the one who sees the most. It is the one who disturbs the least while remaining alive, warm, dry, and aware.

## 30. Final intuitive summary

If I had to summarize the preparation in one image:

You should prepare like a high-altitude cave diver, a winter mountaineer, a blind navigator, and a respectful wildlife observer all at once. Your body must be sealed against cold mineral moisture. Your breath must be enriched and filtered. Your eyes must be accepted as secondary. Your ears must become your primary sense, protected but not sealed away. Your movements must be slow, deliberate, and acoustically polite. Your gear must be quiet, matte, waterproof, and non-contaminating. Your waste must leave with you. Your presence must not alter water, animals, or growing stone.

When you arrive, you will not see the city. You will feel it: thin air in your lungs, wet cold through your gloves, dripping water on unseen ledges, subsonic machinery in your chest, the rustle and thump of countless bodies in the dark. The stone will be slick under your boots. The air will smell of metal and cold minerals. Somewhere nearby, something large will shift. Somewhere farther away, a bell-like call will answer another. A wall may breathe. A bridge may hum. A drain may be full of resting bodies. You will realize that the city is not empty; it is crowded beyond human comfort, but crowded with lives that do not need your approval.

Your task is not to illuminate it, command it, or hurry through it. Your task is to enter as a careful, temporary disturbance: a small warm visitor moving respectfully through a vast, wet, loud darkness that is slowly teaching rock to become a city.
