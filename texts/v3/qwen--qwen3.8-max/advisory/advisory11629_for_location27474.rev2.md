## Preparing to Visit the Terminal High Plain

The most important preparation is not a piece of equipment. It is an attitude.

This is not a place that will test you with monsters, fire, poison, or melodrama. Its danger is subtler: it is a completed world, a landscape that has already reached its final condition, and you are not a completed thing. You are wet, warm, breathing, noisy, purposeful, and full of unfinished motion. You carry water, microbes, heat, electromagnetic gradients, and intention. In that place, you are a temporary exception.

So the ordinary human multiverse traveler should prepare as if visiting a vast, ancient instrument that has no need of visitors. You should not arrive as an explorer conquering distance. You should arrive as a careful, sealed, almost apologetic event.

The governing principles are:

1. **Be small.**
2. **Be sealed.**
3. **Be quiet.**
4. **Be temporary.**
5. **Do not ask the plain to accommodate you.**

Everything else follows from those.

---

## 1. Understand What You Are Entering

Before choosing gear, the traveler should internalize the nature of the destination.

The plain is a terminal peneplain: a landscape reduced to its final geometry. It has no water, no weather in the ordinary sense, no large life, no accumulation, no dramatic ruins, no visible machinery. Its technology is dispersed through the regolith itself, older than mountains, operating through metric smoothing, low-energy topological rearrangement, inertial damping, matter memory, and processes that resemble planetary-scale computation more than engineering.

The surface is active, but only in its skin. Dust, charge, light, and micro-scale mechanical activity move across it in dry storms. These storms build and dissolve forms rapidly, but they do not erode the bedrock. The bedrock is already finished. What churns is only the surface’s remaining restlessness.

The place also distorts ordinary expectations of distance and progress. Movement may not be cumulative. Paths vanish. Tracks smooth over. Objects may appear near but remain unchanged in apparent size for hours. Local metric conditions can shift subtly during storms. Navigation therefore becomes a matter of patience, timing, and humility rather than simple dead reckoning.

The traveler should understand: **this is not a place to build, mark, conquer, or awaken.** It is a place to pass through with extreme restraint.

---

## 2. Decide Whether an Ordinary Human Should Go at All

An ordinary human body is poorly suited to this environment.

You are mostly water. The plain has no water and seems to have forgotten the concept of solvent chemistry. You exhale moisture. You sweat. Your eyes need lubrication. Your skin cracks without humidity. Your lungs need pressure and oxygen. Your mind needs feedback, sound, landmarks, and a sense that effort produces progress. The plain can deny all of those comforts.

A traveler should be medically screened for:

- High-altitude tolerance.
- Cardiovascular stability.
- Respiratory health.
- Dry-eye susceptibility.
- Sinus and ear pressure regulation.
- Skin fragility.
- Claustrophobia inside sealed suits.
- Agoraphobia or panic in vast open spaces.
- Sensory-deprivation stress.
- Dependence on implanted electronic medical devices.

Anyone with a pacemaker, neurostimulator, insulin pump, or other delicate implanted electronics should be treated as probably unfit unless the device can be conclusively shielded against electrostatic, metric, and field anomalies. The plain’s latent technology may not target such devices, but it may “permit” or “forbid” configurations in ways that are not kind to complex machinery.

Psychologically, the traveler should be stable enough to endure:

- Extreme silence.
- No visible reward for effort.
- Lack of response from the environment.
- Perceptual distortions.
- The emotional weight of an ended world.
- The possibility that nothing they do will matter to the place.

This is not a destination for someone who needs the world to answer back.

## 3. Train the Body for Dry, Thin, Silent Conditions

The plain’s atmosphere is described as cool, thin, clear, dry, and stable. It may be breathable. It may not. Even if breathable, it is likely marginal for an ordinary human.

Preparation should include:

### Altitude conditioning

If the elevation is extreme, the traveler should acclimatize to low oxygen partial pressure before arrival. Practice with controlled altitude exposure, if available. Learn to recognize early symptoms of hypoxia:

- Euphoria.
- Poor judgment.
- Headache.
- Nausea.
- Shortness of breath.
- Tingling.
- Clumsiness.
- Visual disturbance.

Hypoxia is especially dangerous here because it may feel like calm or revelation. The plain already has an “administrative clarity.” A hypoxic mind may mistake that for spiritual significance.

### Dryness discipline

The traveler should practice moving, working, and resting in extremely dry air while wearing a mask or sealed breathing system. Learn to:

- Breathe through humidified or reclaimed air.
- Avoid mouth-breathing dry air.
- Keep lips and eyes protected.
- Recognize early corneal dryness.
- Drink small, frequent amounts rather than large desperate amounts.
- Avoid unnecessary sweating.

### Low-stimulation endurance

The traveler should spend time in quiet, featureless, or visually monotonous environments. Not necessarily dangerous ones, but boring ones. The skill is not excitement management. It is patience management.

Practice:

- Sitting without music or conversation.
- Walking without landmarks.
- Repeating simple checks without becoming compulsive.
- Not needing the environment to provide novelty.
- Accepting that hours may pass with no meaningful external event.

The plain will not entertain you. You must be able to remain sane without being entertained.

## 4. Treat Water as Both Survival and Contraband

Water is the traveler’s greatest need and also the plain’s greatest foreign substance.

There is no water there. Not in pools, frost, clouds, soil, or mineral dampness. The local chemistry appears to exist without solvents. Introducing water may not merely be contamination; it may be an ontological offense against the place’s final state.

Therefore:

### Carry all water in sealed systems

For a short visit, carry more water than intuition suggests necessary. Thin, dry air increases respiratory loss. Stress increases thirst. The absence of humidity makes the body lose water through breathing and skin without the traveler noticing.

A conservative rule for an ordinary human in sealed environmental gear would be to carry enough for the planned duration plus a full emergency reserve. If the suit includes a reclamation system, it should be tested repeatedly. But do not trust reclamation alone. Carry immediate drinking reserve in a sealed, insulated, bite-valve system.

### Reclaim moisture where possible

A good suit should capture:

- Exhaled breath moisture.
- Perspiration vapor.
- Urine, if designed for reclamation.
- Condensation from breathing loops.

However, the reclamation system should be simple, shielded, and fail-safe. If it begins to malfunction, the traveler should abort.

### Release nothing

No water should touch the plain.

That includes:

- Drinking spills.
- Condensate.
- Urine.
- Feces.
- Vomit.
- Blood.
- Tears.
- Saliva.
- Wound irrigation.
- Cleaning water.
- Steam.
- Breath vapor, if not captured.

This sounds severe, but the plain is not a desert where one can simply bury waste. Nothing decays there. Anything organic or solvent-based you leave may persist as an alien stain.

All waste must be sealed and carried out. If the traveler vomits, it goes into a sealed internal or external waste pouch. If the nose bleeds, absorb and seal it. If tears form, wipe them into a sealed absorbent surface. This is not merely environmental ethics; it is basic respect for a place whose chemistry has forgotten water.

## 5. Do Not Trust the Atmosphere

Even if the air appears clean and normal, do not remove the helmet.

The traveler should assume the atmosphere is unverified until proven safe across pressure, oxygen partial pressure, carbon dioxide, humidity, particulates, electrostatic conditions, and micro-scale mechanical contamination.

A proper breathing system should include:

- Full-face sealed helmet or mask.
- Closed-loop breathing if possible.
- Oxygen regulation.
- Carbon dioxide scrubbing.
- Humidification or moisture capture.
- Particulate filtration.
- Pressure support if elevation is high enough.
- Redundant seals.
- Emergency reserve.

Avoid oxygen-enriched mixtures unless medically necessary. High oxygen increases fire risk and may make materials behave unpredictably in electrostatic conditions. Aim for a safe, stable breathing mix rather than a performance-enhancing one.

The traveler should monitor:

- Oxygen partial pressure.
- Carbon dioxide.
- Suit pressure.
- Internal humidity.
- Temperature.
- Filter status.
- Condensate storage.

If the atmosphere is later proven breathable, that is interesting. It does not mean the traveler should breathe it openly. Exhaled moisture is still contamination. A closed-loop system remains preferable.

## 6. Design the Suit Around Smallness, Smoothness, and Low Attachment

The plain favors smallness, distribution, and low attachment. Anything ambitious, protruding, or structurally assertive may be slowly unraveled. The traveler’s suit should therefore be soft, compact, smooth, and unpretentious.

Avoid:

- Large rigid backpacks.
- External frames.
- Long antennas.
- Flapping straps.
- Wide capes or cloaks.
- Metallic spines.
- Large sensor arrays.
- Hard armor plates.
- Exposed wiring.
- Complex articulated mechanisms.
- Loose tools.
- Decorative protrusions.

Prefer:

- A close-fitting pressure or semi-pressure suit.
- Smooth outer surfaces.
- Rounded seams.
- Internal load distribution.
- Small distributed pouches.
- Low-profile joints.
- Soft boots.
- Minimal external hardware.
- Materials that shed dust without flaking.

The traveler should not look like an expedition vehicle. They should look like a quiet, sealed body.

### Outer layer

The outer layer should be:

- Dust-resistant.
- Anti-static or dissipative.
- UV-resistant.
- Low-shedding.
- Smooth enough not to collect grains.
- Pale or neutral in color to reduce heating and visual intrusion.
- Non-flammable.
- Chemically inert.

It should not generate large static charges. It should also not become a continuous conductive cage that encourages dangerous currents. A carefully designed dissipative layer with local charge equalization is preferable to obvious grounding wires. No long grounding trails. The plain itself may not provide a predictable electrical reference.

### Thermal control

The air is cool, but the ground may be warm at noon. The suit should handle:

- Cool thin air.
- Radiative heat from pale ground.
- Chilly dusk.
- Possible cold at night.
- Internal heat from exertion.

Use passive insulation where possible. Avoid large active heating elements. If active thermal control is needed, keep it low-power, shielded, and distributed. No concentrated heat sources.

### Eye and face protection

The eyes are vulnerable. Thin dry air can desiccate the cornea quickly. The helmet visor should seal. If the traveler uses a mask instead, goggles must be sealed and humidified if possible.

Bring:

- Anti-fog protection that does not rely on liquid films.
- UV filtering.
- Neutral-density filtering if glare is severe.
- Spare sealed eye protection.
- Sterile lubricating drops in tiny sealed doses, if medically necessary.

Do not rely on blinking or “toughing it out.” Dry-eye pain can become debilitating.

### Boots

Boots should be soft, flexible, and quiet. The sole should provide grip on powdered mineral and ceramic-hard regolith without being aggressive enough to crush rare lichen. Deep lugs may collect dust and create unnecessary disturbance. Smooth micro-textured soles may be better.

The traveler should practice walking gently. This is not a place for heavy footfalls.

### Gloves

Gloves should preserve tactile sensitivity. The traveler may need to operate small controls and inspect equipment. But gloves must be sealed and low-shedding. Avoid thick clumsy gauntlets unless storms require them.

If touching artifacts is ever necessary — and generally it should not be — use an insulating barrier and remote probes first.

## 7. Keep Equipment Simple, Distributed, and Shielded

The plain’s latent technology may “permit and forbid configurations.” Complex assemblies may be vulnerable. Fastenings may forget their purpose. Materials may lose coherence. This does not mean technology will fail violently. It may simply cease to remain coherent.

Therefore, equipment should follow these rules:

### Small over large

No vehicles. No drones. No large tents. No portable habitats. No heavy machinery. No rover. No construction kit.

If an object is large enough to be considered a structure, the plain may eventually disassemble it. The traveler should not test this.

### Simple over complex

Prefer simple mechanical forms over intricate assemblies. Use monolithic parts where possible. Avoid many fasteners. Avoid long chains of dependency.

For example:

- Tied closures may be better than complex buckles if the buckles can forget their shape.
- Continuous woven loops may be better than many small metal rings.
- Ceramic or glass tools may be better than complex alloys.
- Passive devices may be better than active ones.

But simplicity must not come at the cost of life support. Life support should be robust, shielded, and fail-safe.

### Distributed over concentrated

Do not carry all critical systems in one large box. Distribute essential functions across small sealed modules close to the body. If one module is affected, the others may persist.

### Shielded over exposed

Electronics may be disturbed by electrostatic turbulence, metric anomalies, and planetary-scale annealing processes. Use:

- Electromagnetic shielding.
- Electrostatic dissipation.
- Error-correcting memory.
- Redundant storage.
- Low-power modes.
- Physical isolation from dust.
- Non-volatile backups.

Also keep analog backups. A sealed ceramic notebook and stylus may be more reliable than a tablet. A mechanical or optical time reference may remain meaningful when digital systems drift.

### No emitting devices unless necessary

Avoid strong radio transmissions, lidar pulses, electric fields, magnetic pulses, or anything that might be interpreted as a configuration imposed on the plain. If communication is necessary, use low-power, brief, scheduled bursts. Do not broadcast continuously.

The traveler should not announce themselves loudly to a place that has edited largeness away.

## 8. Carry Only What Is Necessary

The plain has very little per volume. A person with too much gear becomes an offense against its density.

A minimal kit might include:

- Sealed environmental suit.
- Closed-loop breathing system.
- Oxygen/pressure reserve.
- Water reserve and reclamation.
- Sealed food.
- Sealed waste system.
- Small first-aid kit.
- Eye protection.
- Thermal layers.
- Soft insulating rest wrap.
- Chronometers.
- Navigation aids.
- Passive environmental sensors.
- Camera or recording device.
- Ceramic notebook and stylus.
- Low-power communication beacon.
- Personal multiverse return anchor.
- Repair patches.
- Small insulating mat.
- Sample containment, only if sampling is authorized.
- Emergency shelter, low-profile and non-rigid.

Every item should be evaluated with three questions:

1. Is it sealed?
2. Is it small?
3. Can I survive if it fails?

If an item is large, fragile, noisy, solvent-based, adhesive, or emotionally tempting, leave it behind.

## 9. Food Should Be Sealed, Quiet, and Low-Residue

Food is another source of water, organics, crumbs, and contamination.

Prefer:

- Sealed paste or gel rations.
- Low-crumb bars.
- Compact calorie-dense foods.
- Electrolyte balance designed for dry-air exertion.
- Minimal packaging waste.

Avoid:

- Powders.
- Crumbly foods.
- Strong smells.
- Excess salt.
- Diuretics.
- Alcohol.
- Foods requiring preparation with water.

All wrappers must be sealed. Do not let a scrap of packaging drift away. In a dry storm, even a wrapper could become an unwanted configuration.

The traveler should eat small amounts before hunger becomes urgent. Hunger can worsen judgment, but overeating increases thirst and waste.

## 10. Navigation Must Be Patient, Redundant, and Non-Assertive

The plain’s most unsettling property for an ordinary traveler is that movement may not behave as expected. You may walk toward something and find it no nearer. Tracks vanish. Paths do not remain paths. Storms can alter local metric conditions. Distance becomes psychological.

Do not navigate here as if on a map.

### Use time and resources, not distance

Set turn-back limits by consumables and elapsed time, not by visible progress.

A conservative rule:

- Use one-third of resources going outward.
- Reserve one-third for return.
- Keep one-third as emergency reserve.

If distance does not behave, this rule becomes even more important. Do not say, “I can see it, so I can reach it.” You may not.

### Use multiple navigation methods

Bring:

- Accurate chronometers.
- Sun or sky-position tables, if the local sun is understood.
- Inertial reference, shielded and tested.
- Visual bearings, with skepticism.
- Personal return anchor.
- Written logs.
- Ceramic notebook sketches.

But assume each method can be subtly wrong.

Shadows may linger a fraction too long. Horizons may seem to step back. Distant objects may overlap with storms. Instruments may disagree. The traveler should not panic when this happens. They should stop, verify life support, compare methods, and decide whether to continue.

### Do not create permanent markers

The plain erases footprints and smooths disturbances. Do not fight this by building cairns, scratching symbols, laying lines, or leaving flags. Such acts are ethically wrong and practically unreliable. They may be unraveled anyway.

If temporary markers are absolutely necessary, they should be:

- Tiny.
- Inert.
- Easily retrieved.
- Non-reactive.
- Never left behind.

But better still: do not rely on markers. Rely on timing, restraint, and the willingness to turn back.

### If progress stops, wait

If an object remains apparently unchanged in size for too long, do not force the issue.

Possible responses:

1. Stop.
2. Check bearings.
3. Check consumables.
4. Observe for storm activity.
5. Wait for local conditions to settle.
6. Try again from a slightly different orientation.
7. If no progress after reasonable time, turn back.

The correct phrase for this place is not “push through.” It is “wait for distance to permit you.”

## 11. Movement Should Be Slow, Soft, and Low

The traveler should not run.

Running increases:

- Water loss.
- Heat.
- Dust disturbance.
- Chance of tripping.
- Static buildup.
- Emotional arousal.
- Perceptual fixation.

Walk slowly. Keep steps soft. Keep arms close. Avoid sweeping gestures. Do not kick dust. Do not spin. Do not lie down and roll for the sake of experience. Do not make large shapes with your body or equipment.

If you carry a walking staff, it should be short, simple, ceramic or glass-composite, and not used to strike the ground forcefully. Better yet, do not carry one unless balance requires it. The plain is flat. Its danger is not terrain.

If the ground feels warm at noon, do not sit directly on rare biological crusts or artifact surfaces. Use a thin insulating mat on bare regolith.

## 12. Prepare for Dry Storms

The dry storms are the plain’s most visible activity. They are not storms of rain or ordinary wind. They are moving architectures of dust, charge, optical distortion, and micro-scale mechanical action. They may loosen local gravity, lift pebbles, arrange grains into lattices, create shapes resembling buildings or writing, and disturb instruments.

They are dangerous not because they are massive, but because they are active, charged, and capable of altering local conditions.

### Learn the signs

Possible precursors include:

- Dust columns rising or twisting.
- Sheets of glittering matter sliding over the ground.
- Ear pressure.
- Hair or suit fibers stiffening.
- Static tingling.
- Instrument jitter.
- Footprints smoothing faster than expected.
- Lichen or filament tufts brightening at the tips.
- Air shimmer with no heat source.
- A feeling of “intention” in the turbulence.

If several signs appear, treat the storm as imminent.

### Storm protocol

When a dry storm approaches:

1. **Stop moving.**
2. **Move away from artifacts, lichen, and any standing structure if possible.**
3. **Choose open, bare regolith.**
4. **Lower your profile.**
5. **Secure all loose items.**
6. **Close external pouches.**
7. **Turn off nonessential electronics.**
8. **Retract or stow antennas.**
9. **Avoid forming large conductive loops with your body or gear.**
10. **Keep your head down and your visor protected.**
11. **Breathe slowly.**
12. **Wait.**

If possible, kneel or crouch on a thin insulating mat. Do not lie flat if that increases contact with charged ground, unless your suit is designed to isolate you. Make yourself small, but do not become a long horizontal conductor.

If pebbles or grains begin to lift around you, do not grab at them. Keep hands close. If you feel light, do not jump. Lower your center of mass and distribute your weight.

If the storm forms shapes that look like buildings, ribs, sails, or writing, do not chase them. Do not try to read them. Do not interpret them as messages. They may be nothing but the plain’s surface metabolism. If they seem meaningful, that is likely your mind completing patterns.

### After the storm

Do not immediately resume travel.

Check:

- Suit seals.
- Visor clarity.
- Breathing system.
- Water system.
- Waste seals.
- Electronics.
- Navigation instruments.
- Charge state of equipment.
- Any sign of material softening or unraveling.

If fasteners seem confused, fabric seems to have lost coherence, or equipment behaves oddly, abort.

Wait until the plain feels settled. Sometimes that may take minutes. Sometimes longer.

## 13. Shelter Should Be Minimal or Absent

The best shelter on this plain is not being there during the harshest periods.

For short visits, no shelter may be needed. For longer visits, shelter becomes a serious ethical and practical problem.

Do not bring a large tent. Do not build a camp. Do not erect walls. Do not anchor anything deeply. Do not create a compound.

If rest is necessary:

- Use a shallow bare area, avoiding lichen and artifacts.
- Use a low-profile insulating wrap.
- Keep the shelter person-sized or smaller.
- Use no stakes if possible.
- Use body weight and small distributed ballast only if necessary.
- Keep the exterior smooth.
- Pack everything away completely afterward.

Existing structures may offer shelter, but they are not neutral. They are calibration points, instruments, or residue of old intelligences. Entering them may disturb their function or contaminate them. If you enter a dome or shelter:

- Do not touch interior surfaces unnecessarily.
- Do not remove artifacts.
- Do not introduce moisture.
- Do not stay long.
- Do not sleep there unless emergency demands it.
- Leave immediately if the stillness feels oppressive or your instruments behave strangely.

A place whose interior air is perfectly still may be maintained by residual fields. That does not mean it is safe for you. It may mean you are the contaminant.

## 14. Treat Artifacts as Active Even When They Seem Dead

The scattered structures on the plain are not ordinary ruins. They are reduced residues, calibration points, anchorages, instruments left running after their purpose became unclear.

Some may be inactive. Some may be only minimally active. Some may be waiting.

The traveler should assume that all artifacts are potentially sensitive.

### General artifact rules

- Observe from a distance first.
- Do not touch.
- Do not lean.
- Do not sit.
- Do not place your equipment on them.
- Do not enter rings or alignments.
- Do not stand in unusual shadows.
- Do not use them as reliable compasses.
- Do not attempt to activate them.
- Do not attempt to “complete” a postponed action.
- Do not remove fragments.
- Do not repair them.
- Do not clean them.

If an artifact hums faintly when touched, that is not an invitation. It is a warning that your contact has registered. Withdraw slowly.

If a black cube is aligned with directions that do not match the horizon, do not try to orient yourself by it. If a ring of posts surrounds a dust-free patch, do not step inside. If a marker needle casts a shadow that lingers too long, do not stand in that shadow.

### Recording artifacts

Use passive observation:

- Sketches.
- Photographs without flash.
- Distance measurements that do not emit strong fields.
- Note-taking.
- Environmental readings.

If scanning is necessary, use the lowest effective power. Do not bathe artifacts in radiation. Do not assume they are inert stone.

If an artifact appears to respond to your presence — by humming, changing texture, altering dust behavior, or affecting your instruments — leave.

You are not there to wake the old machines.

## 15. Respect the Sparse Ecology

There is life, but it is not like water-fed life. It is slow, mineral, conductive, and patient.

### Lichen and crusts

The copper-gray rosettes, brittle filaments, and dark scales may be older than civilizations. They may harvest light, thermal gradients, and electrostatic potential. They may also participate in patterns that are growth, cultivation, or computation.

Do not step on them.

Choose bare regolith. Before sitting or kneeling, inspect the ground. If you see faint metallic glints, regular arcs, or thin crusts, move away. Destroying a handspan of lichen may erase millennia.

### Wiry tufts

The dry filament tufts are not grass. They may stiffen and brighten in charged air. Some may crumble after storms. Do not pluck them. Do not try to collect their mineral grains.

### Veil-like drifters

The transparent sheet-like beings that glide on electrostatic lift are rare and fragile. If they appear:

- Remain still.
- Do not chase.
- Do not wave arms.
- Do not activate high-field devices.
- Do not attempt to capture them.

They may be drawn to charge differentials. Your suit may be interesting to them. That is not friendship. Keep your fields low.

### Plated creatures

The small multi-plated beings near lichen crusts move slowly and may remain still for long periods. Do not pick them up. Do not press them or try to force interaction. They may be exchanging ions or listening in a timescale you cannot share.

### Large storm forms

If you glimpse tall jointed shadows in dust columns or ribbon-like flickers in upper air, do not approach. They may be creatures. They may be temporary assemblies. They may be processes. The distinction may not matter.

Give them distance. If they approach, which is unlikely, remain still, reduce your fields, and prepare to withdraw.

## 16. Prepare for the Silence

The sound environment is almost absent. Thin air carries little. The plain’s latent technology dampens vibration. Storms may pass with only a faint hiss. Dust columns collapse without thuds. The world offers almost no echo.

For an ordinary human, this can be more disturbing than noise.

You will hear:

- Your breathing.
- Your heartbeat.
- Your joints.
- Your suit.
- Your thoughts.
- Possibly tinnitus.
- The small internal noises of existence.

This can create the sensation that your body is embarrassingly loud. It can also produce auditory hallucinations if the mind strains for input.

Preparation includes:

- Practicing silence before arrival.
- Learning not to chase sounds.
- Keeping a soft haptic rhythm if needed, but not filling the environment with noise.
- Avoiding loud speech.
- Using brief, quiet voice messages if communication is necessary.
- Monitoring for hallucination without overreacting.

If you bring audio for psychological comfort, keep it private, low, and short. Do not broadcast sound into the plain. The place does not need your music.

## 17. Prepare for Perceptual Wrongness

The light is normal. Shadows fall where they should. There is no theatrical glow. That normality makes the subtle anomalies more unsettling.

Possible perceptual effects:

- Objects appear near but remain distant.
- The horizon seems to step back.
- Distance feels psychological.
- Storms appear to occupy the same space as far-off ruins.
- Shadows may linger slightly too long.
- The curvature of the world may induce nausea.
- Silence may make time feel stretched.
- Your own movements may feel ineffectual.

Do not interpret these as supernatural attacks. They are likely local metric, optical, and psychological effects. But do not ignore them either.

Countermeasures:

- Frequently check objective measurements.
- Keep a written log.
- Compare instruments.
- Do not stare at the horizon if it causes vertigo.
- Focus on near-ground details when nauseated.
- Sit or kneel if balance is affected.
- Use time-based goals.
- Accept uncertainty without forcing certainty.

If perceptual distortion becomes severe, abort.

## 18. Psychological Preparation: Become Comfortable Being Irrelevant

The plain does not hate you. It does not love you. It does not require your interpretation.

Many travelers will feel an urge to assign meaning:

- “The storms are speaking.”
- “The artifacts are waiting for me.”
- “The plain is testing me.”
- “I am meant to understand something.”
- “My presence matters here.”

Some of that may be poetic truth. But practically, it is dangerous. It can lead to reckless contact, prolonged exposure, or refusal to withdraw.

The traveler should cultivate a mindset of courteous irrelevance.

You are a brief, sealed, temporary event in a completed landscape. Your goal is not to awaken, repair, redeem, or decipher the plain. Your goal is to visit without harming it or yourself.

Useful mental practices:

- Slow breathing.
- Naming observations without judgment.
- Checking body state before emotion.
- Accepting lack of progress.
- Letting go of the need for revelation.
- Treating awe as a signal to be careful, not to advance.
- Treating dread as information, not failure.

If the traveler feels watched, they should remember: the place may contain attention without habitation. That does not imply hostility. It may simply be the residue of ancient calibration.

Still, if the feeling becomes overwhelming, leave.

## 19. Group Travel Should Be Small and Disciplined

An ordinary human may prefer companions. That is understandable. But the plain should not be crowded.

Ideal group size: one or two travelers. Three at most.

Larger groups become a settlement-like event. They generate more heat, moisture, noise, movement, and psychological complexity.

If traveling with others:

- Keep gear individual and minimal.
- Do not cluster into a large camp.
- Maintain visual or periodic contact, but do not rely on sight alone.
- Use low-power communication.
- Establish hand signals.
- Agree on turn-back criteria in advance.
- Agree on storm protocol.
- Do not separate casually.
- If separated, stop and beacon rather than running.

Distance may distort separation. A companion may look near but be far. A companion may appear far but be close. Use time checks and predetermined rendezvous rules.

If someone shows signs of hypoxia, dehydration, panic, or perceptual collapse, the group should withdraw. Do not argue with impaired judgment.

## 20. Multiverse-Specific Preparation

A multiverse traveler has one advantage: the ability to leave. That advantage should be protected at all costs.

### Arrive small

Do not arrive with a large portal, vehicle, or base camp. The plain disfavors large persistent structures. Arrival should be brief, low-energy, and person-scaled if possible.

If the transit method creates noise, light, pressure waves, or field disturbance, delay entry until it can be minimized. Do not open a portal into a dry storm. Wait for a lull.

### Use a personal return anchor

The return system should not depend on local geography, local distance, or local landmarks. The plain’s metric conditions can change. Tracks vanish. Paths fail.

Use a return anchor tied to:

- The traveler’s home reality.
- Personal biological or temporal signature.
- A pre-arranged extraction schedule.
- A passive beacon carried on the body.
- Redundant recall triggers.

Do not bury an anchor. Do not attach it to an artifact. Do not rely on a cairn, dome, or horizon feature.

### Schedule extraction

Set a return window before entering. If the window is missed, activate emergency recall or beacon immediately. Do not assume you can “walk back” later.

For safety, use layered extraction:

1. Planned timed return.
2. Manual emergency recall.
3. Passive homing beacon.
4. Prearranged rescue protocol if all else fails.

But rescue should not be assumed. The plain is not a place where a large rescue team should operate.

### Decontamination before return

Do not bring plain dust into another world.

The regolith may contain latent engineering finer than pollen. It may be harmless. It may not be. An ordinary multiverse traveler should treat it as a quarantine risk.

Before return:

- Inspect suit exterior.
- Remove loose dust gently with sealed methods.
- Avoid shaking or brushing dust into the air.
- Seal outer garments if possible.
- Place contaminated items in containment.
- Do not open sample containers outside controlled facilities.

After return, the traveler should undergo medical and material quarantine. Check for:

- Respiratory irritation.
- Skin changes.
- Eye damage.
- Electrostatic burns.
- Radiation exposure.
- Psychological disturbance.
- Equipment anomalies.
- Unexpected dust behavior.

The plain’s technology should not be given casual access to other worlds.

## 21. Emergency Planning

The plain is not merciful, but it is not malicious. Emergencies will likely arise from the traveler’s own needs: water loss, oxygen failure, hypoxia, suit breach, disorientation, panic, or storm exposure.

### Hard abort criteria

The traveler should define these before entering and obey them without debate.

Abort if:

- Suit seal is compromised.
- Water reserve falls below emergency threshold.
- Oxygen reserve falls below safe threshold.
- Carbon dioxide rises unexpectedly.
- Breathing system malfunctions.
- Static discharge affects the body or suit.
- Instruments disagree wildly and do not recover.
- A storm is approaching faster than expected.
- An artifact responds to you.
- You experience hypoxia symptoms.
- You feel irresistible urge to touch or enter something.
- You become unable to trust your perception.
- You see large forms moving toward you.
- Your equipment begins to lose coherence.

Do not negotiate with abort criteria. The plain does not reward ambition.

### If stranded

If extraction fails:

1. Stop moving.
2. Conserve water and oxygen.
3. Activate beacon.
4. Avoid storms.
5. Do not attempt long-distance travel unless there is a known, reliable extraction point.
6. Remain visible only in the sense of keeping your beacon active; do not build large signals.
7. Do not leave waste.
8. Do not seek shelter in artifacts unless exposure is immediately life-threatening.
9. Keep calm. Panic consumes water and oxygen.

Movement without progress is possible. Wandering may waste resources while making you feel active. Stillness may be safer.

## 22. Ethical Preparation

The ethical rule is simple: **leave the plain as if you were never there.**

But that is harder than it sounds because your body is a contaminant by nature.

You must prevent:

- Moisture release.
- Microbial release.
- Organic debris.
- Footprint persistence, though the plain helps.
- Equipment loss.
- Artifact disturbance.
- Biological disturbance.
- Sound pollution.
- Field pollution.
- Sampling without necessity.
- Emotional projections that justify interference.

Do not try to water anything. Do not try to grow anything. Do not try to repair the ancient structures. Do not try to calm the storms. Do not try to stabilize the dust. Do not try to make the place more comprehensible.

If you find something beautiful, do not take it.

If you find something sad, do not try to fix it.

If you find something that seems unfinished, leave it unfinished.

The plain is not waiting for you to complete it. It is already complete.

## 23. What the Visit Will Feel Like

Imagine stepping onto it during a lull.

The first thing you notice is the clarity. The light is ordinary. The sky is pale near the horizon and darker overhead. Shadows are sharp. There is no glow, no omen, no strange color. Everything is exactly as lit as it should be, and that plainness feels almost severe.

The second thing you notice is the silence. Your breathing fills your helmet. Your heartbeat becomes a private drum. The suit makes small noises. The world does not answer.

The third thing you notice is the breadth. The horizon is too wide. You can see curvature, not as a picture but as a slight wrongness in the shape of distance. Your stomach may react before your mind understands why.

Then you notice the emptiness. There is almost nothing per volume. A stone is an event. A faint swell is geography. A distant marker, if there is one, may take hours to reach. The plain does not fill itself for your comfort. It remains open, cool, mineral, and patient.

If a storm appears, it may begin as a pale column, a glittering sheet, or a pressure in the ears. It will move with strange creativity, building towers, ribs, lattices, and false architecture, then dissolving them. It may be silent or nearly silent. Pebbles may lift. Grains may align. Your instruments may hesitate. You will feel that you are witnessing thought without a thinker.

If you walk, your footprints may smooth behind you. If you try to remember the path, you may find that the plain has already edited it. If you aim toward an object, you may learn that longing does not shorten distance.

If you find an artifact, it will likely be small, cool, too perfect, and uninterested in explaining itself. It may hum with postponed action. You will feel the urge to touch it. You should not.

If you see life, it will be sparse and slow. A veil-like drifter may pass like smoked glass above the dust. A plated creature may remain motionless for longer than your patience allows. The lichen under your careful feet may be ancient beyond ordinary comprehension.

And if you prepare correctly, you will understand that your role is not to become the story of this place. The plain already has its final story. You are only a brief, sealed, courteous interruption.

## 24. Final Preparation Summary

Before entering, the traveler should:

- Be medically and psychologically screened.
- Train for altitude, dryness, silence, and non-progressive movement.
- Use a sealed, smooth, low-profile environmental suit.
- Carry all water in closed systems.
- Release no moisture or waste.
- Avoid large structures, vehicles, drones, and camps.
- Use simple, shielded, distributed equipment.
- Carry redundant navigation but trust it cautiously.
- Set hard turn-back limits.
- Learn dry-storm protocol.
- Avoid artifacts and rare life.
- Prepare for perceptual distortion and emotional insignificance.
- Use a personal, body-mounted multiverse return anchor.
- Schedule extraction.
- Plan quarantine on return.
- Accept that the visit may yield no revelation.

The ideal traveler is not bold, not loud, not curious in a grasping way. The ideal traveler is humble, sealed, observant, and willing to leave without leaving a trace.

To visit the terminal high plain, an ordinary human must prepare to become almost nothing per volume: a small, contained breath moving carefully across a finished world.
