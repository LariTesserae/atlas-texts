Prepare as if you are entering a small, hot, wet laboratory-garden that has already learned to function without you. The basin is not hostile, but it is precise. It will not notice your intentions; it will notice your moisture, your heat, your pressure, your breath, your mistakes. An ordinary human multiverse traveler should therefore prepare less like a conqueror and more like a careful guest in an experiment that has become a landscape.

The core rule is this:

**Do not try to dominate the place. Calibrate yourself to it.**

---

## 1. Understand what kind of visit this is

This is not a grand vista. It is intimate, room-sized, humid, hot, quiet, and intensely active at a small scale. The dangers are not dramatic monsters or obvious traps. They are:

- slick stone and sharp young rock;
- heat and humidity that stress the body quietly;
- water that behaves strangely and may be chemically or physically unusual;
- subtle technology that responds to moisture, charge, or mineral flow;
- self-repairing surfaces that may incorporate foreign matter;
- silence and containment that can unsettle the mind;
- a possible temporal or phase anomaly near the black teardrop artifact.

The traveler should expect a place that feels both new and ancient: new rock, old machines, patient water, sparse life, no visible guardians. The correct attitude is reverent, scientific, and restrained.

You are not entering a ruin to loot. You are entering a process to witness.

## 2. Pre-visit physical preparation

### Heat and humidity conditioning

The basin is hot and close. Because the air is saturated, sweat will not cool you efficiently. Before visiting, the traveler should spend time in safe, controlled humid heat if possible: warm rooms, steamy climates, controlled humidity chambers, or supervised heat acclimation sessions.

The goal is not endurance in the athletic sense. The goal is to learn how to move slowly while overheated, how to keep breathing steady, and how to recognize early heat stress.

Signs to watch for:

- headache;
- nausea;
- dizziness;
- sudden irritability;
- excessive thirst;
- confusion;
- clammy skin despite heat;
- muscle cramps;
- heart rate higher than expected for the effort.

Because the basin sits high and the heat feels “thin” and bright, altitude may also be a factor. Even if breathing is not difficult, an ordinary human may feel subtle strain: faster pulse, lightheadedness when standing, dry eyes, mild shortness of breath on exertion.

If the traveler has heart, lung, blood pressure, kidney, or thermoregulation problems, this environment should be treated as medically demanding.

### Balance and footing

The basin floor is wet, terraced, glazed, broken, and slick. Practice walking slowly on wet stone, moss, and uneven mineral crust. Learn to place the whole foot carefully instead of striking with the heel. Learn to keep the center of gravity low.

Useless habits to avoid:

- rushing;
- long strides;
- pivoting sharply;
- stepping on wet moss without testing;
- trusting shiny surfaces;
- leaning back on steep slick stone;
- putting hands into cracks without looking.

The rock is young and sharp. Falls can cause cuts, abrasions, bruises, or broken bones. In a hot, wet, mineral-rich environment, even small wounds can become irritating or infected.

### Respiratory preparation

The air is breathable but textured: warm mineral, green growth, steam, faint ozone, sour-sweet heated stone. That does not mean it is harmless.

Possible irritants:

- fine mineral dust;
- steam;
- spores or plant particulates;
- unknown microbial aerosols;
- trace gases from heated stone or hidden tech;
- condensation carrying dissolved minerals.

The traveler should be comfortable wearing a light respirator or filtered face covering for extended periods. Breathing exercises can help: slow nasal inhale, controlled exhale, no panic if the mask becomes damp.

If the traveler has asthma, allergies, or sensitive lungs, bring prescribed inhalers and a conservative emergency plan.

## 3. Mental preparation: learn to be quiet

The basin’s strangeness is not loud. It is quiet, damp, and attentive. There are isolated sounds: droplets, steam ticks, sliding beads of water, faint mineral contact. The silence can feel occupied even when nothing visible is present.

An ordinary human should prepare psychologically for:

- feeling watched without evidence;
- hyper-awareness of one’s own breathing, heartbeat, and clothing;
- under-stimulation followed by sudden fixation on tiny details;
- mild claustrophobia from the contained walls;
- mild agoraphobia from the open sky above;
- loss of normal horizon cues;
- the sense that time is moving strangely, especially near the artifact.

Practice stillness before visiting. Sit quietly in a humid room. Listen for small sounds. Move slowly. Do not fill silence with talk, music, or unnecessary noise.

The place has a kind of etiquette: economy of motion. The pale creature near the pool does not flee in terror; it refuses to waste movement. The traveler should imitate that.

Move only when movement is necessary. Observe before touching. Touch only when observation is insufficient. Take only what must be taken. Leave almost everything alone.

## 4. Clothing: wear something that accepts moisture but does not surrender to it

You will become damp. Accept that. But clothing should manage moisture rather than absorb it helplessly.

### Base layer

Use a thin, wicking, quick-drying layer against the skin. Avoid cotton. Cotton will stay wet, chafe, and cool the body unpredictably.

Good options:

- synthetic moisture-wicking fabric;
- lightweight merino if tolerated;
- seamless or flat-seam construction to reduce chafing;
- pale or muted color to reduce heat absorption and visual disturbance.

The traveler may sweat heavily in humid heat. Skin maceration can occur in folds, feet, underarms, groin, and under straps. Anti-chafe balm can help, but avoid strongly scented products.

### Mid layer

The basin is hot, but the air at altitude can feel thin and bright. A very light insulating layer may be useful if the traveler becomes chilled by wet clothing, evaporation, or the cooler building.

It should compress small and dry quickly.

### Outer layer

The outer layer should be:

- water-resistant but breathable;
- abrasion-resistant on shoulders, knees, and forearms;
- close-fitting enough not to snag on sharp rock;
- quiet; matte; not shiny;
- preferably hooded.

A full rain shell may become uncomfortably hot, but a light breathable shell with ventilation can protect against falling condensation, wall-spray, and drizzle generated by the basin’s own weather.

Avoid long flowing garments. They can catch on sharp edges, dip into water channels, or brush devices and plants.

### Footwear

Footwear is critical.

Ideal boots resemble canyoneering or technical wet-rock shoes:

- sticky rubber soles;
- excellent wet traction;
- snug but not tight;
- quick-draining or sealed, depending on design;
- protective toe and heel;
- stable enough for sharp stone;
- flexible enough to feel the ground;
- non-marking;
- no exposed metal parts if possible.

The traveler should avoid heavy mountaineering boots with aggressive lugs that may gouge mineral crust. The goal is to move without scarring the surface.

Bring extra socks. Feet will become wet, mineral-stained, or irritated. Change socks during rest if possible.

### Gloves

Wear thin, grippy, cut-resistant gloves. The rock edges bite. Ceramic shards, metallic filaments, and broken panels may be sharp. Plants may be slick, swollen, or chemically active.

Gloves should allow fine manipulation. Bulky gloves are dangerous if you need to feel a surface before trusting it.

### Head and eyes

The light is ordinary but intense in its high, metallic brightness. Wet surfaces reflect it upward. Bring:

- a light hat with a brim;
- polarized or tinted eye protection;
- clear eye protection for low light or steam;
- side shields if possible.

A brim helps with condensation falling from upper walls. It also reduces glare.

If the traveler wears corrective lenses, use anti-fog treatment and carry a soft cleaning cloth. Humidity will fog them almost immediately.

### Helmet

A lightweight climbing helmet is wise. The walls are young and unsettled. Droplets are not the only thing that may fall; small chips of fresh rock or mineral crust could detach.

The helmet should be matte, vented but not open enough to admit fine spray, and fitted securely.

### Respiratory protection

Carry at least:

- a light filtered face covering for humid air and droplets;
- a compact respirator with particulate filtration;
- spare filters in sealed dry bags.

If the sour-sweet smell strengthens, if steam thickens, or if the traveler feels throat or eye irritation, leave the area rather than pushing forward.

## 5. Hydration and food: bring everything, pack everything out

Do not assume local water is safe.

The water in the basin may be heavy with dissolved minerals, isotopically unusual, biologically active, or threaded with microscopic technological processes. The fact that it looks clear means nothing. Rings spreading slowly from droplets suggest unusual density or surface tension. That is a warning, not decoration.

### Water

Carry more water than you think you need. Hot, humid environments cause deceptive fluid loss. Sweat may not evaporate; it simply sheets off, and the traveler may underestimate how much fluid is being lost.

For a short visit of a few hours, an ordinary human should consider at least:

- 2 to 3 liters of drinking water per person;
- additional electrolyte solution;
- a small reserve for eye or wound rinsing.

Use sealed containers. Avoid bladders with exposed bite valves unless they can be covered and kept clean. A metal or polymer bottle with a sealed cap is better.

Do not refill from pools, channels, wall-beads, or steam condensate unless in an emergency and only after robust analysis and purification. Even then, be suspicious. If the water behaves strangely, do not drink it.

### Electrolytes

Plain water may not be enough in humid heat. Bring:

- electrolyte tablets;
- salted drink mix;
- small oral rehydration salts;
- low-sugar mineral supplements if tolerated.

Avoid overly sweet drinks that increase nausea in heat.

### Food

Eat before entering if possible. If food is needed inside:

- use sealed, low-crumb, low-odor rations;
- avoid anything that flakes;
- avoid strong smells;
- do not eat near pools, moss, devices, or artifacts;
- pack out all wrappers, crumbs, and residue.

The basin is sparse and careful. Food particles could become contaminants.

### Waste

Pack out all waste. Urine, feces, vomit, blood, saliva, sweat-wrung cloth, used tissues, and hygiene products must not be left behind. The basin’s systems sort water and repair surfaces. Do not give them your biology to sort.

Carry odor-proof waste bags and sealable containers. If the visit is long, plan a portable waste system.

Avoid:

- oily lubricants that can spread;

Prefer:

- matte finishes;
- soft-tipped probes;

Everything should be cleaned before arrival. Sterilization by heat or UV is preferable to chemical residues. If chemicals are used, allow complete evaporation and air-out before entering.

## 9. Arrival protocol

Do not step straight into the basin.

Even if the place looks small and calm, begin by observing it from the rim or entrance for at least one local weather cycle.

### First, stop and breathe

Check your body:

- Are you dizzy?
- Are you overheating?
- Is your breathing normal?
- Do your eyes water?
- Do you smell anything sharp or sour?
- Does your skin sting?

If any symptom is strong, do not enter.

### Observe the water

Watch where water moves steadily. Those paths are likely safer.

Watch where water beads and waits. Avoid those places. They may indicate hollow ground, thin crust, hidden heat, or active sensing surfaces.

Watch where droplets fall from above. They may seem gentle, but in this basin even droplets can mark processes.

Watch the pools. If rings spread slowly and heavily, the liquid may be denser than expected. Do not wade into it.

### Observe the steam

Steam seams are not decorative. They are heat sources. Identify them from a distance. Do not lean over them. Do not let condensation from steam settle on exposed skin for long.

If steam smells strongly sour, sweet, metallic, or sharp, retreat.

### Observe the devices

Notice the black thorn-like or seed-like objects. Notice how moss leans toward them when wet. Notice whether they darken when touched by water.

Do not approach them closely unless necessary. Do not touch them with wet gloves, bare skin, or metal tools.

### Observe the artifact

If the black teardrop is visible, note its position but do not focus on it indirectly for too long. If it seems out of phase when viewed from the corner of the eye, treat it as a potential cognitive or temporal hazard.

Do not make it the first object you investigate.

### Observe the building

The low bone-ash building near the highest terrace may be useful as a temporary observation point, but enter it only after checking:

- water sheet flow;
- floor stability;
- hidden drain;
- cooler air;
- absence of creatures;
- absence of active devices;
- absence of falling condensation.

Do not assume it is a shelter. It may be part of the basin’s water logic.

## 10. Entering the basin

If possible, enter during a drier phase of the local cycle: when heat has risen, steam is lifting, and the surface has firmed slightly. But expect the basin to reverse. Moisture may gather higher up and fall later as warm drizzle.

Choose an entry point:

- stable stone;
- away from moss mats;
- away from steam seams;
- away from black devices;
- away from the artifact;
- away from fragile mineral crust;
- not directly under heavy dripping;
- with a clear return path.

If arriving by multiversal insertion, avoid materializing inside water, on moss, on mineral crust, or beside devices. Choose a bare, stable ledge near the edge if possible.

Before committing weight, test the surface.

Use a soft-tipped pole or gloved hand. Press gently. Listen for hollow sounds. Look for water beading nearby. If the surface flexes, cracks, or releases mineral dust, withdraw.

## 11. Moving inside: follow water, but do not trust it completely

The description says water must be followed. This is true, but with caution.

### Where water flows steadily

Steady flowing water often marks a stable channel. The stone there may be safer because water has chosen a continuous path.

But it will be slick. Move with short steps. Keep your weight centered. Do not let your feet cross.

### Where water beads and waits

Avoid these places. Beading water may mean:

- hollow ground;
- thin crust;
- warm stone;
- active surface tension;
- hidden device;
- unstable mineral layer;
- a place where liquid is being assessed or held.

If you must pass near beaded water, test first. Do not step directly beside it unless necessary.

### Where steam rises

Give steam seams wide space. The stone around them may be hot, damp, and slippery. The air above may contain irritating vapor. Do not place hands nearby. Do not set equipment beside them.

### Where mineral crust is pale and rippled

The crust may be strong, but it may also be brittle. Step lightly. Distribute weight. If possible, follow stone rather than crust. Do not scuff it.

### Where built fragments lie

Ceramic shards, metallic filaments, black panels, and fused platforms may be stable or may be active. Do not step on them. Do not move them. Do not use them as handholds.

The three upright posts with round wet beads are especially suspect. Do not touch the beads. Do not lean on the posts. Do not tie anything to them.

## 12. Movement style: be economical

The basin rewards restraint.

### Walk slowly

Take small steps. Let your foot feel the surface before committing. Keep knees slightly bent. Keep hands free.

### Avoid sudden turns

Wet stone punishes pivoting. Turn by taking several small steps, not by twisting one foot.

### Use hands carefully

Touch only when needed. Wear gloves. Test before gripping. Sharp rock can cut through thin gloves if you fall.

### Use poles if helpful

A lightweight trekking pole with a soft rubber tip can help test ground and maintain balance. But do not stab it into crust, moss, or channels. Use it as a sensor, not a weapon.

### Do not sit or kneel on active surfaces

If you need to rest, choose a high, dryish, non-active ledge. Use a small inert sit pad. Do not rest directly on mineral crust, self-repairing ceramic, moss, or near black devices.

The environment may slowly deposit mineral slurry on objects left still for too long. Your gear could become stiff, coated, or partially bonded to the surface.

Check your boots, gloves, and equipment periodically. If you notice a pale film forming, move and gently clean it later outside the basin.

## 13. Breathing, heat, and body management

The air is thick but breathable. Still, treat your body as a delicate instrument.

### Breathe through the nose if possible

This reduces inhalation of droplets and dust. It also helps regulate heat and moisture.

### Keep your mouth closed unless speaking

Talking releases moisture and microbes. In a place that sorts water, your breath is a small contamination.

### Monitor your face and eyes

If your eyes burn, if your throat tightens, if your skin feels coated, withdraw to drier air.

### Manage sweat

You cannot stop sweating, but you can prevent it from dripping uncontrolled:

- wipe face with a clean cloth;
- store the cloth sealed after use;
- do not wring sweat into pools or channels;
- adjust layers before overheating;
- rest in the cooler building briefly if needed.

### Hydrate before you are thirsty

Take small regular sips. Do not gulp large amounts at once.

### Protect against glare

Wet surfaces reflect light upward. Use eye protection even if the sky seems gentle.

## 14. Dealing with the basin’s weather cycles

The basin makes its own minor weather. Expect phases.

### Thickening phase

The air becomes heavier. Wall-weeping increases. Pools tremble with droplets. Moisture gathers. During this phase:

- avoid low depressions;
- protect electronics;
- cover notes;
- wipe lenses carefully;
- watch for increased slickness;
- do not take shelter under overhangs that may shed mineral fragments;
- keep away from upper rim if condensation becomes heavy.

### Steam phase

Heat rises. Steam lifts. Surfaces dry slightly at floor level while moisture moves upward. During this phase:

- avoid steam columns;
- watch for reduced visibility;
- monitor temperature;
- do not assume the basin is becoming safe;
- be aware that moisture may soon fall again.

### Drizzle phase

Warm drizzle may fall from nowhere as condensation drops from higher surfaces. During this phase:

- put on breathable shell;
- protect head and neck;
- slow movement;
- expect new water paths;
- avoid stepping where water suddenly gathers;
- watch black devices, because water contact may change their state.

These cycles may repeat. The traveler should not fight them. Move with the phase, pause when it changes, and do not try to complete a route on schedule.

## 15. Interacting with vegetation

The plants are sparse, deliberate, and strange. They are not decorative. They are participants.

### Moss

Dark moss mats are saturated and likely sensitive. Do not step on them. Do not collect them unless absolutely necessary. They may be part of charge or moisture regulation.

If moss leans toward devices or toward your equipment, back away slowly. Your presence may be altering local charge or humidity.

### Broad leaves

The swollen glossy leaves store water. Do not puncture them. Do not cut them. Do not press them against skin. Their internal fluids may be irritating.

### Fern-like thread plants

These catch condensation. They are delicate. Do not brush against them. Their trembling threads may release droplets in response to touch.

### Translucent reeds

The hollow reeds with dark internal threads are especially concerning. They may be biological, technological, or both. Do not break them. Do not look into their hollows closely. Do not inhale near their openings.

If light reveals dark vein-like threads inside, treat them as potentially active conduits.

### General plant rule

Observe, sketch, photograph. Do not taste, crush, or harvest unless the mission explicitly requires it and containment is possible.

## 17. Interacting with the quiet technology

The technology here is not mechanical in the ordinary sense. It does not hum, glow, or announce itself. It behaves like geology, hydrology, and biology merged into function.

### Assume water activates it

The black devices darken when touched by water. Moss leans toward them. Water moves sideways and upward in capillary channels. Surfaces repair themselves by drawing mineral slurry from the air.

Therefore:

- do not pour water on devices;
- do not spit, drip sweat, or shed breath condensate near them;
- do not place wet objects on them;
- do not touch them with wet gloves;
- do not attempt to clean them;
- do not attempt to open them;
- do not attempt to repair them;
- do not attempt to break them.

### Assume surfaces can self-repair

A crack in ceramic may close by mineral deposition. This is beautiful but dangerous to visitors.

If your equipment is scratched, cracked, or dusty, the basin may begin to “heal” it by adding mineral material. That can jam moving parts, seal zippers, stiffen fabric, or bind tools to surfaces.

To reduce this:

- keep gear clean;
- keep gear moving or stored in sealed bags;
- do not set equipment down for long periods;
- avoid leaving tools on ceramic or fused stone;
- wipe surfaces gently with clean dry cloth if mineral film begins;
- do not use solvents inside the basin.

### Assume charge or field effects

The moss response suggests subtle electrical or electrochemical gradients. Keep electronics sealed. If possible, store them in shielded pouches. Avoid exposing circuits.

If hair stands up, if skin tingles, if devices behave oddly, withdraw from the area.

### Assume the built fragments are not trash

Ceramic shards, metallic filaments, black panels, curved walls, and tilted platforms are not debris. They are components of a system whose logic is hidden. Do not rearrange them.

If a fragment seems to be collecting water through hair-thin channels, it is doing something. Leave it alone.

## 18. The black teardrop artifact

This object deserves the highest caution.

It is small, mirror-smooth, black, and shaped like a flattened teardrop. Water spreads across it in branching lines instead of beading. It seems slightly out of phase when viewed indirectly. No one appears to have used it recently.

That last fact is important.

Do not pick it up.

If study is necessary, use remote methods:

- camera on an extension pole;
- indirect sensors;
- scale markers placed nearby without touching the artifact;
- passive observation from multiple angles;
- time checks before and after;
- distance measurement;
- spectral imaging if available;
- no direct hand contact;
- no water poured onto it;
- no scraping;
- no containment field unless specifically designed for phase anomalies.

### Watch for phase symptoms

If near the artifact, monitor for:

- nausea;
- déjà vu;
- delayed reflections;
- mismatched timepieces;
- sense that your hand arrives after your intention;
- doubled sounds;
- water moving strangely around you;
- peripheral vision slipping;
- feeling that the stone is both new and old at once.

If any of these occur, leave immediately.

If the artifact must be moved — and it should not be — it should be handled only with remote manipulators, placed in nested inert containers, isolated from moisture, and transported with temporal shielding. An ordinary human should not carry it in a pocket, glove, or bag.

The safest choice is to leave it exactly where it lies.

- navigation/timekeeper.

Roles can rotate, but someone should always be responsible for watching the environment rather than the objective.

### Use quiet communication

Because sound is isolated and precise, loud voices feel intrusive. Use:

- hand signals;
- whispered short phrases;
- prearranged gestures;
- gentle taps on equipment if necessary;
- visual markers only if removable and non-contaminating.

### Buddy checks

Check each other for:

- heat stress;
- damp clothing;
- mineral crust on boots;
- cuts;
- fogged eye protection;
- tremor;
- confusion;
- unusual fascination or reluctance to leave.

If one person becomes fixated on an object, especially the artifact, the other should gently intervene.

## 22. What not to do

This list is important.

Do not:

- drink the water;
- wash hands in the pools;
- urinate or defecate in the basin;
- leave trash;
- break plants;
- chase creatures;
- touch black devices;
- step on moss;
- scuff mineral crust;
- move artifacts;
- chip samples from self-repairing surfaces;
- pour liquids onto stone or machines;
- use fire;
- use loud sound;
- fly drones unnecessarily;
- set up permanent anchors;
- hammer into rock;
- mark surfaces;
- apply adhesives;
- leave gear unattended;
- sleep directly on active surfaces;
- ignore steam;
- ignore time anomalies;
- stay too long.

The basin is small. It can be crossed quickly. But quickness is not the point.

## 23. Emergency scenarios

### If you slip and fall

Stop moving. Check for cuts. If bleeding, cover the wound with a clean dressing and pack out the used material. Do not rinse in basin water. If you hit your head, leave immediately.

### If you touch a steam seam

Move away. Cool the burn with carried clean water, not basin water. Apply burn dressing. If blistering or severe pain occurs, exit.

### If you become overheated

Move to shade or the cooler building. Loosen clothing. Sip electrolyte water. Wet a cloth with carried water and place it on the neck, but do not let it drip into the basin. If confusion or nausea appears, exit.

### If you inhale irritating steam or dust

Leave the basin. Use respiratory protection. Rinse mouth and eyes with carried clean water if needed. If breathing becomes difficult, use emergency extraction.

### If your equipment becomes coated with mineral

Do not panic. Move away from active surfaces. Gently brush dry mineral off later outside the basin. Do not force zippers or moving parts. Do not chip mineral off inside the basin, as fragments may fall into channels.

### If a device reacts to your presence

If moss leans toward your gear, if black devices darken, if water changes direction near you, back away slowly. Do not make sudden movements. Remove wet items from the area if possible without touching devices. If the reaction spreads, leave.

### If time seems wrong

Check two timepieces. Check your memory. Check your heartbeat. If there is mismatch, do not investigate the artifact. Leave by the most direct safe route.

### If you lose your return anchor

Do not wander. Stop. Observe. Use external beacon if available. If the basin’s walls block signals, move to the rim carefully. Do not attempt to climb unstable young rock in panic.

## 24. Departure protocol

Leaving should be as careful as entering.

### Before exiting, inspect yourself

Check:

- boots;
- gloves;
- clothing seams;
- equipment pockets;
- bags;
- instrument cases;
- water containers;
- medical kit;
- waste bags.

Ensure nothing is leaking, shedding, or carrying basin moisture.

### Check for hitchhikers

Look for:

- moss fragments;
- spores;
- seeds or plant beads;
- insect-like husk fragments;
- mineral crust;
- black dust;
- water droplets in folds.

Remove them gently into a sealed container if possible. Do not flick them into the basin.

### Leave no new damage

Look back at your route. If you accidentally scraped crust, bent a plant, or moved a fragment, note it but do not attempt dramatic repair. You may make it worse.

### Exit slowly

Wet stone remains slick even when you are eager to leave. The final steps out are often where accidents happen.

### Once outside

Change dry clothes if possible. Dry your skin. Inspect for rashes, burns, cuts, or mineral irritation. Hydrate. Eat something with electrolytes. Rest.

Quarantine gear until it can be cleaned. Do not bring basin water, moss, or unsealed samples into another environment without containment.

## 25. Aftercare and reporting

The basin may have subtle aftereffects.

Monitor for:

- cough;
- skin irritation;
- eye irritation;
- dizziness;
- strange dreams;
- time confusion;
- lingering sense of silence;
- hypersensitivity to dripping sounds;
- emotional flatness;
- fixation on the artifact.

These may be psychological, but they should not be dismissed.

Clean all equipment carefully. Use clean water, not basin water. Dry thoroughly. Check electronics for mineral film. Do not charge contaminated devices indoors.

Record observations while fresh:

- time of entry;
- weather phase;
- water behavior;
- steam locations;
- device responses;
- plant movement;
- fauna sightings;
- artifact distance;
- sounds heard;
- smells noticed;
- bodily symptoms;
- equipment anomalies.

If this is a multiversal travel context, report whether the location appears stable, active, contaminated, or altered by the visit.

## 27. The intuitive heart of the preparation

To visit this basin, an ordinary human must become smaller than usual.

Not weaker. More precise.

You should prepare to be hot, damp, and quiet. You should prepare to move as if every footfall is a question asked of the stone. You should prepare to see water not as scenery but as agency: something deciding, sorting, returning, and remembering. You should prepare to encounter machines so advanced they resemble weather and geology. You should prepare to feel unnecessary there, and to accept that this is not an insult.

The basin does not need you. It does not wait for you. It is already running.

So the best preparation is not merely equipment. It is restraint.

Bring clean tools. Bring sealed water. Bring humility. Bring good footing. Bring a body trained to endure humid heat. Bring a mind trained to notice tiny changes. Bring the willingness to leave before curiosity becomes interference.

If you do that, the visit may be extraordinary: a slow, hot, dripping, silent encounter with a place where engineering has become landscape and landscape has become something patiently alive.

And if you are very careful, the basin may continue as though you were never there. That would be your success.
