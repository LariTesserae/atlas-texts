Preparing to visit this place should feel less like packing for a journey and more like preparing to become a tolerated anomaly inside a precise, living machine. An ordinary human multiverse traveler should not approach it as a flooded cathedral, a garden, or a dive site. It is all three, but none of them in a natural sense. It is a sealed, high-altitude, liquid-filled industrial chamber where fertility has been manufactured, animal life excluded, and every surface assigned a purpose. The traveler’s first and most important preparation is to understand that they are not a guest in the usual sense. They are an animal entering a place built to remain animal-free. They are a source of heat, breath, skin, microbes, movement, and disorder. The preparation must therefore be medical, mechanical, psychological, and ethical all at once.

The central rule is simple: do not arrive as an ordinary biological wanderer. Arrive as a sealed, filtered, slow-moving, self-contained process.

## 1. First prepare the mind, not the luggage

Before thinking about suits, filters, or breathing gear, the traveler should prepare for the emotional and perceptual character of the place.

This is not a wilderness. There is no chance encounter here, no rustle of hidden life, no insect, no fish, no bird, no predator, no scavenger. The silence is total in a way fertile places usually are not. The greenery is lush but not wild. Every leaf seems placed, trained, replaced, or permitted. The machinery is patient. The water is warm. The light is ordinary. The beauty is deliberate.

An ordinary human may find this unsettling without knowing why. The mind may search for animal signs: a fish darting, a leaf moving because something touched it, a sound made by breath or wing. None will come. The visitor must be ready for a world that is full but unwitnessed, alive but not hungry, beautiful but not inviting. One should not expect peace in the romantic sense. One should expect the calm of a perfectly maintained system.

So the traveler should mentally rehearse patience. Movement will need to be slow. Observation will need to be quiet. Curiosity must not express itself as grabbing, picking, tasting, or testing without procedure. The place will not respond like nature. It will respond like industry: slowly, through valves, pressures, filters, and delayed consequences.

A good mental preparation is to repeat, in some personal form, this idea:

> “I am entering a place where my body is the contamination. My task is not to experience it eagerly, but to experience it carefully.”

If the traveler is prone to claustrophobia, panic, compulsive touching, fear of drowning, fear of deep water, fear of machinery, or distress in silent environments, they should train for those conditions before entering. Meditation, slow breathing practice, cleanroom discipline, underwater movement drills, and calm emergency rehearsals are more valuable than heroic improvisation.

## 2. Decide whether an ordinary human body may enter at all

The description says the water has a strange friendliness, that it carries enough oxygen, warmth, and buoyancy for a body to remain without drowning. But an ordinary human should not take poetic language as physiological permission.

The place excludes animals. Its water is “unfit for nerves, shells, lungs, and instincts.” That is a warning. Even if the liquid can temporarily support a human, it may not support an unshielded human for long.
