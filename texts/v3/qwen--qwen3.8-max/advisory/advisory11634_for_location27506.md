Prepare as if you are entering a hot, breathing machine that has been built inside a newly broken stone, and where the machine, the weather, and the cave have begun to become one another.

This is not a normal cave. It is not a normal ruin. It is not a normal underground city. It is a compact, humid, industrial hollow where water is harvested, heated, moved, condensed, and reused; where fresh basalt still sheds sharp fragments; where the air is close and feverish; where electricity and dampness share the same surfaces; and where the absence of animal life makes every mechanical sound feel deliberate. An ordinary human multiverse traveler should treat it with the same respect they would give a sauna, a chemical plant, a construction site, a fragile laboratory, and a sacred but uninhabited engine-room.

The guiding principle is simple:

**Stay cool. Stay dry where it matters. Stay insulated from heat and current. Move slowly. Touch little. Drink only what you brought. Read the water, the heat, and the machines before trusting any space.**

Below is a full preparation doctrine.

---

## 1. Understand What the Place Will Ask of You

The hollow is small enough to comprehend, but dense enough to confuse. It may be only the size of a cathedral transept or a crowded market hall, but it is packed vertically with catwalks, pipes, tanks, boilers, transformers, filters, pumps, ducts, cable trays, brick chimneys, ceramic cooling cells, and suspended platforms. The environment is layered above and below the walkways. Nothing is merely decorative. Nearly every surface participates in some thermal or hydraulic process.

A visitor must be ready for:

- **Hot, humid, close air** that presses on the face and makes exertion dangerous.
- **Intermittent artificial rain** caused by condensation, machine cycles, and ceiling seams.
- **Sharp, freshly broken basalt**, glassy fracture surfaces, loose talus, and young mineral edges.
- **Hot machinery**, including boiler walls, transformer kiosks, pipes, pistons, dynamos, and newly plated metal.
- **Damp electrical systems**, with indicator lamps, switchgear, arcs, conduits, and wet cable trays.
- **Low and local light**, with shadows, silhouettes, amber sodium glow, green indicator lamps, furnace flashes, and sudden electric snaps.
- **Sparse, strange vegetation** that should not be trampled, eaten, or handled casually.
- **No animal life**, meaning no birds, insects, rats, spiders, larvae, or natural warning signs.
- **A quiet but active industrial rhythm**, where pumps breathe, valves sigh, chains shift, and heat arrives in pulses.
- **Limited water**, which is both precious and possibly contaminated.
- **A psychological atmosphere of waiting**, as though the place is listening to itself.

You are not visiting a dead location. You are visiting a process.

## 2. Decide Whether You Are Fit to Enter

An ordinary human should not enter this place casually. It is not necessarily lethal, but it is hostile to careless flesh.

Do not enter if you are:

- Dehydrated.
- Feverish.
- Exhausted.
- Hungover.
- Sleep-deprived.
- Suffering from heat intolerance.
- Experiencing chest pain, palpitations, or shortness of breath.
- Recovering from serious illness.
- Prone to severe claustrophobia or panic.
- Using medications that impair sweating, thermoregulation, balance, or judgment unless you have planned carefully for their effects.
- Carrying unshielded cardiac implants, pacemakers, or metallic devices that could be affected by heat, humidity, or electromagnetic fields, unless they are rated and protected for such conditions.

If you are an ordinary human traveler, assume your body is the weakest system in the hollow. The machines can endure the heat. You cannot.

---

## 3. Enter With a Buddy and a Time Discipline

Do not visit alone.

This is a place where a minor injury can become serious quickly: a cut from fresh basalt, a slip on a wet grate, a burn from a pipe, a dizzy spell from heat, or disorientation in the layered vertical architecture. The absence of animal life also means that if you fall unconscious, nothing will notice except machines, and machines may not care.

Before descending:

- Tell a surface contact or multiverse anchor point where you are going.
- Establish an expected return time.
- Establish a check-in interval if communication is possible.
- Agree on abort criteria with your companion.
- Decide in advance how long you will stay.
- For a first visit, keep the stay short: perhaps thirty to ninety minutes.
- Do not push deeper until you understand the heat pulses and rain cycles.

The descent itself may be short, but it is through fractured rock. Do not assume it is safe simply because it is brief. Fresh stone can shed fragments. Edges can cut rope, clothing, skin, and morale.

## 4. Prepare for the Surface First

Above the hollow lies a hot, dry crust. That means your approach may already deplete you before you reach the underground chamber.

Before entering:

- Hydrate steadily, not desperately.
- Take electrolytes if you have been sweating.
- Avoid entering at the hottest part of the surface day unless necessary.
- Wear sun protection on the surface: wide-brim hat, sunglasses, breathable long sleeves, sunscreen.
- Carry enough water for both surface travel and underground time.
- Mark the entrance carefully. The opening may be subtle, and the crust may look uniform.
- Leave a beacon or marker at the surface if you are in unfamiliar terrain.
- Do not enter already overheated.

The transition matters. The surface is hot and dry; the hollow is hot and damp. Your body may feel as if it has moved from an oven into a wet engine room. That shift can be disorienting. Give yourself a few minutes at the threshold if possible. Let your eyes adjust. Let your breathing settle. Let the air tell you what it is.

## 5. Clothing: Wear the Place Correctly

Your clothing must solve several problems at once:

- Protect skin from sharp rock.
- Protect skin from hot surfaces.
- Resist condensation and brief rain.
- Allow sweat to escape as much as possible.
- Reduce risk from electricity.
- Avoid snagging on pipes, cables, and catwalks.
- Prevent cuts.
- Prevent infection in a warm, damp environment.
- Avoid melting, chafing, or becoming heavy with moisture.

### Base Layer

Wear a lightweight, moisture-wicking, long-sleeved shirt and long trousers. Avoid bare skin. In high humidity, cotton becomes a wet, clinging burden. Use:

- Merino wool blends.
- Technical synthetics.
- Lightweight aramid or cut-resistant blends if available.
- Clothing with flat seams to reduce chafing.

The goal is to keep skin covered without trapping heat completely. That is difficult in this environment, but better than exposing arms to mineral dust, hot metal, sharp basalt, and condensation.

### Outer Layer

You do not need a fully waterproof shell; a full rain suit could overheat you dangerously. Instead, use a ventilated, water-resistant layer:

- A light hooded cape or poncho with side vents.
- A quick-drying work jacket.
- A shoulder cover that sheds drip-water without sealing your body inside vapor.
- A neck flap or light hood to protect against ceiling rain.

The hollow’s rain is brief and localized. You want protection from falling droplets, but you must still allow heat to escape.

### Gloves

Bring at least two kinds of gloves:

1. **Cut-resistant work gloves**  
   For touching rock, railings, ladders, and rough metal. Fresh basalt can be knife-sharp. Mineral fracture can slice skin. Use gloves with good grip and some puncture resistance.

2. **Insulated or heat-resistant gloves**  
   For moments when you must move near hot pipes, boiler walls, or machinery. Do not assume metal is cool. Do not assume brick is cool. Do not assume newly plated sheets are safe.

If there is a chance of contact with energized equipment, avoid conductive fasteners, metal jewelry, and metal-threaded gloves. Ordinary travelers should assume that anything electrical in a damp environment is dangerous.

### Boots

Your boots are critical.

They should be:

- Ankle-supporting.
- Heat-resistant.
- Slip-resistant on wet metal and stone.
- Puncture-resistant.
- Electrically insulating if possible.
- Composite-toed rather than steel-toed, to reduce heat and electrical conduction.
- Comfortable enough for ladders, gratings, and uneven talus.
- Broken in before arrival.

Do not wear sandals. Do not wear soft cloth shoes. Do not wear ordinary sneakers if the soles can be cut or melted.

The floor may include warm grit, sharp basalt blades, wet grating, puddles, and heated metal. The ground itself is not neutral.

### Gaiters

Low gaiters are useful. They keep sharp fragments, mineral dust, and grit out of your boots. They also protect lower legs from brushing against sharp rock edges.

### Headgear

Wear a helmet or at least a robust bump cap.

Reasons:

- Low overhead pipes and cable trays.
- Falling mineral fragments.
- Narrow stairways.
- Crawlspaces.
- The possibility of standing too quickly under a duct or valve.
- Condensation dripping from ceiling seams.

A helmet with a chin strap is better than a loose one. Add a small rear neck flap if possible. A hard hat with a built-in lamp is excellent, provided the lamp is not too bright or blue-white.

### Eye Protection

Use sealed safety glasses or goggles with anti-fog treatment.

The air is damp, temperature gradients shift, and droplets can fall unexpectedly. There may be mineral dust, steam, ozone, sulfur traces, and electrical arcs. You do not want droplets or dust in your eyes while on a narrow catwalk.

Do not stare at electric arcs. They may be small, but they can produce harsh light and ultraviolet irritation. If arcs are common in a section, keep your eyes lowered and move away.

### Respiratory Protection

The smell may be tolerable, but do not assume the air is fully safe. The place contains:

- Warm rock dust.
- Fresh basalt particles.
- Mineral efflorescence.
- Ozone from electrical arcs.
- Faint sulfur.
- Steam.
- Possible metallic or chemical traces from electrolytic processes.
- Microbial films and dried organic particles.

An ordinary traveler should carry at least:

- A well-fitted half-face respirator with particulate filtration.
- If possible, combination cartridges for particulates, acid gases, organic vapors, or sulfur compounds.
- A compact powered air-purifying respirator if available.
- Spare filters sealed in a dry bag.

At minimum, wear a high-quality particulate mask when descending through fractured rock, when dust lifts in thermal spirals, or when you are near electrolytic baths, transformer kiosks, or arcing equipment.

Do not rely on smell. Some dangerous gases, especially hydrogen sulfide, can dull the sense of smell. If the air feels wrong, tastes metallic, burns the throat, or makes your eyes water, withdraw.

## 6. Heat and Hydration Discipline

This is the most important ordinary-human problem.

The hollow is hot and damp. Sweat may not evaporate well. Your body can overheat even without heavy exertion. The machines add radiant heat. The walls radiate warmth. The air is close. Condensation makes the environment feel heavy.

You must manage heat actively.

### Hydration

Carry more water than you think you need.

A reasonable minimum for a short visit would be:

- At least 2 liters per person for a brief entry.
- 3 liters or more if the visit may extend.
- More if you are already warm from the surface.

Use insulated bottles or a hydration bladder protected from heat. Warm water is better than no water, but if the bladder becomes hot enough to be unpleasant, you may avoid drinking enough. That is dangerous.

Add electrolytes. Plain water is not enough if you are sweating heavily. Carry:

- Electrolyte tablets.
- Oral rehydration salts.
- Salt capsules if you are trained to use them.
- Small salty snacks.
