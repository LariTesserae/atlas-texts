Prepare for this place not as if you were entering a hostile wilderness, but as if you were being admitted into a vast, warm, half-awake instrument that is already listening.

The greatest danger here is not obvious violence. There are no great floods, no roaring predators, no blazing sun, no freezing night. The danger is subtler: sharp young ground, deceptive distances, responsive surfaces, humid air, strange water, quiet psychological pressure, and technologies or ecologies that may interpret clumsy behavior as interference. An ordinary human traveler should therefore prepare to be **slow, soft, redundant, observant, and extremely respectful**.

Below is what my intuition says a sensible multiverse traveler should do before and during a visit.

---

## 1. Understand the character of the place before you enter

The first preparation is mental.

This is not a ruined city to be looted, nor a savage landscape to be conquered. It is a newly formed low basin with sharp angles, intermittent moisture, warm dim light, sparse architecture, abundant integrated vegetation, and very few obvious inhabitants. It feels urban, but not crowded. It feels alive, but not noisy. It feels advanced, but not explanatory. It feels prepared for visitors, yet not necessarily designed for ordinary human habits.

You should enter with the assumption that:

- The terrain is young and may still be settling.
- The city is active even when no people are visible.
- The ecology is cooperative with the built environment, not separate from it.
- Moisture is not merely weather; it may be part of the city’s metabolism.
- Sound, distance, reflection, and gravity may behave politely but not normally.
- Quiet is not emptiness. It is attention.

The correct attitude is closer to that of a guest in a monastery, a technician entering a living machine, or a naturalist stepping into a delicate greenhouse. You are not there to dominate the space. You are there to move through it without breaking its rhythm.

## 2. Psychological preparation

This location could be deeply calming to some people and quietly unsettling to others. It is not frightening in the ordinary sense, but it is strange. The strangeness is gentle: a pool reflects the wrong building, a bridge becomes transparent, a leaf turns toward you, condensation stops when watched, distances fail to behave. These things may feel dreamlike, sacred, or uncanny.

Prepare yourself mentally for:

### Profound quiet

The sound is softened, absorbed, and polite. There may be no loud background noise at all. For many modern humans, this can become disorienting. You may begin to notice your breathing, heartbeat, clothing, footfalls, and thoughts more than usual.

Practice beforehand:

- Sit in silence for twenty or thirty minutes.
- Walk slowly without headphones.
- Learn to notice tiny sounds: drips, leaves, fabric, breath.
- Become comfortable with not filling silence.

If you are someone who feels anxious in quiet places, bring a small, silent comfort object: a smooth token, a familiar piece of cloth, a tactile bead. Avoid anything that makes noise. Do not bring a music player unless it is absolutely necessary, and even then keep it private, low, and brief. The etiquette of the place seems to favor silence.

### Anomalies without menace

You may see things that should not be possible but do not immediately threaten you. A shadow may remain after its object disappears. A tower may not get closer even though you walk toward it. A surface may seem to store your image for a second too long.

Adopt a simple protocol:

1. Stop.
2. Breathe.
3. Do not touch.
4. Observe briefly.
5. Note what happened.
6. Continue only if the path remains stable and intelligible.
7. Turn back if the environment becomes inconsistent for more than a few moments.

Do not try to “test” the anomalies aggressively. Do not throw objects at transparent bridges. Do not stare into reflective panels until you feel dizzy. Do not chase a tower that refuses to approach. The place may be richer in causality than ordinary space, but that does not mean you should argue with it.

### Emotional vulnerability

The dim warmth, damp air, lush vegetation, and sparse architecture may produce a strong emotional response. Some travelers may feel peace. Others may feel grief, loneliness, awe, or the sense of being watched by something patient and distributed.

That is likely part of the experience.

If you become emotionally overwhelmed:

- Sit somewhere raised and dry.
- Avoid low basins where water collects.
- Keep your hands warm and dry.
- Drink water.
- Check your breathing.
- Do not make major decisions until your pulse settles.

## 3. Physical preparation

You do not need extreme mountaineering fitness, but you should be prepared for uneven, sharp, newly formed terrain in warm humid conditions.

### Terrain demands

The ground is described as full of abrupt terraces, angular shelves, narrow clefts, planar slopes, and little rounding. Erosion has only begun. This means:

- Edges may be sharp.
- Footing may be uneven.
- New cracks may be unstable.
- Dust may collect in corners.
- Moisture may make smooth mineral surfaces slick.
- Some paths may feel more like acceptance than support.

You should be comfortable with:

- Slow hiking over irregular ground.
- Balancing on shelves and terraces.
- Stepping carefully without sudden lunges.
- Using hands occasionally for balance.
- Descending and ascending short, abrupt slopes.

### Heat and humidity

The atmosphere is warm, humid, and slow. That can be deceptive. You may not feel as though you are working hard, but sweating may not cool you efficiently. Heat stress, chafing, fungal irritation, and dehydration are realistic concerns.

Before visiting:

- Be reasonably rested.
- Avoid alcohol or stimulants immediately before arrival.
- Hydrate well, but do not overhydrate to the point of discomfort.
- Bring electrolytes.
- Know your own limits in humid heat.

If you are prone to:

- Heat exhaustion,
- Fungal skin infections,
- Chafing,
- Sinus irritation,
- Ozone sensitivity,
- Vertigo,
- Anxiety in silent spaces,

prepare accordingly.

## 4. Clothing: soft, breathable, matte, and protective

Your clothing should help you move quietly, stay dry enough, protect against sharp stone, and avoid disturbing the environment.

### Base layer

Wear a breathable, moisture-wicking base layer. Avoid cotton if possible; it will stay damp and chafe. A light synthetic or fine wool blend is better. The air is warm, so you do not need heavy insulation, but shaded areas and cool surfaces may feel unexpectedly chilly.

Recommended:

- Short-sleeve or long-sleeve breathable top.
- Lightweight long underwear only if you are cold-sensitive.
- Soft, anti-chafe undergarments.

### Outer layer

The outer layer should be tough enough for sharp rock but not stiff or noisy. A soft-shell jacket or light overshirt is good. It should resist abrasion but still breathe.

Useful features:

- Hood.
- High collar.
- Matte surface.
- No loud zippers or clacking metal.
- Snug cuffs to keep spores and dust out.
- Ventilation openings.

A waterproof shell is not essential unless the local cycle shifts into sustained rain, but a very light, packable moisture barrier could be useful. However, full rain gear may trap sweat. Better to have quick-drying clothing and a hood.

### Lower body

Wear durable but breathable trousers. Avoid shorts; the terrain is sharp and some vegetation may be dense.

Good choices:

- Soft hiking trousers with stretch.
- Reinforced knees.
- No baggy fabric that can catch on angular shelves.
- No noisy waterproof pants unless necessary.

### Footwear

Footwear is one of the most important preparations.

You need shoes or boots that can handle:

- Sharp new stone.
- Smooth wet mineral panels.
- Thin walkways.
- Occasional shallow pools.
- Warm humid air.
- Responsive surfaces that should not be scarred.

Avoid heavy metal-cleated boots. Avoid aggressive crampon-like soles. Avoid anything that gouges surfaces.

Instead, choose:

- Sturdy approach shoes or light hiking boots.
- Good ankle support if you are unstable on uneven ground.
- Grippy, non-marking soles.
- Quick-drying uppers.
- Protective toe caps.
- Comfortable fit, because feet will swell in heat.

Bring:

- At least two pairs of spare socks.
- Thin liner socks if you are prone to blisters.
- Foot powder or antifungal powder.
- Blister tape.
- Small camp shoes or soft-soled slippers for resting, if you plan to stay long.

If your feet become wet, dry them as soon as possible. In warm damp environments, macerated skin can become a serious nuisance.

### Gloves

Bring thin, tactile gloves. You may need to touch sharp rock or steady yourself on surfaces whose nature is unclear. You do not want heavy construction gloves; you want protection with sensitivity.

Good options:

- Thin climbing gloves.
- Light mechanic gloves.
- Abrasion-resistant knit gloves.

Avoid bare-handed contact with panels that seem skinlike, warm, or responsive. Not because they are certainly dangerous, but because ordinary human oils, salts, and microbes may not belong there.

### Headgear

A lightweight hood or soft cap is useful for:

- Drip from condensation channels.
- Spores or motes in the air.
- Thermal currents.
- Psychological comfort.

Avoid broad-brimmed hats that may snag in vegetation or block your vision on narrow shelves. A close-fitting hood is better.

### Eye and face protection

The light is dim, so you do not need strong sun protection. However, the air may contain spores, dust, motes, pollen, or technological residue.

Bring:

- Clear or lightly tinted wraparound glasses.
- A soft buff or mask for the nose and mouth.
- Compact goggles if you expect dense spore drift.

Do not rely on sunglasses. The environment is already dim. If you need visual clarity, use clear lenses with anti-fog treatment.

### Colors and textures

Wear matte, non-reflective colors. The environment is full of slow reflections, dim gold light, and surfaces that seem to store images. Shiny fabric, mirrors, or bright reflective strips may be visually intrusive and could confuse local systems or fauna.

Suggested colors:

- Deep green.
- Rust.
- Charcoal.
- Brown.
- Violet-gray.
- Dull copper, if muted.

If you are traveling with others and need visibility, use small, dull amber markers rather than bright flashing lights.

## 5. Hydration and food

Although the place is damp, you should not assume water is safe.

### Water

Moisture appears in cycles and pockets. There are temporary pools, condensation beads, brief local rains, and channels that guide water to roots and reservoirs. But the water may contain:

- Mineral films.
- Metallic plant residues.
- Microbial life.
- Technological charge.
- Unknown dimensional contaminants.
- Organic or inorganic substances not present in ordinary biospheres.

Do not drink from pools unless in a dire emergency. Even condensation may not be pure, because it forms on engineered surfaces, roots, filaments, and panels.

Carry enough water for the entire visit, plus a reserve.

Recommended:

- At least two liters for a short visit
