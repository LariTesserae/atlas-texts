## Preparing to Visit the High Wet City

This is a place that will not chase you, frighten you, or announce itself with obvious danger. Its power is quieter than that. It will wet you, cool you, slow you, and absorb your mistakes in mist, stone, and silence. The best preparation is not heroic. It is patient, waterproof, and humble.

An ordinary human multiverse traveler should prepare for this location as if entering a vast, high-altitude, water-shaped architecture where nothing was made for human convenience but everything is intelligible if you pay attention. The city is not hostile, but it is indifferent. It is governed by water, gravity, cold, mineral chemistry, and time. Your task is to become briefly compatible with it: dry enough, warm enough, oriented enough, and careful enough to pass without leaving a wound.

The first rule is simple: **do not prepare to conquer the place. Prepare to move with it.**

---

# 1. Understand What You Are Entering

Before packing anything, understand the essential nature of the location.

It is high, cool, wet, and immense. It is not a forest, not a ruin, not a mountain summit, not a cave, and not a conventional city. It is a plateau-scale mineral landscape shaped by persistent water into forms that resemble urban space: terraces, courtyards, channels, walls, basins, stairways, chambers, drains, and ledges. But there are no builders, no tools, no artifacts, no signs, no roads, and no inhabitants except water itself and the small life that clings to damp stone.

You should expect:

- Constant moisture in the air.
- Fine mist, drifting rain curtains, fog, and localized weather.
- Wet, slippery rock underfoot.
- Cool temperatures, with possible frost.
- Sparse vegetation and very little animal life.
- Excellent visibility in some districts, poor visibility in mist.
- Minimal sound except water noises.
- No fuel, no shelter, no manufactured materials.
- No obvious landmarks, but many subtle repeating forms.
- A landscape that changes slowly but can shift quickly after rain.
- A profound feeling of density without crowds.

This is not a place where you can live off the land. There is almost nothing to gather, hunt, burn, or use. You must carry everything in and everything out.

The environment’s main pressure is not violence. It is persistence. Water will find every gap in your clothing, every poorly sealed bag, every moment of inattention. Preparation should therefore emphasize **continuous moisture management**.

---

# 2. The Right Temperament for the Visit

The ordinary human body can manage this place, but the ordinary human mind may struggle with it.

The city offers little dramatic stimulation. There are no crowds, no voices, no machinery, no bright abundant life. Its events are small: a drop falling, a film of water sliding over stone, mist thickening, a pool filling, frost forming, a midge emerging, a mineral crust brightening after rain.

A traveler who needs constant novelty, loud feedback, or clear forward progress may feel oppressed here. The place rewards steadiness.

Before visiting, cultivate:

- Patience.
- Slow movement.
- Comfort in silence.
- Tolerance for dampness.
- Willingness to turn back.
- Attention to subtle changes in light, sound, and texture.
- Respect for fragile surfaces.
- A habit of checking your body for cold and wetness before they become emergencies.

This is a landscape that asks you to lower your pace and sharpen your senses. You should not arrive with the emotional expectation of spectacle. The wonder here is not loud. It is cumulative.

# 3. Physical Preparation

You do not need to be an elite athlete, but you should be reasonably fit, balanced, and accustomed to cool wet conditions.

Important physical capacities include:

### Balance and Foot Security
Much of the travel will involve wet stone, uneven terraces, shallow basins, slick mineral crusts, and narrow ledges. You should practice moving on wet rock without rushing. Good ankle stability and careful foot placement matter more than raw strength.

### Cold Tolerance
The temperature is not extreme, but constant dampness makes coolness penetrate. You should know how your body responds to cold, especially hands, feet, and core.

### Altitude Tolerance
The landscape is elevated. Even if the air is breathable, it may be thinner than you are used to. Arrive gradually if possible. Do not begin heavy exertion immediately after crossing into the location if you feel shortness of breath, headache, nausea, or unusual fatigue.

### Slow Scrambling Ability
You may need to step over low walls, descend stair-like rock forms, cross ridges, or move through collapsed terraces. This is not necessarily technical climbing, but it requires calm movement and good judgment.

### Foot Care Discipline
Wet feet are one of the greatest practical dangers. Blisters, maceration, and cold injury can develop slowly. You should be willing to stop, dry feet, change socks, and treat hot spots early.

# 4. Clothing: Build Around Constant Dampness

Your clothing system must assume that you will be wet from outside, wet from condensation, and possibly wet from your own sweat. The goal is not to stay perfectly dry at all times — that may be impossible — but to stay warm enough and manage moisture well enough that you can recover.

Avoid cotton entirely. Cotton becomes heavy, cold, and slow to dry.

### Base Layer
Use a thin, wicking layer against the skin.

Good materials:
- Merino wool.
- Synthetic wicking fabric.
- Silk as a very light liner, though less durable.

Avoid loose cotton shirts or underwear.

### Insulating Layer
Bring insulation that works when damp.

Best options:
- Synthetic fleece.
- Synthetic insulated jacket.
- Wool sweater or wool blend.
- Water-resistant down may work if carefully protected, but synthetic is safer.

Do not rely on a single thick jacket. Layering allows you to adjust to cool exertion and stillness.

### Outer Shell
A waterproof and windproof shell is essential.

The shell should have:
- A good hood.
- High collar.
- Wrist closures.
- Pit zips or ventilation if possible.
- Enough room to move comfortably.

A fully waterproof breathable jacket is better than a water-resistant one. In persistent mist and rain curtains, water-resistant shells eventually wet out.

### Lower Body
Wear quick-drying trousers or hiking pants, not heavy absorbent fabric.

Consider:
- Synthetic hiking pants.
- Thin base layer underneath if cold.
- Waterproof overtrousers for prolonged rain or mist.

Avoid denim, canvas, or thick cotton trousers.

### Head and Neck
A surprising amount of discomfort enters through a cold head and neck.

Bring:
- Warm hat or beanie.
- Hood.
- Neck gaiter or buff.
- Lightweight cap for mist or drizzle if you prefer a brim.

A brim can help keep mist out of the eyes, but a hood often works better in wind-driven moisture.

### Hands
Your hands will be exposed to wet stone and cool air.

Bring:
- Thin liner gloves.
- Waterproof or water-resistant outer gloves.
- Spare dry gloves kept sealed in a waterproof bag.

Wet hands lose dexterity quickly. If you need to handle buckles, zips, navigation tools, or food, you will appreciate dry reserve gloves.

### Socks
Socks are critical.

Bring:
- Several pairs of wool or synthetic hiking socks.
- Thin liner socks if you are prone to blisters.
- At least one completely dry pair reserved for camp or sleep.

Do not wear wet socks into your sleeping system if you can avoid it.

### Eye Protection
Wet stone and mist can create glare or flat light. Sunglasses or clear protective eyewear can help.

Useful options:
- Polarized lenses to reduce glare from wet surfaces.
- Clear lenses for foggy or low-light conditions.
- Goggles if wind-driven mist becomes irritating.

# 5. Footwear: The Most Important Personal Equipment

This is not a place for ordinary street shoes, stiff mountaineering boots alone, or smooth-soled fashion footwear. You need footwear that can grip wet mineral surfaces, drain water, and remain stable on uneven stone.

Good choices include:

- Canyoning shoes.
- Wet-sport approach shoes.
- Sticky-rubber hiking shoes.
- Lightweight scrambling shoes with good drainage.
- Sturdy trail shoes with aggressive rubber, though they may become saturated.

The sole should be grippy when wet. Hard rubber that performs well on dry pavement may become dangerous on wet travertine, basalt, or algae-filmed stone.

### Desired Footwear Features
- Sticky rubber.
- Secure heel fit.
- Toe protection.
- Drainage or quick-drying upper.
- Low heel.
- Stable platform.
- Comfortable when wet.

### Avoid
- Deep metal spikes unless ice is expected, because they can damage stone and may slip on wet smooth rock.
- Heavy waterproof boots that trap water inside once submerged.
- Smooth-soled shoes.
- Open sandals or minimalist shoes unless you have extremely tough feet and exceptional balance.

If frost is common, consider lightweight traction devices that can be removed quickly, but use them with care. On wet stone, metal can be less secure than good rubber.

### Gaiters
Gaiters can help keep small stones, mud, and cold water from entering shoes. Short, quick-drying gaiters are better than heavy waterproof ones that trap moisture.

### Foot Care Kit
Bring:
- Spare socks.
- Adhesive tape or blister tape.
- Small amount of antiseptic wipe.
- Skin lubricant or anti-chafe balm.
- Absorbent cloth for drying feet.
- Small plastic bag for wet socks.

At the first sign of rubbing or cold numbness, stop and deal with it.

# 6. Waterproofing Your Gear

In this place, moisture is not an event. It is a condition.

A pack cover alone is not enough. Drizzle, mist, condensation, and contact with wet stone will find every opening.

Use a layered waterproofing system:

### Pack Liner
Place a waterproof liner inside your pack. A heavy-duty dry bag, compactor bag, or dedicated pack liner works well.

### Internal Dry Bags
Divide gear into small dry bags by function:
- Sleep system.
- Spare clothing.
- Food.
- Electronics.
- First aid.
- Fire/stove kit.
- Waste and hygiene.

Color-coded bags help you find things without exposing everything to mist.

### Critical Items Stay on Your Body or in a Small Chest Pouch
Keep essential navigation and emergency items close:
- Compass.
- Whistle.
- Beacon or multiverse anchor.
- Small light.
- Emergency shelter.
- Dry gloves.
- Food bar.
- Water purification tablets.

If your pack is lost or you become separated from it, you should still have survival basics.

### Electronics
Assume electronics are vulnerable.

Protect:
- Communication beacon.
- Multiverse transit device.
- Camera.
- Headlamp batteries.
- Sensors.

Use:
- Waterproof cases.
- Silica gel packets.
- Redundant power.
- Analog backups.

Do not rely on a touchscreen as your only survival tool. Cold, wet fingers and mist make touchscreens unreliable.

# 7. Shelter and Sleep System

There is no wood, no built shelter, and no place where a human can assume they will be protected from the weather. You must carry your own dry micro-environment.

### Shelter
A good shelter should be:
- Fully waterproof.
- Windworthy.
- Easy to pitch on stone.
- Resistant to condensation problems.
- Low profile if the plateau is windy.

Options:
- Lightweight tent.
- Tarp with inner net or bivy.
- Waterproof bivy sack.
- Double-wall tent if condensation is severe.

Because there may be no soil, stakes may be useless. Bring guylines that can be anchored with rocks, gear, or pack weight. Use soft loops to avoid scraping stone. Do not chip, carve, or hammer anchors into mineral crusts.

### Sleeping Pad
Stone is cold. You need insulation from the ground.

Use:
- Closed-cell foam pad.
- Inflatable insulated pad.
- Or both.

A pad with good thermal resistance is more important than extreme lightness.

### Sleeping Bag or Quilt
Choose a bag rated colder than you think you need.

Synthetic insulation is safer than down unless you are extremely disciplined about keeping it dry. If you use down, ensure it is highly water-resistant and stored in a waterproof bag.

### Sleep Clothing
Keep one set of clothing absolutely dry for sleeping:
- Dry base layer.
- Dry socks.
- Dry hat or light balaclava.
- Dry gloves if needed.

This dry reserve is not for travel. It is for recovery.

### Where to Sleep
Do not camp in:
- Shallow basins that may fill with water.
- Channel floors.
- Drainage seams.
- Directly under overhangs with active drips.
- Under loose-looking rock blocks.
- Exposed ridges during wind or lightning risk.
- Fragile moss beds or living crusts.

Look for:
- A broad terrace slightly above drainage.
- Stable rock.
- Gentle slope.
- Protection from wind without becoming a water trap.
- A surface that is already mineral and durable.

In this landscape, “dry” is relative. Even a seemingly flat shelf may receive condensation, mist, or slow seepage. Inspect the site carefully.

# 8. Food and Cooking

There is no firewood. There may be no combustible organic material at all. Do not plan on making a fire.

Use a stove.

### Stove Choice
A reliable compact stove is better than improvisation.

Good options:
- Canister stove if reliable in cool temperatures.
- Liquid fuel stove if cold or altitude reduces canister performance.
- Alcohol stoves are possible but less efficient and harder to see in bright misty light.

Carry enough fuel for:
- Boiling water.
- Warm drinks.
- Simple hot meals.
- Emergency melting of frost if needed.

Cook in a sheltered area, but never inside a sealed tent or bivy. Carbon monoxide and fire risk are real. Use vestibule space with ventilation.

### Food
Choose high-calorie, low-preparation food.

Good choices:
- Instant oats.
- Dried meals.
- Nuts and seeds.
- Cheese or shelf-stable protein.
- Energy bars.
- Dried fruit.
- Chocolate or dense confectionery.
- Electrolyte drink powder.

Warm drinks have psychological value far beyond their calories. Tea, broth, coffee, cocoa, or warm electrolyte drinks can restore morale.

### Water
Water is everywhere, but you must treat it.

Even clear cold water may contain microorganisms, mineral concentrations, bird droppings, larvae, organic acids, or unknown multiverse contaminants. Do not assume purity because the water looks clean.

Use a layered purification method:
1. Filter out particles and organisms.
2. Chemically treat or boil if uncertain.
3. Avoid stagnant pools when possible.

Prefer moving water, overflow channels, or recently filled basins. Avoid pools with scum, dead insects, strong odor, or visible organic stagnation.

Bring:
- Filter.
- Chemical purification tablets.
- Small metal bottle or pot for boiling.
- Enough containers for camp and travel.

Do not wash dishes, hands, or body directly in the city’s pools. If washing is necessary, carry water away from the source and scatter wastewater over broad mineral ground, far from channels and basins. Use no soap unless absolutely necessary, and then only in tiny amounts far from water.

# 9. Hygiene and Waste

This is a landscape of slow biological processes. Human waste and food residue can persist and disrupt it.

### Human Waste
Because the ground is saturated and often rocky, digging may be impossible or destructive.

Best practice:
- Carry waste out in sealed waste bags.
- Use portable toilet bags or “wag bags.”
- Pack out toilet paper.
- Seal everything in odor-proof waterproof bags.

If emergency circumstances force burial, choose mineral ground away from water, but in this place, pack-out is the correct ethic.

### Food Waste
Pack out all scraps. Do not leave crumbs, peels, or broth. Even organic matter can alter microbial films or attract opportunistic insects in ways that disturb the local balance.

### Skin and Body Care
Constant dampness can crack skin, especially around hands and feet.

Bring:
- Small barrier cream.
- Lip balm.
- Hand sanitizer.
- Tiny towel.
- Wet wipes in sealed bag.
- Spare dry cloth.

Avoid over-washing with soap. In this environment, clean water and drying are often better than detergents.

# 10. Navigation: Reading a City Without Roads

There are no roads, signs, paths, or intentional routes. Yet the landscape has structure. The trick is to read it hydrologically.

### The General Rule
Water is the city’s logic.

The wettest seams are often the most passable because water has opened them, but they can also become dangerous during rain. Dry ridges may offer better visibility but can be wind-scoured, rough, and exposed.
