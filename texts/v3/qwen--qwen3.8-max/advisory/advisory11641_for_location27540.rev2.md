Prepare as if you are going to a working water-utility, a small industrial parish, and a wet stone garden all at once. This is not a ruin, not a wilderness, not a festival, and not a place where dramatic danger announces itself. It is loud, damp, orderly, alive, and patient. An ordinary human multiverse traveler should aim to arrive dry-footed, clear-eared, unhurried, and unobtrusive. Your main tasks are simple: do not slip, do not obstruct the water, do not contaminate the channels, do not touch what is not yours, and do not mistake the workers’ raised voices for anger.

The town will accept a visitor who moves like maintenance: carefully, attentively, and without fuss.

---

## 1. Understand what kind of place you are entering

Before packing anything, hold the shape of the town in mind.

It is small, basin-shaped, and newly cut. The stone is young and sharp. The ground is seldom soaked but rarely dry. Mist, drizzle, condensation, runoff, and overflow make everything cool and slick. The settlement is organized around a condensation engine: nets, tanks, pipes, sluices, turbines, dynamos, channels, rail carts, transformer courts, and walkways. It is industrial but not fiery; municipal but not sterile; lush but not wild. It is loud because stone reflects sound and machines run continuously, yet the pace is slow because the whole system is built around accumulation rather than urgency.

This means your preparation should not resemble adventure gear for a jungle or a battlefield. It should resemble the kit of a careful inspector, a canal worker, a field naturalist, or a traveling municipal engineer. You need to be protected from damp, noise, slick stone, and machinery, while remaining modest, mobile, and respectful.

The town is intimate. From an upper walkway, people can see across it. You will be noticeable. That is not threatening, but it does mean you should behave like a guest in a working room, not like a tourist in an open landscape.

## 2. Confirm the basic multiverse conditions first

Even if the place looks ordinary, an ordinary human multiverse traveler should still treat every arrival as a physical and civic environment with local rules.

Before crossing, confirm:

- **Atmosphere:** breathable, non-toxic, with no unusual gas mixture. The description suggests ordinary air, but damp basins can concentrate mold, algae, ozone, or fine mineral dust. If uncertain, carry a light particulate mask.
- **Gravity and pressure:** likely modest and human-compatible, but enough difference to affect balance, fatigue, and the behavior of water. If gravity is slightly higher, wet stairs become more dangerous. If lower, loose gravel and falling drips behave differently.
- **Temperature range:** cool and damp, not freezing, but prolonged dampness can chill an ordinary body. Prepare for a day in wet coolness, not deep cold.
- **Local disease ecology:** waterborne pathogens, algae, rat-borne illness, fungal spores, and insect bites are plausible. Vaccination and hygiene matter.
- **Electrical standards:** the town uses dynamos, cables, transformers, and damp-environment power. Do not assume your devices can safely charge from local outlets.
- **Legal status of visitors:** a small municipal machine may require sign-in, permits, escorts, or declarations. Do not assume you can wander freely through transformer courts or conduit chambers.
- **Quarantine rules:** because this is a water-collection system, local authorities may be extremely strict about introducing organisms, soil, seeds, spores, insects, or foreign water.

If your multiverse travel depends on a return device, anchor, token, or signal, make sure it can function in damp air, near vibration, and around electrical fields. Keep it on your body in a sealed pouch. Do not rely on it being dry if left on a wall hook or bench.

## 3. Choose the right temperament

The most important preparation is mental.

This place rewards patience. It will not give you sudden revelation. It will give you drips, hums, bells, wet stone, green seams, gauges, birds, and workers moving with practiced slowness. If you arrive anxious, hurried, or desperate for spectacle, the town will feel dull or oppressive. If you arrive attentive, it becomes fascinating.

Prepare yourself to:

- move slower than you think necessary;
- speak clearly and briefly;
- listen for bells, valves, footsteps, and warnings;
- stand aside for maintenance crews;
- accept that you are minor compared to the water system;
- notice small changes: a tank level, a new puddle, a patched pipe, a bird’s territory, a valve left slightly open;
- resist touching things.

The town’s ethic is ecological and civic. Water has rights. Channels have purpose. Empty space is not empty. Green growth is allowed but regulated. Machinery is alive in the sense that it breathes, knocks, sweats, and demands attention. A visitor should be humble.

## 4. Dress for damp stone, metal, and noise

Your clothing should keep you warm without overheating, dry without making you clumsy, and quiet without snagging on machinery.

### Base layers

Avoid cotton if possible. Cotton absorbs damp and stays cold. Use wool or synthetic fibers:

- thin wool or synthetic long-sleeve top;
- fitted trousers rather than loose flowing pants;
- warm socks, preferably wool or wool blend;
- spare socks, absolutely essential.

The air is cool rather than cold, but dampness penetrates. A chilled traveler with wet feet becomes careless.

### Outer layer

Use a waterproof or highly water-resistant shell:

- a light rain jacket with a close-fitting hood;
- a jacket that does not flap wildly in the breeze;
- quiet fabric if possible, because loud rustling makes hearing harder;
- no long trailing cape, scarf, sash, or decorative ribbon that could catch on railings, net weights, valves, or rail carts.

A hood is useful for drizzle, but do not wear it so far forward that it blocks your hearing. In a noisy basin, hearing is one of your main safety senses.

### Footwear

This is the single most important item.

Choose boots or shoes with:

- excellent wet-stone grip;
- soft rubber or siped soles rather than hard smooth leather;
- waterproofing;
- ankle support if you are unsteady on uneven gravel;
- no metal spikes or hobnails, which can slip on wet stone, scratch surfaces, conduct electricity, or spark near machinery;
- closed toes, always.

The ground will be coarse gravel, fitted block paving, grated walkways, wet stone lips, mossy seams, and occasional shallow puddles. You are unlikely to wade through deep water, but you may stand on damp surfaces for hours.

Gaiters can be useful if you plan to walk near gravel, reeds, or lower drainage channels. They keep stone chips and damp out of boots.

### Gloves

Bring thin waterproof gloves or grippy work gloves. They help on polished handrails, cold iron, and damp stone. Avoid bulky gloves unless you are doing authorized labor; you still need fingertip awareness. Avoid gloves with metal thread or conductive ornamentation near electrical fittings.

### Headgear

A simple close-fitting cap is enough for drizzle. A broad hat can be blown off or obstruct view in narrow walkways. If you are entering active maintenance zones, ask whether a hard hat is required. The town is not described as a construction site, but young rock, overhead cables, crane hooks, and glass-weighted nets make head protection reasonable if you go near the works.

### Hearing protection

Bring filtered earplugs or low-profile ear defenders. The town is loud but not frantic: turbine drone, water in sluices, pipes knocking, boots on gratings, birds, bells, pressure releases, voices. The noise is layered and reflective. Over time it can fatigue an ordinary human.

However, do not make yourself deaf to the environment. You need to hear:

- bells marking work cycles;
- shouted warnings;
- pressure releases;
- approaching rail carts;
- machinery changes;
- workers calling to you.

Use musician’s plugs, filtered industrial plugs, or electronic attenuation earpieces if available. Carry plain foam plugs as backup for sleeping.

### Eye protection

Clear safety glasses are sensible if you are near maintenance, wire nets, or pressure fittings. They also protect against drifting mist, algae flecks, dust from fresh stone, and insects. Anti-fog treatment is useful; everything here encourages condensation.

If you wear prescription glasses, bring a spare dry cloth and a hard case. Wet lenses will be a constant nuisance.

### Mask or face covering

Not always necessary, but useful if:

- you are allergic to mold, algae, or pollen;
- you have sensitive lungs;
- you are cleaning or inspecting channels;
- there is a midge swarm;
- you are in a low airy corner with bird droppings or accumulated organic matter.

A lightweight particulate mask is enough. You probably do not need heavy respiratory gear unless local authorities warn of specific vapors or spores.

## 5. Pack a small, sealed, municipal-style kit

Do not bring a large expedition pack. Bring a compact waterproof bag that does not snag. A sling bag, small rucksack, or belt pouch is better than something enormous.

### Essential documents

Keep these sealed in waterproof sleeves:

- multiverse travel papers, if such things exist;
- local permit or letter of introduction;
- identity documents;
- medical/vaccination record;
- emergency contact information;
- a written note of your origin, allergies, blood type, and any conditions affecting treatment;
- local currency or agreed trade tokens;
- a small waterproof notebook and pencil or waterproof pen.

Paper is useful here. Electronics can fail or fog. A notebook can record water levels, bell intervals, names of lanes, instructions, and contacts.

### Water and food

Even though water is everywhere, do not assume it is safe to drink directly from nets, troughs, tanks, or channels. The water may be clean by local standards, but it may contain algae, mineral residues, animal contamination, pipe oils, or microbes unfamiliar to you.

Carry:

- a sealed water bottle or flask;
- purification tablets or a small filter if needed;
- a few dry, sealed snacks;
- no loose crumbs or strong-smelling food that attracts birds or rats.

Ask where the town’s official drinking water is drawn. There may be a tap, cistern, or blessed/regulated source. Respect the hierarchy: water from the high nets belongs first to the tanks, then to the town, then to the lower gardens. Do not intercept condensation for personal use unless invited.

### Hygiene and sanitation

Bring:

- hand sanitizer in a sealed bottle;
- small soap, but never use it in the town’s channels;
- a quick-dry towel or cloth;
- tissues or packed-out waste bags;
- any personal hygiene products sealed and carried out if no proper disposal exists.

Do not wash hands, dishes, clothes, or wounds in settling tanks, drainage troughs, or overflow channels. These are part of the municipal machine.

### First aid

A small kit should include:

- adhesive bandages;
- antiseptic wipes;
- blister care;
- gauze and tape;
- tweezers;
- pain relievers if personally appropriate;
- antihistamines;
- antifungal powder or cream if prone to damp-foot issues;
- any personal medications;
- waterproof wound covering.

Cuts should be cleaned and covered quickly. Wet industrial environments are unforgiving to open skin. Make sure your tetanus protection is current if you have access to ordinary medicine before travel.

### Tools and equipment

For an ordinary visitor, avoid carrying obvious tools unless invited to assist. But useful items include:

- small flashlight or headlamp, preferably with a red or dim mode;
- spare batteries in a dry tube;
- multi-tool only if socially acceptable and legally permitted;
- small roll of clean dry cloth;
- zip ties or cord, but do not use them on town property;
- lens cloth;
- desiccant packets;
- a compact dry bag for electronics;
- a collapsible sitting pad if you expect to wait on damp stone.

Avoid umbrellas. In narrow walkways, near nets, under cables, and around machinery, an umbrella becomes a pole hazard. A hood or cap is better.

## 6. Protect your electronics from damp and interference

If you carry phones, translators, cameras, sensors, or navigation aids, assume the basin will try to fog them.

Prepare by:

- keeping devices in sealed pouches until needed;
- using desiccant in your bag;
- avoiding rapid temperature changes that cause condensation on lenses and circuits;
- not charging from local power unless you understand the system;
- keeping devices away from transformer courts and dynamos unless authorized;
- using straps so nothing drops into channels or tanks;
- avoiding drones entirely unless explicitly permitted.

Drones would be especially bad here: they could tangle in condensation nets, disturb birds, collide with overhead cables, interfere with maintenance, and violate the town’s mist-flow and noise norms.

A simple waterproof camera or a pencil-and-paper method may be more respectful and more reliable.

## 7. Health precautions for a damp industrial ecology

The town is not described as poisonous or hostile, but ordinary humans should respect four hazards: water, noise, machinery, and biology.

### Waterborne and damp-related illness

Do not drink unknown water. Do not put hands in your mouth after touching channels, pipes, moss, or railings. Wash or sanitize before eating. If you have open wounds, cover them.

Be aware of:

- algae-related irritation;
- bacterial contamination from animal presence;
- rat-borne disease;
- fungal skin infections;
- trench-foot-like discomfort if feet stay wet too long.

### Respiratory sensitivity

The air is wet, mineral, mossy, and faintly electric. Most healthy travelers will be fine, but if you are sensitive to mold, mildew, ozone, or fine stone dust, carry a mask and limit time in low poorly ventilated corners.

### Noise fatigue

The sound is constant. It may not seem painful, but after hours it can cause headache, irritability, and poor judgment. Take breaks in quieter interiors if available: administrative hall, workshop office, lodging room, or a sheltered yard away from turbines.

### Slips and falls

This is probably the most common danger. Wet stone, algae-edged tanks, mossy brick seams, polished steps, grated walkways, and fresh gravel all invite slips. Move with short steps. Test unfamiliar surfaces. Use handrails where provided, but remember they may be damp and polished. Do not jump channels. Do not balance on pipe housings. Do not climb retaining walls.

### Electrical safety

The town is powered and damp. Treat all cables, insulators, transformer courts, and metal housings with respect.

Do not:

- lean on electrical boxes;
- touch frayed or hanging wires;
- place metal bottles or tools near live fittings;
- enter fenced transformer courts without permission;
- use damaged personal electronics;
- assume a surface is safe just because it looks dry.

If you have an implanted medical device, keep a sensible distance from strong dynamos, transformer courts, and heavy cable runs. Ask local workers if uncertain.

## 8. Arrival: how to enter the basin properly

Your arrival should be calm and visible. Do not materialize suddenly in the middle of a channel, on a turbine platform, or beside a pressure drum if you can avoid it. Ideally arrive at an upper walkway, administrative yard, or designated gate.

When you first enter, pause.

The sound will likely reach you before the details: turbine drone, water sliding, pipes knocking, birds calling, boots on metal. The air will feel cool and wet-mouthed. The light will be pale and even, making wet stone bright. The town may look smaller than expected because the basin holds it like a room.

Then do the following:

1. **Check your footing.** The first steps may be damp gravel or slick stone.
2. **Lower your voice and observe.** You do not need to whisper, but do not shout.
3. **Look for official signage or a central building.** The administrative hall with the bell tower is likely the best first destination.
4. **Register if required.** Ask whether visitors must sign in, receive a water token, or wear a marker.
5. **Ask where you may stand.** This is a working precinct. Some walkways may be clear, others reserved for valve access or cart movement.
6. **Ask about current cycles.** The bell tower marks work intervals. Learn whether a sluice will open, a net will be lowered, a turbine will be serviced, or a transformer court will be energized.
7. **Ask about water.** Confirm whether your bottle can be refilled and where.

Useful phrases, translated if necessary:

- “I am a visitor. I will stay out of the channels.”
- “Where may I stand without blocking work?”
- “Is there a visitor register?”
- “May I refill my bottle from the town supply?”
- “Which walkways are closed?”
- “What does the bell mean?”
- “Who should I notify if I see damage?”

## 9. Move like a guest, not like water

Water belongs here. You do not.

The town is built to guide moisture from air to surface to gutter to tank to pipe to use. Your body, bag, and behavior should not interrupt that sequence.

### Walking

Walk slowly. Use short steps on wet stone. Keep your bag close to your body so it does not swing over channels or catch on fittings. Watch where your feet land, especially on:

- mossy brick seams;
- algae-edged tank margins;
- grated walkways with dripping condensation;
- newly cut rock with sharp loose chips;
- rail spurs and cart tracks;
- shallow drainage troughs;
- polished steps.

If a grating hums strongly, do not stand on it unless you know it is safe. Local children may know which ones are stable; you may not.

### Standing

Do not stand:

- under lifting nets unless invited;
- beneath crane hooks;
- in front of open sluice outlets;
- beside pressure relief valves if they are active;
- on rail tracks;
- in transformer courts;
- in the middle of named lanes or covered walkways;
- where your body blocks a mist-facing surface or inspection route.

If you want to observe, choose a wall side, platform edge, or bench if one exists. Ask before setting down equipment.

### Touching

As a rule, do not touch:

- valves;
- handwheels;
- gauges;
- levers;
- electrical fittings;
- nets;
- glass weights;
- turbine housings;
- hatch covers;
- drying racks;
- water channels;
- green growth near wiring.

Touch handrails only for support. If a surface is polished by gloves, it is used; that does not mean it is yours to operate.

If a worker asks you to move, move promptly but calmly. They are likely not angry. They may be making room for water, machinery, or sound.

## 10. Respect the town’s water ethics

The settlement has rules that feel ecological as much as civic. A visitor should internalize the main ones quickly.

### Do not block mist flow

The upper terraces and nets are designed to catch damp. Do not stand, hang gear, set up equipment, or stretch cloth where you might obstruct the prevailing mist unless you are certain it does not matter.

### Do not intercept water

Water that falls from the high nets belongs first to the tanks, then to the town, then to the lower gardens. Do not hold your bottle under a net drip, catch overflow, or divert a trickle unless invited.

### Do not put anything into channels

No coins, food, soap, paper, dust, offerings, seeds, insects, or foreign water. The channels are for collection, settling, inspection, and life. They are not trash bins, wishing wells, or drains for personal waste.

### Do not wash in the system

If you need to clean hands, clothes, or equipment, ask where wastewater should go. Some towns have designated basins or sealed drains. If none is offered, clean with sanitizer and pack out waste.

### Do not disturb green growth unnecessarily

Moss, ferns, algae, reeds, liverworts, and seedling willows are part of the town’s living margins. They are permitted until they threaten wiring or machinery. Do not pick, trim, or transplant them unless asked. Do not collect “souvenirs.”

### Do not feed animals

Birds, frogs, rats, cats, insects, and eel-like creatures have learned the works. Feeding them changes behavior and may draw them into dangerous places. Cats are probably working animals. Do not distract them.

## 11. Prepare for the noise without becoming isolated

The basin is loud, but its loudness is layered. There is a low turbine drone, water moving through stone and iron, metal ticking, chains clinking, boots ringing, bells, birds, and voices. It is not chaotic, but it can overwhelm someone expecting rural quiet.

Prepare by:

- bringing filtered earplugs;
- taking periodic silent breaks;
- learning the important sounds;
- not playing music;
- not using loud personal devices;
- speaking in raised but controlled voices;
- watching faces and hands when sound becomes confusing.

Workers speak loudly from habit. Their short phrases are adapted to the environment. Do not interpret this as rudeness. If you need clarification, ask directly and briefly.

If you use hearing aids, protect them from moisture and carry drying materials. If you are deaf or hard of hearing, consider written notes, visual signals, or traveling with someone who can alert you to bells and warnings.

## 12. Prepare for the young terrain

The hollow is newly carved. Its edges are sharp. The rock has facets, cracks, raw lips, angular shelves, and loose chips. This gives the place a crisp, unfinished feel, but it also means the terrain is not fully settled.

Be cautious around:

- fresh rock faces;
- exposed strata;
- root scars where vegetation was stripped;
- loose gravel;
- cracked shelves;
- low but definite slopes;
- retaining walls;
- newly exposed seams.

Do not climb on raw slopes. Do not sit on unstable rock lips. Do not lean over newly cracked retaining edges. If you see fresh fallen chips or a crack that seems wider than it should be, report it to maintenance rather than investigating alone.

After rain, freeze, thaw, or heavy mist, the young stone may shed small fragments. Ask whether any upper edges are restricted.

## 13. Prepare for machinery that is patient but strong

The machines are not violent, but they are continuous. Turbines turn with weighted insistence. Dynamos hum. Pumps operate. Pressure drums hold force. Valves change state. Cranes lift. Rail carts move. Nets collect weight over time.

Do not underestimate slow machinery. Slow does not mean weak.

Rules:

- keep hands away from moving parts;
- do not step over chains unless invited;
- do not stand where a cart could roll;
- do not walk on rail spurs unless instructed;
- keep clear of crane loads;
- do not touch gauges even if they seem still;
- do not lean on pipes that tick or expand;
- watch for pressure release vents;
- listen for changes in tone before moving near turbines.

If a worker offers you a task, follow instructions exactly. If you are told to stand still, stand still. If you are told to step back, step back. If you are invited to turn a valve, do so only as directed and only as far as directed.

## 14. Electrical caution in a wet town

The town’s power system is practical and municipal, but electricity and damp are a bad combination. The presence of porcelain insulators, timber poles, iron brackets, and overhead cables suggests the inhabitants know this. You should too.

Do not:

- touch cables;
- move wires;
- hang items on poles or brackets;
- enter fenced transformer courts;
- place metal objects near exposed terminals;
- use damaged cords;
- assume grounded metal is safe;
- stand in puddles while handling electronics near power fittings.

If you see a fallen wire or sparking fitting, do not touch it. Warn others calmly and notify a worker or administrator.

## 15. Biological etiquette: animals, plants, and invisible life

The town is full of life because it offers water, warmth, shelter, and food. This is beautiful, but it also requires restraint.

### Birds

Dippers, wagtails, sparrows, wrens, herons, gulls, and starlings all use the town. They may be tame or at least accustomed to humans. Do not feed them. Do not chase them. Do not photograph nests closely. Be careful where you place bags or hands; nests may be hidden behind gauges, in beams, or in sheltered corners.

### Frogs and aquatic creatures

Frogs in lower gutters and eel-like shapes in conduit chambers are part of the system. Do not lift hatches to look for them. Do not put your hands into dark water. Do not drop objects into channels.

### Rats, voles, and cats

Rats and water voles use hidden routes along masonry. Cats watch from doorsteps and boiler platforms. Store food sealed. Do not leave crumbs. Wash hands before eating. Do not handle dead animals. If bitten or scratched, clean the wound immediately and seek help.

### Insects

Midges, beetles, moths, and water skaters are abundant. Most are nuisance rather than danger, but if midges are thick, a fine head net or mask can help. Avoid perfumed soaps or sweet scents that attract insects.

### Plants

Moss, ferns, algae, watercress, reeds, rushes, liverworts, and seedling willows grow vigorously in damp margins. Do not eat wild watercress or other plants unless a local confirms they are safe. Do not collect seeds or cuttings. In a multiverse context, moving plant material can be ethically and biologically serious.

## 16. Multiverse biosecurity: do not contaminate the basin

Because the town is a water-collection system, contamination is not a minor issue. Any foreign organism, spore, seed, insect, microbe, or chemical residue could enter the condensation engine and spread through tanks, channels, gardens, and drinking supplies.

Before entering, if possible:

- clean boots and soles;
- remove soil from previous worlds;
- empty and dry old water containers;
- seal any biological samples;
- avoid carrying fresh fruit, seeds, flowers, soil, or live insects;
- brush off clothing that has been in other wild areas;
- make sure your bag has no hidden mud or plant matter.

While there:

- do not release foreign water into drains;
- do not empty containers into channels unless authorized;
- do not discard organic waste;
- do not plant anything;
- do not leave food scraps;
- do not transfer organisms from one part of the basin to another if instructed not to.

When leaving:

- inspect boots, cuffs, and bags for seeds or gravel;
- dry equipment;
- dispose of waste properly;
- report accidental spills or dropped biological material.

An ordinary traveler may not think of a single seed or drop of foreign pond water as important. In a condensation town, it can be.

## 17. Social preparation: how to behave with the inhabitants

The people here likely value steadiness, attention, and respect for water. They may be accustomed to maintenance crews, inspectors, traders, and perhaps travelers, but they will not appreciate careless curiosity.

### Speak clearly

Raise your voice enough to be heard, but do not shout theatrically. Use short phrases. Ask direct questions. Repeat if necessary. If you do not understand, say so.

### Ask before entering restricted spaces

Especially:

- transformer courts;
- conduit chambers;
- settling tanks;
- turbine housings;
- administrative offices;
- rail yards;
- maintenance workshops;
- high net platforms.

### Ask before photographing

The town is visually striking, but infrastructure may be sensitive or private. Photographs of valves, gauges, channels, and worker routines may be acceptable, but always ask. Avoid sketching or mapping in a way that looks like surveying unless permitted.

### Do not romanticize the workers

They are not scenery. They are people doing patient technical work. Do not interrupt them for charming conversation unless they initiate or the setting allows. If you want to learn, ask if you may observe or help in a small approved way.

### Gifts

If you wish to offer a gift, choose something practical and non-contaminating:

- clean dry rags;
- quality work gloves;
- sealed lubricants if local standards allow;
- replacement glass beads or mesh only if you know the specification;
- sealed tea or dry food if culturally acceptable;
- neutral coins or tokens;
- written knowledge only if invited.

Avoid:

- live plants;
- seeds;
- soil;
- animals;
- unmarked liquids;
- foreign medicines;
- random machinery parts that may not fit local standards;
- decorative objects that could become litter.

When in doubt, ask the administrative hall what the town needs.

## 18. Prepare your body for a day of cool damp

Even if the temperature is mild, dampness makes cold feel personal. Metal stays chill. Breath may whiten in shade. Your fingers may stiffen. Your feet may tire. Your voice may get hoarse from talking over noise.

Practical measures:

- eat before arriving;
- carry warm or room-temperature water;
- avoid alcohol before moving near machinery;
- take breaks to warm hands;
- change socks if they become damp;
- keep a dry spare layer sealed in your bag;
- wipe hands before handling paperwork or food;
- stretch calves and feet if walking on stone for hours.

If you begin shivering, becoming clumsy, or feeling unusually irritable, stop. Find a drier interior, change wet layers, and rest.

## 20. Prepare for time to feel different

The town measures time by water levels, bell cycles, sound patterns, and maintenance intervals rather than by abstract clock-face alone. The bell tower marks slow intervals, not minutes.

Do not schedule tight appointments as if you are in a quiet office city. Build buffers. If you are meeting someone, agree on a bell cycle rather than an exact minute if possible. Learn local markers:

- “after the second tank fills”;
- “when the high nets are lowered”;
- “at the third bell”;
- “before the overflow shift”;
- “when the turbine room changes crew.”

If you become impatient, remind yourself that the town’s function is accumulation. Mist thickens over hours. Tanks fill by degrees. Pipes are adjusted by sound. You will get more by slowing down than by pushing.

## 21. Safety checklist for moving through the town

A practical visitor should remember:

- **Keep dry feet.** Wet boots cause mistakes.
- **Keep hands free.** Do not carry loose items near channels.
- **Keep your voice clear.** Speak up, but do not scream.
- **Keep your hearing available.** Use filtered protection, not total deafness.
- **Keep off the tracks.** Rail carts may move quietly.
- **Keep out of transformer courts.** Unless invited.
- **Keep away from valves.** Do not turn, lean, or hang on them.
- **Keep channels clear.** Do not block inspection paths.
- **Keep food sealed.** Birds and rats learn quickly.
- **Keep water contained.** Do not pour unknown liquids into the system.
- **Keep your pace slow.** The town will not reward urgency.

## 23. If you plan to stay overnight

The town does not become silent at night. The machines continue. Water continues. Frogs may become louder when machines pause. Moths gather around lamps. Condensation thickens. Stone and metal grow chill.

Prepare by bringing:

- earplugs for sleeping;
- eye mask if lamps or early light disturb you;
- dry sleeping clothes;
- synthetic or wool insulation, not cotton;
- a sleeping bag liner if local bedding may be damp;
- sealed food;
- toiletries that do not pollute channels;
- a dry bag for tomorrow’s clothes.

Ask where the designated washing and toilet facilities are. Do not relieve yourself in yards, channels, or drainage troughs. The town’s water system is too intimate for that kind of carelessness.

If you are sensitive to noise, sleep with filtered plugs but keep some awareness of bells or alarms. Ask whether night cycles include pressure releases, cart movement, or turbine changes.

## 24. If you are a scholar, artist, or observer

The place is rich for study, sketching, photography, and quiet note-taking, but it is not a museum. It is a working machine.

### For writers and note-takers

Use waterproof paper or a notebook tethered to you. Keep pages from blowing. Write short notes while observing. Do not stand in walkways while absorbed in your notebook. Step aside.

### For artists

Graphite, waterproof ink, or pencil are safer than loose charcoal or pastels that can shed. Avoid setting up a large easel in narrow lanes. Do not block maintenance routes. Do not dip brushes into channels.

### For photographers

Use a wrist strap or neck strap. Keep the camera sealed when not shooting. Expect lenses to fog. Wipe often. Do not lean over tanks or channels for an angle. Ask before photographing workers, valves, or sensitive infrastructure.

### For scientists

If you intend to sample water, moss, stone, algae, or animal signs, get permission first. Use sterile containers. Record exact locations. Do not introduce sampling equipment contaminated by other worlds. Do not leave markers, stakes, or tags unless approved.

## 25. Prepare for the emotional character of the town

The town has a quiet contradiction: industrial and lush, built and open, loud and slow. It is not mysterious in the sense of being hidden or ominous. Ordinary daylight reveals everything. That can be disarming. A traveler expecting secrecy may feel there is nothing to discover. But the discovery is in the patience.

You may notice:

- a worker listening to a pipe instead of looking at it;
- a bird returning to the same turbine niche;
- moss spreading along one brick course and stopping near a wire;
- water moving through a ceramic channel with a different sound after a valve changes;
- a child stepping carefully over a humming grate;
- steam rising briefly from a warm pipe and vanishing;
- the soft clicking of glass weights on the fog nets;
- the bell marking not the hour, but a phase of work.

To prepare emotionally, practice being unimportant. The town does not need you. It needs its channels clear, its machines tended, its water respected. If you can accept that, the place becomes generous.

## 27. A simple arrival protocol

If you want a practical rule, follow this:

1. **Arrive at a public or administrative edge, not inside machinery.**
2. **Pause and listen before moving.**
3. **Check boots, bag straps, and hood.**
4. **Go to the administrative hall or ask a worker where visitors should register.**
5. **Ask what is restricted today.**
6. **Ask where you may stand, walk, refill water, and rest.**
7. **Keep to marked or inhabited walkways.**
8. **Do not touch channels, valves, nets, cables, or hatches.**
9. **Move slowly and step aside for workers and carts.**
10. **Leave before you are tired, cold, or careless.**

## 28. The deepest preparation: learn to be harmless

The town is not asking for heroism. It is asking for restraint.

An ordinary human multiverse traveler prepares best by becoming someone the basin can ignore. Your boots should not slip. Your bag should not swing. Your voice should not confuse. Your hands should stay at your sides. Your water bottle should remain sealed until you know the proper tap. Your curiosity should be patient. Your waste should leave with you. Your presence should not change the flow of mist, water, work, or animals.

If you can do that, the town will reveal its ordinary beauty: the bright wet stone under pale daylight, the green moss in brick seams, the heron beside the settling tank, the soft click of glass weights on the nets, the bell marking slow cycles, the turbines breathing in their stone housings, the workers listening to pipes, the next drop forming on a copper fin.

Bring dry socks. Bring earplugs. Bring respect. Bring patience.

Then go lightly, stand aside, and let the town drink the air.
