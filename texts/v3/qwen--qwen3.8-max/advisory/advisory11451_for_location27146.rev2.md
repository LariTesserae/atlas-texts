## Preparing to Visit the Drowned Plain at the End of Erosion

The first thing to understand is that this is not a place one “swims through” in the ordinary sense. It is not a clear sea, not a lake, not a cave pool, not a river, and not a trench. It is a saturated, cold, particle-filled medium over a landscape that has already been worn into its final condition. You are not entering a body of water so much as entering the interior of a vast, slow, mineral body.

For an ordinary human multiverse traveler — a person without supernatural resistance to pressure, cold, darkness, or unbreathable fluid — preparation should be governed by three principles:

1. **Survive without relying on the environment.**  
   The place offers no air, no warmth, no light, no orientation, no surface rescue, and no structures.

2. **Move and perceive without expecting sight.**  
   Visibility will be poor to nonexistent. You will navigate by sound, touch, pressure, chemistry, inertia, and instruments.

3. **Leave no permanent argument of yourself.**  
   The world rejects permanence. Anything you build, drop, anchor, or abandon will either be buried, erased, or become a contamination problem. Visit as a sealed guest, not as a builder.

Below is a full preparation guide, moving from practical survival to the more subtle psychological and ethical preparation required for such a place.

---

# 1. Decide How You Will Physically Enter the Location

An unprotected human cannot stand there. The cold, pressure, darkness, suspended particulate load, and lack of breathable medium make direct exposure fatal or quickly incapacitating. Even if the pressure is not extreme, the environment is too dense, too cold, and too chemically complex for ordinary skin and lungs.

You need a sealed, pressure-rated interface between your body and the drowned plain.

## Best option: a one-atmosphere deep-rated environmental suit or observation pod

The most appropriate method for an ordinary human is a **sealed atmospheric-pressure suit** or a **small one-person observation pod**. This lets you remain inside a survivable bubble while moving through the packed medium.

The suit or pod should:

- Maintain a comfortable internal pressure, ideally near one atmosphere.
- Be rated for unknown or high external pressure. If the depth or overburden is uncertain, assume a generous safety margin.
- Have no exposed breathing loop to the outside.
- Use closed-loop life support.
- Have thermal regulation.
- Have sealed joints protected against fine silt.
- Use external cameras, sonar, and haptic sensors rather than relying only on direct vision.
- Be mechanically simple enough to repair or abort safely.

A large submersible may be safer for longer stays, but it is also more intrusive. The drowned plain is broad and soft, but its medium is crowded and easily disturbed. A large vehicle will stir clouds of sediment and may overwhelm the delicate chemical and biological textures of the place. A small suit, moved carefully, is more respectful.

## Secondary option: ambient-pressure diving equipment

Only consider this if preliminary probes confirm that the pressure is modest, the chemistry is not toxic, and the suspended load will not immediately clog regulators or vision systems. Even then, open-circuit scuba would be inappropriate. Exhaled bubbles would disturb the medium and introduce foreign gases. You would need:

- Full-face mask or helmet.
- Closed-circuit rebreather.
- Fine-particle filtration on any external gas pathways.
- Thermal protection.
- Redundant breathing systems.
- Excellent low-visibility training.
- A strict no-touch, no-drop discipline.

Even under favorable conditions, this is less safe than a pressure suit. The water is not merely dark; it is dense with silt, microbial films, flocs, brine, and colloids. Anything that can clog, foul, or freeze probably will.

## Do not rely on surfacing

The description says the surface is “not far away, yet might as well be absent.” Rising toward it would not bring daylight. It would only reduce pressure slightly before reaching a darker, colder boundary. There may be no breathable surface, no sky, no rescue platform, no horizon.

Do not plan upward as an escape route. Plan exit through your multiverse transit system, a tether, a habitat lock, or a prearranged extraction point.

# 2. Confirm the Local Physical Constants Before Entry

Before an ordinary human enters, the site should be probed. Multiverse travel may allow direct insertion, but direct insertion into an unknown pressure regime is dangerous.

At minimum, probe for:

- **External pressure.**
- **Temperature.**
- **Gravity.**
- **Fluid density and viscosity.**
- **Chemical composition.**
- **Oxygen or breathable gas absence.**
- **Toxic compounds: methane, sulfide, ammonia, heavy brines, metallic salts, acidic or alkaline zones.**
- **Current patterns and density flows.**
- **Sediment firmness.**
- **Biological contamination risk.**
- **Whether the “surface boundary” is liquid, gas, semi-solid, or another medium.**

If the pressure exceeds your suit rating, do not enter. If the chemistry is corrosive or toxic, upgrade your external materials and seals. If the suspended load is so dense that sensors cannot function, reduce your ambitions and shorten the visit.

This place may be physically normal, but “normal” does not mean gentle. It is normal in the way a deep internal organ is normal: incompatible with casual human presence.

# 3. Choose the Right Pressure Suit or Pod

Your suit is not merely clothing. It is your body for the duration of the visit. It should be designed for dark, silty, cold, high-pressure, low-visibility conditions.

## Structural design

The suit should be:

- Pressure-resistant.
- Abrasion-resistant.
- Corrosion-resistant.
- Smoothly faired to reduce snagging and turbulence.
- Free of unnecessary external cavities where silt can collect.
- Equipped with protected joints.
- Equipped with sealed, silt-resistant manipulators or gloves.

Avoid large fragile viewports. A broad glass dome is tempting, but here it would be weak, easily blinded, and quickly coated with microbial film. Better to use:

- Small protected viewports.
- External cameras behind sacrificial windows.
- Imaging sonar.
- Low-light sensors.
- Haptic feedback systems.

If you want the sensation of “being there,” use a helmet with a protected narrow viewport and external sensor translation. Do not depend on your natural eyes.

## Materials

The local water is briny, mineral-rich, and biologically active. It may contain sulfides, methane, iron-poor sediments, compressed ash, and microbial slicks. Exposed metals may corrode or create chemical disturbances. Prefer:

- Ceramics.
- Coated composites.
- Inert polymers.
- Glass or sapphire sensor ports.
- Sealed titanium or other stable alloys only where necessary.

If possible, avoid externally exposed reactive metals entirely.

## Thermal protection

The cold is steady and patient. It does not strike dramatically; it accumulates. You need:

- Insulated underlayers.
- Active heating.
- Heated extremities: gloves, boots, head/neck loop.
- Redundant heating power.
- Temperature monitoring.
- No reliance on venting heat into the environment.

A suit that only delays cold is not enough for a long visit. You need a closed thermal system. Electric heating powered by batteries is preferable to chemical heat packs or combustion. There should be no open heat source, no flame, no exothermic venting into the medium.

## Life support

Use closed-loop life support:

- Oxygen supply.
- Carbon dioxide scrubbing.
- Humidity control.
- Pressure monitoring.
- Emergency bail-out gas.
- Internal atmosphere purification.
- No external venting of waste gas.

If your suit uses external water for cooling or ballast, it must have robust filtration. In this place, intake clogging is a serious hazard. Better to use sealed internal thermal loops and fixed or transferable ballast.

## Waste and hygiene

For anything beyond a short visit, you need:

- Drinking water.
- Food that can be eaten inside the suit or pod.
- Urine collection.
- Fecal containment.
- Moisture management.
- No discharge of any biological waste into the environment.

You are a sealed organism. Nothing leaves you except carefully controlled sensor emissions and, if necessary, a tiny sampling footprint.

# 4. Prepare Your Senses for a World Without Light or Horizon

The drowned plain is not merely dark. It is materially dark. The suspended load eats illumination. Any light you bring will scatter back from particles and reveal little except the density of the medium itself.

Your sensory preparation should assume that vision is secondary.

## Visual systems

Use:

- Low-intensity lights.
- Narrow beams.
- Short pulses.
- Angled lights, not directly forward-facing beams.
- Red or narrow-spectrum light if local organisms are less sensitive to it.
- External cameras with image enhancement.
- Sacrificial clear panels or wipers.

Avoid:

- Bright continuous floodlights.
- Sudden flashes.
- Long-duration illumination unless necessary.

Bright light will likely create a wall of backscatter. It will blind you more than reveal. It may also disturb organisms that live in near-total darkness.

## Sonar and acoustic sensing

Sound may be muffled, but it is still likely more useful than vision over distance. Use:

- Passive hydrophones.
- Low-power active sonar.
- Short-range imaging sonar.
- Pressure-wave sensors.
- Contact microphones or hull transducers.

Be careful with active sonar. The place is quiet, and its creatures may be sensitive to pressure changes. Use the lowest effective power. If you hear or feel clicks, whispers, or shell movements, pause and listen before pinging again.

## Haptic and tactile sensing

The environment is crowded and granular. You may perceive it better through touch than sight. Equip yourself with:

- Probe poles or tactile feelers.
- Force-feedback manipulators.
- Floor-contact sensors.
- Current sensors.
- Pressure gradient sensors.
- Vibration sensors.

A gentle tactile interface can translate the outside world into something your human nervous system can interpret: a tap here, a drag there, a soft pulse of pressure, a grainy whisper against the suit’s skin.

## Chemical sensing

The plain is shaped by chemistry as much as geology. Carry sensors for:

- Salinity.
- pH.
- Redox potential.
- Oxygen, if present.
- Methane.
- Sulfide.
- Ammonia.
- Organic decay markers.
- Suspended particle concentration.
- Microbial activity proxies.

Chemical gradients may guide you toward life. Sparse vegetation and rare animals will cluster where seepage or favorable chemistry exists.

# 5. Navigation: Prepare for a Landscape Without Landmarks

There are no cliffs, no ridges, no monuments, no walls, no roads, no structures. The horizon is absent. The floor is nearly flat, though not perfectly flat. It has small relief: shallow depressions, faint swells, furrows, low undulations, rippled mud, soft fields of floc.

This is not empty space. It is saturated openness. But it does not offer easy orientation.

## Do not rely on visual landmarks

There may be none. Any landmark you create yourself may be buried or erased quickly. Navigation must be instrument-based.

Use:

- Inertial navigation.
- Gyroscopic heading.
- Doppler velocity log, if it can function in the medium.
- Acoustic ranging.
- Pressure depth sensing.
- Chemical or acoustic “texture” mapping.
- Dead reckoning.
- Time-based mission limits.

The plain may have subtle acoustic backscatter differences: firmer mud, softer floc, denser brine, thin chert layers, buried lenses. These can become your map. But they are moods, not monuments.

## Use a tether if possible

If your entry point is stable, a tether can be invaluable. It gives:

- Physical return path.
- Communication line.
- Power, if needed.
- Psychological reassurance.

But the tether must be managed carefully. It should not drag across microbial mats, snag on anything, or leave a permanent scar. It must be fully retrieved on exit.

If you use a tether, keep it floating or neutrally buoyant if possible. Do not let it plow through the sediment.

## If untethered, use retrievable beacons

If you must deploy beacons, make them:

- Retrievable.
- Non-permanent.
- Acoustically quiet unless needed.
- Chemically inert.
- Tethered or tracked.
- Removed when you leave.

Do not leave permanent markers. The environment will try to bury them, and any surviving object becomes an unnatural artifact.

## Treat “up” with suspicion

Up is not necessarily safety. The surface layer may be dark, cold, and inaccessible. If you ascend, do so slowly and with sensors. Do not assume there is air. Do not assume there is light. Do not assume there is a boundary you can rest against.

Your primary direction should be “back to exit,” not “up.”

# 6. Movement: Learn to Move Without Arguing with the Medium

The water resists like a crowd. It does not stop you; it occupies you. Movement through it leaves trails that close slowly. Every motion creates clouds, eddies, and disturbed sediment.

The proper way to move here is slowly, patiently, and with minimal displacement.

## Neutral buoyancy is essential

You should be close to neutrally buoyant. Too heavy, and you sink into soft sediment. Too buoyant, and you rise into denser suspended layers or struggle to stay near the floor.

Use:

- Adjustable internal ballast.
- Sealed buoyancy material.
- Fine control of trim.
- No external ballast that can be dropped and lost.

If you must jettison weight in an emergency, use only materials that can be fully recovered or that are environmentally benign and approved by your multiverse biosecurity protocol. Ideally, avoid jettisoning anything.

## Walking

If you stand, the floor is soft but not bottomless. It is fine-grained, slippery, occasionally firm, and often coated with microbial slicks. Walking will stir clouds.

If walking is necessary:

- Use broad footpads, like snowshoes.
- Keep steps shallow.
- Place feet gently.
- Wait for sediment to settle before the next step if visibility is impaired.
- Avoid stepping on microbial mats.
- Avoid depressions filled with fresh floc.
- Do not push off hard with your toes.
- Keep your center of gravity low.

Walking may be useful for precise sampling, but for most travel, hovering or slow gliding is better.

## Hovering and gliding

If your suit or pod uses thrusters, they must be:

- Ducted.
- Low-speed.
- Low-disturbance.
- Positioned to avoid blasting the floor.
- Filtered or shielded against particulate ingestion.

Even good thrusters can create sediment storms. Use them sparingly. Sometimes the best movement is to adjust buoyancy and drift with the slow density currents, correcting gently.

## Anchoring

Do not anchor if you can avoid it. The floor is soft, and any anchor will leave a wound that may fill slowly but should not be made in the first place.

If you need to stay in place:

- Use dynamic positioning with minimal thrust.
- Hover.
- Use a broad, temporary skid only if absolutely necessary.
- Retrieve all equipment.
- Avoid resting on living mats.

The place rejects permanence. Work with that.

# 7. Prepare for the Cold

The cold here is not dramatic. It is not a knife. It is a slow claim. It enters through time.

You need to prepare not only with equipment but with behavior.

## Monitor core temperature

Have continuous monitoring for:

- Internal suit temperature.
- Skin temperature at extremities.
- Battery temperature.
- Life-support loop temperature.

Do not trust your subjective feeling alone. In steady cold, humans adapt until they are already impaired.

## Protect extremities

Hands and feet will be the first to suffer. If manipulators are clumsy, you may be tempted to remove gloves or reduce insulation. Do not. Instead, design better haptic interfaces.

Use:

- Heated gloves.
- Heated boots.
- Loose inner layers that do not restrict circulation.
- Hand warmers only if sealed and non-polluting, but electric heating is better.

## Plan for heating failure

If heating fails:

- Abort the excursion.
- Return to habitat or extraction point.
- Reduce movement if movement increases heat loss.
- Use emergency insulation.
- Do not attempt heroic exploration.

Cold will make your thinking slower before you notice it.

# 8. Prepare for Poor Visibility and Sensory Deprivation

The darkness here is not like a night sky. It is close, granular, and textured. Your light may only reveal a short cone of suspended particles. Your eyes may search for a horizon and find none.

This can produce strange psychological effects: claustrophobia despite openness, disorientation despite calm, a sense of being buried while suspended.

## Train in analogous environments

Before visiting, practice in:

- Silted underwater caves.
- Dark quarries.
- Deep training tanks.
- Sensory deprivation pools.
- Night diving in turbid water.
- Submersible simulation.
- Closed-space survival drills.

Learn to be calm when you cannot see your hands clearly. Learn to trust instruments and breathing. Learn to move slowly when every instinct says to thrash.

## Bring psychological anchors

Because there is no external day or night, use:

- Mission clock.
- Checklists.
- Verbal self-reports.
- Breathing rhythm.
- Tactile routines.
- Small, repeatable tasks.
- Buddy check-ins if not alone.

Do not let your mind drift into the medium. Give it structure.

## Expect the environment to feel attentive

The description says the water feels inhabited by texture and intention. This does not necessarily mean it is conscious. It means it is full of processes. Every particle is moving, settling, coating, being coated. The medium responds to you.

Prepare for the feeling of being inside something alive without being able to identify a single living face.

# 9. Prepare for the Turbulence

The plain is turbulent, but its turbulence is muffled, internal, and granular. There are no waves or roaring currents. Instead, there are density currents, sediment plumes, floc sheets, pressure waves, and slow rearrangements.

This turbulence will not usually throw you violently, but it can:

- Drift you off course.
- Blind you with sediment.
- Change local density.
- Alter chemical gradients.
- Bury small objects.
- Disturb sampling.
- Create false readings.

## Learn to read the water

Watch for:

- Rising clouds of particulate matter.
- Sudden changes in current pressure.
- Floc tearing apart or resettling.
- Temperature or salinity shifts.
- Low hush or granular whispering.
- Soft shivering of the medium.

If a density current passes through, do not fight it violently. Stabilize your orientation, track your displacement, and wait.

## Avoid active sediment events

If you see or sense a plume rising like breath from the sediment, do not move into it. It may be a natural redistribution event, a burrow collapse, a gas release, or a biological disturbance.

Let it settle. The place is patient; you must be too.

# 10. Prepare for the Floor

The floor is not a simple seabed. It is the accumulated end of erosion: clay, silt, mineral flour, brine, compressed ash, organic sludge, occasional chert, iron-poor sediment, buried lenses, low blunt shapes.

It is soft enough to accept your imprint, but not necessarily soft enough to swallow you. Still, there may be patches of fresh floc, looser mud, depressions, or chemically unstable pockets.

## Test before committing weight

Use a probe or gentle foot contact before standing fully. Look for:

- Firm compacted mud.
- Slight resistance.
- Low rebound.
- Absence of fresh floc.
- Absence of microbial mat.
- No rising gas or plume.

Avoid:

- Depressions where fine matter thickens.
- Freshly settled floc.
- Dark, chemically sour pockets.
- Areas with active bubbling or seepage unless sampling is your purpose.
- Microbial mats.

## If you begin to sink

Do not thrash. Thrashing stirs sediment and can make the situation worse.

Instead:

- Increase buoyancy slowly.
- Spread your weight.
- Use gentle lateral movement toward firmer ground.
- Avoid deep foot placement.
- If in a suit, use controlled limb motions rather than panicked stepping.

The floor is not likely to be a trap, but it can be sticky, slippery, and disorienting.

# 11. Prepare for Life: Microbial Mats, Pale Threads, Blackish Weeds, Rare Animals

Life is sparse but present. It is chemically fed, not sun-fed. It is pale, thin, patient, and easily disturbed.

## Microbial mats

Microbial mats may appear dull, slick, ash-colored, or bone-colored. They are fragile and ecologically important. Do not land on them. Do not scrape them casually. Do not shine bright lights on them for long periods.

If sampling:

- Use sterile tools.
- Take the smallest possible plug.
- Seal it immediately.
- Avoid dragging across the surface.
- Allow the surrounding sediment to settle.

## Threadlike filaments and blackish weeds

These organisms grow toward chemical nourishment, not light. They may be anchored in denser mud where gradients are favorable. They are sparse because the place is old and poor.

Do not pull them free unless necessary. If you must sample, cut cleanly and take only a fragment.

## Rare animals

Animals are few, slow, and widely spaced. They may include blind worms, flat soft-shelled crawlers, ribbonlike drifters, scavengers, grazers, and patient predators.

Do not chase them. Do not attempt to feed them. Do not corner them. Do not illuminate them strongly.

If an animal approaches you, remain still. It may be tasting your pressure wake or chemical trace. Let it decide whether you are irrelevant. Then move away gently.

## Tool-using creatures

Some creatures may use simple tools: stone flakes, shell valves, spicules, fragments used to dig, scrape, anchor, or wedge. This is technology at its first gesture.

This is ethically significant.

If you encounter tool use:

- Observe from a distance.
- Do not interfere.
- Do not offer tools.
- Do not leave objects behind.
- Do not attempt to teach.
- Do not mimic their behavior in a way that alters their choices.
- Do not take their tools.
- Do not replace their tools with superior materials.

The introduction of a single durable object could distort the developmental path of such creatures. A metal knife, plastic fragment, or polished artifact could become a temptation, a hazard, or a false inheritance.

If one of them takes one of your tools, the fault is yours for failing to secure it. Try to retrieve it without violence or pursuit. If retrieval would cause greater harm, withdraw and record the incident. Better still: prevent it by tethering everything.

# 12. Biosecurity: You Are a Contamination Risk

This place has its own microbial ecology. It is full of suspended fines, microbial films, flocs, dissolved salts, and colloids. It may be ancient, isolated, and vulnerable.

You must prevent contamination in both directions.

## Before entry

Your suit, pod, and tools should be:

- Sterilized.
- Free of Earth microbes, if Earth is your origin.
- Free of organic residue.
- Sealed.
- Inspected for leaks.

Do not bring food, waste, skin flakes, hair, fibers, or microbes into the environment through careless venting or shedding.

## During entry

Do not discharge:

- Air.
- Gas.
- Waste.
- Coolant.
- Lubricants.
- Ballast water.
- Cleaning fluids.
- Organic material.

If you must vent anything in an emergency, it should be into a sealed containment system, not the plain.

## After exit

You should undergo:

- External decontamination.
- Sample containment.
- Quarantine depending on your multiverse protocol.
- Suit cleaning in a controlled buffer.
- Inspection for local organisms clinging to joints, filters, or sensors.

Do not carry the drowned plain into another world.

# 14. Plan the Visit Around Time, Not Distance

There is no horizon. There is no daylight change. There is no weather overhead. You can easily lose track of how far you have gone or how long you have been there.

Use hard time limits.

## Turnaround rules

Set turnaround based on:

- Remaining oxygen.
- Remaining power.
- Heating capacity.
- Battery reserve.
- CO₂ scrubber capacity.
- Psychological comfort.
- Navigation confidence.

A good rule is: turn back when half your consumables remain, or earlier if visibility, navigation, or current conditions degrade.

Do not say, “Just a little farther.” There is no “farther” here in the usual sense. The plain extends. Its moods repeat. You can move for a long time without encountering a boundary.

## Mission goals

Choose modest goals:

- Record the acoustic texture of a small area.
- Map a density current for a limited time.
- Sample one sediment core.
- Observe microbial threads without touching them.
- Photograph or sonar-image a rare animal.
- Measure chemical gradients near a seep.

Do not try to “conquer” the plain. It is already finished. It does not need your ambition.

# 15. Buddy System vs. Solo Travel

An ordinary human should not visit this place alone unless the suit or pod is exceptionally reliable and the extraction system is redundant.

A buddy provides:

- Redundant observation.
- Emergency assistance.
- Psychological grounding.
- Backup navigation.
- Help if one becomes disoriented.

But two travelers also create more disturbance. If traveling together:

- Maintain distance.
- Avoid crossing each other’s sediment clouds.
- Use quiet communication.
- Do not cluster around rare life.
- Agree on abort signals.
- Have separate navigation logs.

If solo, maintain constant communication with a habitat, relay, or automated monitoring system if possible. Do not rely on external rescue alone.

# 16. Communication in a Muffled World

The place absorbs sound. It is not silent, but its sounds are low, granular, and compressed. Communication may be difficult.

## Use multiple channels

- Hardline tether communication if tethered.
- Low-frequency acoustic modem.
- Pressure-pulse transducers.
- Light signals for short range.
- Vibration codes through the suit or floor.
- Text-based logging inside the helmet.

Do not rely on voice alone. The medium may swallow it.

## Keep communications quiet

Loud signals may disturb creatures or mask the subtle sounds of the environment. Use short, low-power messages.

If you hear local sounds — soft clicks, shell shifts, granular whispers — pause before transmitting. Listen first.

# 17. Emergency Preparation

The drowned plain is not malicious, but it is indifferent. Emergencies must be handled calmly because panic will worsen sediment disturbance, increase gas consumption, and impair judgment.

## Suit leak

If the suit leaks:

- Identify the location if possible.
- Seal internal compartments if the suit allows.
- Abort immediately.
- Return to extraction point.
- Do not remove the helmet or open the suit.
- If flooding begins, use emergency breathing apparatus inside the suit if available.
- Do not attempt to explore through failure.

A leak here is not merely water entering. It is cold brine, silt, and possibly chemicals entering your survival space.

## Loss of orientation

If you lose your way:

- Stop.
- Stabilize buoyancy.
- Reduce movement.
- Check inertial log.
- Check last known bearing.
- Use passive sonar.
- Look for your own disturbed trail if visible, but do not rely on it.
- Return by the most certain route, not the shortest imagined route.
- If uncertain, conserve energy and activate extraction beacon if available.

Do not swim faster to find a landmark. There may be none.

## Stuck in sediment

If you feel suction or soft mud holding you:

- Do not jerk.
- Increase buoyancy slowly.
- Spread limbs to distribute weight.
- Use gentle rocking rather than pulling.
- Avoid deep thruster use.
- If with a buddy, ask for assistance without both of you becoming stuck.

## Caught in a density current

If the medium begins moving you:

- Do not fight it with violent motion.
- Orient yourself.
- Track displacement.
- Maintain neutral buoyancy.
- Wait for the current to weaken.
- Correct gradually.

## Fire or electrical fault inside suit

This is rare but severe.

- Cut affected circuits.
- Use internal fire suppression if safe and non-toxic.
- Abort.
- Do not vent combustion products into the environment.
- If the suit atmosphere is compromised, use emergency breathing loop.

## Contamination breach

If local fluid enters the suit:

- Avoid breathing it.
- Use internal emergency air.
- Seal the affected area if possible.
- Exit immediately.
- Begin decontamination and medical monitoring.

# 18. Ethical Conduct: Visit as a Passing Pressure, Not as a Mark

The drowned plain is unimproved. It has no built structures. Its rare tool-users may be on the edge of intention, but they have not made a world of artifacts. You should not change that.

## Do not build

Do not construct:

- Walls.
- Shelters.
- Platforms.
- Monuments.
- Cairns.
- Roads.
- Anchors.
- Permanent sensors.

Even a small structure is an argument against the nature of the place. It says, “Let this remain.” The place says, “Everything settles.”

## Do not leave objects

Every object should be tethered, contained, or retrieved. If you drop something, recover it. If recovery would cause more harm than leaving it, that is a failure of preparation, not a justification for contamination.

## Do not improve the local creatures

The temptation may arise to help a creature, give it a better tool, protect it, or feed it. Resist.

This is not a place for kindness expressed through interference. Kindness here is restraint.

## Do not harvest more than necessary

If scientific sampling is authorized, take the minimum. The biomass is sparse. A small animal or microbial patch may be rare over a vast area.

## Do not introduce sound, light, or chemical noise

The environment is quiet and chemically structured. Excess noise or light can disrupt behavior. Chemical leaks can poison gradients that life depends on.

---

# 19. Scientific Preparation, If You Are a Researcher

If the purpose is study, prepare carefully. The plain rewards patience and punishes sloppy sampling.

## Geology and sedimentology

Measure:

- Grain size distribution.
- Clay/silt content.
- Compaction.
- Buried lens depth.
- Chert or iron-poor layers.
- Sediment chemistry.
- Floc behavior.
- Current redistribution patterns.

Use small cores. Avoid large excavations.

## Hydrology and medium physics

Measure:

- Density gradients.
- Suspended load concentration.
- Viscosity changes.
- Current speed and direction.
- Pressure waves.
- Acoustic attenuation.
- Temperature stability.
- Brine movement.

## Microbial ecology

Study:

- Mat composition.
- Filament growth direction.
- Chemical gradients.
- Microbial floc interaction.
- Sulfide/methane use, if present.
- Colonization of rare hard surfaces.

Sterility is critical.

## Faunal behavior

Observe:

- Movement patterns.
- Feeding behavior.
- Response to pressure waves.
- Response to light.
- Tool selection.
- Use of shells or stone flakes.
- Burrow maintenance.
- Scavenging intervals.

Do not provoke. Do not bait. Do not trap.

## Tool-use studies

If tool use is suspected:

- Record from a distance.
- Use passive imaging.
- Do not alter the object.
- Do not remove it.
- Do not add competing objects.
- Note whether the object is carried, selected, wedged, pressed, or abandoned.
- Distinguish natural settling from intentional manipulation.

Be humble. The line between natural behavior and early technology may be subtle.

# 20. Psychological Preparation: Learn to Accept Completion

The most difficult part of visiting this place may not be the cold, darkness, or pressure. It may be the emotional atmosphere.

The landscape is finished. It is after most life, after most shape, after most ambition. It is not ruined. It is not tragic. It is complete.

An ordinary human mind may struggle with this. We are accustomed to horizons, skies, landmarks, growth, construction, progress, and visible change. Here, change is small, circular, and redistributive. Nothing becomes a mountain. Nothing becomes a city. Nothing becomes a monument. The energy of the place is maintenance, not becoming.

Prepare yourself to feel:

- Small.
- Compressed.
- Watched by processes rather than beings.
- Unneeded.
- Quiet.
- Possibly melancholy.
- Possibly relieved.

Do not interpret the place as hostile because it is indifferent. Do not interpret its emptiness as poverty. It is full of particles, currents, chemical gradients, microbial films, and slow animal patience. It is not empty. It is saturated.

Bring a mindset of reverence without conquest.

You are not there to claim, name, build, or improve. You are there to witness a world that has already been worn down to what it will forever be.

# 21. Arrival Procedure

A careful arrival might look like this:

1. **Probe the insertion point.** Confirm pressure, temperature, chemistry, and current conditions.

2. **Enter through a sealed lock.** Do not open a portal directly into the medium unless the transit system itself is pressure-rated and biosecure.

3. **Equalize gradually.** If using a habitat or bell, verify external pressure before final exposure.

4. **Check suit systems.** Pressure, oxygen, heating, communication, navigation, sensors.

5. **Stabilize buoyancy.** Do not immediately touch the floor.

6. **Use passive senses first.** Listen. Feel pressure. Measure current. Observe darkness.

7. **Turn on minimal lights only if necessary.** Let your instruments build a picture before you illuminate.

8. **Move away from the insertion point slowly.** Avoid stirring a cloud that will blind your return.

9. **Log your starting position.** Use inertial markers, acoustic signature, tether origin, or habitat lock coordinates.

10. **Proceed only if navigation confidence is high.**

---

# 22. Conduct While on the Plain

Once there:

- Move slowly.
- Speak quietly inside your suit.
- Avoid sudden light.
- Avoid touching anything unless necessary.
- Let sediment settle after any disturbance.
- Keep tools tethered.
- Watch your consumables.
- Record data in short bursts.
- Pause often.
- Do not chase rare animals.
- Do not follow bioluminescent flashes unless they are part of your approved observation.
- Treat every microbial mat as fragile.
- Treat every depression as possibly unstable.
- Treat every silence as meaningful.

If you see a faint blue-green glimmer, do not immediately shine light back. It may be a biological response to disturbance. Watch, record, and withdraw.

If you hear a soft granular whisper, stop. It may be silt releasing, a burrow wall shifting, or an animal moving through the packed medium.

If you feel the floor shiver without sound, you are sensing the internal weather of the plain. Do not panic. Note it. Adjust your position if necessary.

---

# 23. Departure Procedure

Leaving should be as careful as arriving.

1. **Return before reserves run low.**

2. **Approach the exit point slowly.** Do not create a final sediment storm that obscures the lock or tether.

3. **Check exterior for clinging organisms, floc, or sediment.**

4. **Enter airlock or habitat without dragging local material inside.**

5. **Seal samples immediately.**

6. **Begin decontamination in a controlled buffer.**

7. **Inspect suit for leaks, abrasions, clogged joints, or microbial film.**

8. **Record all observations, especially any contact with life or tool-using behavior.**

9. **Report any lost objects, even if small.**

10. **Rest and psychologically debrief.**

Do not step immediately into another vibrant world without ensuring you have not carried anything from the drowned plain.

# 24. What the Visit Will Feel Like

Imagine standing, if standing is possible, on a floor that offers no resistance of personality. It is not dramatic. It does not rise to meet you. It accepts your weight with soft, cold patience.

Around you is darkness, but not empty darkness. It is thick. It presses. Your light reaches out and becomes a small cloud of particles, not a beam. You see the medium more than you see the world.

The water pushes evenly against your suit. It is not violent, but it is everywhere. It feels crowded. When you move, your motion leaves a disturbed trail that closes slowly behind you, as if the place is quietly erasing your passage.

There is no horizon. There is no up-glow. There is no distant shore. There may be no sense of above at all except instrument readings. You are inside a regional floor that extends beyond easy comprehension.

The cold is not sharp. It is steady. It makes you aware of your heating system, your breath, your small warm body inside the shell.

Sound is muffled. You hear your own life support. You hear the faint movement of particles against the suit. Sometimes a soft whisper passes through the medium. Sometimes a pressure wave touches you, not loud, but intimate.

You may feel as though the plain is breathing at the scale of grains.

If you are lucky, you may see a brief biological spark: a blue-green smear along a disturbed filament, a faint glow where something living moves too quickly. It will not illuminate the landscape. It will only prove how vast the darkness is.

You may encounter a rare creature. It will not behave like a fish in a sunlit sea. It will be slow, cautious, perhaps blind, perhaps tasting you through pressure and chemistry. It may ignore you. It may drift away. It may use a shell or stone flake with a simplicity that feels older than thought.

You will feel the urge to do something — to mark the place, to build, to prove you were here.

The preparation should teach you to resist that urge.

The drowned plain does not need your mark. It has already been worn down to patience. Your visit should be like a pressure wave passing through: real, brief, and then gone.

# 25. Final Summary: The Essential Preparation

To visit the drowned plain at the end of erosion, an ordinary human multiverse traveler should:

- Use a sealed, pressure-rated, thermally regulated suit or pod.
- Assume no breathable surface exists.
- Bring closed-loop life support and redundant systems.
- Protect against cold, pressure, silt, and chemical exposure.
- Use sonar, touch, chemical sensing, and inertial navigation more than vision.
- Move slowly and avoid stirring sediment.
- Avoid anchors, permanent markers, and dropped objects.
- Respect microbial mats and rare fauna.
- Observe tool-using creatures without interfering.
- Carry all waste and samples out in sealed containment.
- Plan by time and consumables, not by landmarks.
- Prepare psychologically for darkness, openness, and absence of horizon.
- Accept that the place is finished and does not require human improvement.
- Leave no permanent trace.

The deepest preparation is not technical. It is attitudinal.

You must prepare to enter a world where there is nothing to conquer, nothing to build, and nothing to rescue from incompleteness. You go to be present in a place that has already finished becoming. You go to be quiet, sealed, warm, careful, and temporary.

In the drowned plain, the highest skill is not to leave an argument of yourself behind.
