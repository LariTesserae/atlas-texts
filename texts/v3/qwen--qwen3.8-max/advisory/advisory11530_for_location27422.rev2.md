An ordinary human multiverse traveler should prepare for this place not as if going into a wilderness to be conquered, nor into a normal city to be toured, but as if entering a carefully held balance in which the traveler is the main disturbance. The basin-city is wet, warm, high, silent, young, fragile, spacious, and almost faunaless. A human visitor is therefore not “just another person.” In that setting, a human being is an animal, a source of heat, sound, scent, skin, breath, waste, microbes, and motion. Preparation should be aimed at becoming as harmless, dry, quiet, and water-compatible as possible.

Below is a practical, intuitive preparation guide for an ordinary human multiverse traveler.

---

## 1. Understand the central problem: you must become compatible with water

The first rule of the place is that water has right of way. The city does not fight wetness; it distributes it. Every wall, path, stair, platform, cistern, weir, and causeway exists because someone gave water a route. A visitor who blocks, diverts, dams, contaminates, or carelessly crosses water channels is already acting against the logic of the place.

Prepare yourself to think in terms of flow:

- Do not stand in runnels, even shallow ones.
- Do not place gear in drainage channels.
- Do not block weep holes, sluice gaps, or overflow paths.
- Do not step across a channel if a bridge, causeway, or stepping arrangement exists.
- Do not try to “improve” water movement unless explicitly invited by a local maintainer.
- Do not assume that because water is gentle, it is unimportant. In this place, small water paths are sacred infrastructure.

Your body, pack, shelter, waste, and even your footprints can become obstructions or contamination events. The aim is to pass through without altering the water’s behavior.

---

## 2. Decide whether you should go at all

Because the location lacks animal life, an ordinary human visitor is an ecological anomaly. Before traveling, ask:

- Is the visit necessary?
- Is it permitted by local custodians, if any exist?
- Can the purpose be fulfilled from the rim, by observation, by remote sensing, or by a shorter visit?
- Are you medically fit for high elevation, damp heat, slippery terrain, and limited rescue?
- Are you psychologically prepared for silence, emptiness, and slow movement?
- Can you adhere to strict biosecurity and leave-no-trace discipline?

If there are inhabitants, they may be few, quiet, and highly attuned to water. They may not welcome unnecessary presence. If the city is maintained but empty, that does not mean abandoned. It may be visited seasonally, ritually, or intermittently. Treat it as occupied until proven otherwise.

The ideal visiting party is very small: one to three people. Larger groups create too much sound, too many footprints, too much waste, and too much pressure on fragile surfaces. If possible, travel with at least one companion, but avoid a crowd.

## 3. Prepare your body and habits beforehand

This is not a place for heroic fitness. It rewards steadiness, balance, patience, and careful breathing.

### Physical preparation

Train for:

- Walking slowly on wet stone.
- Climbing and descending wet stairways.
- Balancing with a staff or pole.
- Wading through shallow water without splashing.
- Carrying a moderate pack over uneven, step-filled terrain.
- Mild altitude exertion.
- Long periods in damp warmth.

You do not need mountaineering strength, but you need good ankles, careful knees, and the ability to move without urgency. Practice walking at half your normal speed. Practice stopping completely. Practice placing your foot before committing your weight.

### Altitude preparation

The basin is high. Even if it feels warm, the air may be thinner than your body expects. Warm dampness can mask altitude strain.

Before visiting:

- Learn the signs of altitude sickness: headache, nausea, dizziness, unusual fatigue, shortness of breath, poor sleep, confusion.
- If possible, acclimatize at a similar elevation before entering.
- Avoid heavy exertion on the first day.
- Hydrate steadily.
- Bring a pulse oximeter if available.
- Know your evacuation plan if symptoms worsen.

Do not assume that because the air is mild and moist, altitude will not affect you.

### Heat and humidity preparation

The warmth is not extreme, but the dampness makes it cling. Sweat may not evaporate efficiently. You can become overheated, chilled, or both depending on whether your clothing stays wet.

Prepare to:

- Move slowly enough to avoid heavy sweating.
- Change base layers when damp.
- Keep a dry sleep layer sealed and reserved only for sleeping.
- Recognize that warm wet skin macerates easily.
- Rest before you feel exhausted.

### Psychological preparation

The silence is one of the major hazards. Not because it is frightening, but because it changes your inner rhythm. There are no birds, no insects, no animal calls, no rustle of creatures, no ordinary countryside soundscape. A human mind may begin to fill the silence with imagined sounds, anxiety, or a strong urge to speak.

Practice beforehand:

- Sitting quietly for long periods.
- Listening to water without trying to fill the silence.
- Walking without unnecessary noise.
- Breathing through your nose.
- Keeping gestures slow.
- Avoiding the impulse to comment aloud on everything.

If you are prone to anxiety, claustrophobia, loneliness, or sensory under-stimulation, prepare grounding techniques: counting water drops, tracing the edge of a stone with your eyes, slow breathing, or focusing on the texture of your clothing.

## 4. Choose materials carefully: avoid animal products, noise, and contamination

The local rule of life is plant-and-water based. There are no animals, so there is no leather, bone, horn, wool, hide, silk, down, shell, fish glue, beeswax, or animal fat in ordinary local use. Even if imported items are not forbidden, they are ecologically and symbolically out of place.

As much as possible, avoid bringing:

- Leather boots or belts.
- Wool clothing.
- Down insulation.
- Silk liners.
- Bone or horn tools.
- Shell ornaments.
- Animal-fat soaps.
- Meat, fish, dairy, eggs, honey, gelatin, or animal-based rations.
- Hide glue or animal-derived adhesives.
- Fur or feather decorations.
- Any live animal, including insects, pets, pack animals, or stowaways.

This is not only cultural respect. Animal-derived materials carry scents, microbes, and possible residues. They may also rot in the damp. Plant-based and synthetic materials are safer for this environment.

But be careful with synthetics too. Many modern materials shed fibers, creak, smell of industry, or look alien. If you must use advanced gear, conceal it under simple plant-fiber outer layers. Choose muted colors: grey, green, brown, pale stone, dull black. Avoid bright colors, reflective surfaces, and glossy plastics.

Your goal is to look and sound as if you belong to the basin’s quiet material world.

## 5. Clothing: dress for damp warmth, sharp stone, and silence

The clothing system should be breathable, quick-drying, quiet, and fully non-animal where possible.

### Base layers

Bring at least two or three base layers. They should wick moisture but not shed lint.

Good options:

- Fine hemp or flax blends.
- Low-shed synthetic moisture-wicking fabric.
- Lightweight plant-fiber knits.

Avoid:

- Wool.
- Silk.
- Heavy cotton that stays wet.
- Fuzzy fabrics that shed fibers.
- Strongly scented laundry products.

Use unscented detergent before travel. Rinse thoroughly. Do not wear perfumed clothing.

### Insulation

Even though the basin is warm, high elevation, wet clothing, and still air can produce chill, especially at night or during mist.

Bring one quiet insulating layer:

- Synthetic insulation is practical.
- Plant-based padded layers are preferable if available.
- Avoid down. Down fails when wet and is animal-derived.

The insulation should compress small and stay inside a dry bag.

### Outer layer

You need protection from constant moisture: dripping air, wet stone, seepage, and occasional condensation.

A good outer layer would be:

- A hooded rain cloak or jacket.
- Woven plant-fiber fabric treated with plant wax or oil.
- Quiet, non-crackling material.
- Muted color.
- Long enough to protect your thighs when sitting or bending.

If your actual waterproof layer is advanced synthetic, cover it with a simple woven or oiled cloak to reduce visual and auditory intrusion.

### Headwear

A wide-brimmed hat is useful. It keeps dripping condensation away from your face and protects against high-elevation light, even when the sky is pale.

Good choices:

- Woven reed hat.
- Bark-fiber cap.
- Simple plant-fiber hood.

Avoid large, noisy, or brightly decorated hats.

### Gloves

Bring thin, quiet gloves for handling rope, wet wood, sharp stone, and cold sluice boards. Plant-fiber gloves are best. If you must use synthetic grip gloves, choose plain ones and keep them hidden when not needed.

### Socks and foot care

Your feet are a critical failure point. Warm wetness can soften skin rapidly.

Bring:

- Several pairs of dry socks.
- Plant-fiber or low-shed synthetic socks.
- At least one pair reserved only for sleeping.
- Antifungal powder or balm, if compatible with your body.
- A small dry bag exclusively for foot care.

Change socks at least once a day, more if they become saturated. Air your feet when possible, but do not place bare feet on contaminated or fragile surfaces.

### Footwear

This is difficult. The ideal local footwear would be simple, plant-based, and quiet. But an ordinary traveler needs protection from sharp basalt, glassy volcanic seams, slippery algae, and hidden stone edges.

Practical footwear should:

- Have excellent wet-rock grip.
- Drain or dry reasonably well.
- Protect the sole from sharp stone.
- Support the ankle on wet stairs.
- Be easy to clean before entering the basin.
- Make minimal noise.

If you use modern rubber-soled boots, choose muted colors and clean them thoroughly. If possible, cover them with simple woven overshoes or bark-fiber gaiters to reduce visual intrusion.

Avoid:

- Barefoot travel.
- Sandals without protection.
- Heavy metal-shod boots.
- Crampons or spikes, which can scar stone.
- Footwear with deep mud from other worlds.
- Footwear that squeaks.

Your boots should be sterilized before arrival and inspected for seeds, soil, or organisms.

## 6. Shelter and sleep: stay dry without altering the ground

You cannot sleep directly on saturated ground. You also should not dig trenches, drive stakes into fragile channels, crush vegetation, or build new structures.

### Sleeping platform or barrier

You need separation from wet stone or soil.

Options:

- A lightweight insulated pad.
- A closed-cell foam pad, preferably covered in quiet fabric.
- A plant-fiber mat placed over a waterproof groundsheet.
- A raised cot if absolutely necessary, but this is bulky and may look intrusive.
- A hammock only if there are suitable trees and if you can avoid damaging roots or bark; in this basin, trees are scattered and their roots may be exposed, so do not assume hammocking is appropriate.

Do not sleep on moss, cushion plants, reed beds, or algal mats. Choose bare stone, gravel, or an existing built platform only if it is clearly safe and not in use.

### Shelter

A low tarp or simple overhead cover is better than a full tent. The basin may not have heavy rain, but it has constant drip, condensation, and wet air.

Good shelter characteristics:

- Low profile.
- Muted color.
- Quiet fabric.
- Breathable if possible.
- Angled to shed drips.
- Ventilated to reduce condensation.
- Easy to rig without damaging stone or plants.

Avoid:

- Bright tents.
- Large structures.
- Tents that require many stakes.
- Shelters that block water channels.
- Shelters pitched under seeping cliff faces.
- Shelters in gullies or overflow paths.

If you need weights, use removable soft bags filled with gravel, not driven stakes. If you use cordage, protect contact points from abrasion. Do not tie to fragile plants or young trees.

### Dry sleep clothing

Keep one complete dry sleep set sealed in a waterproof bag. It should never be worn while traveling, cooking, or handling waste. This may be the most important comfort and safety item you carry.

## 7. Water: assume all water is beautiful, plentiful, and unsafe

Water is everywhere, but that does not mean it is safe to drink. The absence of animals does not mean sterility. Water may contain bacteria, fungi, protists, algae, mineral contaminants, geothermal chemicals, or human waste from upstream settlement.

Treat all water unless you are certain it comes from a protected, safe source and you have permission to use it.

### Water treatment

Bring multiple methods:

- A fine prefilter cloth for silt and algae.
- A portable water filter.
- Chemical purification tablets, preferably chlorine dioxide or equivalent.
- A UV purifier if power is available.
- Boiling capability only if you can do so safely and without fire risk.

Because the water may be silty, algae-laden, or mineral-rich, prefilter before using chemical or UV methods.

If you backflush a filter, do not release the waste water into the basin. Capture it in a waste container and pack it out.

### Containers

Bring enough capacity for at least two to three liters per person per day, more if exertion and altitude increase fluid needs.

Suitable containers:

- Fired clay vessels if you are adopting local style.
- Stainless steel or neutral-lined metal, if hidden or covered.
- Clean synthetic bladders or bottles, if sealed and durable.
- Plant-fiber bottles sealed with pitch, if locally appropriate.

Avoid open containers near channels or cisterns. Do not dip your personal container into a spring, pool, or cistern if you can avoid it. Draw water carefully without contaminating the source.

### Drinking discipline

Drink before you feel thirsty. Warm damp air can make dehydration less obvious. Add electrolytes if you are sweating or working.

Do not drink directly from:

- Pools in cut stone.
- Algal mats.
- Black mud edges.
- Warm geothermal seeps.
- Mineral-stained seepage.
- Standing water with an oily sheen.
- Water with a sulfurous smell.
- Water that tastes metallic or unusual.

If local inhabitants offer water, observe whether it has been boiled, fermented, or drawn from a protected cistern. If you are uncertain, politely rely on your own treated supply.

## 8. Food: bring plant-based, sealed, low-waste rations

There is no animal husbandry here. No fish traps, no beehives, no pastures, no stables. Food systems are plant-and-water based. Your food should match that logic.

Bring:

- Plant-based ration bars.
- Sealed dry grains or prepared cakes, if acceptable.
- Nut or seed pastes in sealed containers, if they do not risk sprouting or contaminating.
- Dried fruit only if sealed and non-viable.
- Electrolyte tablets.
- Simple teas or infusions, if you can prepare them without waste.

Avoid:

- Meat, fish, eggs, dairy, honey.
- Crumbly foods.
- Strong-smelling foods.
- Fresh seeds or pits that could germinate.
- Food requiring extensive cooking.
- Food that produces peels, shells, bones, or scraps.

Eat in a controlled area, preferably over a cloth or tray to catch crumbs. Pack out all food waste, including tiny fragments. Do not bury food. Do not compost it into the basin. Do not leave “natural” scraps. Even plant matter from another world can be contamination.

If you stay longer and are invited to share local food, be cautious. Local marsh plants, tubers, reed shoots, algae, and seeds may be safe for residents but unfamiliar to you. Eat small amounts only if offered, prepared, and explained. Do not forage on your own.

## 9. Waste: pack out everything, especially human waste

In a saturated basin, waste is a major hazard. The water table is close to the surface. Anything you deposit can spread quickly through channels, marshes, and cisterns.

### Human waste

Do not dig catholes. The ground is too wet, and contamination would be immediate.

Use:

- Portable toilet bags with absorbent plant fiber or gel.
- Sealable waste containers.
- Charcoal or odor-control inserts, if needed.
- A dedicated hygiene kit.

Urinate into a container and pack it out. In ordinary wilderness travel this may seem extreme, but here it is appropriate. Do not urinate into water, marsh, channels, or soil if avoidable.

Fecal waste must be sealed and carried out. If your visit is extended, plan for sufficient sealed capacity or arrange extraction of waste through your multiverse support system.

### Other body waste

Humans are animals, and your body will shed. Minimize it.

- Trim nails before arrival and collect clippings.
- Tie hair up and cover it.
- Collect hair from brushing.
- Avoid shaving in the basin; if necessary, collect all hair.
- Manage menstrual products as sealed waste, not buried waste.
- If you bleed from a wound, contain and pack out dressings.
- If you vomit, use a bag.
- If you must spit, do so into a waste container, not into water.

This may feel severe, but the basin has no animal life to absorb or buffer such inputs. You are the contamination risk.

### Greywater

Avoid washing with soap. If you must clean hands, dishes, or wounds, do it over a container and pack out the water. Do not release wash water into the basin.

Use unscented, plant-compatible, minimal cleaning methods. Better still, rely on sealed hygiene wipes and pack them out.

## 10. Hygiene and skin care: prevent maceration, fungus, and infection

Warm wet environments are hard on skin. The main daily medical task is keeping skin intact.

### Foot care

Each day:

- Inspect feet for softening, redness, cracks, or blisters.
- Dry feet carefully.
- Change socks.
- Apply antifungal or barrier preparation if you are prone to problems.
- Treat hot spots immediately.

Do not walk barefoot in marsh, mud, or standing water.

### Skin folds and chafing

Damp heat can cause chafing in armpits, groin, under breasts, between thighs, and under pack straps.

Bring:

- Anti-chafe balm.
- Soft, clean base layers.
- A small dry cloth.
- Plant-based barrier cream if compatible.

Apply away from water sources. Do not let creams wash into channels.

### Wounds

Any cut can become infected in wet conditions. Volcanic glass and sharp basalt are especially dangerous because they can produce clean, deep cuts.

Carry:

- Waterproof dressings.
- Antiseptic wipes or solution in sealed packets.
- Sterile gauze.
- Cohesive bandage.
- Wound closure strips.
- Small trauma dressing.
- Disposable gloves.

Clean wounds immediately, keep them covered, and monitor for redness, swelling, heat, pain, or fever. If infection develops, leave the basin and seek care.

### Respiratory and allergy protection

The air may contain fungal spores, plant spores, mineral mist, or geothermal gases. There are no insects, but there may still be microscopic irritants.

Bring:

- A lightweight mask or respirator.
- Antihistamines if you are allergy-prone.
- Any inhalers or respiratory medications you require.

If you see pale fungal blooms, dusty spore structures, or fine mineral deposits, do not lean close and inhale.

## 11. Medical kit: prepare for damp, altitude, cuts, and isolation

A basic kit should include:

- Pain and fever relievers.
- Anti-inflammatory medication if appropriate.
- Electrolyte powder or tablets.
- Oral rehydration salts.
- Anti-diarrheal medication.
- Anti-nausea medication if needed.
- Antihistamines.
- Antifungal cream or powder.
- Antibacterial wound care, used responsibly.
- Blister care.
- Waterproof bandages.
- Tweezers and small scissors.
- Pulse oximeter.
- Thermometer.
- Personal medications.
- Emergency medical information in waterproof form.
- A small sharps container if you use injectables.

If possible, choose plant-based or synthetic medical products rather than animal-derived capsules, gelatin, or collagen dressings. If a life-saving medication contains animal-derived components, prioritize survival, but keep it sealed and pack out all waste.

Because the basin is remote and quiet, assume rescue will be slow. Be conservative. Do not push injuries.

## 12. Geothermal and volcanic hazards: respect the young land

The basin is newly formed. Its ridges are sharp, its gullies young, its rock faces fresh. This means beauty, but also instability.

### Unstable ground

Watch for:

- Loose gravel.
- Fresh escarpments.
- Angular rock debris.
- Newly cut gullies.
- Fractured tuff.
- Glassy seams that may be sharp or brittle.
- Warm patches on the ground.
- Soft crusts over seepage.

Use a walking staff or probe to test uncertain ground. Do not jump between stones. Do not trust a surface because it looks dry.

### Cliff faces and seepage

Cliffs that sweat are not safe resting places. Constant seepage can lubricate rock, encourage rockfall, and hide unstable seams.

Avoid:

- Sitting under cliff bases.
- Camping beneath weeping faces.
- Leaning against wet rock walls.
- Traveling close to fresh escarpments in mist or poor light.

### Geothermal water and gases

The warmth may come from geothermal seepage. That can be harmless, but it can also mean:

- Hot pockets.
- Acidic or alkaline water.
- Sulfur smells.
- Carbon dioxide pooling in low areas.
- Hydrogen sulfide exposure.
- Mineral staining and skin irritation.

Be cautious around warm pools, steaming seeps, and low hollows with little air movement.

Carry a small personal gas detector if possible. If not, rely on symptoms and smells.

Leave immediately if you notice:

- Rotten-egg smell.
- Sudden headache.
- Dizziness.
- Nausea.
- Shortness of breath unrelated to exertion.
- Eye or throat irritation.
- Unusual warmth underfoot.

Move to higher, more open ground. Do not enter low steam-filled depressions.

## 13. Navigation: use landmarks, not only instruments

The basin is open and visible from many points, which helps. But wet stone, mist, and magnetic basalt can confuse simple navigation.

### Landmarks to learn

Before entering, identify:

- The rim of the basin.
- The central wetlands.
- Major causeways.
- Terraced slopes.
- Prominent cliff faces.
- Raised platforms.
- Large cisterns or sluice structures.
- The direction of water flow.

Water generally moves from springs and slopes toward lower marshes and central pools. Streams are young and may run straight. If you become disoriented, following water downhill may lead you into marsh, so use that knowledge carefully. In doubt, move toward built terraces or causeways, not into central wetlands.

### Instruments

Bring:

- A waterproof map.
- A notebook and pencil.
- A watch or timepiece.
- A compass, but do not rely on it completely near basalt.
- A personal locator or multiverse beacon, protected from moisture.
- If available, inertial or local positioning devices.

Protect all navigation tools in waterproof cases. Write notes with pencil or waterproof ink. Avoid loose paper that can become wet pulp.

### Movement rules

- Prefer causeways, stairs, and built paths.
- Avoid central marsh unless absolutely necessary.
- Do not shortcut switchbacks or terraces.
- Do not cross open water of unknown depth.
- Use a staff to test footing.
- Move one at a time across narrow or slippery structures.
- Stop before you are tired.
- Do not travel in mist unless you know the route well.
- Return before dark.

The city is sparse, so help may not be nearby. Navigate conservatively.

## 14. Movement technique: walk as if every step matters

The basin is not violently dangerous, but it punishes carelessness.

### On wet stone

- Take short steps.
- Keep your center of gravity over your feet.
- Avoid polished or glossy surfaces.
- Avoid dark algal mats.
- Place the full sole of your boot down rather than leaning on the edge.
- Use your staff for balance.
- Do not pivot sharply on wet rock.

### On stairs

Many stairways have side channels for water. Stay on the dry tread. Do not step into the side channel. Use three points of contact if the stairs are steep or slick.

### On causeways

Causeways are raised to stay passable while water moves beneath them. They are likely the safest routes across wet ground.

But:

- Do not block the causeway.
- Do not sit on narrow sections.
- Do not lean over edges into water.
- Watch for slippery stone weights or loose edging.
- Yield to water features, not the other way around.

### In marsh or shallow water

Avoid entering marsh unless required. If you must:

- Probe ahead.
- Move slowly.
- Do not trust reeds to indicate solid ground.
- Do not step on algal mats.
- Keep your pack secured but easy to release if you fall.
- Use a staff or rope line with a companion.
- Avoid deep black mud.

If you become stuck, do not thrash. Distribute your weight, use your staff, and back out slowly along the path you came.

### Falling into water

If you fall in:

- Protect your head.
- Find a stable edge.
- Avoid inhaling spray.
- Get out as soon as possible.
- Rinse off if the water may be mineral-rich.
- Dry yourself thoroughly.
- Change into dry clothes before continuing or sleeping.

Even in a warm basin, wet clothing can lead to chill and impaired judgment.

## 15. Campsite selection: choose durable ground, not beautiful ground

A good campsite in this basin is not necessarily the prettiest one. It should be:

- On bare stone, gravel, or an existing platform.
- Above standing water and overflow paths.
- Away from cliff bases.
- Away from young gullies.
- Not blocking any channel or runnel.
- Not on vegetation.
- Not in the middle of a causeway or plaza.
- Not near cisterns or sluice mechanisms unless permitted.
- Sheltered from constant drip if possible.
- Ventilated enough to avoid condensation buildup.

Before setting up, look for signs of use. If the platform seems maintained, do not assume it is abandoned. If you see tools, baskets, mats, ropes, or food-processing traces, withdraw and choose another place.

### Shelter etiquette

- Rig shelter without cutting plants.
- Do not drive stakes into stone joints or channel linings.
- Do not move existing stones to “improve” the site.
- Do not create new drainage.
- Do not dig.
- Do not leave compression marks on moss or cushion plants.
- Keep gear inside the shelter or on a groundsheet.
- Store waste in sealed containers.
- Keep food sealed.
- Keep dry bags closed.

When you leave, the site should look as if no one slept there.

## 16. Fire, heat, and light: avoid open flame if possible

The basin is wet, but reeds, dry roof mats, plant fibers, and possible marsh gases can make fire dangerous. The local culture appears to rely on simple, quiet infrastructure, not loud industry or open burning.

Best practice: avoid fire.

For food, choose ready-to-eat rations. If hot drinks are necessary, use a sealed, stable, low-flame stove only on bare stone, away from vegetation, with water nearby. Do not leave it unattended. Pack out all fuel residues.

For light, use:

- A shielded low-level lamp.
- A warm-colored LED if using electronics.
- A plant-oil lamp in a stable, enclosed container, if culturally appropriate.
- Reflectors to minimize light spill.

Avoid:

- Bright white beams.
- Flashing lights.
- Candles.
- Open torches.
- Fire signals unless in life-threatening emergency.
- Lanterns that leak fuel.

The basin’s quiet darkness is part of its character. Use light sparingly.

## 17. Technology: keep it dry, quiet, hidden, and minimal

An ordinary multiverse traveler may need advanced devices for navigation, communication, medical safety, or return transit. But in this place, technology should be subdued.

### Protect devices from moisture

Every electronic item should have:

- A waterproof case.
- A dry bag.
- Desiccant packets.
- A sealed backup container.

Open cases only inside a shelter or under cover. Do not let condensation form inside devices.

Use desiccant carefully. Do not discard spent desiccant in the basin. Seal it with waste.

### Conceal advanced gear

If local inhabitants are present, advanced devices may disturb or contaminate their cultural pattern. Keep them out of sight unless needed for safety.

Cover modern packs with woven mats or bark cloths. Hide antennas. Use earpieces instead of speakers. Avoid lights and sounds.

### Communication

Use:

- Text or written notes if possible.
- Hand signals.
- Whispered speech.
- Shielded personal communicators.
- Radio with earpiece, if necessary.

Avoid:

- Speakerphones.
- Loud alerts.
- Musical ringtones.
- Broadcasting.
- Drones.
- Active sonar or loud sensing equipment.

If you must carry an emergency beacon, set it to silent or visual emergency mode if possible. Use sound only as a last resort.

### Drones and animals

Do not bring drones unless absolutely essential and permitted. A drone would be loud, visually intrusive, and animal-like in a place without fauna. It could also disturb water surfaces, plants, and any inhabitants.

Do not bring robotic animals, surveillance creatures, or biomimetic devices. They are ethically inappropriate here.

## 18. Biosecurity: you are the invasive event

The absence of animal life makes the basin uniquely vulnerable. You cannot become non-animal, but you can reduce your biological footprint.

### Before entering

At the staging area or rim:

- Empty your pack.
- Inspect cuffs, seams, boot lugs, straps, and pockets.
- Remove seeds, lint, soil, or plant fragments.
- Brush and clean footwear.
- Check for insects or stowaways, even if you believe none exist.
- Seal all food.
- Transfer gear into clean bags if necessary.
- Ensure no animal products are visible unless medically necessary.
- Cover hair.
- Wash or sanitize hands without releasing wash water.
- Confirm that no gear from previous worlds carries soil or organisms.

If you arrived from another wetland, forest, farm, or animal-rich world, be especially careful. Your gear may carry invisible contaminants.

### While in the basin

- Do not pick plants.
- Do not touch fungal blooms.
- Do not collect spores, seeds, or algae.
- Do not move soil.
- Do not carry plant material from one terrace to another unless invited.
- Do not shake out clothing over water or vegetation.
- Do not discard hair, nails, skin flakes, or food scraps.
- Do not release stored water from another world into the basin.
- Do not leave wet gear dripping into channels.

### When leaving

Before departure:

- Pack out all waste.
- Inspect the site.
- Pick up micro-trash: crumbs, threads, wrappers, hair ties, bandage fragments.
- Dry gear as much as possible.
- Seal wet clothing and contaminated items.
- Brush footwear again at an appropriate staging point.
- Do not carry basin water, soil, plants, or organisms into another world unless authorized and sealed.

Multiverse travel can spread organisms across realities. Treat the basin’s water and mud as potentially valuable and potentially dangerous in both directions.

## 19. Behavior in the built environment: touch little, alter nothing

The city is made by hand. Its walls, channels, stairs, cisterns, weirs, and platforms are not decorative. They are functional. Even an unused-looking stone platform may be part of water management, ritual spacing, or future use.

### Do not move or adjust

Avoid touching:

- Sluice gates.
- Wooden planks.
- Rope bindings.
- Weir stones.
- Channel linings.
- Cistern covers.
- Retaining wall stones.
- Roof weights.
- Stair edge channels.
- Carved markers or statues.
- Moss-covered platforms.
- Stored tools or baskets.

If something creaks, leaks, or seems about to fail, do not automatically fix it. It may be working exactly as intended. Report it to local maintainers if possible. If there is no one to report to, leave it unless it presents immediate danger to life.

### Do not block water

Before setting down a pack, sitting, or pitching shelter, ask:

- Is this a path for water?
- Could my object divert a runnel?
- Could it dam overflow?
- Could it press against a weep hole?
- Could it force water toward vegetation or walls?

If yes, move.

### Do not fill empty spaces

The emptiness is part of the city. Open plazas, broad water gaps, unused stairways, and silent terraces are not wasteland. They are breathing room. Do not try to make the place feel more inhabited by leaving gear spread around, marking paths, or creating new sitting areas.

Leave emptiness empty.

## 20. Social conduct: silence, humility, and restraint

If there are inhabitants, they likely move slowly, speak softly, and work with patience. They may be accustomed to water, stone, and silence. They may find ordinary traveler behavior loud, clumsy, and intrusive.

### Approach

If you see people:

- Stop.
- Lower your shoulders.
- Keep hands visible and relaxed.
- Do not approach quickly.
- Do not shout.
- Do not wave broadly.
- Wait to be acknowledged.
- If possible, bow slightly or incline your head.
- Use open palms rather than pointing fingers.

If they ignore you, do not pursue. Move aside and continue quietly if passage is appropriate.

### Speech

Speak only when necessary. If you must speak, use a low, soft voice. Do not fill silence from nervousness.

If you do not know the language:

- Use slow gestures.
- Mimic observed behavior.
- Point to yourself, then to your mouth or water container only if asking for water.
- Do not make demanding gestures.
- Smile gently, but do not assume facial expressions are universally interpreted.
- Be prepared to wait.

If they speak, listen before answering. Match their volume and pace.

### Requests

If you need help, ask simply. Use gestures to indicate:

- Water.
- Passage.
- Shelter.
- Injury.
- Need to leave.

Do not ask for souvenirs, tools, plants, or access to cisterns unless invited.

### Gifts

Avoid bringing gifts unless you know the culture well. Foreign objects can become contamination or cultural disruption.

If you must offer something:

- Choose clean, plant-based, useful, and non-invasive items.
- Better yet, offer labor rather than objects.
- If invited to work, imitate exactly what is shown.
- Do not introduce advanced tools unless asked and understood.

The safest gift may be respectful restraint.

### Recording and observation

Do not photograph, record, or map people without consent. If you are uncertain, do not record. Silent observation is safer than documentation.

If you take notes, do so discreetly. If you sketch, sit in one place and avoid blocking paths.

### Cultural non-interference

Do not introduce ideas that could destabilize local practice. Do not praise or criticize their technology in ways that imply your world is superior. Do not suggest “better” methods. The city’s simplicity is not ignorance; it is adaptation.

If you see inefficiency by your standards, remember that the system may prioritize quiet, water flow, durability, and ecological balance over speed.

## 21. Daily routine: live slowly and check yourself constantly

A good day in the basin follows a disciplined rhythm.

### Morning

- Wake without noise.
- Check shelter for condensation.
- Inspect dry gear.
- Change into dry socks if needed.
- Inspect feet.
- Drink water.
- Eat sealed rations.
- Pack waste securely.
- Check weather, light, and visibility.
- Check for gas symptoms or altitude symptoms.

### Movement

- Travel only when necessary.
- Use established routes.
- Move slowly.
- Pause often to listen and observe.
- Keep gear tight and quiet.
- Avoid vegetation.
- Avoid water channels.
- Keep your group close but not crowded.

### Midday

- Rest on durable stone.
- Hydrate.
- Check feet again if feet are wet.
- Do not nap directly on wet ground.
- Keep dry sleep clothing dry.

### Evening

- Return before dark.
- Set up shelter carefully.
- Dry what can be dried.
- Seal waste.
- Eat without creating crumbs.
- Perform a gear check.
- Review route and next day.
- Keep voices low.

### Night

- Use minimal light.
- Avoid leaving shelter unless necessary.
- If you must go out, use a staff and move slowly.
- Do not approach water features in darkness.
- Keep waste containers sealed.
- Listen for changes in water or wood sounds.

The basin may feel timeless. Keep a schedule anyway.

## 22. Sound discipline: your noise is a form of pollution

The silence is not empty. It is composed of small sounds: water beads, dripping leaves, reeds whispering, wood creaking, stone ticking. Human noise can feel shockingly intrusive.

Prepare by:

- Wrapping gear in soft cloth.
- Removing bells, charms, or loose metal.
- Tightening straps so they do not flap.
- Avoiding crinkly outer layers.
- Choosing quiet containers.
- Not setting metal or stone down hard.
- Avoiding laughter, shouting, or sudden exclamations.
- Coughing into a cloth if possible.
- Using hand signals instead of calling out.

If you drop something, do not curse loudly. Pause, retrieve it if safe, and continue.

Your voice carries across water. Use it as if each word costs something.

## 23. Mental preparation for the absence of animals

The strangest part for many travelers will not be the water, but the lack of animal life.

There will be:

- No birds.
- No insects.
- No fish visible.
- No worms in mud.
- No tracks.
- No nests.
- No bones.
- No calls.
- No swarms.
- No movement except water, plants, wind, and perhaps people.

This can produce several reactions:

- A feeling of being watched.
- A sense of wrongness.
- Loneliness.
- Hyper-awareness of your own body.
- The urge to speak just to prove you exist.
- Auditory illusions.
- Emotional reverence or sadness.

Prepare for these. Remind yourself that the silence is equilibrium, not hostility.

Ground yourself by noticing:

- The sound of a single drop.
- The shine of wet basalt.
- The slow movement of water in a runnel.
- The texture of stone.
- The temperature of the air.
- The rhythm of your breath.

Do not try to fill the basin with human presence. Let your presence be minor.

## 24. Safety around plants and fungi

The vegetation is lush but restrained. Plants here are not riotous, but they are alive and fragile.

### Do not disturb

Avoid:

- Stepping on moss.
- Crushing cushion plants.
- Breaking reeds.
- Pulling roots.
- Disturbing algal mats.
- Touching fungal blooms.
- Collecting seeds or spores.
- Picking rare green growth from rock.

Flowers may be rare or absent. Do not expect color. The palette is green, black, rust, pale stone, and fungal bloom. Respect that restraint.

### Allergies and spores

Plants may reproduce by wind, water, vegetative spread, or simple seeding. Fungi may be important in decomposition. Spores may be present.

If you are sensitive:

- Wear a mask near fungal growth.
- Avoid leaning over decomposing plant matter.
- Do not stir black mud.
- Keep antihistamines accessible.

### Food plants

Local edible plants may include tubers, reed shoots, seeds, algae, cultivated mosses, or wet-tolerant grains. Do not harvest them unless invited and taught. Some may require careful preparation. Some may absorb minerals or geothermal contaminants.

## 25. Emergency planning: expect quiet emergencies, not dramatic ones

Emergencies in this basin are unlikely to involve floods, storms, predators, or violent water. They are more likely to involve:

- Slips and falls.
- Cuts from sharp stone.
- Infected wounds.
- Foot maceration.
- Altitude sickness.
- Gas exposure.
- Getting lost in mist.
- Wet gear and chill.
- Contamination mistakes.
- Psychological strain.

### Basic emergency kit

In addition to the medical kit, carry:

- Emergency shelter or bivvy.
- Extra dry layer.
- Chemical or physical water purification backup.
- High-calorie emergency rations.
- Waterproof map.
- Beacon or locator.
- Visual signal mirror.
- Small roll of cordage.
- Repair tape or patches.
- Whistle, but use only in true emergency.
- Compact oxygen supply if altitude is severe and evacuation uncertain.
- Gas detector if possible.

### If injured

- Move to durable high ground if possible.
- Stop bleeding.
- Keep the wound dry.
- Splint or stabilize if needed.
- Activate beacon if the injury prevents safe exit.
- Choose an extraction point on open stone or a platform, not in marsh.

### If lost

- Stop.
- Do not enter marsh or climb cliffs.
- Find a durable place to wait.
- Use map and landmarks.
- If visibility is poor, wait rather than moving dangerously.
- Use visual signals sparingly.
- Conserve water and food.

### If exposed to gas

- Move uphill or toward open air.
- Avoid low pockets.
- Do not return for gear if symptoms are strong.
- Monitor breathing.
- Exit the basin if symptoms persist.

### If gear becomes soaked

- Protect sleep gear first.
- Move to shelter.
- Dry essential items using body heat, absorbent cloth, or ventilated shelter.
- Do not sacrifice dry sleep clothing for daytime comfort.
- If you cannot dry enough to sleep safely, shorten the visit.

### If you contaminate the environment

If you accidentally spill waste, drop food, or introduce foreign material:

- Stop.
- Contain it if possible.
- Collect all visible material.
- Pack out contaminated water or soil if safe and feasible.
- Do not spread it further by washing.
- Report the incident if local authorities exist.

## 26. Prepare for interaction with the city’s water infrastructure

The most important practical skill is reading waterworks.

### Canals and runnels

- Small canals branch from larger ones.
- Shallow runnels follow path edges.
- Overflow weirs lead water into marsh beds.
- Stairways may have side channels.
- Retaining walls may have weep holes.

Your rule: do not stand, sit, place gear, or camp in these features.

### Sluice gates

Wooden sluice gates may creak under pressure. They may be adjusted slowly by maintainers. Do not touch them. If a gate seems to be moving, stand clear. Do not try to stop it or help unless explicitly invited.

### Cisterns

Cisterns catch springwater before it spreads. They are likely protected. Do not open, enter, wash near, or drink directly from them unless permission is clear.

### Causeways

Causeways are raised to allow water beneath. Use them for crossing, but do not treat them as general sitting areas. They may be narrow, wet, and culturally significant.

### Terraces

Terraces may look empty but may be part of water distribution or cultivation. Stay on obvious paths. Do not walk across planted or mossy terrace surfaces unless they are clearly built for walking.

## 29. Prepare your return: do not carry the basin away

A multiverse traveler must protect both the destination and other worlds.

Before returning through any gate or transit system:

- Dry gear as much as possible.
- Seal wet clothing.
- Clean mud from boots at an appropriate staging area.
- Pack out all water, mud, plant fragments, and waste.
- Do not carry open containers of basin water unless authorized.
- Check pockets and cuffs for seeds, spores, or fragments.
- Ensure no local organism is hidden in gear.
- Report biosecurity concerns if your multiverse hub requires it.
- Sterilize gear before entering another ecosystem, but do so in a way that does not spread basin contaminants.

The basin should not be diluted into other worlds, and other worlds should not be diluted into it.

## 30. If you stay longer: learn, do not improve

If the visit extends beyond a short survey, your preparation changes. You will need more food, more waste capacity, more foot care, and more psychological discipline.

Longer-stay principles:

- Establish a routine.
- Keep a strict dry-sleep system.
- Inspect gear daily for mold or rot.
- Maintain foot hygiene obsessively.
- Monitor mental state.
- Avoid becoming complacent about silence.
- If inhabitants are present, ask before helping.
- Learn by observation before touching.
- Do not redesign water channels.
- Do not introduce new tools unless invited.
- Do not harvest plants for comfort.
- Do not create permanent camp improvements.
- Move your camp if it begins to wear the ground.

If you are invited to participate in maintenance, understand that the work is likely slow, repetitive, and subtle. You may carry baskets, clear runnels, move stones under direction, weave mats, or repair rope bindings. Do exactly what is shown. Do not optimize. Do not rush.

## 31. A visitor’s mental checklist

Before entering, memorize something simple:

2. I am the animal here.
3. I carry in, I carry out.
4. I do not block, divert, or contaminate.

## 32. Final intuition: prepare to be as light as rain

The basin-city will not feel dangerous in the usual way. It will feel warm, wet, clean, spacious, and extremely still. The danger is that its gentleness may make you careless. The water does not rage, but it governs. The stone does not loom, but it cuts. The silence does not threaten, but it exposes every sound you make. The city does not crowd you, but it asks you to respect its emptiness.

An ordinary human traveler should prepare to become small, clean, and careful. Bring plant-based clothing. Keep dry gear sacred. Treat water with suspicion and reverence. Pack out every scrap of waste. Move slowly on wet stone. Speak rarely. Do not touch the waterworks. Do not disturb the plants. Do not bring animal products, animals, or animal habits. Keep your technology quiet and hidden. Prepare for altitude, damp heat, fungal spores, sharp volcanic rock, and possible geothermal gases. Expect no rescue. Expect no audience. Expect no dramatic events.

Above all, prepare to visit without adding yourself to the place. The best traveler here would be one who passes through the high wet city and leaves only the temporary impression of careful feet on stone, soon washed away by the next quiet drip.
